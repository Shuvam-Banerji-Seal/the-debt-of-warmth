I keep the barley measure and the seed account for the six houses at Cold Acre, and nobody asked me for this. I write because the well gave water on the ninety-fourth day of the ice and no slate was set at it, and a thing nobody enters somebody will come at last to enter. He stood with his hand on the well-rope and spoke first to the rope, then to the stone lip, the dish, the ridge, and the sky above the ridge, and last of all to us, which is the order a well-man uses when six households are waiting with their dishes and somebody may be listening who wants a figure. I have set his words down in Grathic, with the verbs where he left them, because a saying said over water is a thing a house has to use.

The lime at the head came off the lip when he set his hand on it to brush the frost away. The frost-crisp in the gutter is from the night I left the bark under the eaves. The ring is a woman's dish that stood from the first voice to the last and was never drunk from. The grey came off a cuff and I have not swept it. The rest is set down at the end.

# The manner of the drawing, as the well has it

*Written small, from watching, not from any order the Court has printed.*

- Come at first grey, before the house fires are stirred. A man who comes at noon draws the day's warmth off the lip with his shadow and is none the wiser.
- Set the dish on the stone, not in the water. A dish set in the water is a thing left to be fetched, and a well that is fetched at twice gives the same water, which is a kind of insult if you have stood here thirty winters.
- Let the bucket down on the chain. Eleven links go to the water. The twelfth hangs in your hand and never wets. Do not straighten it, and do not say where you think the water would be if it did.
- Draw what the day wants. A bucket left standing on the lip draws nothing at all, and the level in the well is the same at the end of the winter as it was at the beginning, which I have watched and cannot explain and have not tried to.
- Taste before you carry. It tastes of stone and a little of iron and nothing else. Do not say what it tastes of where a man with a slate is standing.
- When the bell comes up the ridge from the Cross on a still morning, stop until it has gone. It is counting something that is not your well. Four, four and a gap, and the gap is not ours to fill.
- There is no ash in the corner here. The well has no corner. On the Last Day we leave the grey in the houses down the ridge and come up and draw, and that is the whole of our observance and the end of it.

# The sermon, as he said it

## Of the well

He put his palm flat on the lip and left it there while the rope swung against the stone.

“It does not freeze,” he said. “The Ilm has lain under ice for ninety-four days and the well has given every morning of them. I put my wrist in on the morning the ice went and it was the same water. It is not warm; do not go home and tell your households it is warm. It is water that has not given up, which is a different thing and a thing I have never seen the like of.”

A priest come up from Greybank in the first winter of the ice had called it a little sister of the Nine Wells and a mercy of the Hearth drawn through the ridge. I have set that down because I heard it with my own ears and because it is comfortable. The well has never given a sign that it heard him.

## Of the twelfth link

“Eleven go down and one hangs dry,” he said, and held the chain up so the women could see which was which. “A man from the College stood here in the autumn and told me the twelfth is kept back for the Hearth. I said the water comes up on the eleventh and the twelfth hangs in my hand, and if the Hearth wants its share let it send a man with a longer arm. He said one in twelve is printed on the notice and is not a lie. I said a printed thing travels and a chain does not. Both stood in the air between us, and neither went into the other.”

A girl from the upper house asked what the dry link was for, and he did not give her a year, as the women of Sill do when asked about a mark they would rather not explain. He said: “Lower the bucket. You will see which links go in.” She counted eleven and said nothing more, which is the only answer this ridge is good at.

> *Margin, in my hand:* He said the sentence about the printed notice twice, once to the well and once to the man with the slate, and I have not joined the two sayings. A man who uses one word for two things has a use for the word.

## Of them that come up the ridge

“Three times an assessor has come up from Greybank since I took the rope,” he said. “He measures the lip with a cord, asks how deep it goes, asks who pays for the drawing, and sets a figure in a column of his slate. The column has never had anything in it. He does not leave it empty out of kindness. He leaves it empty because there is nowhere in the house of the world where a figure like that would stand.

“He asked what the well was worth. I told him I had never sold it. He asked what it cost to keep. I told him it keeps itself and us, and he wrote that down too, though I do not think he heard it.

“What I fear is not that they will carry the well away; a well cannot be carried. I fear the figure. Once a figure stands at a thing, the thing is a gift, and a gift can be refused, and a man who refuses water in a winter like this has paid for it somewhere else, where nobody was standing to see.”

## Of them that are Kept

“They tell us the Kept are blessed and feel no cold,” he said. “There is one in the Hearthseat who cannot feel this ridge, and I have never met him. The well does not feel it either, and I have met it every morning for two and thirty winters. That is the whole of the likeness. Carry it further and you will come back with a doctrine, and a doctrine is an account that has found somebody to pay it.”

He said this to the ridge and not to the women, and I have written it as it was said and not as it was meant.

## Of the dead and the drawing

“Six houses draw here and six have buried their dead down at the river,” he said. “A pot is broken at the door and the feet are set to the water, and the well draws for the house that is left behind. It has never asked which house. I have looked into the bucket every morning of my life and never once seen a name come up in it.

“At the Weighing they read a whole return in a clean voice for a person not in the room. Here a woman comes up with a dish and drinks standing, because she would not sit down in her own house since the cough came on her. I have not put the two sayings together. They are two rooms, and the well is a third.”

## Of the close

“The measure is banked. The account stands,” he said, and I could hear he had had those words from a man in a clean coat and did not like the feel of them. “That is said over a slate in a room I have never stood in. Here the water comes up and goes into a dish and somebody drinks it, and there is no third sentence. If the Hearth wants a twelfth of this water, let it send a man with a bucket up the ridge in the dark. It has not sent one, and I have drawn here two and thirty winters.”

He crossed two fingers on the stone lip then, as hearth-maids cross them over a birth-flower, and covered the act with nothing at all, not even a date. I have written the act and left the covering where it was.

# The drawing-song

*Sung low, standing, with the dish on the lip and a hand on the rope. The row says the last four lines with him. There is a gap before those four, the one the bell leaves when it comes up the ridge, and I have left it here as it is sung.*

> Eleven links to the water, one hangs dry;
> the well gives what the bucket carries;
> the ridge keeps snow and the lip keeps frost,
> and the water keeps neither, and comes up.
>
> Six for the houses, six for the dead,
> and a place where the thirteenth would stand;
> the bell comes up and the bell goes down,
> and the gap is not ours to fill.
>
> Draw what the day wants and leave the rest;
> a dish set in the water is a dish left to fetch;
> what it keeps it gives, and what it gives it keeps,
> and no slate has stood at the lip.
>
> The twelfth link hangs where no water goes;
> say it is kept, or say it is spare;
> say what it did and not what it meant,
> and carry the dish to the house that asked.

A woman at the well asked what the place where the thirteenth would stand was for. Nobody answered her. The clerk put a round nought against it later, which I have left where he set it.

# The column, as the slate would have it

*I keep this because the figure that arrives at the well in a coat goes down the ridge again in the same coat and leaves nothing on the stone. These I have set down where they stood.*

| When | What was drawn | What was entered |
|---|---|---|
| The ninety-four days, Y.H. 811 into 812 | Water for six houses, morning and evening | nothing |
| The morning the ice went | A dish for the woman with the cough | nothing |
| The levy week in Stone, Y.H. 812 | Water for the house that kept a child not its own | nothing |
| This morning, after the assessor | The same water | a figure struck out, a column standing |

There were other mornings and Nils could set a winter to each of them, and I have not written them, because a row of winters is a thing a clerk can carry away and a dish of water is not.

# What the clerk did

An assessor's clerk come up from Greybank to measure the winter wells borrowed the bark on the morning after, with his cuff soft at the edge from washing and ink in two fingers, and gave it back with the verbs taken out.

- Where Nils said *the well gives what the bucket carries*, he wrote *the measure is retained in the spring and is lawfully drawn* above and left the old words below.
- Where he said *the twelfth hangs dry*, he wrote *one part in twelve reserved to the Hearth* and underlined it twice. He did not ask to see the chain.
- At the column he put a round nought and wrote *vacancy*, and when I asked what a column was worth with nothing under it, he said nothing a clerk could enter, which is the first true thing I have heard out of that coat.
- At *I have never seen a name come up in the bucket* he wrote *third party unidentifiable; nil*, a true thing said by a man who has never drawn water in the dark.

I have put the verbs back underneath and left his hand standing above, as the well leaves the old water showing under the new. He takes them out because a thing always true has no need of verbs. I put them back because a woman at first grey wants to know who is doing the giving, and whether it was her own hand that lowered the bucket.

# At the head and in the gutter

The lime at the head is not salt and has never been entered. The frost-crisp came up out of the bark itself under the eaves, and the writing there is thin and I have not gone over it, because a word written thin is a word said quietly, and most of ours are. The ring is the woman's dish, taken away still full. The awn in the fold is barley and has not sprouted. The grey print came off a cuff and nobody will own it; there is no Last Day on this roof and nothing gets swept. The tar on the tie is off the rope that has lowered eleven links into the water every morning for two and thirty winters and has never once lowered twelve.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The well asks nothing. They come up the ridge to look at it all the same.
>
> The twelfth link has never wet, and the water has never failed.

I asked Nils, the woman who kept the dish, a girl from the upper house, and the clerk, and had four answerings and no hand. I have left the line struck through and standing, which is the only honest way to keep a sentence you cannot sign.

The bark goes back in the fold and the fold under the thatch above the beam. Next winter the ice will come on the Ilm and the well will give as it gives; the clerk will come up from Greybank and take the verbs out. I shall put them back and leave the column the length it is.
