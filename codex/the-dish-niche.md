I keep the dish-shelf at the shrine off the Cross, and nobody asked me for this. I write because he came back thin and keeps the dishes now, and because a factor's clerk took the verbs out of his sayings before the week was out, and a saying with the verbs taken out travels well and arrives empty. He spoke first to the dish in his hand, then to the water in it, then to the shelf and the mark on the shelf, and last of all to us, which is the order a man uses when he has come back from a country he will not describe. I have set his words down in Grathic, with the verbs where he put them, because a saying said over water is a thing a household has to use before the water goes into the ground.

The tallow at the head is mine; I held the notice down in the draught under the door and did not know I had marked it. The silt came up on the shrine step in the flood and dried in the grain. The pale chip in the fold is off a dish's lip. The wax with the hair in it fell from the shrine candle as he spoke. The grey came off a cuff and nobody will own it; I have not swept it, and there is no Last Day behind a shelf.

# The manner of the dishes, as the niche keeps it

*Written small, from watching, not from any order the Temple has printed.*

- Come at first grey, before the ovens are stirred. A woman who comes at noon sets her dish on a shelf other women have breathed on and calls it the same shelf. It is not.
- Set the dish lip to the wall on its own mark. Not on the ground. The ground is for what is poured out and the shelf for what was brought.
- Skim the surface with your thumb. Do not drink, and do not let a child drink, though children ask.
- Pour it out at the foot of the wall, not into the Ilm. The river carries what it is given where no household can follow. The wall takes nothing and stands where it stood.
- Wipe the dish and set it back on its mark. A wiped dish is not a clean dish.
- Say nothing over the water. A word said over water comes up in another mouth and nobody here can tell you whose.
- When the bell comes over the Cross, stop. Four, four and a gap; the gap is not ours to fill.
- Leave the place at the end of the row as it is. Do not set a dish there to make the row come out even. A mark is not a dish and cannot be entered.

He said the last twice, once to the shelf and once to a woman from the kilns. I have set the sayings down as one because the shelf does not answer.

# The sermon, as he said it

## Of the dish

 He took the dish from the shelf and turned it so the row could see the mark worn under the thumb.

“This is not a measure,” he said. “It holds what is put into it and gives it back when it is poured out. A measure goes up the road in a box and comes back as a figure. This goes nowhere. I have kept this shelf two winters and never carried a dish past the Cross.”

He set it down with the lip to the wall, the way the women had set theirs.

“There is a man in a clean coat who tells us nothing is lost that we have kept. I have heard him say it at the Weighing and once over a person not in the room. I believe the first half. The keeping is here: a chip in the rim, a mark under the thumb, kept by a woman with wet sleeves. The other half I cannot put my hand to.”

> *Margin, in my hand:* He said *the keeping is here* twice, once to the shelf and once looking at his own hands. I have not joined the two sayings. A man who uses one word for two things has a use for the word.

## Of the water

 He lifted the dish a little and let the light go across the surface.

“There is generally silt,” he said. “The roof is old and the yard is clay and the wind comes off the terrace and puts its dust in everything. I skim it with my thumb and pour it at the foot of the wall and wipe the dish and set it on its mark. That is the whole of my preaching and I have two winters to find another sentence and could not.

“A priest come up from Greybank told me the water is a keeping of the Hearth and that the Hearth knows what is poured into it. I asked him where the water went when it left the dish. He said it was received. A man says *received* when he has not stood at this wall in the dark. I have stood here two winters and the wall has never once been wet above the stone.”

A girl asked what became of the silt on his thumb. He said it went into the ground with the water and had been in the ground before it was on his thumb, which is a difficult sentence for a girl to carry home, and she did not try.

## Of the figure

 “They read one in twelve this year in a clean voice and nobody here challenged it,” he said. “It is printed and it is true and it is said to a room that is not this one. I asked a man from the College how much of this dish was the Hearth's. He gave me the printed figure, as though a twelfth of a dish of water were a thing a man could carry away in his fist and set on a shelf elsewhere.

“I asked him again which part went. He gave me a year. A date is an answer that does not spoil in the mouth, and the Court likes a date and does not ask what it covers. I have set his year in the margin and left my question under it.

“They call the rest of the dish a return. I do not know what word they have for what is poured at the foot of a wall, and I have never heard one out of that coat.”

> *In the narrow upright hand of a factor's clerk who came up from Greybank on the second morning and borrowed the leaf while I was at the door:* A receptacle of retained measure; retention and diversion are lawful conditions of a vessel. Enter as nil.
> *
> *Underneath, in mine:* I have put *dish* and *water* and *poured* back under his. A sentence with the verbs taken out will go a long way in a cuff and arrive with nothing in it, which is what a man wants who has to carry it to a table.

## Of the name

 “Do not say a name over the dish,” he said. “A woman comes up with a dish and wants to say who it is for, and I tell her not to. Said over the water, a name goes down with it and comes up in another mouth.

“At the Weighing they read a whole return for a person not in the room. I was in that room once and they read nothing for me, and I was not asked what I wanted read. I do not say the Return is a lie. I say I have stood on both sides of that sentence and it is a different room from each side.”

 He crossed two fingers over the empty place at the end of the row then, as the hearth-maids cross them over a birth-flower, and covered the act with a date, which is what people here do when asked about a mark they would rather not explain.

> *In my hand:* I asked him what the year meant and he gave me Y.H. 747, the year of the Cold Pass agreement. I asked again in another way and he gave me the same year. The clerk has set a different year above it, and both stand, and the empty place stands under both of them and has no dish in it.

## Of them that come back

 He turned the dish a quarter and set it back where it had stood.

“I came back thin,” he said. “They say it low at the Weighing and loudly in the north, where they buy it. I have heard both prices. A thin return is not a person mended; it is a person with less in him and a hankering for a table he never sat at.

“The nine who went after what had run away did a work, and I do not dispute it. But a man came back here and nobody sent for him, and he wipes dishes with cold fingers that do not answer as they used to. I will not preach that. A doctrine is an account that has found somebody to pay it, and I have no figure to put under mine.

“I keep the shelf because a man who has come back knows which way the water goes.”

He spoke the last to the dish and not to the women, and I have written it as it was said and not as it was meant.

# The skimming-song

*Said low, standing, with the thumb on the rim and the dish set on its mark. The row says the last four lines with him. There is a gap before those four, the length of a dish being wiped dry after the water has gone into the ground, and I have left it here as it is sung.*

> The dish goes up at the going of the light,
> the water stands all night and does not stir;
> at first grey I skim it with a thumb
> and pour it where the wall will not drink.
>
> The shelf keeps the mark and the dish goes home;
> there is a place at the end where none stands;
> four go back and the fifth is a mark,
> and the mark is not ours to fill or to count.
>
> They read a figure over a room I know;
> the figure goes up the road in a coat.
> I keep the water that never went up
> and the place where a dish would have stood.
>
> *Here the dish is wiped. No sound.*
>
> Say what it did and not what it meant;
> set the rim down where the rim was worn;
> the man who came back does not ask the wall
> what it did with the water he poured.

A girl from the kilns asked what the space before the last four lines was for. Nobody answered her. The clerk put a round nought against it later, which I have left where he set it.

# The dish-row, as the shelf keeps it

*The figure that arrives here in a coat goes down the Clay Road again in the same coat and leaves nothing on the stone. These I have set down where they stood.*

| Dish | Set by | What stood in it at morning | What was entered |
|---|---|---|---|
| The chipped milk dish | A woman of Low Kiln, for her child | Water gone grey with fine slip-dust; not drunk | Nothing |
| The dish beneath the shelf's end | Nobody came to claim it | Clear water, skimmed once and poured at the wall | Nothing |
| The dish with the twist of grey wool under it | A neighbour who would not give a name | Water with a hair in it; the hair taken up on the thumb | Nothing |
| The empty place at the row's end | Nobody | A mark worn in the wood where a dish once stood | A round nought; vacancy |

There were dishes before these that he could set a winter to, and I have not written the winters, because a row of years is a thing a clerk can carry away and a dish is not. The shelf has never agreed with the factor's book and has never been asked to.

# What the clerk did

He came up from Greybank on the second morning with ink in two fingers, borrowed the leaf while I held the door, and gave it back with the verbs taken out.

- At *this is not a measure*: *a vessel is a lawful receptacle of retained measure*.
- At *the wall has never once been wet above the stone*: *retained and expended; no charge against the keeping*, underlined twice. He did not ask to see the wall.
- At *I came back thin*: *returnee classified thin; figure withheld pending assessment*, a true thing said by a man who has never been poured out of one country into another.
- At the empty place a round nought and *vacancy*. When I asked what a place was worth with no dish in it, he said nothing a clerk could enter, which was the first true thing I heard out of that coat.

I have put the verbs back underneath and left his hand standing above, as the shelf leaves the old mark under the new dish. He takes them out because a thing always true has no need of verbs. I put them back because a woman at first grey with a dish in her hand wants to know who is doing the pouring.

# At the head and in the gutter

The tallow will not come out of the grain. The silt has eaten the edge off two words at the foot, which I have not written in again, because a saying with two words gone is a saying said slowly, and most of ours are. The pale chip is clay off a rim wiped one time too many. The grey wool is mine and I tied it for the word I could not put down, and I will not untie it. The ring stood from the first voice to the last and was never drunk from. The wax is from the candle and the hair in it is grey. The Ash came off a cuff and nobody will own it; there is no Last Day behind this shelf and nothing gets swept.

After he went, the women poured their dishes out at the foot of the wall and set them back on their marks, and the empty place was left as it was found. His words went down the Clay Road in a clean coat with a correction on them. The water went into the ground and the dishes went home.

# At the foot

Struck through once and written again underneath, very small, in a hand that is not mine, not his and not the clerk's:

> What goes into the ground is not lost and is not kept.
>
> The mark at the end of the row has no dish to enter.

I asked the woman from the kilns, the girl who asked about the gap, a carter under the Cross and the clerk, and had four answerings and no hand. I have left the line struck through and standing, which is the only honest way to keep a sentence you cannot sign.

The leaf goes back behind the shelf with the thread at its tie. Next Weighing one in twelve will stand unchallenged and the women will set their dishes on their marks, and the clerk will come up from Greybank and take the verbs out. I shall put them back and leave the empty place the length it is.
