I did not mean to keep a book. A woman came over the mud road in the lean week with the thumb of her right hand split down the side by a peat-knife, and asked what I put on such a thing, and I answered her in the short way my mother had before I had decided whether it was true. A thing said before it has been looked at is a thing that has gone a long while without being looked at. I have looked at some of mine. I have left the rest standing where they stood, because a woman at three in the morning wants a hand held steady more than she wants a correction.

The peat gets under the nail in this hamlet and stays there through the washing. It is the only thing in Mud End that keeps a better count than I do. I write on the back of a tally a wet thumb spoiled at the moot. The figures went into the byre and out of it and nobody charged them, which is a mercy of a kind here, where a figure that reads is a figure that travels. The brown at the head is off my own thumb. The grey in the gutter came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying; I have not swept it, and there is no Last Day in a peat byre.

# The order of the byre

*Written small on the first leaf, with one word gone over in silt off the Ilm.*

- Set the child on dry sacking and never on peat. Peat holds water, and a wet child cools faster than a dry one, which every woman here knows and no man come down from Sill has ever written down.
- Turn the slate face down before you go to the fold. I keep no slate-name and never shall. A child is not an entry, and a name written before the face is looked at is a name chosen by somebody who was not in the room.
- Try the stone on your own wrist before it goes to a belly. It came off somebody's peat fire, and somebody in these nine houses cut the peat and carried it and never called it a gift.
- When the red branch shows, cross two fingers over it and say the word the mother gives. Do not wash it. I have seen a washed one and an unwashed one and have not joined them.
- If a man comes up the mud road with a cold tube and a slate, give him the mother and keep the child behind the pail. This is not refusal. It is the order of the room.
- Keep a place at the fire for the ninth house. It is the farthest from the road, and a woman from it will have walked the longest before she calls you.
- When the fourth morning comes, let the woman do the counting. A number counted for her belongs to whoever counted it, and there is no figure in this byre to give away.

Against the pail the physician wrote, *a wet child cools faster than a dry one; true, and of no use where there is no dry sacking.* I have put my line above his, because a woman with no sacking still has a hand.

# The remedies, as they are used

| Trouble | What is set to it | The manner of it | Who carries it |
|---|---|---|---|
| The byre-cough | Steam of barley-water under a cloth, honey if the house has it | We say the smoke of our own peat cannot hurt a throat that has breathed it since childhood. A physician come down from Sill says it is the smoke, and the only physic is a high chimney, which nine houses cannot build. I sent two down to Sill; both came back worse for the dust of the road. Both stand. | The byre, which cannot stop and does not ask. |
| A peat-knife cut | Tallow, then a clean fold, never the grey from the corner | The women put grey from the corner in a cut to dry it, believing the grey is clean. I dressed four such and found grey under the skin in all four, and could not get it out. The physician says the grey is not clean, and writes it in a hand that scores the leaf. | The hand, in a white line it keeps. |
| The child come up drowned, in the Breaking | Lay belly-down across a warm sack, turn with the forearm, clear the reed from the mouth, warmth to the middle, a watch | We hold such a child by the heels and let the water run out, which is what our mothers did. A physician from Sill says the water in the belly is not what kills, and that there is a manner of turning I do not know. I held six by the heels and turned two; one of the two breathed by morning. I have not written which house the one came from. | The river, which takes what the Court does not count and enters nothing. |
| The sweet line | Sour barley-water, wood-ash at the gum | We do not taste our glaze in Mud End. The green comes down from the Glaze Room at High Kiln with a girl who says a sweet answers a sweet. The physician says lead. I have seen the line go off one who left that room and come back on one who stayed. I have not seen the lead. | The glaze room, which cannot stop and does not ask. |
| The birth-flower | Nothing. Two fingers crossed, and the word the mother gives | A woman of the ninth house washed one in salt water and it went the redder; four I did not wash went pale in nine days. I have set the one beside the four and not joined them. When asked what it is I give the day of the week and no more, because a day does not spoil in the mouth. | No account has named it. The child's skin, if a body that cannot speak can pay. |
| The chill that follows the warming | Warmth at the middle, barley-water, a watch to the fourth morning; we walk them still | It comes after the warming, not before. The physician says a warmed body spends faster and walking faster still, and that the child should be held and counted with. I walked fourteen and held six; of the walked three went thin, of the held one went thin and one did not rise. I kept the tallies on a strip of willow under the straw and never added them. A tally summed is a tally that can be carried off the mud road. | The household, one night's sleep; sometimes the child. |
| The sleep that is called merciful | Nothing. Leave the damper as it was found | A man of the ninth house who had worked the kilns at Sill came home and slept with the damper shut, and they that found him called it merciful. The physician says a shut damper and a sleeping man make a death and not a mercy, and writes it twice. I have left his sentence above mine and my word underneath, where a woman at three in the morning will find them. | The house, which did not know the damper had been shut. |

Nothing here is free. It passes from one house to another and is called a cure, and the peat that carries it is paid for by whoever cut it. That is the only sentence I will stand behind.

# The note under the knuckle

The women at the drying racks in Sill tap a green pot before it goes to the kiln. Sound answers full, cracked answers flat, a small dead sound that stops at the lip. I borrowed the trick from them, as every trade borrows from the trade beside it.

I tapped a man once, gone thin after a thin return. The flat I thought I heard was not in him. A body is not a pot, though we set pots on the bench in the shape of bellies. There is no clock in Mud End, and the pot on the bench knows more about whether the night took the room than any slate up the road.

# The moot byre

The moot sits in the driest byre of the nine houses, and fines stand there as they stand at the Post, until the mud is held to have paid them. Last winter one stood three months and was paid in three days' turf, and nobody entered what the turf cost the man who cut it. A reeve come up the marsh road said a fine paid in peat is a fine that has been stood on and walked off. I have set his sentence beside the slate.

# The byre-song

*Sung low to the peat and not to the person, sitting with the child on the knee and the wool drawn up. Said to the floor, the sack or the byre door; said looking at a person it becomes an argument. The tenth place is held the length of a hand going into the pail and coming out empty. Nobody fills it.*

> Nine for the houses and one for the road,
> the peat says dry and the child says cold;
> lay it on sacking and never on the floor,
> and say the word to the byre door.
>
> The knife goes in and the grey goes round,
> the sweet at the gum comes down from the town;
> the line goes west and the line comes east
> and the glaze room never asks what it cost.
>
> Warm the middle and wrap the end,
> let her count, do not count for her;
> the damper stood as the damper stands
> and the house said merciful and did not look.
>
> Nine I have kept and the tenth I hold
> for the child with no house and no name.

I changed one line in three-and-twenty years. I sang *the byre keeps it* as I was taught, and now sing *the house said merciful*, because a byre cannot say anything and a house can say a thing that is not true. Nobody singing with me has asked why the line is different, which is how I know it wanted changing. A factor's clerk put a round nought in the tenth place and wrote *vacancy* beneath it. A nought is a figure that travels light. The tenth is a place and has to be stood in.

# What the physician from Sill put in the margin

He came down the mud road in Ash of Y.H. 813 with a book that had the word *smoke* in it and a hand that wrote without asking. Against the byre-cough he set *the smoke, and no physic but departure*; against the heels, *the water is not what kills*; against the merciful sleep he wrote *a shut damper, a sleeping man, a death*, and underlined it twice. He was right in all three and had put nothing in their place that a house of nine could pay for. I have left his sentences above mine.

# What the factor's clerk did

A clerk of the factor borrowed the leaves at Greybank when the winter turf was counted; the ink sat in two fingers of a soft cuff washed too often. He struck out my column headed *Who carries it* and wrote *No cost entered*, true in the language he writes and false in a byre where a man's thumb is split to the bone. Where I wrote that the river takes what the Court does not count, he wrote *no toll arises*, the most beautiful sentence I have ever read in a hand that has never stood in silt. I have put my column back underneath his. A clerk can write without verbs because he is never in a byre with a child that will not breathe.

# In the margin, in charcoal, a reeve's hand

> The mud road takes a day and gives no receipt. I have stood on it and still cannot tell you which boards are safe. I can tell you which houses paid. --- R.

He wrote it while I was at the peat-stack. A man who has counted other men standing is allowed one sentence in a book of physic.

# At the head and in the gutter

The peat at the head came off my thumb in a wind off the river and has worked into the grain, which is why this leaf smells of the byre in any room. The silt tideline came up the second leaf after the thaw and took two words clean off it; I have not written them back, because a remedy with two words gone is a remedy said slowly. The peat-fibre in the fold went stiff as a splinter, and the straw came off the dry bed in the moot byre. The ring of barley-water went sour over one word of the ninth row and lifted the ink; I have not written that word in either. The grey along the outer fold came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying, and I have not swept it. The green bead in the lower corner was pressed in by a girl come down from High Kiln with the sweetness at one mouth-corner; I did not ask whose glaze it was. There is a Last Day in Sill and the corners are swept and burnt there in the morning and called dealt with. There is no Last Day in a peat byre.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> Peat keeps the print of the boot and not the man.
> The slate keeps the man and not the print.
> Between the byre and the mud road a child breathes or does not, and nobody has entered the breath.

I asked a physician, a factor's clerk and a reeve come up from the Post, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The book goes back under the loose straw in the byre. Tomorrow a woman will come over the mud road with a thumb split by a peat-knife or a child that will not breathe, and I shall turn the slate face down, try the stone on my own wrist and leave the tenth where it is, because a child is not a house and the mud road has taken enough figures.
