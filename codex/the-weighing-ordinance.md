I keep the notice that stands beside the Weighing Hall. It is a board with one part in twelve printed on it, and on Weighing the year's measures are read aloud in Slate Row while the fire burns behind its grating of cold iron and no priest is allowed within a pace of it. An officer comes up from a district to hear what the year has entered against him, and I copy him out this ordinance beforehand so that he may know the reading before he hears it. The ordinance is in Ledgerhand, and there is nobody in any of its sentences. I put the people back underneath. A year read aloud with nobody in it is a year nobody lived, and that is the only kind this Hall has ever wanted entered.

The articles were gathered here after the Rising and set down in Y.H. 671, when it was found that a whole year could be read aloud in the time it takes a man to walk the length of Slate Row. They are copied forward each Weighing from the College book and not from the board, because the board is scraped clean and written again and a scraped board keeps nothing. A rule with no voice on it is a rule nobody has had to read. These have been read.

# The ordinance as it stands beside the Hall

## I. Of the day and the fire

1. Weighing is the second of the Five, between Kindling and the Long Night. It belongs to no month and to no tend, and no market is held on it.

> *Margin:* On Kindling every hearth in Sarn is lit from the fire drawn up through the Keel. I have put *every hearth* back under *the hearths are lit*, because a fire lit in a house was lit by somebody who got up in the dark to do it. The ordinance has no word for the getting up.

2. The fire behind the grating is the Hearth drawn up through the stone. It has never gone out and no priest has ever been allowed to touch it. The Kept are not asked to stand near it.

> *Margin:* One of the Kept kept his face turned toward the grating through the whole reading. He did not speak and was not asked to. I have entered neither the face nor the turning, because a face held toward a fire is not an entry, and I have no column that will hold it.

3. The year's measures are read aloud in Slate Row, in a clean voice, with the notice standing where the reader can see it and the crowd cannot.

> *Margin:* The figure I heard at the reading and the figure a man told me afterward at the foot of the Row were the same figure spoken twice. I entered it once, as the ordinance provides. The second hearing goes at the edge, where a hearing has no column.

## II. Of the figures

4. One part in twelve is printed on the notice, and that is the figure read. No other figure is read in the Hall.

5. A figure that will not agree with the notice is copied again in the same hand. What cannot be read aloud is not a figure of this year.

> *Margin:* In the thin years a different figure goes around the yards and comes up as far as the notice-board before it goes away again. I have heard it twice and set it at the edge, where it has no column and asks no question. A figure a clerk cannot weigh is not one he can enter, and a figure he cannot enter is safest where he can neither deny it nor keep it.

6. A whole return may be announced at the Weighing for a person who is not in the room. The reader does not ask where the person is.

> *Margin:* A whole return was announced for a woman who was not there, and a man standing beside me put his hand to the middle of his palm where a ring had once been worn tight and long, and kept it there through the clean voice. I entered the return. The hand went at the edge, the length of a hand.

## III. Of the survey table

7. The Vorr sit at the survey table and are not asked to translate. The Deep Hand is copied and not read.

> *Margin:* I asked one of them what the oldest numbers said. He looked at the table a while and said, *The numbers are correct.* That is one sentence and not a translation, and I have set it here because the ordinance has no place for an answer and the board has room at the edge.

8. The copy of the Deep Hand is shut in its tin and not carried beyond the Hall. What is copied in this room is not read in any district.

> *Margin:* I have watched the reader's hand go to the copy before his eye went to the stone, which is the order of this trade and not the order of a man who wants to know what a repair was made of. I have put no gloss on it. A clerk who explains a slate has begun to write a sermon, and sermons get copied.

## IV. Of the Kept and the names

9. Fewer than twenty vessels are kept in rooms near the fire. They hold three hundred measures and feel nothing. No return is due to them and no deduction is entered against them.

> *Margin:* Three hundred is a figure no household can spend. I have entered it because the notice has it and not because any man in this Hall has ever held that much warmth in his hands. A figure entered is not a figure seen.

10. The rolls of the dead are carried forward and never closed. On the Remembering, names are spoken in a clean voice, and whole returns are announced for persons not in the room.

> *Margin:* A woman stood here on the Remembering to hear a name spoken, and the clean voice announced a return instead. I entered the return and set the name at the edge, the length of it. She asked me whether the roll had closed. I told her no roll closes. That is true, and was not what she asked.

## V. Of faults and their correcting

| Fault | As entered at the Hall | What the clerk sets beneath |
|---|---|---|
| A verb in the ordinance | The page copied again, in the same hand | The verb restored where it stood |
| A figure read with feeling | The reader set back one Weighing | The person whose figure it was |
| A whole return for one not in the room | Entered as announced | The empty place where the person stood |
| A question put to the Vorr | No entry | The one sentence, at the edge |
| The Deep Hand read aloud | The page copied again | The length of the line read, and nothing of it |
| A name spoken on the Remembering | Entered as a return | The name, at the edge, the length of it |

# The form of the reading

*Set out at the end. I have copied it with the blanks as they stand. In nine Weighings no reader has filled the last line with his own name.*

> Read this day, being Weighing, in the year of the Hearth ----, the year's measures in the sum of ----, the deduction one in twelve as the notice says; the whole returns announced for ----------------------, who is not in the room; the Kept not called, the fire not touched, the survey table not translated; to be carried forward on the Opening of the Slates and not closed; read by ----------------------, whose voice was clean and who put nobody into the sentence.

# The stair-song, pasted inside the board

*Said low going up from Slate Row to the Hall on Weighing morning, never while the notice is in sight of the Steward's door, and never twice in one year.*

> Four for the fire and four for the roll,
> and a place where the fifth should stand;
> the clean voice carries what the yards gave up,
> and the figure goes where the hand cannot.
>
> One in twelve as the notice says,
> read for the moot and again for the late;
> the Kept hold three hundred and want no return,
> and a whole return comes back for a name.
>
> Up the stone stair with the slate to the sleeve,
> hand to the copy before the eye;
> the Vorr say the numbers are correct,
> which is one sentence and no reply.
>
> Four for the fire and four for the roll,
> and the gap goes up with the rest;
> the fire keeps the year and the board keeps the sum,
> and the voice keeps nothing and does not rest.

The second verse came in with the Thin Year and is not sung by the readers, who have no reason to sing it and several to keep quiet. I have copied it as it came.

# What the borrowed hands did

The College man came down from Slate Row on the morning of the reading with ink in two fingers of a cuff gone soft from washing. He struck *are read* out of article three and set *were read* above it, and above that *the measures stand recorded*, which is further from the voice than the first. I put mine back. Against the face turned toward the grating he wrote *no entry arising*, which is true of the Hall and cost him one look at the edge of the leaf. He drew a line under all my margins and wrote *No such place* above them, so that the edge would read as though it had never held anything. He had to look at every margin before he could write that there was nothing in any of them worth keeping, and I watched him do it.

The other hand is tall and leaning and comes from the Steward's room. Against *one in twelve* it wrote *the lawful deduction*, and against the whole return announced for a person not in the room, *an article of value, not entered to a person*. I have not struck either. A hand that writes whole returns for persons not in the room knows the difference between a person spoken of and a person standing there, and has made a trade of not saying which.

# In the head and in the gutter

The brown at the head is iron-gall and mine, from holding the quire open through a reading; there are clerks in Slate Row who can tell the notice-clerk by his thumb before he has spoken. The slate dust in the gutter came off a district slate laid on this board while a runner come up from the Clay Road gave her figure and looked at the fire instead of at me. The scorch in the second leaf is a spark off the grating, caught while I held the paper too near in order to read it in the draught; it has taken the corner off one line and not the sense. The grey in the fold is Ash off the skirting of a Kept room, which comes away on a sleeve and will not wash out. The curl of grey wool came off a cradle blanket carried up on the Remembering; it went through the tie-hole and I have not untied it. The tallow thumb at the foot is mine, from holding the lamp low while somebody behind me said something I wanted the end of; she did not tell me the end. The pale ring is where a collector set his tube down while he went for a cup; the tube was not used in this room and the ring is not a wound, but it is the thinnest paper on the leaf. The green bead is lead glaze, pressed in by a girl come up from High Kiln who leaned to see the form of the reading with the sweetness still at her mouth, touched the paper, said nothing, and went back down the stair. The tide-line along the foot came up out of the cold of the sealed-file room through the wall; it takes the corners off figures and leaves the figures. There is no Last Day beside a Hall. The board is scraped and written again each Weighing, and the old figures go into the fire with the sweepings, and nobody calls it dealt with.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The fire keeps the year. The notice keeps the figure. Between the two a clean voice reads a life that nobody standing in the room has to answer for.

I asked the College clerk, a man come down from the Steward's room, and a woman who stood beside me at the notice on Weighing, and had three answers and no hand, as I have had before. I have left the line where it was written.

The leaves go back into the tin on the notice-board. Tomorrow the Long Night comes and there is no fire after dusk in any room of the Hall, and on the Remembering a clean voice will announce whole returns for persons not in the room, and the roll will be carried forward with no disposition recorded. The board will be scraped for another year. I have put the verbs back where I could and left the scorch where it fell.
