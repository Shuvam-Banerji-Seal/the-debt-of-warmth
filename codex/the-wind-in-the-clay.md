I beat the clay at a bench three doors up the Clay Yard from the Vail kiln-house and I have done it for two-and-twenty years. A man come up from Greybank with a soft cuff and a slate under his arm asked a girl at the wheel what the hands said over the lump before it went to the wheel, wrote her answer down before she had finished it, and went off up the yard with it. A beating-song taken down by a man who has never had a lump go hollow on him in the firing is a song with the air let out of it already. So I have set the words down as we say them and put the verbs back where he took them out. The tallow at the head is mine; I held the tally down in the draught under the bench and did not know I had marked it.

The clay comes off the treads wet and full of little rooms you cannot see. You draw the wire through it, lay one half along the other, and bring the flat of your hand down on the seam, and cut again and beat again until the rooms are closed. That is the beating-out. The wire is an honest tool because it shows the cut you made; a hollow is a lie with clay round it, and the fire is the only reader that never misreads. We say the words down into the lump, not up into the shed. Said up into the air they grow a tune in them, and a man in a clean coat can carry the tune away and leave the lump behind him.

Three strokes to a verse: the cut, the turn, the fall. The fourth is a hand laid flat to listen. It is not a stroke and it is not counted, and a girl who counts it aloud will beat air into a lump instead of out of it. A clerk may put a figure in that place if he has a mind to. There is room for figures in every trade; there is not a figure in this one.

# How it is beaten

- Cut toward the light, so that you can see the grain you are closing. A cut made in the dark is a cut you will have to find again in the firing.
- Lay one half along the other and beat on the seam, not across the lump. A blow across the lump drives the hollow deeper; a blow on the seam shuts it.
- Keep the hand flat. A fist beats a lump hollow in half the time and says it is sparing the wrist.
- Three strokes and the ear. Do not say the fourth aloud. A fourth called a stroke is a stroke the lump did not get, and somebody will pay for it at the draw.
- Knuckle the lump before it goes to the wheel. A sound one answers full; a hollow one gives back a small dead sound that stops at the skin. If it answers flat, take up the wire again, however late it is.
- Say the refrain into the clay and not to the girl beside you. Said to a person it becomes instruction, and instruction at this bench is instruction she will unlearn at somebody else's.
- Do not sing it at the Cross, and do not sing it on an Account-day. The bell there goes four, four and a gap, and a beating-song set against that gap comes home wrong in the hand.
- The last part is kept back. It is for when the kiln-master has gone up the terrace and the man from Greybank has gone back over the north bridge with his satchel under his arm and the yard can hear itself again.

# The song

*The call goes down into the lump. Refrain after every verse, the last line held until the hand is off the clay. One line of the refrain to one stroke.*

> The wire goes through where the thumb went in
> and leaves the cut standing where the hand was;
> cut it open, look at the grain ---
> a lump can be fair outside and hollow through.
>
> *Cut it, turn it, let it fall.*
> *Lay the hand flat. That is all.*
> *Three are the strokes; the fourth is the ear.*
> *What the ear lets go the fire will not.*

> Lay one half along the other and bring the flat down,
> not so hard that you kill it, not so soft that you leave it;
> the clay wants to hold its breath the way a woman does
> when a man with a slate comes into the room.
>
> *Cut it, turn it, let it fall.*
> *Lay the hand flat. That is all.*
> *Three are the strokes; the fourth is the ear.*
> *What the ear lets go the fire will not.*

> Three strokes and the hand laid flat to hear
> what the eye will never be told;
> a hollow answers hollow under the palm
> the way a pot answers flat under the knuckle.
>
> *Cut it, turn it, let it fall.*
> *Lay the hand flat. That is all.*
> *Three are the strokes; the fourth is the ear.*
> *What the ear lets go the fire will not.*

> What you leave in the lump the fire will find ---
> not with a wire, not with a hand,
> but in the throat, with a small flat knock,
> and a crack set by the door for the neighbours to see.
>
> *Cut it, turn it, let it fall.*
> *Lay the hand flat. That is all.*
> *Three are the strokes; the fourth is the ear.*
> *What the ear lets go the fire will not.*

> Six under the heel and three under the wire,
> two at the wheel and four paces to the rack;
> every count in this yard leaves a place it won't enter,
> and the pot goes through the fire with the whole of it.
>
> *Cut it, turn it, let it fall.*
> *Lay the hand flat. That is all.*
> *Three are the strokes; the fourth is the ear.*
> *What the ear lets go the fire will not.*

> Beat it for the girl who will sit where you sit,
> for the thumb that goes at the hollow place;
> you cannot see what you have driven out,
> and that is the work, and the only part you keep.
>
> *Cut it, turn it, let it fall.*
> *Lay the hand flat. That is all.*
> *Three are the strokes; the fourth is the ear.*
> *What the ear lets go the fire will not.*

There is a space before the last refrain in the singing, the length of a hand laid flat. I have left it on the tally. A clerk put a round nought in it once, which is a thing you can hold up and look at; a listening is not.

# The part they keep back

*Not sung while a man from the terrace or a man with a satchel stands in the yard. Said low, to the bench and not across it.*

> One in twelve, says the notice, read twice at the Cross,
> but the box goes up the clay road with another figure shut in it;
> the number you hear is not the number you pay,
> and a hollow in the house is a hollow in the slate.
>
> There's a place in the returning row we go around with a look,
> where a hand went into the strips and did not come back;
> no figure stands in it, no slate is set down there,
> and the clay goes on being beaten where the hand used to beat.
>
> Cut. Turn. Fall. The fourth is the ear.
> The yard keeps the place. The clerk keeps the nought.

# What the kiln-master did

He came up from the terrace on the wrong wind with the sweet edge of the glaze at one corner of his mouth and read the leaf with one eye on the yard. Against *a lump can be fair outside and hollow through* he wrote in broad charcoal: *A lump that answers hollow under the palm is not yet beaten. No pot goes to the wheel on the word of a hand.* He underlined it twice.

Underneath, in mine: He is right about the lump and wrong about the song, which was never about the lump alone. He has read every line with one eye on the kiln, which is how a man reads who has never had to beat out a lump he cannot see into.

# What the clerk did

The factor's clerk borrowed the leaf on an Account-day, ink sitting in the first joint of two fingers of his soft cuff and not coming out. He took the verbs out and set his words above mine, and I have left his hand standing and put ours underneath, as a bench keeps the cut and the fire keeps the hollow.

- At *a hollow is a lie with clay round it*, he wrote *air enclosed; defect, not a deduction*. True in the figure and false in the yard, where the lie is the whole of the thing and the air is only what it is made of.
- At *the fire will find it*, he wrote *the firing discloses latent fault; no entry required.* He thinks a fault found is a fault entered. In this yard a fault found is a pot set by the door with the crack showing.
- At the fourth place he put a round nought and wrote *vacancy*. It is a true thing in a language nobody at this bench speaks. We do not say the fourth and we do not strike it out.
- At *every count in this yard leaves a place it won't enter*, he wrote *unaccounted residue*. There is no word in Ledgerhand for a listening, so he has made one that will travel and arrive empty.
- At *a hand went into the strips and did not come back*, he wrote *one absence, nil*, and set a figure against the place in the row. I have left his figure standing beside our look. Both stand.

# What the rack-woman put in the margin

The thick charcoal belongs to a woman who keeps the drying racks four doors up and has carried more wet pots than she will count. I have copied it as it stands, without making the hands agree.

At *the clay wants to hold its breath the way a woman does*: *I have held mine seven years and set it down on the Last Day with nothing in it. A lump is not the only thing that keeps a room shut.*

At *a hollow answers hollow under the palm*: *I knuckled a cup that answered full and watched it go into the throat and come out with a crack a straw would lie in. The note was true and the lump was not. Both stand.*

At *what you leave in the lump the fire will find*: *It finds it and does not ask who left it, which is more than can be said for the collector.*

At the part they keep back: *I have asked three times what the beating is worth and been given the weather, the river and the price of barley. I have stopped asking.*

# At the head and in the gutter

The wet-slip thumb at the head is mine and will not come out of the grain, which is why I use it to sign. The brick dust in the fold came off the bench and is the only thing in this leaf put there by the trade and not by a person. The wire-nick went through the second leaf when the cutter caught the fold; I have not mended it, because a song with a cut through it is a song that has been with the wire. The barley-water ring stood through the third verse while a girl stood over me telling me to keep the hand flat; she took her cup up when I had done, and the ring has gone the colour of the pot it stood on. The green bead came off a girl who leaned to see the writing with sweetness still at her tongue; she touched the tally, said nothing, and went back up the terrace. I have not washed it. The hair the colour of wet wool is not mine and has caught in the seam and will stay there. The flake between the third and fourth leaf came off a lump that answered hollow after I had beaten it nine times; it is lighter in the hand than a sound flake, which is the whole of the lesson and no use at all to a clerk. The grey along the gutter came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying on a bench. Nobody will own it. There is no Last Day behind a bench.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The wire keeps the cut. The fire keeps the hollow.
> The hand keeps nothing and goes on beating.

I asked the rack-woman, a girl come up from the wheel and a man waiting with a barrow at the yard gate, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The tally goes back under the broken pot at the bench. Tomorrow the clay comes up off the treads and I shall cut it, turn it, let it fall, and lay the hand flat where no stroke falls, and the fire will find whatever I have left in it, and the clerk may have his copy of the nought.
