I keep the slate at the landing below the north bridge, where the Ilm comes down four steps and there is a flat place where a fifth should be. The market goes up and down over that place all morning and nobody falls, and if somebody does nobody calls it a fall. I am set for a measure. I have been set since Ilmen and it has not been called, and I write the dues of the eel-boards in the meantime, because a hand with nothing to enter will enter what it can find.

There are three of us with slates in this town and our entries do not touch. The man at the Court's table at the Cross touches the top slate before he looks at the woman waiting, and says it's entered as it stands. The Drawing collector keeps a book I have never seen open. I am neither. My cuff has gone grey from washing and the strap of my slate has worn a place in the coat opposite my wrist, so that the two wearings meet when I raise it. I have asked nobody to take me off. Word came up twice that nobody had, and the eels went on being sold over the top of me.

Write first. Look after. That is the order at this landing and it is the whole of the art. If a buyer names a figure I have it before I know his face, and if he asks me who it was I can tell him the figure and not the man, which is nearly always what he wanted. A clerk who looks before he writes is a clerk who has heard something he will later have to forget.

# The rates of the landing

| Entry | As the slate has it |
|---|---|
| Copper bit | twelve to a silver mark |
| Silver mark | twenty to a gold crown, a Sere coin that does not stay in Grath |
| Ember | five marks, by the Court's book; no man has exchanged one for the other |
| Eel, smoked, a basket at these boards | 3 bits |
| Eel, salted, a basket | 2 bits |
| Landing due, a basket set on the flat place | 1 bit, entered before the buyer is seen |
| Reeds, an armful | not entered; judged by the arm that carried them |
| Deduction | one in twelve, as the notice says |
| Newborn vessel | twelve to fifteen embers |
| Labourer in good case | forty to sixty embers |

I have set the reeds in the table with no figure against them, which is how the yard sets them. A cutter told the girl with the creel to ask the arm that carried it, and she did, and went off satisfied. When a man come down from Slate Row asked what the reeds cost, he was sent to the pot with *the house has said it* and got no number, and wrote nothing. I have copied his nothing honestly.

Draws made: none. Measures set to this post: three. Measures called: none. Dues entered: sixty-three bits. Columns struck: one. Figures struck: none; I do not strike figures, I leave them standing until somebody tells me which one to take away, and nobody has.

# The order of reaching

The order is hand to slate, then eyes to the boards, then mouth. I have watched myself do it until I know the three parts as separate motions, the way a potter knows the foot from the belly of a pot. The woman at the outer boards moves eel-bones while she decides whether to lie, and when a buyer names a figure her thumb goes under her apron and stays longer than the figure wants. I have entered the figure and not the thumb. There is no column for a thumb that stays too long, and a clerk who makes one is a clerk who will soon be asked to read it aloud at a Weighing.

A girl with a creel takes the out-of-line landing sideways, one hand out to the rail, and goes on selling without looking at her feet. I have entered her baskets and not her foot. The district goes over the flat place carefully, the way it goes over a crack in a floor it has known since childhood, and calls the whole of it weather. When the Ilm came up on Silt 10 it stood on the second step and put the flat place under an inch of brown water, and gave the steps back one at a time through Silt 11 to 13, four, four, and the place again a hand's breadth out of line. I kept the slate dry under my coat. A wet slate is a slate that can be read twice, and a figure read twice is a figure that has been made to say more than it said.

# The column the College struck

Beside *Called* I had written a column of my own with no heading. In it I had set down, for each measure set to this post, the hour at which the slate was raised and the hour at which it was put down again. The man from the College borrowed the leaf on Reed 10 and struck the column through from the first row to the last, and wrote *No such column* above it in his narrow upright hand.

I have drawn the column back underneath and left his words standing over it. He had to count the rows before he could write that there were none worth entering, and I watched him do it, standing as he stands, with his cuff gone soft and his ink settled in two fingers. A man does not count what he has not looked at. I have put no heading over the column still, because a heading would turn an hour into an entry and an entry into something a clerk may be called to answer for. An hour held is not a measure drawn. The College has eleven words for a deduction and not one for a holding, which is the whole of the difficulty between us.

# The verse the eel-women carry below the bridge

*Sung at the boards at dawn while the baskets are counted, never on Account-day and never while a slate is out.*

> Four to the water and four to the stone,
> and a flat place where the fifth should lie;
> the eel comes up and the figure goes down,
> and the slate keeps what it never was given.
>
> Hand to the slate and then to the stall,
> and the mouth comes last, as the mouth should do;
> one in twelve, says the notice, read twice at the Cross,
> and the landing reads once and reads it to you.
>
> Ask the cord, ask the arm, ask the river, they say;
> the Court has a figure for each of the three.
> I have a slate for a measure not called
> and a place in the count that the count keeps for me.

A woman with pale slime dried on her sleeves sang the first two lines while she worked and would not sing the third while the factor's man stood on the bridge, which is how I know which line is the one that matters. I have copied it as it came and put no gloss on it. A clerk who explains a song has begun to write a sermon, and sermons get copied.

# What the borrowed hands did

The College hand struck *Nobody called one* on the Ilmen 9 row and set *No measure presented* above it, which is true and further from the landing than my words. I have put mine back underneath. *Presented* is a word for a thing that was carried to a counter; a measure not called is not carried anywhere, it is simply not there, and the slate is held up all the same.

The other hand is a numbering hand, narrow and leaning, and not the College's. Against the three rows where I left the *Called* column bare it set a round nought in each. I have not struck the noughts out and I have not set them above my blanks either; they stand beside them. A nought is a figure and a blank is a place, and this leaf now holds both, which is more honest than either alone. A man who wanted those noughts to be true had to write them himself, and he did it in a hand that is not mine, in the hour before the Reed 15 baskets came down.

# In the margins

The eel slime along the fore-edge is mine, from holding the quay board still with the same hand that held the slate; it dries pale and comes off again when the damp gets into it. The silt in the gutter came up off the second step on Silt 10 and I have never got it out. The white bloom at the head is salt off a basket from the pans, and it has eaten one letter of the first date so that I cannot now read whether Ilmen 9 or Ilmen 8 stood there. The green bead in the lower corner is not slime. It is lead glaze from a woman who leaned over to see the tally with the sweetness still at her mouth, and touched the paper, and said nothing, and went back up the terrace. I have left it where she put it. The pale round ring was made by setting a cold iron tube down on the leaf while I reached for a basket, and the paper is thinner inside it; the tube was not used and the ring is not a wound, but it is the only mark on this page that looks like one.

The small bare square in the lower margin is where I scraped a word away with a knife and put no other in its place. I had written *sixth* there, meaning the sixth morning I had raised the slate for a measure that did not come, and then I counted and found I could not say which mornings were the same and which were not, so I took the word off. A figure that cannot be checked is a figure I have no right to leave standing, even in the margin.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The dues are entered. The measure is held. Both were taken from the same morning.

I asked the man at the Court's table, the Drawing collector, and a woman who sold eels at the outer boards, and had three answers and no hand, as I have had before. I have left the line where it was written.

The slate goes back on my forearm and I go back to the flat place where the fifth step should be. Tomorrow the bell at the Cross will go four, four and a gap and nobody will cross himself, and a figure will be read twice over eight pigs, and I shall set down the baskets before I look at the boards, and hold the slate up for a measure nobody has called, and put it down at the same hour I put it down today. The noughts will stand beside the blanks. The leaf goes into the oiled pocket and the silt stays in the gutter, and there is no Last Day for a landing.
