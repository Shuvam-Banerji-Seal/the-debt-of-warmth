I keep the wash-hearth below the sill and I teach the little ones their first words. They come to me before they can hold a spoon, and what I put in their mouths first is not bread but the Return; that is the order the Keeping settled on long before I was born, that the Return is the first thing a child learns and the last thing a dying person hears. I have never seen the last thing. I have seen the first, every morning, with the pots still wet and the rake marks in the hearth not yet cooled over. I have taught it for thirty-one winters and I have never once had a child say it the way the book has it, which is the whole reason I have set this down. The book's answers arrive clean. Children's answers arrive with the slip still on them.

A clerk from the College come up from the assessor's house in Slate Row borrowed the leaf on a morning when I had coals out for the wash and took the verbs out of it and put a figure against the silence, and I have put both back where they stood, his hand above mine and mine under it, the way the flagstone keeps the hearth's old crack under the new mortar. A mother who comes to the hearth with a child that came back last year has written in the margin and I have not asked her to explain. At the foot there is a line that was never taught to anybody and I have not found the hand.

The tallow at the head is mine, from holding the leaf over the banked coals to dry the charcoal; it has gone into the grain and no wash takes it out. The pale along the gutter is slip off an apron, not Ash, and I have kept it because there is enough grey in this house without bringing a stranger's into it. The grey print came off a child's cuff where a child's cuff has no business, on a hearth that is counted. The milk ring is sour and will stay sour. The charred edge is where I laid the leaf too near the coals and lets any reader know it has been used, which a clean leaf can never claim. One word I scraped away with a knife on the morning the clerk came and I have not written it back. There is a crooked mark in the corner made by a child who could not yet make a letter and wanted one all the same. I have left it.

# The manner of the teaching, written small at the head

- Say it at the wash-hearth, after the pots are put away and before the coals are banked. A child taught over a pot learns the pot and not the words.
- The little ones sit on their heels with their hands on the warm stone. A hand on the stone is a hand not in the ash.
- I ask; they answer. The answer is the same at every station, which is why it is a litany and not a lesson. They say it badly and I do not correct the saying, only the order.
- After "we come back" there is a silence the length of a name. They are told not to fill it. One of them fills it every morning and I have stopped pretending not to hear.
- Do not say it to a collector. If a collector is on the road, give him the tally and not the leaf. He cannot enter what a child says with her hands on the stone.
- Twelve bits to a mark and twenty marks to a crown. An ember is five marks in the Court's book. No child at this hearth has held an ember and I tell them so, because a figure they cannot hold is a figure they will believe.

# The litany

*I ask. The children answer. Between the question and the answer the hearth does the rest.*

## I. Of the first thing

> *Dame:* What is learned first?
>
> Before the spoon, before the pot,
> before the hand can hold the bread,
> the Return. It is put in the mouth
> before the breast is put there.

*Answer:* We are kept; we are counted; we come back.

## II. Of the breaking

> *Dame:* How did the world go?
>
> The world was fired whole and the lid went up in one anger.
> The Warm ran out where no wall held it
> and went into the cold that keeps nothing.
> Do not go looking for the crack in the floor.
> The crack has a name. Say it once.

*Answer:* We are kept; we are counted; we come back.

## III. Of the Nine

> *Dame:* Who went after what ran away?
>
> Nine hands, and they drove the repairs home
> the way a potter drives a staple.
> Name them by their number, not by their houses.
> Nine went. One is still abroad.

*Answer:* We are kept; we are counted; we come back.

## IV. Of the iron

> *Dame:* What comes up the Clay Road?
>
> A man with a cold tube and a slate.
> His hand goes to the slate before his eye goes to the person.
> The iron does not keep. It opens a way.
> The slate keeps, and what it keeps is not the person.

*One of the children said this last as "what it keeps is not the person" before I had taught it to her, and I have not corrected her, because I did not teach it to her.*

*Answer:* We are kept; we are counted; we come back.

## V. Of the figure

> *Dame:* What does the bell say?
>
> Four, and four, and nothing.
> Twelve bits to the mark and one in twelve for the fire,
> as the notice says, read twice, printed, not leaning.
> Count embers, not years; a year is a pot nobody weighs.

*Here the children say "and nobody crosses himself," which is not in the litany and is true. I have left it standing where they said it.*

*Answer:* We are kept; we are counted; we come back.

## VI. Of the pouring

> *Dame:* What comes back in the cradle?
>
> One measure into one vessel: he comes back with his name
> and the handle of the cup and the price of bread.
> Many into many: they come back as they can,
> with a hankering for a table they never sat at.
> Both are poured. Both go into a clean hand.

*Answer:* We are kept; we are counted; we come back.

## VII. Of the Kept

> *Dame:* Who is past wanting?
>
> Fewer than twenty, kept near the fire.
> They do not feel the cold and do not ask for bread.
> Stand a moment at their place and do not ask them if they are warm;
> they will say no, and you will have spent your only question.

*Answer:* We are kept; we are counted; we come back.

## VIII. Of the coming back

> *Dame:* What is left in the corner?
>
> The grey. It is left where it lies on the Last Day
> and taken in the morning and called dealt with.
> Do not sweep it. A thing left in place is still a thing kept.
> Come back as you can. Come back to your own fire.

*Answer:* We are kept; we are counted; we come back.

*The silence after the last answer is the length of a name. I have kept it the length it is.*

# What the clerk did

A clerk of the College borrowed the leaf with ink in two fingers and the cuffs of his coat soft from washing. He writes in the narrow upright hand and takes verbs out as another man might take bones out of a fish, and sets a figure where a person stood.

- At *before the breast is put there*, he wrote: *the faculty of retention is instructed before weaning.* Above mine. I have put the breast back underneath, because a child at this hearth knows which came first and will not be argued out of it by a man who was weaned in Slate Row.
- At *the crack has a name. Say it once*, he wrote: *the First Breaking is a mystery of the faith and not a place in a room.* I have left my line standing under his. There is a seam in the Keel behind the cistern and I have seen it mended twice; I have not told him so, and he has not asked.
- At *his hand goes to the slate before his eye goes to the person*, he wrote: *the officer proceeds in the order of his office.* This is true in the same way that a knife is true. I have left both.
- At the bell, against *and nobody crosses himself*, he put a round nought and wrote *observance not recorded.* It is the only honest thing he wrote all morning, and it cost him nothing to say it, because a nought has never had a hand in it.
- At the pouring, against *a hankering for a table they never sat at*, he wrote: *residual attachment; value nil.* I have put the verb back and left his above it. He cannot say why a child turns toward a cup before the cup is shown; I cannot either; the child can, and is not asked.
- At the answer, against the silence, he wrote *vacancy* and numbered it one. He has entered the name-shaped space, which is more than the children have done. I have put the space back where it was.

I have left every line of his standing above mine. He takes the verbs out because a thing always true has no need of them. I put them back because a child who has just learned the words will ask me who did the keeping, and a woman at a wash-hearth has to answer with something in the sentence.

# What the mother put in the margin

The slanting hand is Annet's, who comes to the hearth with a child that came back last year in a thin pouring from three districts she will not name. She has written where I set the sixth station:

> "He turns his mouth toward the cup before it is shown. The three who went into him are not here to be asked which one he wants, and I am the only one who remembers that there were three. I have said the answer with the rest of you for a year, and I have never once said the last word of it as if I knew what it meant."

And at the silence, underneath the clerk's nought:

> "Not a vacancy. A place set at the table."

I have copied her as she wrote it. She has taken her child home and I have not gone after her with a correction.

# At the head and in the gutter

The tallow is mine and goes on working further into the fold each winter. The slip is the wash-apron's and is the colour of the floor under the pots; a child who leans on the leaf leaves it without knowing, which is how most things get onto a leaf. The Ash came off a cuff on a hearth that is counted and I have not swept it, though I have swept the hearth twice since. The milk ring has gone sour and a sour ring lasts longer than a sweet one, which is a fact about milk and not about the Keeping. The charred edge lets a reader know the leaf has lain near a fire, which is the only certificate this hearth can give. The scraped word was gone before I knew what I had written; I have not put it back and I have not forgotten the length of it. The crooked mark is a child's first attempt at a letter and is not a letter. I have put the leaf where the child can find it again, under the third flagstone with the copper bit shut in the crease, and I have never moved either.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

Struck through once and written again underneath, very small, in a hand that is not mine, not the clerk's and not Annet's:

> The children learn the figure before the name.
> They never learn the road between.

I asked the clerk, a mother at the hearth, a collector come up the Clay Road and a child with slip on her hands, and had four answers and no hand. I have left the struck line where it was struck. The leaf goes back under the third flagstone and in the morning they will sit on their heels with their hands on the warm stone and I shall ask the eight questions and they will answer badly and I shall leave the silence the length of a name, and the ones who cannot yet say it will be taught, as I was, before they can hold a spoon.
