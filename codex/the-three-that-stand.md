To Dill Peat at the Standing Post, who was chosen by standing and has never once said what the mud said to her. From Tess of the Dry, at the edge of the Quiet Acre, in the last tend of Stone, Y.H. 812, the winter the peat did not take.

I write on the back of the tally the factor's man spoiled with his figure, and I have thinned the charcoal with peat-water so that it will bite. Do not rub the fold. It is wet through at the second crease and will come apart between your fingers. I learned my letters off a man come to take a receipt, so that I should know what I was signing when he set the slate in front of me. I know enough to write to you and not enough to trust what I write. If any of it reaches you with the verbs gone, know that I put them in.

The stick goes with the man. Twelve knots in grey wool, as I tied them on the ninth of Ilmen when he took her, and nine of them untied, one at the going of each month I could bear to count. Three stand: the last of the cold, the Five, and the coming-back. He says the term runs to the thaw. In his book the thaw is a figure. Here it is a place where the water comes up over the lower boards and the eels go upriver, and a place cannot be counted off on a stick.

I said the name into the ground before he put his hand on her, three times, into the clay beside the hut, with her own mouth too young to help. It is a reed-name and we do not cut it. They gave her another in the south, and the buyer's man wrote it on the back of his slate and asked me to say it after him so that he could hear he had written it right. I said it. A woman who has just sold a year of her child's breath will say almost anything to a man with a slate. I have not repeated it since and I will not write it here. A written name travels where the child does not.

# The knots, as I tied them and as he entered them

| Knot | What I did with it | What the factor's man wrote against it | What stands here |
|---|---|---|---|
| The first, tied at the buying | Untied at the going of the first month, with my own hand | Consideration paid | A month bought and a month paid. |
| The second through the ninth | Untied one at the going of each month, saying nothing | Term current; no claim lies | Eight months paid, and the child thinner each time a knot came off. |
| The tenth | Left standing when the cuttings stood full of water and there was nothing to sell | The measure is drawn as provided | A month I cannot sell back. |
| The eleventh and twelfth | Stand as they were tied | Vacancy | The Five, and the coming-back. |

He paid in a crown and a sack of grain. The grain went damp between Greybank and the Line and we ate it anyway, and the crown will not change at the Post or anywhere north of the water. I have it under the third board from the fire, where a coin lies that nobody can spend. A thing you cannot spend is a thing somebody has already paid for, which is a true saying here and has never once failed me. When I asked him what a year of her cost beyond the crown he said the account stood. I have never heard a man say *stood* with so much weather in his cuff.

The peat did not take this winter. We cut and we turned and the water came up in the cuttings and stood there, brown and flat, and the stacks did not dry behind the hut. Up in Grath they call this the Long Cold and give it a name in a book. Here we have no name for a winter except this one. A dry with nothing dry has nothing to sell, and a woman with nothing to sell cannot buy back a month of her child. I lie face down in the peat the way the old women do and ask the ground to keep what it has and hand back what it can. It keeps. It does not hand back. Or it hands back slowly, which is the same thing to a woman standing in water to her calf.

# The song, said to the ground

*Said through the nose, slow, with the last word left to fall into the mud. Never said looking at a person. I have set it down as it was said to me and not as it would be written.*

> The bog keeps what the bog was lent,
> and the dry is a loan and not a gift;
> twelve in the wool and nine undone,
> and three that stand where the water is.
>
> A crown lies cold beneath the board;
> the grain went damp on the Greybank road;
> a child comes back as the child went,
> or thinner, and answering to another name.
>
> Say it down, say it down to the clay,
> three times for the ones who cannot say;
> what the ground keeps, it keeps for good,
> and what it hands back comes back as its own.

I have three questions and they are not the same question said three times. First: can a term be bought back before the thaw by a woman with nothing dry to sell, or is the figure in his book the only figure there is? Second: is a clay-name a door shut, or a door with a dirty hand on it; and when she comes back, must I ask what she answers to before I say the reed-name, or after? Third: the third knot came off in Seed and she was thinner in the wrist when I saw her at the Line in Frost. Is thinness a thing a buyer pays for and a mother keeps, or is it weather? Do not put the answers on the front where a clerk can read them alongside the figure. Put them where you write to the ground.

Stand where you stand, sister. I will stand where I stood.

# In the margin, in a slow hand set close to the lines

It was the eighth of Ilmen, not the ninth. You tied the first knot a day late and he wrote the term from the day he paid, not the day he took, so there is a day in this that belongs to nobody. I have struck the ninth through and put the eighth underneath in your own charcoal, so that you cannot say a stranger wrote it.

I asked his man at the Post. He said the term runs to the thaw and the thaw is entered. I said a thaw is a place. He said a place is entered as nil and went back to his tea. You cannot buy back a month with wet peat. A bought breath has a price at the buying and another price at the coming back, and the second is never written, which is why he will not name it. Bring your eels to the Post in Ilmen and I will stand with you. Standing is what I am for.

Say our name after, not before. A child who has had a name put into her mouth by a man with a slate will say that one first, because it is the one that has been fed. Let her say it. Then say ours three times into the ground beside her, not at her and not to her, so that she can hear it without being made to answer. If she answers to it we have her back. If she does not, we have her back anyway and will say it again another day.

She came back on Ilmen 16 to the dry, thin, with his name in her mouth and ours somewhere under it. I said our word three times into the water below the Post. She turned her head toward the sound a breath after the third saying. I have set that down as a thing I saw and not a thing I know, and I have not put it beside the weather.

Thinness is not weather. It is what was paid and not entered. Keep the crown. It will not change, and that is the only honesty it has. Untie the twelfth when she stands on the board at the Post, and not before. A knot untied early is a debt denied, and a debt denied is a debt with a man's name in it.

# What the factor's clerk did

A clerk out of Greybank borrowed the leaf on the morning the eel-trader came down, being a man who cannot leave paper alone. He writes in the narrow upright hand, with the verbs taken out and everything in the passive. Against *a year of her breath* he set *term, one year, consideration discharged*, and the breath went out of the sentence as though a breath were a thing a man could carry in a cuff. Against *the peat did not take* he wrote *seasonal shortfall; no claim lies*. Against the twelve knots he set *twelve; nil*, and in the fifth place he put a round nought and wrote *vacancy*, where my sister's hand had been. Against the name said into the ground he wrote *name not entered*, which is true in a room with a slate in it. Against *she turned her head a breath after* he set *reflex, not a figure*.

He can enter the shortfall and the nil and the reflex, because all of those have a place in his book. He cannot enter the hand that left the tenth knot standing in the wet or the three sayings into the water, and he has not tried. I have put my verbs back underneath and left his hand standing above, as a board keeps both the man's weight and the water's mark. He has numbered the coming-back, which is a place. A nought is a number. The coming-back is not.

# At the head and in the gutter

The peat at the head is mine, wet from holding the tally down on the cutting, and it has gone into the grain so that the whole leaf smells of a wet winter. The tideline is bog-water and has taken two words off the second verse; I have not written them in again, because a saying with two words gone is a saying said slowly, and everything here is said slowly. The bog-myrtle came from the fold where I keep a sprig against the smell of the cuttings, and has pressed flat and kept its green. The eel slime at the outer crease came off the man who carried the stick; he set it down while I was tying the wool and did not wipe his hand. The grey along the gutter came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying. Nobody will own it and there is no Last Day under a loose board. The scorched corner is where the leaf slipped against the peat fire while I was writing the questions. The salt at the foot is not mine; it came up out of the factor's man's cuff when he set the tally down on the bench, and it comes and goes with the weather. The knot through the hole is mine, tied for the word I could not write. I will not untie it.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The board keeps the foot and the water keeps the board;
> the knot keeps the month and the clerk keeps the nil;
> say it down three times and the mud will know,
> and the name that comes up is the name that went down.

I asked a reeve at the Post, a man who trades eels down the marsh road, and a man who reads the figure at Greybank, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The leaf goes back under the loose board with the stick of twelve notches, and in the spring the young will cut their bundles in the dark and come back wet or dry, and we shall say it to the ground as before. Three knots stand and I have not laid a hand on them. I have tied another knot for the word I could not put down, and I will not untie it.
