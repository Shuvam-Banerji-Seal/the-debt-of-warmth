I keep the slate-box at the shrine. A girl is brought to me with her hands red from the tubs and her hair cut level with her jaw, and I read her what is below before I let her touch a stone. The trade is learned at the bench and not out of a book. There is no Last Day at a bench; the grey stays where it fell and goes into the next year under a birth-cloth. I have set the rules down as they stand. Where a hand has corrected me I have left both readings.

They were first gathered in Y.H. 782, the year a birth in Grath became a slate-entry and the Court began to pay the maids who had kept the slates for nothing since anyone could date. A rule with no thumb on it is a rule nobody has had to use. These have been used.

# The articles as they stand at the bench

## Of the taking

1. A maid takes a girl, not a house. The maid answers for the girl's bread, her bed and what she breaks; the house gives her up for the years and has her back at the end with a trade in her hands and no figure written against her. A house that will not give her up for the years has given nothing.
2. She is taken at twelve, or at thirteen if her house has kept her through a winter of sickness. Not before. A girl who cannot carry a pail the length of a room without spilling it has no business at a birth; she will spend the heat on the floor and call it the child's.
3. Her name goes on the back of the birth-slate book in the worn corner. It is not a slate-name and never becomes one. A maid who has no slate-name has one thing the College's officers have not: a name that can be called across a room without entering anybody.
4. She is kept, not hired. She eats from the maid's dish and sleeps under the maid's roof. What she earns at a birth goes to the house that sent for the maid and comes back as bread. She is paid in the only coin this trade uses, which is a stone put in a tin and not marked.
5. No girl is asked whether she wants the births. She is asked whether she understands what is carried: a child, a slate, a stone, and the hours between the second day and the fourth in which nobody comes to take the room off her hands. The wanting comes after, or it does not come; the order has never claimed to teach that part.

## Of the stone and the slate

6. Warm the stone first and test it on your own wrist. A stone fresh from the fire will burn a belly as easily as a hand. The wrist knows. The eye does not.
7. The stone goes to the middle, under the wool and against the skin, never to the ends. Hands and feet are wrapped and left; they are not rubbed and are not set to the fire. If a neighbour tells you to hold the foot to the hearth, say the house has spoken, and carry the stone back to the middle. A woman who spends her heat on the extremity has none left for the part that can keep it.
8. Set the slate face down on the bench before you go to the child. The hand goes to the slate before it goes to the child. A hand that goes the other way has already decided what the slate will say and will spend the rest of the evening making the child agree with it. Write the day first; the face will tell you the rest in its own time and will keep telling you for a year.
9. At the red flower, cross two fingers at the corner of it and say nothing. If somebody asks, give a date. Dates are safe; the Court likes a date and does not ask what it covers. Alda Reed crossed two fingers over a flower in the Thin Year and gave the day when she was asked, and nobody has since asked her the other question. A girl who asks what the sign means is told to ask the stone. The stone will not answer, which is the whole of the answer.
10. A child that goes worse on the third day has the second chill. It is not a sickness and not an entry. Call it that where the mother can hear you and call it nothing where the father can; a name spoken in a room becomes a figure by supper in this country. Keep the watch till the fourth morning. Do not send for the collector because the child has got worse. That is what the day does.
11. When the man with the tube comes, give him the mother and keep the child behind the slate. Do not say no. We do not say no. Put the pot between the child and the door and talk to the pot about the weather until he has gone back over the bridge; the pot has never once been asked what it held. If he leaves a pale ring on a wrist, do not rub it and do not cover it before he has gone. Cover it after. A thing covered too soon looks like a thing hidden, and a thing hidden is a thing that will be asked about twice.
12. Keep a stone from every birth in a tin under the bench, and never put a mark on any stone. A marked stone is a slate, and a slate can be read by anybody who can hold it. There is no count in the tin. There are stones in the tin.
13. When the bell goes at the Cross, stop and begin again after. It is counting something that is not your room.

## The three years

| Year | At the bench | What is taken | What is put in its place |
|---|---|---|---|
| First | Carry, warm, wash, sweep; hold the stone while the mother sleeps | The girl's own haste | A wrist that knows the stone before the eye does |
| Second | The slate face down; the day before the face; the watch | Her own name in a sentence | The house, the day, the hour before first grey |
| Third | A birth kept alone with an older woman in the next room | Her own answer to the question about the sign | The thing the room did, carried under an apron |

14. At the end of the third year the girl is set to a birth with the slate on the bench and nobody between her and the child but the wool. She enters the day and the house and leaves the rest of the line for the mother to fill. If she writes down what the child said in the night, the page is copied again with those words left out; they are not a birth and have no column. The bare place is left the length of what was taken away.
15. On Ilmen 15 she stands on the flat stone at the Weir with the other maids and blesses the weir and the eel-traps. The traps have their mouths upriver, so an eel goes in and cannot find the way out. The eels run whether anybody blesses them or not. She says the words because the words have been said. If asked what they do, give the date.

## Of faults and their correcting

| Fault | What the older maid does | What goes on the slate |
|---|---|---|
| Hand to the child before the slate | The girl washes and begins again | The day, and nothing beside it |
| A stone too hot at the belly | Held to the wrist; the girl waits with the child in her arms | Nothing |
| A foot rubbed or set to the fire | Unwrapped, wrapped again, left | Nothing |
| The flower covered with a word | Two fingers crossed at the corner; a date given | The date |
| A figure put upon a chill | The figure taken off; the watch kept | The fourth morning, and no cause |
| What the child said in the night | The page copied again with the words left out | The space the words were |
| The tin counted | The stones tipped out and put back unmarked | Nothing |

16. A fault copied twice in the same tend is not two faults. It is one fault the girl has not yet understood, and the older maid sits with her at the bench until she has. The slate is not a rod. It is a piece of stone with a place on it where a person stood.

# The form of a birth-entry

*Set out at the end of the articles. Copied with the blanks as they stand. In nine years I have never had a mother who wanted the last line filled by my hand.*

> Entered this day, being ---day of -------, in the year of the Hearth ------, the birth of a vessel in the house of ----------------------, the stone set to the middle before the ------- hour, the flower crossed at the corner and not spoken of, the third day watched through to the fourth morning, the name given by ----------------------, or the date standing where the name should stand; written by ----------------------, whose hand went to the slate before it went to the child.

# The warming-song, pasted inside the cover

*Sung low to the stone at the bench while the girl learns to hold it against her wrist. Never across the room. The second verse is my mother's and I have set mine underneath it so a girl can hear where the words change.*

> Stone from the fire and wool from the ewe,
> hold it to the wrist before it goes to you;
> warm the middle and leave the ends alone,
> and the house will keep what the house has known.
>
> Slate on the bench and the child on the knee,
> write the day before the face you see;
> the hand that writes is the hand that holds,
> and the name comes up where the date once stood.
>
> Cross two fingers and give a date;
> a date is safe and a flower is not.
> Three days, three watches, a fourth-day bread;
> what comes back is not what went.
>
> Stones in a tin and not one marked,
> a pail at the well and the pail not counted;
> put the verbs back where they were struck,
> and come back warm. Come back as you can.

The wet reed across the fourth verse came from the Weir, where the cloth went down in the rain at the blessing and lay until the eels had run. I have flattened it as well as I could. The words are plain under it.

# What the borrowed hands did

A clerk of the College came up from Slate Row with a cuff gone soft from washing and the ink sitting in two fingers of it. On an Account-day he borrowed the leaf and struck the verbs out of article eight, setting *the entry is made before attendance is given* above *the hand goes to the slate before it goes to the child*, which is a sentence true of every birth and therefore of none. Against the two fingers at the flower he put a round nought and wrote *vacancy* beneath it. A nought is a number and two fingers are a thing a woman does. I have put my words back underneath and left his above, where they belong, the way the Ash is left in the corner until morning and called dealt with.

The other hand is a physician's, come up from Greybank, leaning and careful. In Y.H. 651 a physician struck a word out of his own report and paid the hearth-maid who had written it for her trouble, and the word has not come down to us. On article ten he set *a common event; no entry; a settling of the graft*, which is a kinder phrase and costs less. I have struck it through and written *the second chill* underneath in my own hand, and left his above it. Both stand. Where a word has been taken away twice I leave a place the length of it, and do not fill the place.

# At the head and in the gutter

The wax at the head is off the shrine candle; it went into the grain in a drop and no knife takes it out clean. The fine grey at the fold is slip from the upper pit; it stayed in the fold instead of falling through, which is how I knew whose hand held the slate. The brown in the doubled leaf is birth-water and has eaten two words off the back; I have not written them in again, because a verse with two words gone is a verse said slowly and all of ours are said slowly. The grey along the gutter came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying in the room where the slates are kept; I have not swept it. The curl of grey wool through the tie-hole is mine, and I tied it there for the word I could not put down, and I will not untie it. The tallow is off the lamp I kept low so that a mother would not know I was writing and change the account for me. The tideline along the foot is well-water from a pail set down while I was telling a girl to warm the stone first; it has taken the corners off two lines without taking the sense. The green bead is not slip. A girl with sweetness still at her mouth leaned over to see the form of entry, touched the paper with her thumb and went back to her work without asking. I have not washed any of it. There is no Last Day in a slate-box.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The stone keeps the belly and the slate keeps the day. Between the two a girl carries the child, sets it down, and goes to the next room.

I asked the woman who brings the well-water, a girl new to the slates, and a man who reads the notices at the Cross, and had three answers and no hand, as I have had before. I have left the line where it was written. The leaf goes back into the cloth and the cloth into the box among the slates that have been answered. Tomorrow a girl will be brought up from the tubs with red hands and her hair cut level with her jaw, and I shall read her what stands here, and put the verbs back where a borrowed hand has taken them out, and say nothing about the tin.
