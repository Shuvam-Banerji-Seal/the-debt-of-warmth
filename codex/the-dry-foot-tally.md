title: The Price of a Dry Foot, Being the Market of the Standing Post and the Dues of the Boardwalks, the Peat Paths and the Marsh Roads, Cut on Count-Sticks and Copied into the South's Letters
kind: A board-man's tally of the marsh's prices and road-dues, cut as notches in a count-stick and written on oiled reed for a factor who cannot read a notch; the figure told below set above the figure taken here, one place left smooth and not noughted, the verbs put back where a borrowed hand took them out, two corrections in other hands, and a line at the foot nobody will own

I keep the boards at the Standing Post. Four planks lie across a cut no man has sounded, and a fifth place where no plank is laid, and a stick in my coat with a notch for every foot that went over and a mark for what it gave. A stranger comes up the marsh road, sets his boot on the first plank and asks what the crossing costs. I tell him the crossing costs nothing and the ground costs a bit. He takes this for a jest, because he is a dry man and believes ground lies still under him. I have watched dry men go down at the third plank. The water does not argue. It takes the boot and leaves the man to explain it to a reeve.

Fen Cant is not written. What is owed is cut in a stick: one notch for a bit, a deep notch for a mark, a cross-cut for a term, and a smooth place where nothing is entered. A factor's clerk at Greybank can read my notches and cannot read what they are for, which is how I know they are honest. I write in the south's letters so a reeve's daughter may read this. The words that will not go into them I have tied in the margin, with no word for what the knots are for.

Twelve copper bits to a silver mark; twenty marks to a Sere crown, which comes north with the buyers, will not change at Greybank, and goes south again with them. The Court books in embers and calls five marks an ember. I have cut no notch for one. In sixteen years I have never seen an ember paid, and have seen a man try.

> *In the narrow upright hand of the factor's clerk, written above my first line:* Ember: five marks. Reckoning entered. *Underneath, in mine:* I have left his figure standing and cut a smooth place beneath it. A thing nobody pays has no notch. A smooth place is where it belongs.

# What the Post keeps, and what it sells

*The left figure is what a man is told in Sill or at the Greybank beam. The right is what is given here, at the boards or in the cut. Nothing in the right column is weighed where a clerk can stand.*

| Article | As told below | As given here |
|---|---|---|
| Peat, a barrow | 2 bits in Grain, 5 in Stone | a barrow cut by the buyer, or a day of bought breath; never by the stack, for a stack is a house's winter |
| A winter stack for two rooms | not cried on any board | thirty marks below; here a term paid in work, and the term has a hook in its mouth |
| Reed bundle, an armful | not on any board | ask the arm that cut it; the arm knows weight and not cost |
| Hemp, a hank | 1 mark at Greybank | 3 bits, in kind; wet hemp weighs more and no buyer has ever noticed why |
| Eels, a basket | 3 bits smoked, 2 salted, at the Fishstairs | 2 bits at the boards, or a stick notched under an apron; the eel-women set their own figure and do not say it |
| Eel-sinew, a hank | not on any board | 1 bit; it sewed these leaves, and the seller would not take the bit back |
| Bog-cow, a milking | 24 marks below for the cow | a share of the morning's milk, by the morning; no cow has ever stood on a board in this marsh |
| Salt herring | 2 bits at Sill | 3 bits; the salt has been up from the Sallow and knows it |
| Dried leeches, a dozen | 3 bits at the Fishstairs | not sold here; taken off children and counted, which is a different trade |
| Grey, a thimble, bottled | 60 marks in a Court jar, licensed | 4 bits at the Post, in an unmarked jar; the same grey in two vessels, one with a Court name and one with none |
| Thin return, paid by a household | 30 marks to the Court, in labour | a third of that, in eels and peat, and no questions about the source; what comes back is a stranger the house keeps |
| Bought breath, a season | not spoken of below | a term paid in iron or peat, entered in months; the hook is in the word the seller cannot say |
| A child named before the collector comes | no figure | a name said three times into the ground under the boards, which no stick can hold |

> *Cut deep in the margin:* The thin-return row is the only figure on this strip that a man from the Court asked me to change. He wrote *one third of the Court tariff, subject to licence* above it. I cut *no questions* beneath. A man who asks where the warmth came from has decided not to hear it.

There is a figure spoken in the yards at Mud End which is not the printed one. I have heard it and cut nothing for it. I have no beam to weigh it on, and a figure cut without a beam will be asked to read itself aloud at the Cross. Nobody in this marsh wants to stand under that bell.

# What is paid in the mud and not entered

| | What is taken | What I cut |
|---|---|---|
| A fine at the reeve's post | peat to the winter stack | a notch; unpaid, it becomes a term, and the term becomes a measure |
| A feud settled by standing | the side that stands longest is called right by the mud | no figure; a reeve is chosen the same way |
| The draining of a cut | fever follows the ditch up into the water | nothing; the ditch pays and no slate carries it |
| A collector's boots | children are taught those before their own names | not a due, but a thing every hut knows the price of |
| A name said at home | kept in the house, not entered on any roll | a door, which a knot in the margin stands for |
| The grey in the corner | gathered, bottled and sold at the Post | nothing swept and burnt and called dealt with; there is no Last Day under a board |

A dry man is a dangerous man, we say, and say it to the water and not to him. He thinks a promise is a thing with a weight he can carry. Here a promise is tied in wool and left where the water can find it.

# The dues of the marsh roads

*The figure asked is what a man is told at the road's end. The notch is what left his pocket, or what left his back, which is the dearer of the two.*

| Road and place | As asked | As cut on my stick |
|---|---|---|
| Boardwalk from the marsh road to the Post | 1 bit for a dry foot | 1 bit, or a stick notched for a wet one; I do not tell a stranger which planks bear |
| The fifth place at the Post | no plank, no crossing | a smooth place, not a nought; nobody steps on it and nobody's foot is entered |
| East marsh road to Reedholt, a beast | 1 bit | 1 bit; a man on foot gives a date where a figure is asked and is let go on |
| The peat paths through the cut | paid in peat, by the arm-load | paid in peat, and the load is heavier going out than it came in |
| The smugglers' paths | no name on any board | a stick left at the cut, notched by the bunch; no questions, and no name |
| The road up to Mud End and Sill, a beast | 1 bit at the marsh road | 1 bit, and the same beast weighed again at the Cross; a road has two ends and both have a hand |
| The road into the Quiet Acre | no gate, no due | nothing cut; there are wells and doorways there and nobody to come back through them |
| The Peat Line, going south into Sere | tribute in eels, paid at the Line | eels by the basket; the South calls it a tariff, and north of it a man is a neighbour, which costs less |
| The drain cuts, after the ditch | no figure | fever, paid by whoever dug and by nobody who entered it |

> *In the narrow upright hand of the factor's clerk, against the fifth place:* Vacancy. Nil. *Underneath, in mine:* A nought is a notch cut deep enough to hold a figure. The fifth is a smooth place. I have not cut it, and he has not made it agree with anything.

> *In a hand with a little gold in the ink, come down from Sere:* Bought breath entered as seasonal labour; thin return as a graded return, rate one third. *In mine:* A season is a term with a hook in it, and a graded return is a stranger a house must keep. I have left both readings standing, as a board keeps the mark of the water under the mark of the foot.

The roads have two ends and the figures do not agree between them. The tally-man at Greybank cuts what goes out on the quay, the clerk at the Cross cuts what is paid under the pig-board, and I cut what is taken here. Between us a basket of eels is counted twice and a man once, which is the proportion the Court has always liked. The Hearth Road takes nothing at any gate, having taken it at home; the Salt Road calls its toll a contribution freely entered. I have cut neither. They are not roads of this marsh.

# The song the board-men carry

*Said low going in over the Post, never coming out, and said to the plank or the water and not to a man. The last word falls and is not finished.*

> One for the dry foot and one for the wet,
> a notch for the peat and a cross for the debt;
> four for the boards and a smooth for the fifth,
> and the mud keeps the count that the reeve does not.
>
> A bit at the Line and a bit at the plank,
> a stick for the eels and a term for the stack;
> the Court writes the return and the river writes none,
> and the man who comes over is dry till he is not.
>
> Say it to the board, say it down to the water,
> the last word falls where the board has no foot;
> a name said at home is a door in the mud,
> and a notch in a stick is the whole of the account.

> *In the factor's hand, against the last line:* Three figures entered. *Underneath, in mine:* There are four, and the fourth is a smooth place. He has put a nought where I left the stick bare. A nought can be carried away. The smooth place stays where it is.

# The hands that are not mine

The factor's clerk writes upright and narrow and leaves the doer out of every sentence, as though a bit could pay itself and a foot could cross a board without a man in it. He put *vacancy* where I keep a place and *subject to licence* where I keep *no questions*. I have set my notches under his words and left both standing. The Sere hand calls a term a season, which is a thing a house can empty and refill; I have called it a term, which a man carries in his mouth and cannot put down. A strip with three hands on it is the only kind a man at these boards will believe. A strip with one hand is a Court notice, and a Court notice has never told a man which plank will bear him.

# At the head and in the gutter

The cross-notch at the head is the fine I paid last Reed, cut with the reeve's own point so no wet sleeve takes it out; I have not said what it was for, and a cross-cut is the only signature this Post allows. The brown at my thumb is dry mud, not peat; I know the two the way a woman knows her own debt from another's. The tideline in the gutter is bog-water from the morning the Post stood under and has taken two figures clean off the second leaf. I have not written them back, because a figure with two notches gone is a figure cut slowly, and everything here is cut slowly. The nick is a reed-wolf's tooth, from a man who paid with his hand still on the knife. The slime at the foot came off a basket set down while a buyer was counting and a seller was not. The dark mark across the third leaf is the wet board laid on the strip to keep it from curling; it looks like a figure and is not one. The grey in the gutter is not mine and not the Court's, and I have not swept it. The knot through the hole is mine, and holds the word that does not go into the south's letters. There is no Last Day in a tin under a board.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The board keeps the foot. The stick keeps the debt. The mud keeps both and asks no figure.

I asked the reeve, an eel-trader, and a man come up from Greybank to count the winter peat, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The strip goes back into the tin under the loose board. Tomorrow a man will come up the marsh road, give me a date where I ask for a figure, set his foot where I do not tell him, and pay a bit for the dry and a notch for the wet, and by evening it will be a thing that was true. I shall cut it under the figure they told him and leave the space between the two.
