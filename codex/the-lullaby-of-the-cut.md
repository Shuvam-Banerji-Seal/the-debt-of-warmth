I sing above the shaft. That is where our women stand: at the mouth, at the weigh, at the cradles in the row behind the count's room. We do not go below. A woman's measure is said to lie too near the surface and would draw the sweat out of the stone, and the stone is not ours to trouble. I have sung to three children of this row and borne two of them myself, and my youngest turned twelve in Bone and goes down in the thaw. The cradle has stood empty three years. I sing to the nail in its board now, because a cradle with a child in it will look at you, and a woman who is singing about a knife does not want to be looked at.

I set the words down the night before his birthday, on the back of a tally a fall of slate dust spoiled, and cut a notch in the edge where the Grathic would not come. A survey-clerk from Greybank borrowed the leaf at the weigh in Ilmen and wrote his hand above mine, with the doing taken out and a figure standing where the nail stood. I have left his words where he put them and put mine back underneath, as the board of a cradle keeps the shape of a child after the child has gone out of it.

The bone at the head of this leaf is a shaving off a tally-stick. Keldish is cut in bone, not written, and a notch stays when paper has gone soft. I sign with charcoal because charcoal goes into the grain and no wet sleeve takes it out. The smut in the second fold came off the sleeve I wipe the keys with. I did not know I had marked it.

# The manner of it

- Said to the arm and to the nail, never to the child. Said looking at a child it becomes a promise, and there is no promise in this song that a woman can keep.
- The hand goes to the nail before the eyes go to the cradle. This is the order of the room: the record first, then the person, then the mouth opening after both. A lowlander does the opposite and thinks nothing of it.
- One verse to one finger. Count down the fingers of the left hand and stop at the thumb. The thumb is not a number and does not go down.
- The stone is not put into the hand at the cradle. It is held back and given after the knife, by a woman at the mouth who has counted the numbers. A stone in a cradle is a stone somebody has paid to warm for nothing, and there is no warmth here that has not been bought.
- The name is not sung. Where the name would stand in the last part I have cut a notch in the edge of the leaf. A notch keeps better than a word and cannot be carried up the road in a cuff.
- Sing the first parts while the child still has a face you can look at. Keep the last part until the boots have gone up the Rim Road and the count's room is quiet. A song with a name in it travels, and this one must stay where the cradle is.
- Do not sing it at the weigh and do not sing it while a number is being checked in red. A lullaby said during a checking is entered as a disturbance, and the mine-reeve does not ask twice.

# The song

*Said low, to the nail. The hand goes to the nail before the first line. One verse to one finger, counting down and stopping at the thumb. The last word of each part is said to the iron and not to the room.*

The refrain, as the cradle keeps it after every part:

> *Hush. Count. The arm has spoken before the face.*
> *One for the cradle, one for the knife, one for the stone.*
> *The number goes down. The name stays here.*
> *Say it once. The mountain keeps its own.*

## I. Of the arm

> Before you had a face you had an arm.
> Before you had a name the number was kept in it.
> The arm speaks first in this house; the face comes after.
> We say what we hold before we say who we are.
> Hush. The arm is listening.

> *Hush. Count. The arm has spoken before the face.*
> *One for the cradle, one for the knife, one for the stone.*
> *The number goes down. The name stays here.*
> *Say it once. The mountain keeps its own.*

## II. Of the knife

> Thirteen is a short road. I have walked to it twice.
> The knife goes across the surface way and stays there.
> A way once cut is a door and does not close again.
> It aches before the thaw. It will ache after.
> You will answer to the number the room gives you.

> *Hush. Count. The arm has spoken before the face.*
> *One for the cradle, one for the knife, one for the stone.*
> *The number goes down. The name stays here.*
> *Say it once. The mountain keeps its own.*

## III. Of the stone

> They will put a stone in your hand after the knife.
> I cannot put it there. My hands do not go below.
> A stone keeps its night longer than a mother keeps a child.
> It is paid for at the mouth; no man here has called it ours.
> Hold it. It is the only warmth with a price I can pay.

> *Hush. Count. The arm has spoken before the face.*
> *One for the cradle, one for the knife, one for the stone.*
> *The number goes down. The name stays here.*
> *Say it once. The mountain keeps its own.*

## IV. Of the iron

> Hold the rail going down. It is iron and it takes.
> Hold the rail coming up. It is the same iron.
> The clamp that closes a crack will close a fist;
> the iron has never been asked which one it meant.
> Hush. I have asked it. It does not answer.

> *Hush. Count. The arm has spoken before the face.*
> *One for the cradle, one for the knife, one for the stone.*
> *The number goes down. The name stays here.*
> *Say it once. The mountain keeps its own.*

## V. Of the number

> State what you hold. Then say who you are.
> The number stands. We do not say it twice.
> Two go down and the count comes two.
> Whether two come up is a thing the mountain keeps.
> Do not ask the mountain. It will tell you the number.

> *Hush. Count. The arm has spoken before the face.*
> *One for the cradle, one for the knife, one for the stone.*
> *The number goes down. The name stays here.*
> *Say it once. The mountain keeps its own.*

## VI. The part kept back

*Not sung while boots are on the road or a clerk is at the weigh. Said to the nail with the thumb laid over the notch.*

> I gave you a name at the cradle and the road will not carry it.
> Where the name would stand in this song I have cut a notch.
> A notch is not a figure and cannot be entered.
> I did not carry you down. I cannot carry you up.
> I can hold the board until it remembers your shape.

> *Hush. Count. The arm has spoken before the face.*
> *One for the cradle, one for the knife, one for the stone.*
> *The number goes down. The name stays here.*
> *Say it once. The mountain keeps its own.*

There is a space before the last line of the last refrain, the length of the notch in the edge of the leaf. I have left it on the tally. It is the right length for a name and for nothing else, and the women leave it when they sing, though none of them has ever said what stands in it. My thumb is in it now and has been since I cut it.

# What the survey-clerk did

He came up from Greybank with a cuff gone soft from washing and the ink sitting in two fingers of it, and borrowed the leaf while the pair before ours was being weighed. He writes narrow and upright and takes the verbs out as though a verb were a thing a man could smuggle past a weigh in the seam of a cuff. He set his hand above mine and did not look at the notch.

- At *before you had a name the number was kept in it* he wrote *capacity declared at birth; designation pending*, and underlined the figure twice. It is true in a counting room and false at a cradle, where the child had a name before he had a number and the number was still waiting in the arm for the knife.
- At *the number stands* he wrote *the vessel was re-entered*, which is the same sentence with nobody in it and with a man's thirteenth year made to sound like a correction.
- At *I cannot put it there. My hands do not go below* he set *stone issued after procedure; no gift arises*. He is right about the stone and has left out the hands, which is the whole art of that hand.
- At *whether two come up is a thing the mountain keeps* he wrote *no return due; remains retained on premises*. It is the cruellest line on the leaf and the truest, and I have not crossed it out.
- Where the notch stands he put a round nought and wrote *vacancy* beneath it. A nought is a figure and travels in a cuff. A notch is a place and stays in the edge of a leaf that nobody from the factor's house has ever wanted to carry.

I have put my words back underneath everywhere and left his standing above. He takes the doer out because a thing always true has no need of anybody doing it. I put the doer back because a woman singing to a board wants to know which hand held the child, and the answer is in the sentence if there is somebody in it.

# What the grandmother put underneath

The thick charcoal is my mother's. She counted at this mouth for thirty-one years and has never set her name to anything. She pressed these lines in below the verses while I was finding the end of the fourth, and would not read them over to me. I have copied them where she set them.

> At *a stone keeps its night longer than a mother keeps a child*: He came up with two sets in one arm. The later cut was deeper. I have looked at that arm and I have not asked him which set he answers to, and he has not told me.

> At *you will answer to the number the room gives you*: Your father answered to two numbers in one year and held the cup with the other hand. The room entered the later one. I have kept both, which is a thing a room cannot do.

> At the notch: I put my thumb in it when you wrote it and I will not tell you why. You have cut it the width of your own thumb, which was the width of his.

I have set all three where she wrote them. She would not sign them and I have not made her.

# At the head and in the gutter

The bone shaving at the head came off a tally-stick my mother kept, and is the only part of this leaf that was written before it was said. The coal in the second fold is off the sleeve I wipe the keys with; it goes into the grain and no wet sleeve takes it out, which is why I sign in charcoal and not in ink. The tallow is a thumb off the lamp I kept turned low while I wrote, so that the women in the next room would not know I was setting down what we sing and change their words for me. The curl of hair is his, cut at two years with the same knife that cuts the tally notches; I shut it in the sewing and have not opened it since. The crust is barley from the Last Day bread, pressed flat between the third and fourth leaves; there is no Last Day at a shaft mouth and nothing here gets gathered and burnt in the morning and called dealt with. The dent is where the warm-stone stood on the leaf while I found the sixth verse. It held through the writing and was cold before the charcoal set, which is the order of things here. The red at the second corner came off the mine-reeve's point while he checked a man who had given a figure that would not hold; he did not look at me and I did not look at the man. The frost-feather was on the mouth stone after a warm night and was gone before I came to put it in the fold. The small-ale ring at the foot is where the cup stood from the first verse to the last and was not lifted, which is not the cup on the nail and belongs to no Order. The grey along the outer fold came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying above ground; nobody will own it and I have not swept it.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The cradle keeps the shape. The arm keeps the number.
> The road keeps the name and will not give it back.
> The stone keeps one night. The mountain keeps the rest.

I asked a woman who has counted here longer than I, a man off the Duelling Floor, and a surveyor who carries the slate up, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written.

The leaf goes back into the tin with the keys, and the nail goes back into the board. Tomorrow the thaw will come up the Rim Road and the pair will go down and the count's woman will put the stone into whichever hand the knife leaves free, and I shall sing these words to the board and stop where the notch is. He will have the number and I shall have the name, and the road will have neither, and the mountain will keep its own and will not be asked to give it back.
