I keep the cup at a peat-dry three leagues in from the Post. That is my office, such as it is: to see the coals are blind by morning and the cup goes round and round and nobody pours for himself. A man who pours for himself in a dry drinks alone, and a man who drinks alone in the Fen is a man the water has begun to mark. I have kept the cup since the Glaze Wars were ended and I have never once seen a man refuse it, which is not the same thing as saying he wanted it.

I set this down because a clerk come up from Greybank to count the winter peat stood at the dry with a cuff soft from washing and asked what the women sang when the cutting was done. I told him. He wrote it in a book while I was still saying it, and what he wrote had the doing taken out of it and a figure put where a place should stand, and he thanked me and went back over the boards with his boots clean. So I have set down what we said before he came and what we said again after he had gone. His hand stands above mine where he set it, and the reeve's peat score stands above his, and the mud-wife's gloss is pressed in where she wrote it, and my words lie under all three, as the dry keeps the old cut under the new turf.

The thumb at the head is mine. I held the reed down on the bench in a wind off the bog and did not know I had marked it. Peat goes into the grain and no damp takes it out, which is why we sign with it here and not with ink.

# How it is sung

- Sitting, never standing. The cup goes to the left, and the man whose turn it is does not pour; his neighbour pours for him, so that no man holds his own drinking in his own hand. Said standing, it becomes a grace, and we have no grace in a dry that the Court has not already entered.
- Said to the fire, not to the company. The last word of every verse is let fall into the floor and not finished. A verse said looking at a man becomes an argument, and there is enough arguing at the Post without carrying it three leagues in.
- Through the nose, slow, half-sung. Where a word cannot be written I have tied a knot in the margin; it is made at the back of the throat with the nose shut and does not go into Grathic. A clerk can carry a figure away. He cannot carry a knot away, which is the whole reason for knots.
- One verse for the ground, one for the warmth, one for the boots, one for the Mother, one for the grey. There is a place where a sixth should stand and it is not scored and not filled. The factor's clerk has put a nought in it; the nought is his and the place is ours.
- Not sung on the Post and not sung at a death. There is a different thing said at a death and it is said to the water.

# The song

*Said low to the fire, sitting. The table keeps the refrain; one voice begins it and the rest come in on the second line. The bracelet of grey wool goes round with the cup.*

The refrain, as the table keeps it after every verse:

> *Drink to the dry. It's lent.*
> *Drink to the warm. It's spent.*
> *What the cup forgets the mud keeps,*
> *and gives it back to whom it wants.*

## I. Of the ground

> We stand on a loan. The water calls it in
> when it has a mind to, which is most years;
> the board's a promise the bog has not kept,
> and dry's a thing we rent by the hour.
>
> *Gloss, in the mud-wife's small hand:* The last word is let go into the floor. Do not say hour as though it had an end to it.

## II. Of the warmth

> They sell it whole in the south and dear;
> we buy it thin and ask whose, and don't know;
> a third of the figure, a quarter of the account,
> and the child comes quiet, and likes the fire.
>
> *In the narrow upright hand, written above:* The deduction is retained. No vendor arises.
> *
> *Underneath, in mine:* A vendor arises in the house that got the child. He is not named here because a name said in a dry travels with the cup, and the cup goes as far as the Post.

## III. Of the boots

> Our young know a collector by the boot
> before they know the word for their own name:
> no peat on the heel, no water to the cuff,
> and a slate held level, the way you'd hold a knife.
>
> *Gloss:* The knife is not drawn. It is held the way a man holds a thing he has put away and may take up again. Children are not told this part and learn it.

## IV. Of the Mother

> The Mother keeps it warm under a skin of water
> and hands it up to whoever can reach;
> don't ask her who paid --- she'll tell the mud,
> and the mud has kept the answer since the First.
>
> *In the narrow upright hand:* The measure is retained in the peat.
> *
> *Underneath, in mine:* He has taken the verbs out again. *Keeps* and *hands up* are in my mouth and in theirs, and a woman lying face down in the cut wants to know whose hands are doing it. Both stand.

## V. Of the grey

> In Grath they leave the grey where it fell
> and burn it on the morning after the Last;
> we jar it and cork it and sell it south,
> which is a kind of sweeping, the reeve says.
>
> *Gloss:* He says it with a straight face and a dry one, which is the same face he uses for the fines. Nobody has bought our grey back and nobody has asked to.

## VI. The place that is not a verse

*Here the leaf has a hole worked in it and a knot of grey wool tied through. There are no words to the sixth place. The reel of the cup goes round once more with the knot standing.*

> *In the narrow upright hand, against the hole:* nil.
> *
> *In mine:* A nought is a figure and can be carried away. The sixth is a place and stays where it is. I have not untied the knot and I will not.

# What the clerk did

He struck out three verbs and numbered the sixth place and wrote *vacancy* under his nought. When I asked him what he wanted with a drinking-song, he said the factor wanted to know what the Fenfolk owed. A man who asks what you owe has written the answer before you open your mouth; all that is left him is to watch where you put your stop. I have put my verbs back under his and left his above, as a dry keeps the old cut showing under the new turf. A sentence with the doing taken out travels well and arrives empty, which is what the Court wants of a sentence. A knot arrives as it was tied.

# At the head and in the gutter

The peat thumb at the head is mine and has gone into the grain. The tideline came up the second leaf the morning the Post stood under and has taken two words clean off the third verse; I have not written them back, because a song with two words gone is a song said slowly, and ours are all said slowly. The press of wet sedge is from the morning we lay flat in the cut until a beast's pushing had gone past. The sour ring at the foot is small ale, set down at the first verse and taken up at the last by which time it had gone the colour of the floor it stood on. The eel slime at the fore-edge came off a man who set his cup down with his hands still wet from the baskets; he did not stay and I have not washed it. The grey along the outer fold came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying; nobody will own it and I have not swept it. There is no Last Day at a peat-dry and nothing here is gathered and bottled and called dealt with. The knot of grey wool through the hole is mine and holds a word that does not go into Grathic. I will not untie it.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> A cup goes round and comes back empty.
> The ground it stood on is still owed.

I asked the reeve, the mud-wife out of Reedholt and a woman whose term is not out, and had three answerings and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The leaf goes back under the boards and the cup goes back on its nail. Tomorrow the cutting begins again and the cup will go round and nobody will pour for himself; and if the clerk comes up again from Greybank with his soft cuff and his pen, I shall put the verbs back and tie another knot for the word he cannot hear.
