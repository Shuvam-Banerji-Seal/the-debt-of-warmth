I keep the guild's bench at the Cross on market-days, between the pig beam and the Court table set at the Slate Row fringe. A house comes to take a child, or a child is brought to be tried, and I read what is below so that what is read in High Kiln is the same as what is read in the yard. The bench is worn smooth in the middle where thumbs have wiped it longer than any man can date, and this leaf has a nail-hole at the head and a green bead of glaze in the lower corner that came off the Thane's thumb in the Thin Year and has not been swept since. The grey along the gutter came off a cuff laid in a corner where no cuff has business lying, and there is no Last Day for a bench-copy. I have set the rules down as they stand. Where a hand has corrected me I have left both readings.

They are not new rules. They were gathered in the year the Clay Road was made a Court road, when a man from the Cross could first reach Greybank in five days and the guild found it had neighbours it had not chosen. A rule with no thumb on it is a rule nobody has had to use. What follows has been used.

# The standing rules, as read at the Cross

## Of the taking

1. A house takes an apprentice, not the guild. The house answers for the child's food, his bed, his tools and the debt he makes; the guild answers only for the note. A man who takes a child and will not answer for him has taken nothing, and the child is sent back to the moot.
2. A child may be taken from his ninth year, and not before he can carry a board of wet pots the length of the yard without setting it down. A child bound by debt goes where the debt sends him, and his years count from the day the debt is entered, not from the day he could lift a board.
3. The taking is written in the house's own roll, in a careful hand, and the roll is kept in the house. It is not taken to Slate Row and it is not shown to a man who comes down with a slate. A slate is for the measure; the roll is for the hand. The two are not the same book and must not be set side by side.
4. A foundling is named by whoever writes the slate, and has no house-name until a hearth claims him. Until then he is a coat any house may wear. See that the coat is mended where it tears, not worn till it thins.
5. A widow may hold her husband's fire by the mark confirmed at the Cross and by the factor at Greybank. She touches the mark before she opens the kiln and at no other hour. A dusk is not an opening, and a bank is not an opening.

## Of the first year

6. The apprentice sweeps, carries, mixes slip and sets pots to dry. He does not touch the wheel, and he does not cut the glaze. A child set to the wheel too soon learns to humour the clay instead of hearing it, and a pot humoured is a pot that will not say what it is.
7. The apprentice is kept, not hired. He eats from the house's dish and sleeps under the house's roof. What he earns in the yard goes into the yard, and what the yard gives back comes back as bread, not as a figure in a man's hand.
8. No child tastes glaze. Glaze is tasted by the master, with the side of the thumb and the back of the tongue, and spit out. A pale line at the gum is the glaze's mark and not a mark of industry; a master who calls it a good sign is selling the child short by the ounce.
9. A wet pot is not set in the sun and a wet pot is not set near the door. You will pay for both in the thaw. Fire does not know a child is sorry; feed it and say nothing.

## Of the wheel and the ear

10. Knock with the knuckle, never the nail and never the flat of the hand. The hand says what it wants from the pot; the knuckle says what the pot has. This rule is the whole trade and the rest are its corners.
11. A sound pot under the knuckle gives its full note. A cracked pot answers flat, small and dead, and the sound stops at the surface and will not go through. A pot that answers flat is a pot gone wrong. It is broken at the door, not mended and sold, and the yard goes around the place where it stood.
12. A mended pot gives a note nearly full and never quite, and the yard calls it a pot that'll do. Leave the crack showing. A repair that will not sing is a lie in clay, and a lie in clay is the only lie a kiln cannot burn clean.
13. The cupworm hides a flaw no knuckle will find. If a hand says a pot is wrong and the knuckle says it is right, set the pot aside another year. The yard has been wrong about pots and has never once been wrong about a hand that would not let a thing go.

> *Crossed hard in charcoal across rule 13 in the Thin Year, in a potter's hand that did not sign:* Strike *the knuckle says it is right*. Set above: *the ear hears it before the hand gets there, and the hand gets there before the knuckle*. I have not swept the charcoal. I have not put the struck words back. Both readings stand, because the bench has held both and the bench is older than either of us.

## Of the kiln and the breath

14. No child sleeps in a closed kiln house. The kiln's breath kills in sleep and is called a merciful death by those who have not had to carry the body out. A damper is a thing to be moved, not argued with.
15. Coals are banked under ash at dusk with the back of a broken pot, not with a shovel. Iron on iron wakes what should lie. The fire is left as it was found, so that the morning's hand knows the night's hand was there.
16. The master opens the kiln. The apprentice stands back from the lid, because a face over an open kiln learns nothing and loses its eyebrows. The kiln takes its breath in fours and a gap; a man who counts the gap aloud will be told to count the pots instead.

## Of the term and the debt

| Condition | Term | What gathers |
|---|---|---|
| House child, taken at nine | seven years | The note of the pot; nothing written against the boy |
| Child bound by debt | seven years, or as entered | Grey Ash in the household account, by the year and by the loaf |
| Debt left uncalled | as long as the house holds it | The debt is not forgiven. It lies where it was put, and a man goes around it as around Ash in a corner |
| Kept hand | until returned thin | No measure in the house's roll; the house keeps the body and not the name |
| Foundling with a slate-name only | until a hearth claims him | A coat anybody can wear, and a debt nobody has entered yet |
| Widow holding a fire | by the mark, confirmed | The fire must not go out; the mark is touched before openings only |

17. Seven years is the common term and not a short one. Interest gathers as grey Ash in the household accounts, which is why the house keeps its roll dry and why a debt left uncalled is worse than a debt called: a called debt has an end, and an uncalled one has only a place.
18. A clay-bound child sleeps in the loft and a taken child sleeps under the roof. This is a poor household's sorting of its own members, and it is called kindness, and it is not entered anywhere. Ask the pot. The pot is more use.

> *In the Thane's narrow upright hand, above rule 7:* The apprentice is kept. *Underneath, in mine:* I had written that the apprentice is paid. He is not paid. He is fed, and the feeding goes into no figure, which is why it can be done. I have left my word standing under his, since a man should be able to see the thing he was told to change.

## Of the levy and the First Firing

19. Two levies are called at the Cross, and the moot shouts at both. The levy of clay is worked out in barrow-loads and the levy of warmth in figures. The second is the heavier, and the notice says one in twelve. A household that has heard otherwise has heard wrong. The notice is what a man can be seen reading.
20. The levy is called; it is not called by the Thane, or by the bench, or by the house that pays it. The figure stands as read, and there will be a slate at the watch if the house wants it again. The red wool cord is folded into its drawer before the Thane goes home, and the bench does not follow him.
21. At twelve the child makes a pot, fires it and knocks it with a knuckle. A clear note means a feast; a flat note means another year in the yard. A foundling with no kiln of his own is judged on a pot made in the yard of the house that took him. The feast is bread and a pig's trotter and nothing that needs entering.

# The wheel-song, pasted to the back

*Sung at the second firing, standing, with the slip still on the forearms. The yard says the refrain together. Nobody sings it at the First Firing, and nobody says why.*

> A dish of slip and a dish of water,
> a knuckle for the thing that matters;
> the wheel goes round and the yard goes round,
> and the pot says what the pot has found.
>
> Leave the crack showing,
> feed the fire, ask the pot;
> four for the corners, four for the wall,
> and the place where the fifth should be.
>
> I made a pot and the pot said no;
> I knocked it twice and it told me so.
> The sun's no friend and the fire's no friend
> to a pot that's cracked before the end.
>
> Leave the crack showing,
> feed the fire, ask the pot;
> seven for the hand and one for the debt,
> and the slate stands in for the lot.

The press of wet reed across the second verse came from the bench standing in the rain at the Weir, where it went with a survey party that wanted a rule read in the open air. I have flattened it as well as I could. The words are plain enough under it.

# What the two borrowed hands did

The Thane's clerk struck *the apprentice is paid* and set *kept* above. He left my figure for the term standing, saying a number is a number and a word is a word and the one has no business correcting the other. A potter struck the knuckle in rule 12 and put the ear in its place, and would not sign. The charcoal has come off on the fold and I have not brushed it away. A rule that two men have argued over is a rule that has been used.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The fire is not entered. The hand is.
> That is the whole of the trade.

I have asked the potters, the Thane's clerk and a man who came up from Slate Row, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the two lines where they were written. The leaf goes back under the moot-post nail and the bench onto the Cross; tomorrow a house will come to take a child, and I shall read what stands here, and by afternoon the charcoal will have come off on another sleeve.
