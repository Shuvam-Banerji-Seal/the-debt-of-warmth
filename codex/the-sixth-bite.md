I have taken ice-mice for nine winters and kept this book for three. It is a record of snares. It is not a book of reasons. If a man wants reasons let him go to a College and ask them there, where they will write him one in a clean hand and charge him for the ink.

The paper is the backs of fish-bills from the north bank, gone stiff with frost and soft again in the thaw. A skin is worth a bit in Sill and a mark in Keld, and honest paper is worth a bit as well, so I write on what the eel-men throw away. The frost-white at the head is mine, off the thumb I set the sheet down with on Stone 9. I have not knocked it off.

# Where it keeps

It lives under the ice and in the thatch. In Stone, when the Ilm has lain frozen three tends and the eaves along the north bank have a white lip to them, it comes into the houses and nests in the clothes a man leaves hanging by the door. I have found one in the fold of a coat that had been worn down to the Fishstairs and back, and two in the straw of a bed whose occupant had not risen in a week. It does not come for the person. It comes for the place where the warmth has been, which is very often the same place, and the poor do not thank it for telling the difference.

You find it by the small cold patch. A man comes in off the bank, puts his hand to his own sleeve and says his arm has gone to sleep, and it has, but not from the sleeping. Where the mouse has sat there is a place the size of a thumb that does not warm when the rest of him does. It spreads if you leave it. Rub it toward the heart; do not cut it, for there is nothing in it to let out. I have seen a man put a knife to such a patch because he thought to drain it, and he drained nothing but the use of two fingers, which did not come back before the thaw.

# The taking

A noose of horsehair, set where the thatch has sagged and not where the frost sits highest. The mouse goes along the under edge of the eave where the house gives off a little warm through the roof, and a noose in the open gives you nothing but a dead bird and a cold hand. Under the ice you set a bent sliver in a crack and lead it with a crumb; the ice is clear in Stone and you can see the thing come to it. Do not crush it. A skin with a hole in it goes thin in the tanning and a thin skin will not come round, and there is no market for a skin that will not come round.

The fur is warm past its weight. A skin the size of a man's palm will keep a cup from freezing on a shelf where a fold of good wool will not. This is the whole reason the trade exists, and the reason is not a pretty one. The skin goes into the lining of collectors' coats, sewn there by a man who has never held a collector's tube and has no wish to. The collectors say it is a good joke. I have stood by a brazier with one of them in a coat lined with nine or ten of my skins and watched him hold his grey fingers out to the coals, and he could not get them warm, and he said it was the weather. It was the weather. It is always the weather. I have never told him what was sewn into his coat and I never will, because a man who has been given a joke is allowed to keep it.

I have left his hand standing. He counted the skins; I counted what the skins cost, and the two columns are not the same column. A bit is a bit and a child that does not wake is not entered, which is the whole of the accounting in one line. I wrote the bites in the margin below where his column cannot reach, and put my own mark beside them in charcoal.

# The verse

*Said on the bank when the lines are set, not at a fire. The fifth line is the one the old men leave out and the young ones put back.*

> One for the eave and one for the ice,
> one for the coat that has come home cold;
> two for the thumb that will not come round
> and one for the hand that could not hold.
>
> Set it where the thatch has sagged,
> not where the frost has made a crown;

> and a thin skin will not come round.
>
> Six for the cradle, six for the bank,

> and leave the last one for the snare to take.
>
> A draft is a thing that a house can bear;
> a bite is a thing that a house can't.
> Keep the window. Mind the coat.
> Count the skins and let the child count.

I do not know why the old men leave out the fifth line. I have asked two of them and had two answers, and neither answer was about mice. I have set it down as it is said and let it stand.

# The sixth bite

It bites cold. There is no more to the bite than that: a small nick, not much blood, and a place that goes white around it. If the wound is warmed it comes back. If it is not warmed the whiteness travels, and a foot or a hand goes the way that the old people call the cold that stays, and stays. Salt does not hold it, and I have never found a physic that does. Keep the part warm, get the person to a fire, and do not put a knife to it.

A sleeping child bitten six times will not wake. This is not a saying. I have seen it in Stone 26 in a house above the Fishstairs road, and I had set the line myself and knew how many of the little bodies had gone into my hand that morning, which was seven, and one of them had already bit and I had not known it. The child had four nicks along the jaw and two at the wrist, and the sixth was the one at the wrist. There was no blood to speak of and no sound. The mother put a warm stone to him and would not take her hand away, and the midwife came and said the second chill had taken him, and said it in the voice midwives use when they have said a thing many times and have never once been thanked for it.

> *In a small hand, in the margin beside this passage, pressed small and close as though the writer feared the room might read it:* The second chill comes to children who were never bit. I have laid out eleven. Do not let this book say six for the midwives. Four at the jaw and two at the wrist is a count, not a cause. --- A.

I have not struck her line out. I have not changed my count either. We are both right in the way that two people standing on opposite banks are both right about the river, which is a thing I have learned in this trade and nowhere else.

# What the hearths say

They say it is the cold made small enough to be killed, a bit of winter that got legs and went looking for a coat. There are songs in the Fenmarch with a man's name in them and a wrong done at a weir, and I have heard them and have no quarrel with a song. I have opened a great many of these and found a mouse. It is a field mouse that has lived where the cold comes up through the ice, and the cold has gone into it the way the smoke goes into a hound that lives by a kiln; the place changes the creature and then the creature carries the place about with it. I have written what it does and not what it wants. A man who writes what a mouse wants is a man who will be asked to write what a collector wants next, and there is no end to that sort of work.

What it does is take. It sits in the warm place and takes the warm out of it, and the place it has taken from stays cold after the mouse has gone. We say it takes the head from the place. A collector sets iron to a wrist and takes by the same road, only with a slate to make the taking look like keeping, and the collector's fingers go grey where the mouse's coat goes warm. I have set the two side by side below and I have not written a word between them. Let the College join them if it can get a figure out of it.

# At the head and in the margins

The frost-white at the head is mine, and came off the ninth day of Stone when I set a sheet down on the step and picked it up again with the frost already in it. The silt thumb at the fold is from the bank above Sill, where I knelt in the grey to lead a line under the ice; it has dried into the grain and will not rub out. The mouse-drop on the second leaf is the only part of this book that has entered itself. The singe on the lower corner is where the tallow end caught while I was reading by it, and the hand that pulled it back was mine and is not entered. The coin-rub is a bit I carried loose in that pocket for a month before I spent it; I do not remember what I bought, which is the common fate of bits. The grey print along the gutter came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying. I have not swept it and nobody will own it. There is no Last Day under the eaves and nothing in this book gets swept.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> It does not eat what it takes. Neither does the iron.

I asked a midwife, a collector who wears the coat, and a man who lost two fingers to a patch he cut, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written, which is the only honest way to keep a sentence you cannot sign.

Next Stone I will set a line in the eaves below the north bridge, where the ice comes up wrong in the thaw and the thatch stays wet when the rest has dried. I will count the bites as well as the skins, and if six come up together in one house I will set that down too, in charcoal, with the verb left in it, and let the collector take what figure he can out of it.
