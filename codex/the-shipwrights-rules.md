I keep the moulds and the roll of hands at the third yard, where the ways go down into the water and the tallow takes a handprint. A house brings a boy and goes away; the boy stays with the wood. A Council man comes with a cuff soft from washing, reads the thirty marks off a paper kept dry when every other paper here is left to rot, and asks what the curse is worth. I tell him it is included. He writes *included* and goes away satisfied, which is the whole of his trade and none of ours.

There is no guild in Vhal. There is a price, and a curse said every hour of the work, and the men who stand at the ways when a hull goes down; between them a trade is kept. We hang our rules on a spike in the mould-loft, where the tar takes the head of the leaf and nothing takes it off again.

These rules were gathered in Y.H. 605, when the Council first bought a galley by contract and a yard that had carried its rules in its mouth was made to put them on paper. A rule that has never been launched is a rule nobody trusts. These have been launched. I keep the leaf dry, which is against the practice of the country and I know it. A contract left in the rain is no contract here, and we call that a virtue. A rule left in the rain is no rule either, and I have met no man who sees the virtue in that. Where a hand has corrected me I have left both readings.

# The rules as they stand at the third yard

## Of the taking

1. The yard takes a boy, not a house. A house may bring him and feed him, but from the day he holds a chisel with the edge toward him he answers to the yard. The Council takes the hull and leaves the boy alone. In this port that is the last thing anybody has thought to tax.
2. A boy is taken at twelve, set to the adze at thirteen and not before he can lift it with the edge up. A boy taken later is not made up the year he missed; the yard keeps no count of hulls it did not build.
3. He is kept, not hired. He eats from the yard dish, sleeps in the loft above the moulds, and what he makes goes to the yard and comes back as bread, which is not entered. A shipwright is paid in the only coin this trade uses, a curse said at the hour and not set down.
4. His birth-name is said once at the taking and not used afterward at the yard. What he is called is the shaving, given at his first stroke of the adze and kept in the mouth. A shaving in the mouth cannot be entered, and there is nothing in this country the Council has not tried to enter.
5. The wood is read before the boy. The price is given before the name and the curse before the hull. A man who names a boy before he has seen him handle an adze has mistaken the yard for a counting house, which is across the water.

> *In the narrow upright hand of a Council clerk, against rule 3:* Retained at the yard. No wage arising. *Underneath, in mine:* I had written that he is kept. He strikes a mouth wherever he finds one and puts a figure where it stood. I have put the boy back underneath, because a figure eats no bread and sleeps in no loft. Both readings stand.

## Of the wood and the curse

6. The adze says what the man wants of the wood. The wood says what it has. A shipwright who asks the wood what it ought to be has lost the boat, and the boat goes to Sere with a figure on it and a flaw nobody here will own.
7. Green wood set into a hull in Ember will season in the hold and split three winters out, in the hand of a man this yard has never seen. Cut it out, burn it, set dry wood in its place and enter nothing. The yard calls it the weather, as the clay country calls what it has not measured.
8. No boy is set to pitch before his second year. Hot pitch takes the skin off and gives it back in a shape that will not close around a tool. A master who calls the scar a trade-mark is selling the boy's fingers one at a time.
9. The curse is said every hour of the work and is included in the thirty marks. A man who pays less gets more of it; it does not sink the boat, which would be a gift, and no shipwright in Vhal has ever given anything away. Say it to the wood, not the buyer; said to a buyer it becomes a bargain, and a bargain here is a debt with no date.
10. Count four strokes to the scarf and a slack. The slack is not a stroke and is not entered; it is the tide you have not seen and the peg you did not drive. A boy who sets a fifth peg in the slack is told to take it up, and told nothing more.
11. Keep the leaf dry and the ways wet. A contract left in the rain is no contract, which the Council calls a virtue and the yard calls a morning's work undone. We do not say *kindly* to a yard-master. Say it and name your two bits, with a date.

## Of the ways and the launching

12. The keel is laid in Ember, when the ways are greased with tallow and the Quay stands to see. Nobody sings while a hull goes down; the song is carried up the ways, not down them, and nobody has given me a reason why, which is the same as giving a good one.
13. Carry a barrel of rain aboard every hull before she launches. The salt-thing follows boats at night and taps the hull where the store is; fresh water stops it as a wall stops a man. Every captain denies the barrel and every captain carries one. A shipwright who launches without one has sold a debt to the sea and entered nothing.
14. A brine-gull will lift a warm shaving off the ways and go off with it under its crop. No malice in it. Do not set a boy to watch the shavings in place of the seam; the gull is honest and the seam has a master.
15. Name the tide your boat will not see, the way the young name one at the pans. Write it in salt on the keel-block and let the sun have it. What is in salt belongs to noon; what is said belongs to the speaker. Do not put it on paper. A thing that rots is no protection for a tide that was never a contract.

# The table of years

*Kept by the girl who sweeps the mould-loft, in a hand that does not change between one line and the next.*

| Year | At the yard | What is taken |
|---|---|---|
| Twelve | Carry, sweep, boil tar, hold the chisel with the edge up | The birth-name, said once and not used again |
| Thirteen | The first shaving; the adze kept level; four to the scarf and a slack | The habit of asking what the wood ought to be |
| Fourteen | The first peg, driven and left standing; the curse said to the wood | The shaving-name, given by the first stroke and kept in the mouth |
| Fifteen | Read a mould; turn a keel; pitch on the shore side of the fire | The belief that a thing measured is a thing known |
| Sixteen | Lay a keel alone and set the ribs to it | The last of the boy's account with any house |
| Seventeen | Launch a hull and stand at the last peg to hear the curse | The common end of a keeping, which is not a short one |

16. Seventeen is the common end and not a short one; a boat is longer in the building than a girl is at a furnace. A boy who has been through the years is not paid out and sent away. He stays where the yard needs him, at the third yard or on a boat whose share goes through his mother. The yard does not choose. The wood does, and the Council after it.

# The table of faults

| Fault | As corrected at the yard |
|---|---|
| Green wood set in a hull | Cut out, burnt, dry wood set in its place; nothing entered |
| A shaving-name written on salt-paper | The paper left out in the rain; the name said again to the adze |
| *Kindly* said to a yard-master | Two bits owed, named on the spot, with a date |
| A hull launched without rain aboard | The barrel carried three paces and set down; the hull not launched till it is aboard |
| The Hearth beneath the salt asked about | Answered once: under the salt, and the Court's fire its chimney. Asked twice: send it to the dippers |
| A curse sold to the Council and the other kept | Not entered. There is no law against it, which is why the yard does not speak of it |

17. A fault copied twice in a building is a fault the boy has not understood, and the master sits with him at the mould until he has. The adze is not a rod, though men who never held one have called it both.

# The launching-song, pasted inside the cover

*Carried up the ways while the keel is laid, standing, with a tallow hand on the beam. Never sung when a hull goes down, and never while the Council's man is on the ways. The third line of the last verse is the boy's.*

> Tar on the ways and tallow under,
> the keel goes up where the hull goes down;
> four for the scarf and a slack for the sea,
> and the curse goes into the price.
>
> Thirty marks and a curse an hour,
> the Council enters the marks alone;
> the share goes down where the mother went,
> and the man come aboard says half.
>
> Carry the rain in a barrel by,
> the gull takes the shaving and flies;
> name her a tide that she'll never see ---
> salt says yes and the sun says no.
>
> Tar on the ways and tallow under,
> one hull for the tally and one for the sea;
> the one they pay for isn't the one I built,
> and the fifth peg stands where the slack should be.

The wet rope across the third verse came from the song being carried up in rain at the outer yard. I have flattened it; the words hold.

# What the borrowed hands did

A Council clerk borrowed the leaf at Greybank with ink in two fingers of a soft cuff, and took the verbs out as though a verb were a thing a man could carry off in a sleeve.

- Against rule 9 he set *the imprecation is comprised in the consideration* above *the curse is said every hour of the work*, a sentence true of no boat that went down the ways. I have put mine back underneath.

The other hand is charcoal, a shipwright of the inner yard who would not sign it. Against *no shipwright in Vhal has ever given anything away* he struck the words hard and wrote above, *I gave a man a hull in Y.H. 611 and never entered it, which is the only curse I ever said with a name in it.* I have left his line struck above and mine standing under, so a boy can see what was said and what was done to it. The charcoal has come off on the fold and I have not brushed it away. A rule two men have argued over is a rule that has been used.

# At the head and in the gutter

The salt bloom at the head came up in a wet week and has eaten the last of a word, which I have not written back; a word the weather has taken is truer than one a clerk can still read. The tar thumb is mine, off the rope I coil at dusk. The shaving in the second fold is keel, dry as bone, laid there by a boy who wanted the leaf to hold a place open. The rope fibre has gone soft across the song and tells me when to shut the leaf away. The gull's peck went through a rule and nobody has claimed the bird. The fish-hook nick came from a disputed count of herrings. The ring of wine has salt at the rim; the man set it down and drank without the salt, which is a clay-country habit, and has not been back. The pitch-scorch took the corner of rule 7 without the sense. The green bead is Cliffs glass from a girl who leaned to read the faults and would not ask after the curse; I have kept it where it fell. The grey along the outer fold is not mine and not the Council's, and I have not swept it. There is no Last Day at a yard, and nothing here is burnt in the morning and called dealt with.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The wood keeps what the adze took. The Council keeps what the wood was sold for.
> Between the two a boy learns a trade that has no column, and the curse stands in the gap.

I asked a yard-master, a Council clerk and a woman who holds a share from her mother, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written. The leaf goes back on its nail and the tar takes another word by the next launching. Tomorrow a boy will be brought at twelve with a house-name he will not use, and I shall say the curse once to the wood, put a chisel in his hand with the edge toward him, and say nothing about the name.
