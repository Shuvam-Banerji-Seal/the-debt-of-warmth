I keep the slate-box at the shrine in Low Kiln. It stands on the shelf beneath the niche, and the women bring slates to it on the evening of the third day and take them away on the Long Night to lie under the crossed mark cut into the stone above. There is a bench, a tin of stones beneath it, and a birth-cloth folded in the box with these sayings written on the back of a rubbing that spoiled. I write on the back because the face of a slate is for a name, and a name on the face of a slate is a thing that travels.

No girl learns this from a book. A book goes up the road and comes back with a figure in it. These go from mouth to cloth and from cloth to mouth, said low with the wool drawn up and the hand on the belly. A rule that has never had tallow on it is a rule nobody has had to say. These have been said.

They were gathered in Y.H. 782, the year the College made every birth a Court entry and paid the maids to keep it. We took the money; a woman with cold children takes money and asks the giver what it buys. What it bought was the three days, which were ours before they were entered. Where a borrowed hand has corrected me I have left both readings, as a mended pot leaves the crack showing.

# The sayings as they stand at the shrine

## Of the taking

1. The shrine takes a girl, not a house. A house may bring her and feed her, but from the day she holds a stone at a belly she answers to the maid, who answers for her bread, her bed, her stones and the figures she writes. A house that will not give her up has given nothing.
2. Taken at nine, set to the stones at eleven, and not before she can hold a stone against her own wrist and say whether it is too hot. A child set to the belly before she has learned the wrist has carried a stone that burned somebody. The wrist knows; the eye does not, and the mother is not there to tell her.
3. She eats from the maid's dish and sleeps under the maid's roof. What she earns goes to the shrine and comes back as bread, not as a figure in a girl's hand.
4. She keeps the name her mother gave her. We are not the furnace and do not name a child after a thing it did. The slate-name is a different name, and the two must not be set side by side where a man with a soft cuff is standing.
5. No girl is asked whether she wants the stone. She is asked what she is bought for: a warmed thing at a stranger's middle, a hand to the slate before the child, and the not saying of what she sees beneath two crossed fingers. The wanting comes after, or it does not; we have never claimed to teach that part.

> *In the narrow upright hand of a clerk of the College, against rule 3:* Retained at the shrine; stipend arising from Y.H. 782. *In mine, underneath:* The stipend came in 782 and is paid to the maid, not the girl. The girl is fed. I have put *fed* back, because a girl who has eaten is a thing his sentence has no room for. Both stand.

## Of the stone

6. Put it at the middle and not at the ends; warm the middle and wrap the ends. The chill comes up from the ends and settles in the middle on the second or third day, and it is the second chill and not a sickness and not a thing to be entered.
7. Hold the stone to your own wrist first. A stone fresh from the fire will burn a belly as easily as a hand, and the belly cannot tell you to wait. Three breaths at the middle and three more, said low enough that the mother hears a woman counting and not a figure being taken.
8. Do not rub the ends and do not set a foot to the fire. A neighbour will tell you to hold the foot to the hearth; say the house has spoken and carry the stone back to the middle, for a woman who spends her heat on the extremity has none left for the part that can keep it. You will pay for that in the thaw.
9. Keep a stone from every birth in a tin beneath the bench, and never mark one. A clerk would call the tin a count; give him no figures and he will write *unentered residue* above it and go away satisfied, for a figure with nobody in it pleases him more than one with a person. A marked stone is a slate, and any man who can hold it can read it.

## Of the three days

*Kept on the face of the rubbing. The day goes down before the face is looked at, which is the order of this trade and not the order of a counting house.*

| Day | To the child | On the slate | Not on the slate |
|---|---|---|---|
| First | Stone to the belly; the child to the breast; the birth-flower covered | The house, the day, the hour before first grey | A name you would keep |
| Second | Wrap the cold ends and leave them; count the breaths; do not rub | The day again, in the same hand | What the child said in the night |
| Third | Keep the stone if the chill came; give the child over | The name, if the mother has given it; else the date and no more | The chill, the stone, the neighbour's advice |

10. A hand goes to the slate before it goes to the child. A hand that goes the other way has decided what the slate will say and will spend the evening making the child agree with it.
11. Write the day first. The face will tell you the rest in its own time and will keep telling you for a year. A birth is entered within three days, a rule the College wrote and ours before it was theirs.
12. If the mother has not given a name by the third day, write the date and no more. Leave the place where the name should stand the length of a thumb, and do not put a nought in it. A nought is a figure and a place is not.
13. There is a second name for this chill which the Court struck out of its record in Y.H. 651. We do not write it. A maid who writes it has put a figure where a child was, and the figure will be read by a man who never held the child.

> *In a small quick hand, set above rule 13 by a collector's clerk who stood in the doorway while I was saying it:* A common event; no entry arises; no remedy applied. *In mine, underneath:* He has called the chill common and taken the child out of the sentence. A thing called common has still got a belly in it. Both stand.

## Of the man with the tube

14. He comes before the three days are out if the house has owed, which in Low Kiln is most houses. He takes the mother's attention first, a kindness the Court has never entered: a woman asked for her warmth looks at the man asking, not at the child. Give him the mother and keep the child behind the slate.
15. If he asks what the stone is for, tell him it is for the warmth. It is for the warmth: a true answer, with no figure in it, and he cannot put it on a slate without putting a warmth on a slate, which no man does standing where the warmth is.
16. Do not say no. We do not say no. Put the pot between the child and the door and talk to the pot about the weather till he has gone back over the bridge. The pot has never been asked what it held.
17. If he leaves a pale ring on the mother's wrist, do not rub it or cover it before he has gone; cover it after. A thing covered too soon looks like a thing hidden, and a thing hidden is asked about twice.

## Of the flower and the not saying

18. A child may come among you with a red branch across the ribs or down the arm. Cover it; do not wash it; do not call it by a name. It is a red branch, and the day will tell you what it was if it wants to.
19. Cross two fingers over it and say nothing. The day you tell somebody what you saw is the day it gets written down, and a thing written down here does not stay a thing you saw. It becomes a figure in a column, and the column goes up the road.
20. One in twelve is printed on the notice and unchallenged. The other figure is not printed. I do not write it here, and a maid who writes it has made a figure of the house she was sitting in. Ask the pot. The pot is more use.

## Of faults and their correcting

| Fault | As corrected at the shrine |
|---|---|
| Stone set to a foot or a hand | The stone taken back to the middle; the end wrapped and left |
| A stone given fresh from the fire | Held to the wrist first; the belly not burned |
| Hand to the child before the slate | The slate set down first; the day written again in the same hand |
| The chill entered on the slate | Struck; *no entry* set above; the words left underneath |
| A mark put on a kept stone | The mark rubbed away with the thumb; the stone put back in the tin |
| The unprinted figure spoken where a clerk can hear | Not entered. The printed figure stood, and the other is not written here |

21. A fault copied twice at one birth has not been understood; the maid sits with the girl until it has. The stone is not a rod.

# The washing-song, sewn into the fold

*Said low to the stone, sitting, with the child at the belly and the wool drawn up. Never across the room. The second verse is the one the old maids sang and the girls sing it wrong on purpose, putting the wrist after the belly where the words will not bear it.*

> Stone from the hearth and wool from the ewe,
> three at the middle and three more too;
> warm the middle and wrap the end,
> and the house keeps what the house can mend.
>
> A hand on the slate before the eye,
> the day set down and the face left by;
> put a place where the name should be
> and the clerk reads clean what the mother cannot see.
>
> Two fingers crossed and a mouth shut tight,
> the red branch covered and out of sight;
> the pot talks weather while the boots go through,
> and the stone goes back to the tin unmarked too.

The curl of grey wool in the sewing caught there the morning a girl sang the second verse in the rain and would not sing again. I have flattened the fold.

# What the borrowed hands did

A clerk of the College borrowed the leaf on a market morning, ink set in two fingers of a soft cuff. He takes the verbs out as though a verb were a thing a man could hide in a sleeve. Against *the girl is fed* he set *retained at the shrine; stipend arising*, true about a figure and false about a girl who eats from the dish. I have put my word underneath and left his above, where the money is.

The other is a collector's clerk, who wrote while I was still saying rule 13. He took the belly out and left the event standing. He can enter the chill as common and the tin as residue and the ring as a thing provided, because all of those have a place in his book. The wrist that tested the heat and the fingers that stayed crossed he cannot enter, and he has not tried.

# At the head and in the gutter

The wax at the head is off the shrine candle, worked into the grain in a drop no knife takes out clean. The tallow is mine, from holding the leaf down in the draught under the niche. The grey in the gutter came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying; I have not swept it, and there is no Last Day in a slate-box. The brown in the fold is birth-water and has eaten two words off the back; I have not written them in, for a saying with two words gone is said slowly, and all of ours are. The pale round came off a tube-wrap set down while the measures were counted, and nobody will own the wrap. The green bead came off a girl with sweetness at her mouth who leaned over to see the writing; I have not washed it. There is no heat here to take a word into glass and keep it for the light; the damp corrects instead, one word at a time, and keeps no roll.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The stone keeps the middle and not the name; the slate keeps the name and not the chill. Between the two a maid carries a child over the bridge and sets it down, and goes back for the next.

I asked an old maid come down from Cold Acre, a clerk of the College, and a woman who brings well-water, and had three answers and no hand, as before about the grey print and the two words gone. I have left the line where it was written. The cloth goes back into the fold. Tomorrow a house will bring a girl at nine, and I shall put a stone into her own hand before I let her near a belly, and tell her what came up and not what she ought to have found, and by afternoon the tallow will have come off on another sleeve.
