I keep the last watch at the post. There is a post and no gate, and the Warden's men who set me here count those who go up and those who come down, and do not promise the third thing. I state what I hold before I say who I am: six and twenty, and a hand that has never been entered. I have put the number where a name would stand. A post keeps measures, not men.

The book is a spoiled requisition for warm-stone, and the figure went off it before anybody could charge it. I write in charcoal, which goes into the grain and cannot be taken off with a cuff, and I carry the leaves in oiled cloth in the breast of my coat with a flint and a piece of warm-stone that is cold when I wake and warm again by the middle of the watch according to where I sit. The coal smut at the fold is off the sleeve I hold the leaf down with, for the draught at the post takes paper before it takes a man. I have not brushed it away.

# What comes

It comes from beyond the last stone, walking toward any vessel with a head. That is the whole rule. It does not choose a man and it does not pass a man by; it comes toward the head the way water goes downhill, and a man standing still is the easiest thing in the Waste to come toward.

It is tall and thin and the colour of the post stone after a wind, and it has the look of a thing that has been walking a long time in one direction and has forgotten which. It makes no sound. I have stood within four paces of one and heard nothing but the wind getting out of its way. It has no language. A Wasteling who came through with a stone told me the word they have for it is the one that means *stands*, and when I asked for another he pointed at the cold, which is an answer in that country and a silence everywhere else.

It does not howl. It does not eat. These are the two things a man from the clay countries gets wrong, and he generally gets them wrong once.

# What it does

It comes close and stands. That is the doing of it. A man's Warm goes out to it, slowly at first and then faster, the way a cup set on a cold stone loses by degrees and all at once. There is no struggle. I have not seen a man struck or thrown or bitten, and I have watched more of it than is good for a man who still wants to sleep.

I watched a surveyor come back from beyond the last stone in Ash. He came down the road on his feet, which is more than the Warden promises, and sat on the bench at the post with his hands open on his knees, and could not say what he had gone for. He was older. That is the word I use and I have looked for a better one and not found it. He had a day missing somewhere he could not place, and a word gone out of his mouth that he had carried up with him and did not bring down. He asked for the warm and I gave him the stone from my coat. He held it a while and did not know what it was for.

The physician at Greybank would put a figure on the day. I have set down that the man did not know he had lost it, which is not the same thing as a figure and cannot be carried into a room with a slate.

It does not kill. It takes. A man who has been taken is not dead and is not whole, and the two states have different names in every country but this one, where they call both *coming to agree*.

There is one danger I have set above the rest, because it is the one that can follow a man home. A cold-eater that comes back over the Rim with a party does not stop at the post. I have known one stand in the lee of a house for a tend and take the warm out of the room where a family slept, and the family went on sleeping and went on being cold, and could not say why. A whole village is needed to drive one off, and the villages here are three huts, a board and a stone. We do not drive them off. We shut the door and sit on the warm side of the fire and count the nights.

# The count of the watch

*Said going up beyond the post, never coming down. The fourth line of the first part is left out and nobody fills it, because it is the place a man leaves and a place has no name. The Wastelings say the words badly on purpose, which is a kindness I have not measured.*

> One for the post and one for the stone,
> one for the man who goes on alone;
> the shape comes on and the shape stands still,
>
> and one for the man who stays on the hill.
>
> Count what you hold and count what you gave,
> count the day that you cannot recall;
> the cold takes nothing and leaves no mark
> and the road comes back with a shorter name.
>
> No gate at the post and no fire to bear,
> no word for owing and none to spare;
> it stands where it stands and you go where you go,
> and the post keeps the count and does not let go.

# The tally

These are the watches I kept a true account of. There were others I did not set down because my hands were full, and one I did not set down because I had the cold in me and could not tell which day it was. I have set down both sorts as they stand. The last column is not mine.

> *In the narrow upright Ledgerhand of a clerk of the factor at Greybank, written along the foot:* Cold is not a vessel. Days and words not entered. No consideration recorded. Account stands.

I have left his hand standing. He is right by his law and wrong about the road. He has put *nil* against a man who sat on my bench with his hands open and could not name his own son, and *not entered* against a house that sat cold for a tend, and *account stands* over both, which is the neatest piece of work I have seen come up from Greybank. I have written the day and the word under his line in charcoal, where his ruler cannot reach.

> *Below, in my hand:* Stone 6. A day and a word. The man asked for the warm and did not know what the stone was for. No figure, and none asked for.

> *Cut into the edge of the leaf with a tally-knife, in the hand of a man who would not write:* Our numbers are on the arm. Theirs are in what they have not let go. Both can be read. Only one can be entered.

I asked him to write it and he cut it instead. He has been up to the stones and came back with a number he will not say, and the cut has gone grey in the fold.

# What can be counted here

There is no market in it. I have been offered marks for a stone a Wasteling gave me for nothing, and refused, because a thing given without a price is the one thing a lowlander cannot leave alone. A Wasteling will give a stone, a name, or a day's warmth and ask nothing back, and a man from the south will call it theft or folly or a virtue done badly, because he has no column for a gift.

The Vorr at the stones will look at a place where one has stood and say *the numbers are correct*, which is not a translation and not an answer and is the only answer they have. I have asked them what the numbers count. They said the numbers are correct. I have written their sentence down and made nothing of it, because a man who makes something of a Vorr's sentence generally loses a day and does not know where it went.

# What the hearths say

They say it is the Waste come to fetch what is owed, a thing that walks out of the cold to collect the debt a man has left standing. There are songs in the Fenmarch with a man's name in them and a wrong done at a weir, and the Wastelings have a verse with a place in it where a word should be. I have heard both and I have no quarrel with a song. But the Wastelings say there is nothing owed out here, and that a man who stands his ground against the shape is not brave but has misunderstood the arrangement. I have set the two sayings side by side and joined neither, because the joining of them is a map, and a man with a map comes up from Greybank with a list.

I have put my hand on the stone where one stood and on stone a spear-cast away. Under the one it was cold, and colder than I have found elsewhere, and by morning frost had written a branching flower upon it where the Warm had gone out. I know the pattern from the clay countries, where it grows on the wall of a room a thin person has slept in all winter. Here it grows on stone in the open air and nobody calls it weather. I have written the shape of it and no cause.

# At the head and in the margins

The frost at the head came up out of the grain and pressed the first two lines together, so that *stands* and *you go* lie closer here than they were said; I have not prised them apart, because they are generally said in one breath. The coal smut is mine. The flake of warm-stone in the second fold kept its heat one night on the stone floor and is cold now, which is the order of things here. The grey stone in the gutter was given by a woman who would not take a bowl for it, and I have stitched it in so the count and the thing it counts travel together. The thread of blue-grey is off a gift-knot she tied and left on the bench and did not take up. The tallow is my lamp set too near the leaf. The notch goes clean through a word and I have not written the word back, because a notch is the nearest this trade comes to a place. The charcoal is thin where the wrap lay against my breast. The clerk's line I have not touched and the cut line I have not answered, because a man who cuts instead of writing has said all he is going to say.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The cold keeps what nobody kept.
> It does not ask to be paid. Ask the post.

I asked a Wasteling who came through with no stone, a surveyor come down from the stones, and a clerk at Greybank, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written.

There is a place beyond the last stone where the snow lies flat and unbroken and no track has ever shown, and I have not gone to it. Next Ash I will take a coil of cord and a piece of warm-stone and go up as far as the last stone, and I will count what goes up and what comes down, and if a man comes down who did not go up I will set that down too, in charcoal, with the verb left standing and no figure at the end of it.
