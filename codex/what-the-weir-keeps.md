To Nivva of the Quay, who holds a twelfth through her mother and will not say it where a factor's man can hear. From Halla at the lower boards, a league upriver of Sill, where the water drops four feet over hard stone and the eels come up against the draw.

I write on the back of a sorting tally I cut from a strip of birch bark this morning, because there is no paper down here that has not been near the water. The charcoal takes the grain in spikes and I have gone over the hard words twice. Do not wet your thumb at the fold. The slime is in it and will come off on you, and you will carry the smell of the Weir up the Salt Road a fortnight and be asked what you have been doing, which is a question no woman in Vhal has ever answered well.

# The draw

The run came thin. Nine baskets at the turn of the month where there should have been fifteen, and the dippers said the draw ran against the wind again, which it always does and which the Council is always surprised by. I have worked these boards nineteen autumns and I have never once seen the Sallow run with the wind for a whole tend, and I have seen a man from Greybank stand on this stone and write that it did. He wrote it in a clean hand and went away warm and nobody on the Quay was the worse for it, so I suppose it is a true account in the only place accounts are made true.

A brine-gull took a coal off the brazier while I had my hands in a creel. I threw a chip of cliff-glass at him and missed, and he went off upriver with the coal warm under his crop, as though he had been invited to the fire. The eels came up grey and thin and did not fight the basket. When they fight the basket you know the water is cold under them; when they lie down in it like wet wool you know nothing at all, and that is worse.

Your mother used to say the river keeps what the river takes. I have thought about that for nineteen autumns and I still do not know whether it is a comfort or an account.

# The buyer who counted twice

A man in a coat with the river in its hem came down on the fourth morning and named a figure for two pair. He named it twice. I gave him the same answer I always give — *Two marks the pair and two short. You've said it twice and I've counted it twice. There's nothing in the sentence that wants an answer* — and he took the eels and went, because a man who says a figure twice has already heard himself and does not need a woman to agree with him.

He left a strip of salt-paper on the stone. Salt-paper is a good thing in Vhal; everyone says so, and it is held a virtue that a contract rots in the damp, so that a debt made in Ilmen is nothing by Silt and nobody is cheated for longer than a season. Down here the damp took the figure off it before I had carried it six paces. I put it in the brazier and the paper went with a small sweet smell, and I thought of the factor at Greybank, who cannot leave a thing alone, and how he would have entered the figure before the damp got to it and then charged us all for the entering.

One in twelve, as the notice says. The figure read is not the figure paid. There is a space between them with no signature on it and no name standing under it, and a man could put his whole life in that space and no clerk would turn a page to look.

# What I counted and did not set down

| When | What came up | What I kept | What I set down |
|---|---|---|---|
| Turn of the month | Nine baskets, grey and quiet | Nine bones in the creel string | Nothing |
| Second morning | Two pair, a short one let go | Two bones and a chip of the third | Nothing |
| Fourth morning | A buyer's figure, named twice | The count in my apron, not on his slate | Nothing |
| Seventh morning | One eel up where the wind was down | One bone, tied with a twist the wrong way | Nothing |
| Last tend | The draw went out on the ebb | The whole string, untied | Nothing |

# The bone in the creel

I keep the count in eel-bones on a string in my apron. There are days I could not tell you how many baskets went over the boards if a man held a knife at me, but I can put my thumb on the bone for the seventh morning and tell you the eel went up when the wind was down and lay in the basket as though it had been waiting for me. A slate takes a figure off a thing and gives back a number, and the number is always cleaner than the thing. A bone gives back nothing. That is why I keep them.

Do not tell Nivva about the string, I wrote that line and then crossed it out, because you are Nivva and you will have read it already. Keep the string to yourself if you ever come down here. There is no word in Grathic for a count that is kept in order to be kept, and I have got along without one for nineteen years.

# The sorting song

*Sung low over the boards, with the creel on the knee and the bones under the thumb. Never across the water. The third verse is my mother's and I have set mine under it so you can see where the words change.*

> Nine came up where fifteen should,
> the draw went against the wind;
> the gull took coal, the buyer took two,
> and the river took the rest.
>
> Bone for the short and bone for the thin,
> a twist the wrong way on the seventh;
> put the thumb on the bone and not on the slate,
> for the slate has a hand at the other end.
>
> Salt in the fold and silt at the head,
> and a figure that rots where it falls;
> keep the string and let the paper go,
> and the count comes back to the one who kept it.
>
> Four at the drop and one gone down,
> the eel came up where the wind was low;
> the boat goes out on the turning tide,
> and the one that stayed does not go.

# In the margin, in a second hand, close to the lines and leaning

The draw ran up, not down, on your seventh. You have put your twist on the wrong bone and always did. I have struck the seventh through and set the fourth underneath in your own charcoal so that you cannot say a stranger wrote it.

Do not tell me the run is thin. Tell me you are coming down. I am not a dippers' woman and I can bear a thing said straight if it is said once. The twelfth is mine through my mother and I will not say it where a factor's man can hear it, and I would thank you not to say it for me in a letter that goes through Greybank.

# What the factor's clerk did

A clerk at the factor's house borrowed the leaf on a market morning, being a man who cannot leave paper lie. He has a cuff soft from washing and the ink sits in two fingers of it and will not come out. Against *nine baskets where fifteen should be* he set *short draw, current*. Against *two marks the pair and two short* he wrote *price agreed*, which is true and cold and leaves out the twice that it was said. Against the bone-string he set *unentered residue*, and against the eel that went up when the wind was down he put a round nought and wrote *weather; no entry*.

He takes the verbs out of a thing and leaves the figures standing, as though a thing always true had no need of anybody to keep it. He can enter the draw as current and the string as residue and the short as agreed, because all of those have a place in his book. He cannot enter the thumb that found the seventh bone or the mother who said the river keeps what it takes, and he has not tried. I have put my words back underneath and left his standing above, where they belong, the way the Ash is left in the corner until the morning after the Last Day and called dealt with.

# At the head and in the gutter

The grey at the head is eel-slime rubbed in with the thumb that held the creel; it will not wash out of birch and I have stopped trying. The silt is river silt, pale in the grain where I laid the strip on the stone to copy the buyer's figure, and it is finer than the silt in the yard at home and does not grit between the teeth. The white in the gutter is salt bloom, from Vhal and not from here; I have never known a letter to arrive with salt already in it, and I can only suppose the carrier carried it in his coat from the Quay before he brought it up. The green bead in the lower corner is cliff-glass off the Glaze Cliffs, five leagues along the shore from you, and it caught in the bark at the coast pass in the rain; it is the colour of a wasp and I would not put it in my mouth for anything. The nick in the second fold is from the fish-hook I use to lift the lid of the creel, and it went through when I folded in a hurry at the going of the light. The tar is mine, off the cord that ties the string of bones, and it is a better smell than the slime.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The slate keeps the figure and the river keeps the eel; between the two a woman keeps a string of bones, and sets it down, and goes to the next board.

I asked the glass carrier who brought this up and a man who reads the notices at the Cross and a woman who sells rope at the foot of the Salt Road, and had three answers and no hand, as I have had before about the green bead and the bone tied the wrong way. I have left the line where it was written. The leaf goes back into the tin with the boat tallies. Tomorrow the draw will go out on the ebb and I will put a bone on the string and not figure it, and Nivva will keep the twelfth where no factor's man can hear her, and neither of us will call the string an account.
