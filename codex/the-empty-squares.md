I keep the annals in the factor's house at Greybank. The office stands on the roll and the hand does not; when the factor wants a name for the hand he writes *the roll-room*, which is as much a name as a man with no house is allowed to have. What follows I copied in the last tend of Stone, in the Thin Year, from an original kept face down in the Hearthseat, because the man who set it there did not care to have it read standing up. I have put verbs back where I found them taken out. A chronicle with no verbs in it is a thing a man can read aloud in a clean voice and go home believing, and I have had to stand at this table and listen to too many clean voices.

The Empty Cantons lie sixty leagues south of Sill, between Grath and the Fenmarch, on no road a cart can make in a day. A man can walk into them, and several have, and come back with nothing to tell except the lie of the ground. There are roads still rutted where no wheel has gone in eight hundred years; wells with the stones laid tidy about them and the water down out of sight; doorways standing where the walls have gone; and in every third doorway a square of bare clay where something used to hang. The Fen reeves call the place the Quiet Acre. The Court calls it the Empty Cantons. The temple calls it nothing at all, and that is how a place is made to disappear.

> *In the narrow hand of a clerk of the College, against the five rows of squares:* Squares are not entries. Enter as nil.
>
> *Underneath, in mine:* A square is where a figure stood and was taken away; a nought is a figure put in its place. He has put noughts in the squares and the column balances. That is how I know it does not.

The figure the College likes is six whole returns in the third year after the lighting, which is the year the first Returns were recorded and the year the roll begins to have something in it worth counting. I have not checked the addition of that column, and I have learned not to. The last time a clerk of this office added a column he got a number that would not agree with the printed one, and the factor told him to copy the printed one again, in a better hand.

# What the temple tells the children

The temple tells it this way. The Cantons were taken up in the first Returns, and their people are whole somewhere, though no record says where. The cost of those first whole returns was entered against a province, and the province is this one. I set that down because it is written in the roll-room and not because I can say what it means. A chronicler who explains a figure has begun to write a sermon, and a sermon is a thing that gets copied more often than an account.

The oldest ledger says only that the fires went out in a season of no frost. The words *returned elsewhere* were added by a hand that writes with its verbs taken out, and every copy since has been smoother than the one before. I have set the smooth words under the rough ones and left both standing. When the temple voice reads them at a Weighing it makes a mercy of a line of squares, and a household hears a mercy and goes home, which is the whole art of that voice.

> *In a hand I take to be a temple hand, struck through* went out in a season of no frost *and written above,* were gathered into the Hearth. *Both stand. A hand that strikes a thing out and writes above it is a hand that wants the thing gone and cannot bear to lose the place where it was.*

# What the ground keeps

Three surveyors have walked the Cantons that I know of. Two went for the Court and one went for the Keldish, and none put his name to the account, which is the first thing the three accounts agree on.

The river road is there under the turf, rutted to the depth of a cart's wheel. The wells are dry but not fallen in, and the stones are laid round them in the old order, which a man does not do when he is leaving in a hurry. In the doorways the squares of bare clay are where the house-rolls hung, and not one of them has a nail in it now. In one doorway a slate still stands in its notch. The figures on it are not figures a clerk of this office can read, and the Vorr, being asked, said that the numbers were correct, which is an answer a man can carry away and still know nothing. The deepest marks were not cut with iron. The surveyors of Keld have one word for such marks and the Court's copy has another; I have written both in the margin and chosen neither, because a chronicler who chooses between two guesses has made a fact of one of them and will have to answer for it.

The hearths are cold, and the ash in them is not grey. This is the thing the surveyors set down last and speak of least. A hearth that has been used has grey in it, and a hearth that has never been used has none; these have a white dust like bone meal, and no fire has ever been in them that any man can find. A man of the Fenmarch told me the white is what is left when a promise has been kept too long. I have not put that in the account, because I do not know it for a lie and I do not know it for the truth, and the annals are not the place for a thing I cannot weigh.

# The song the reed-cutters carry

*Sung on the marsh road going in, not coming back. I took it down from a reeve of the Standing Post who would not sing it while a man in a clean coat stood on the boardwalk.*

> Sixty leagues and never a smoke,
> the wells give down and the doorways stand;
> the reeve counts ruts and the ruts count back,
> and nobody lives in the quiet land.
>
> Four for the corners and four for the wall,
> and a square where the name of the house should be;
> the fox has the hearth and the reed has the well,
> and the slate in the door keeps a figure for me.
>
> They went up in the thaw and came not in the ice;
> the Court has a figure for how many went,
> and a square for the ones the figure forgot,
> and the square is the only one I have spent.

The reeve said the last line was about the song and not about the Cantons, and when I asked him what the difference was he said a man who needed the difference explained would not have sung it. I have copied the line as it came and put no gloss on it.

# The matter of the fifth spring

In Y.H. 618 the Court sealed a fifth of the Nine Wells and hanged the keeper of it for drawing without a key. The roll gives the office and not the name, which is the Court's way of hanging a man twice. The Fen reeves say the spring that was sealed is not under the Keel at all but under the Cantons, and that the Court's survey puts it twenty leagues from the Hearthseat so that no collector need walk the south road. I have copied their saying and the Court's figure side by side. One of them is a place and one of them is a number, and a chronicler who joins the two is drawing a map, which is a thing I have no licence to make.

# What was struck out, and what I did not

Where I wrote *who paid for the sixty empty fires*, a narrower hand set *the account stands* above and left mine under it, saying a chronicle is not a slate and need not be argued with. I have kept both. A question that a clerk can answer in four words is a question that has been answered already, and not by me.

In the margin of the third leaf a hand I do not know has copied a sentence, struck through twice and written again underneath: *Another kept badly, and we inherited the crack.* I have seen the same words on the back of a slate that came out of the Old Kiln and lies now in a kiln-house in Low Kiln, where nobody knows what it is. The man who copied them here wanted them found and wanted them not to count, which is the state of most things in this office that matter. I asked a Vorr who came through Greybank on the Hearth Road what the words meant. He said the numbers were correct. I asked a man of Keld and he turned the slate face down and said he had not seen it.

# At the foot

The brown thumb at the head is mine; iron-gall will mark a man who keeps a quire open through a tend, and there is no washing it out of the creases. The slate dust in the gutter came off a surveyor who leaned over my table with the south-road dirt still on his cuffs, and he would not sit down. The tar along the fore-edge is from the quay, where I set the leaves while a barge unloaded salt and I counted a carter's figures and let my own leaf take the weather. The reed between the second and third leaves was put there by the reeve who sang; he folded it the way a man folds a thing he means to come back for. The small square of bare paper is where I scraped a word away with a knife and put no other in its place. It is the only square in this quire that I made myself, and I made it deliberately, which is more than I can say for the others.

The leaves go back into the drawer at Greybank. At the Opening of the Slates I copied them again and put the squares in again with my own hand, because a square is the only figure the Court has never learned to read. The Cantons have no people and no smoke, but they have roads, and a road is a thing somebody meant to come back along. The annals go on. Next tend I copy the quay tallies, and after that the Weighing figures from the Hearthseat, and the squares will keep their places in the roll for as long as there is a hand to set them there.
