I keep the lamps at the Tallow Works. That is my whole office: to pour the rendered fat while it is still running, to trim the wicks, to carry the light into the byre where the men sit at the turning of the year, and to know how much a flame takes before it gives. In Sill they know us by the smell when the wind is north. They do not know us by our names, and we have stopped minding.

I set this down because a man come up from the factor's house at Greybank stood in the door of the fat-house with his cuff soft from washing and asked what we sang when we poured the fourth cup out. I told him we poured it on the floor. He wrote that down and went away satisfied, which is how I knew he had written the wrong thing. This is the song as we say it, with the fourth where we put it and not where he put it.

The brown at the head is tallow. I held the leaf down in the draught that comes up under the rendering pot and did not know I had marked it. A thing written in tallow keeps its mark when the words have gone, which is more than can be said for the Court's paper.

# When it is sung

- At the turning of the year, after the last fat is run off and before the lamps go up, with the small ale watered the way we water it and the pot set on the floor between the feet. Not at the feast and not at the Weighing. This is a song for a room with smoke in it.
- Pour the first three into cups. The fourth goes under the bench, on the trodden ash, and is not drunk. A cup poured out is not a cup wasted, and a man who calls it wasted has begun to count what he has not paid for.
- Sing to the lamp and not to the company. Said to a man, the refrain becomes a question, and there is nobody in this room who can answer it without a slate in his hand.
- The lamp stands with its wick trimmed to a fingernail. A flame that gutters while the fourth is poured is read as an answer and nobody says what the question was.
- Say the last line twice. The second saying is for the one whose cup is under the bench, and the third, if anybody stays to say it, is for the one who poured the fat before us and never had a lamp of his own.
- Do not sing it on the Long Night. On the Long Night there is no fire after dusk, and a song that mentions a flame is a song with a cost in it that the house cannot meet.

# The song

*Sung low, with the ends of the words let fall as a tallow-drop falls. The refrain is said to the lamp. The cup goes under the bench on the fourth line of the last part and nobody watches where it goes.*

> One for the ewe and one for the pot,
> one for the smoke that stands for Sill;
> the fourth we pour where the ash lies deep ---
> pass the cup and mind the wick.

> The ewe gave over what she kept,
> we took the fat and left the name;
> a sheep has no slate and no return,
> and goes on giving after death.
>
> *Pass the cup and mind the wick.*
> *The fat remembers what the flame forgot.*

> Skim and skull and trotter go
> into the pot and come out light;
> what sank comes up, and what came up
> stands all night where a hand set it.
>
> *Pass the cup and mind the wick.*
> *The fat remembers what the flame forgot.*

> The north wind carries our smoke to town
> and Sill says tallow without a nose;
> it is the only thing we make
> that nobody asks the price of.
>
> *Pass the cup and mind the wick.*
> *The fat remembers what the flame forgot.*

> On Kindling the coal comes up in clay
> and every hearth touches and passes on;
> we pour our own out of the rendered pot,
> and no man says what the first one cost.
>
> *Pass the cup and mind the wick.*
> *The fat remembers what the flame forgot.*

> On the Long Night we let the lamp speak last
> and sit with our hands where the light can see them;
> three cups go round and a fourth goes down,
> and the flame takes four and keeps a fifth for itself.
>
> *Pass the cup and mind the wick.*
> *The fat remembers what the flame forgot.*
> *The fourth is under the bench. The wick stands where it stood.*

There is a space before the last refrain in the singing, the length of the pause between the pouring and the wiping of the bench. I have left it on the leaf. It is the right length for a name and for nothing else.

# A verse we do not sing when the factor's man is in the room

*Said only when the door is shut and the lamp is down to a knuckle. There is no refrain. The last line is said to the floor.*

> The collector came on Kindling with a tube of iron
> and took the first measure of the year before the ewes were shorn;
> one in twelve, he said, as the notice says,
> and wrote it in a hand that did not shake.
>
> We poured the fourth on the ash beneath the bench
> and called it for the ewe, which was a lie we have kept for nineteen years;
> a ewe has no account, and a man who wants one
> may go to Greybank and ask the factor for it.
>
> The lamp takes from the pot and gives to the room.
> The room gives nothing back and is not asked to.
> Three go round and the fourth goes under,
> and the name we do not say stands where we put it.

I have set this down because a woman who had been at the pouring told me that a verse with the factor's man outside it is a verse that keeps, and a verse with him inside it is a line in a ledger, which does not. She would not give me her name and I have not asked twice.

# What the hearth-maid put in the margin

The thick charcoal belongs to a woman come up from Sill who keeps the birth-slates and crosses two fingers over a birth-flower and says nothing about the sign. She was at the turning of the year and carried a lamp home under her coat. I have copied her words as she set them.

At *the fourth we pour where the ash lies deep*: *I have kept slates at a birth for nineteen years and I have seen a hand go to the slate before it went to the child. This pouring is the same hand turned the other way. Both give what they cannot get back.*

At *a sheep has no slate and no return*: *We break a pot at a death-house door to let the heat out. We do not break one at a ewe's throat. I asked a render-woman why, and she asked me whether I thought a ewe had a door.*

At *no man says what the first one cost*: *The year-fire is carried up the yard in a clay dish and every house touches its own to it. In our district nobody says where the flame was first lit. Here they say nothing either, but they say it standing over a pot, which is not the same nothing.*

At the space before the last refrain: *The third saying is not for the one under the bench. I have asked whose it is and been given a date, which is a door that shuts.*

# What the clerk did

A factor's clerk borrowed the leaf at Greybank, being a man who cannot let paper lie. The ink sits in two fingers of his cuff and will not come out. He writes in the narrow upright hand and takes the verbs out as though a verb were a thing a man could smuggle past a weigh.

- At *we took the fat and left the name*, he wrote *the product was rendered and the donor is not entered*, which is true in the figure and false in the room I sang it in. I have left it.
- At *the fourth we pour where the ash lies deep* he wrote *libation: nil*, and underlined it twice. A cup on the floor is nothing to a man who has never stood in a room that wanted the floor drank to.
- At *the lamp takes from the pot and gives to the room*, he struck out *takes* and *gives* and set above it *transfer, current*, which leaves the lamp standing and takes the room out of it.
- Where I had left the space before the last refrain, he put a round nought and wrote *vacancy* beside it. He has put a number where a name should stand, which is the whole art of that hand.

I have put my words back underneath and left his standing above. He takes the verbs out because a thing always true has no need of them. I put them back because a woman pouring the fourth cup at the turning of the year does not know she is hearing something always true, and wants to know who poured before her.

# At the head and in the gutter

The tallow at the head is mine and has gone into the grain, and no wet sleeve takes it out, which is why I sign with a dip and not with ink. The scorch on the second leaf is a wick-end that touched while I was writing and went out under my hand; the hand was mine and I have not entered the burn. The ring near the refrain is small ale, set down at the first verse and taken up at the last, by which time it had gone the colour of the pot it stood against. The bone-meal across the third leaf came off the sleeve of a man carrying a skull to the pot, and when I asked him to stand clear he stood clear and said nothing, which was more politeness than I expected and less than I wanted. The sheep's-ear mark in the gutter is a press from a fresh ear laid on the leaf while the tally was being made up; the edge of it took a little of the second verse and I have not written the words back in, because a song with two words gone is a song said slowly, and everything here is said slowly. The grey along the outer fold came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying; I have not swept it and there is no Last Day in a lamp-room. The wick sewn through the fold is the one that burned down on the night the fourth was poured and would not stand again. I have kept it where it is.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The pot keeps the fat and the lamp keeps the flame.
> The floor keeps the fourth and gives none back.
> The slate keeps neither and is read aloud all the same.

I asked the woman who pours, the hearth-maid who carries a lamp home under her coat, and a man who stood clear when I asked him, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written. The leaf goes back under the cracked saucer in the lamp-room. Tomorrow the wicks will be trimmed to a fingernail and the fat poured while it is still running, and the fourth will go under the bench for a name nobody says where a clerk can hear it. If an officer finds the leaf, let him take the verbs out. The next hand will put them back, and will mind the wick.
