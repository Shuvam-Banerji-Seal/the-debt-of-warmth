I dress stone for a living and have set bones and caught children in this yard for three-and-twenty years. Nobody asked me for this book. A man came in off the bank in the lean week with both hands gone white to the wrist and asked what I did for the lime, and I heard myself answer in my father's voice, and did not know whether I believed it. A remedy you have to say in your father's voice is a remedy nobody has looked at in a long while. I have looked at some of mine here; the rest I have left standing, because a man at three in the morning wants a hand more than a correction.

I write on the back of a lime-warrant that a wetting spoiled. The figures went before anybody could charge them, which is a mercy of a kind: a figure that will not read cannot be entered. The lime at the head is off the cuff I wipe the chisel with. The blue in the gutter came off the Ferr when I washed a man's arm in it, which I have not done since. Nothing in this book is free. Here a thing that costs nothing has usually been paid for by whoever carried the lime.

# The order of the dressing yard

*Cut small into the first leaf with the tally-point, with one word gone over in lime.*

- Lay the sick one on sacking, never on bare stone. Stone takes from a body faster than a bed does, and a man laid on it goes cold from the side he lies on. A physician from Greybank says a cold stone draws the heat out, which is the last thing a thin man wants. I have set my line above his. Both stand.
- Ask what he carried before you ask what ails him. A man who has carried lime knows where his back hurts; a man who has carried nothing will tell you where it ought to. This is not kindness. It is the order of the yard.
- Turn the slate face down before you go to a child. I have no slate-name and never shall; the weigh-clerk here sets his figure to a tooth before he looks at the man who brought it, and I have no wish to be taken for that kind of reader.
- Put the warm stone in wool at the belly and try it on your own wrist first. The stone comes off the kiln bank, and somebody paid for the coal that warmed it.
- Set the splint loose enough to see a finger under it.
- When the third night comes, let the woman do the counting; a number counted for her belongs to whoever counted it.
- Do not drink from the Ferr or wash a wound in it. We have said the opposite for four hundred years and have put the old line underneath rather than lose it.

# The remedies, as they are used

| Trouble | What is set to it | The manner of it | Who is spent |
|---|---|---|---|
| The lime-burn (both hands, or the forearm where the sleeve rides back) | Tallow first, then a clean fold; never dry lime | The men shake lime on a burn to dry the wet, believing white is clean. The physician says lime eats and does not dry. I have dressed three such hands with lime on them and found a white line under the skin in all three, and have not done it a fourth time. | The hand; and the household that bought the lime. |
| The white cough | Steam of hot water under a cloth; honey if the house has it | We say the dust of the good stone cuts the dust of the bad. The physician says it is stone in the lung, and the only physic is to leave the yard, which no house here can do. I sent three up the ridge; two came back worse, because the ridge has stone of its own. | The man, in the morning he coughs up a colour he will not discuss. |
| The Ferr-thin (a season at the well) | Barley-water, a stone at the belly, and work | We say it is the cold of the water and that a warm body keeps it out. The physician says the water carries lead down off the old working and that warmth has nothing to do with it. I have sent men warm to the kiln and watched them go thinner by the fifth month; one went off it after his daughter took him up to Cold Acre. I have not sent anybody else. | The household, which cannot move six leagues to good water. |
| The sweet line at the gum | Sour barley-water; the gum rubbed with wood-ash | The pots from High Kiln carry the green, and the women taste the glaze to get the colour, and say a sweet answers a sweet. The physician says it is lead and that sour water only troubles the teeth. I have seen the line go off a girl who left the glaze room and come back on one who stayed. I have not seen the lead. | The glaze room, which cannot stop and does not ask. |
| A fall from the bank | Two hands, a lath of straight sandstone, a fold of wet cloth; set it before the swelling takes | We say a bone dressed green takes the tool the way a block does. The physician says wet cloth about a limb cools what should be kept warm. I have set eleven and had three come crooked; one hand I bound too tight for fear of its moving, and it did not come back. | The man, in the holding still. This is the row where I have been least honest. |
| A lead-rat's bite | Tallow and a clean fold; burn the tails, two bits a score | We say one bite is nothing and a hundred make a thin child. The physician wrote the year Y.H. 641 in the margin, when this was known, and noted it was forgotten; he says the rat and the glaze carry the same lead. I have put the year where he wrote it and left the rat under it. | The household, whose children go thin by degrees; the catcher, paid by the tail. |
| The birth-flower (the red branch) | Nothing. Two fingers crossed, and the word the mother gives | A woman of the washing crossed two fingers at a child of this yard and covered it with a date, the way they do at Sill. I have put the date in the margin. The physician says it is a flowering of the blood and not a flower, and is right in a language nobody here speaks. | Nobody knows. The child's skin, perhaps. |
| A child that comes with the cold on it | A stone in wool at the belly, a living body under the fold, a watch to the fourth day | It comes when the birth is in Stone, and the hands go first. We walk them, believing a moving child keeps its heat. The physician says a child made warm spends faster and that a cold child cannot clot. I have watched nine and held two; all saw the fourth morning, and one went thin. I have left the count with the woman who did the watching. | The household, one night's sleep, and sometimes the child. |

Nothing here is given away. It is carried from one body into another and called a cure. That is the only sentence in this book I will stand behind.

# The note in the block

The masons tap a block with the butt of a hammer-handle before they dress a face. A sound one answers full and a flawed one answers flat, a small dead sound that stops at the skin of the stone and will not go through. This is the one thing in this country that costs nothing and is still allowed to be true.

I tapped a man once, gone thin after a winter at the well, and put my ear to the little flat I thought I heard. The flat was not in him. A body is not a block. I did not say so at the time, because there was a weigh-clerk in the room. I have written it here, which is a different matter.

The cup on the bench knows more about whether a room held its warm through the night than the man in it knows about himself, and I set the stone by the cup's note and not by the yard bell, which is counting something of its own.

# What the well by the Ferr keeps

The well was never sweetened. The clause is on the treaty slate and the factor at Greybank pasted his salt-paper over it, and the strip has lifted at one corner so that six words show and the rest do not. I have washed men at that well for three-and-twenty years and have never once tasted lead in it, which is a thing a man can say who has not looked into a slate.

In the year of the water the households drank all winter and called it thin. The well-song, taken down at the washing from a woman who had learned it from women who had, says they did; the road-book at Sill entered three hundred households and no dead. A small hard hand put seventy-one against Stonemouth, and twenty-one went downriver and were not entered again.

The physician wrote *visitation of the water* above our *cold water*, which is a true thing in a language nobody at this well speaks. He was right about the lead and had nothing to put in its place that a household here could pay for, and the well kept the larger part, as the song says. I have set his sentence above mine and left the twenty-one underneath, where a clerk carrying a round sum will not think to look.

# The washing-song

*Sung low at the tubs, to the woman and not to the child. It is not sung at the kiln. There is a place after the second verse the length of a row that was never entered, and no singer has filled it.*

> The Ferr came down with a blue-grey skin,
> the clause was pasted, the charge not paid;
> a pale line sits where the lip should be
> where the glaze is tasted at the rim.
>
> Three hundred stand where a slate has them,
> and seventy-one by the well we know;
> a man goes thin in the fifth month
> and the cup gives back what the water took.
>
> Sing to the tub, sing to the flat cup,
> sing to the woman who counts under wool;
> what comes back is not what went,
> and comes back as it can ---

A clerk of the College put a round nought in the place and wrote *nil* beneath it. A nought is a figure and can be carried out of a yard in a cuff. The twenty-one went downriver and stayed a row.

# What the physician pressed in the margin

He came down from Greybank in Smoke of Y.H. 811 with a book that had the word *lead* in it and a hand that wrote without asking. Against the lime he set *caustic; no efficacy*, against the birth-flower *a mark in the blood, nothing more*, and against the white cough *the dust of the stone, and no physic but departure*, which is true and of no use to a household whose living is cut out of that stone.

He was right about all three. I have left his sentences above mine, so the next reader can tell which is which, and put my words back underneath where a man at three in the morning will find them.

# What the College clerk did

He borrowed the leaf at Greybank when the winter lime was being counted.

- Against my column headed *Who is spent* he wrote *No cost entered*, which is a true thing in a dishonest place.
- Where I wrote *we drink all winter and call it thin*, he set *a reduction in the measure was recorded* above, as though the drinker had gone out of the sentence and left the measure standing by itself.
- Where I wrote *the well was never sweetened*, he wrote *the obligation remains outstanding*, which is the most beautiful sentence I have ever read in a hand that has never drawn water in the dark.
I have put the verbs back underneath. A clerk can speak without verbs because he is never in the yard; a man who has carried lime wants to know who paid for the coal.

# In the margin, in chalk, the bank-master's hand

> A block tells you when it is dressed. A man does not. I have dressed four hundred blocks and two hands, and neither hand gave a note. --- R.

He wrote it at the foot of the remedies while I was at the well. I have set his line above my row and mine under it, as the yard keeps the old cut showing under the new dressing. A man who has lost a hand to his own trade is allowed one sentence in a book of physic.

# At the head and in the gutter

The lime at the head is mine and will not wash out. The blue tideline came up the second leaf the morning I washed a Ferr-thin man's arm and has taken two words clean off it; I have not written them back, because a remedy with two words gone is a remedy said slowly. The tooth in the corner was pressed there by a buyer who paid two marks and said a good tooth remembers the shape of a way. The ring of barley-water came from a cup set down while I told a man to drink and he would not. The grey along the fold is not mine and not the physician's; I have not swept it, and there is no Last Day in this yard. The salt-paper strip has lifted at one corner, so a correction shows through that was meant to be covered, which is the only honest thing that paper has done here.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The block keeps the cut and the well keeps the dust;
> neither was asked who drank, or who paid.

I asked the physician, the College clerk and a woman at the washing, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. Tomorrow a man will come in off the bank with a hand gone white or a child that will not breathe, and I shall put the verbs back and turn the slate face down before I look at the child --- because a child is not a block and a body is not a cup, and the yard has room for both truths if a man leaves them side by side.
