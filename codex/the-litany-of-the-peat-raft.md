I keep the reed-cut outside Reedholt and I have set this down because the row at the cut asked for a sentence they could say with their hands full of wet reed. The Court has an order for the draw and an order for the pressing and a verse for the pot broken at the door of a death-house. It has nothing for a raft going out into the reeds, where there is no table, no slate that will stay dry, and no person who has asked to be entered. I have said this seven years and have never once said it over a person. It is said to the water, which does not answer and does not owe an answer. Where the collector has been first there is a pale round place on the arm, and the raft carries what he left. Where he has not been, the raft carries the whole of it. I have laid the two down side by side and have not made them one.

The brown at the head is peat and will not wash out of the grain, which is why the cut signs in peat and not in ink. The tideline took two words clean off the fourth station and I have not written them back; a station with two words gone is a station said slowly, and all of ours are said slowly. The knot through the margin hole is mine. It is tied for a word that will not go into Grathic, and I will not untie it, because a knot untied is a debt paid and this one was never owed to the south.

# The manner of it, written small at the head

- Say it at the cut, before the raft is loosed, with the reed still in your hands. A saying over a reed is a saying to the ground. A saying looked at a person becomes a request, and the person on the raft cannot grant it.
- Stand where you have stood before. The board minds the foot that knows it. If a board shifts under you, stop, find the place again, and begin at the station where you stopped.
- Say the name three times into the water and not once to the row. If the slate took the name before you got it, leave the place where the name would stand and say nothing into it. A place is not a nought.
- Tie the wool after the fourth station and before the sixth. Tied earlier it is a debt; tied here it is a keeping. Untie nothing on this day, even if a term has served out and the man standing beside you has been let go.
- If the collector's boat comes round the bend, stop where you are. He is counting something that is not in the raft. Begin again when the water has gone flat behind him.
- The answer belongs to the row, including the child who has not yet been given a name and the man who has served a term and cannot say the word he would need in order to complain. A bought breath says it through the hook in his mouth. We do not correct him.

# The litany

*One woman reads from the cut-stick. The row answers. The answer is said to the water and the water is not asked to answer, which is the courtesy here.*

## I. Of the board

> The board is laid across the water and the water owes the board nothing.
> We stand where we have stood and the board minds us.
> Dry ground is a loan called in at the hour the reed is cut.
> Do not ask the raft where it will put him. A raft has no opinion.

*Answer:* The mud keeps what it is given. The water owes nothing. Come back as you can.

## II. Of the breaking

> The world was a pot set down whole and the lid lifted in one anger.
> The warm went out where no wall held it
> into the country that keeps without asking and sends no tally.
> The Court has a name for that country. We do not use it at the cut.
> Say the wet instead, which is the name the water answers to.

*Here the reader taps the cut-stick once against the raft. It is how the row knows the reading has begun.*

*Answer:* The mud keeps what it is given. The water owes nothing. Come back as you can.

## III. Of the Nine

> Nine hands went after what had run away.
> They drove the repairs home the way a boatwright drives a treenail,
> and eight are entered in a book the ninth never opened.
> He is out in the reeds with water in his boots and has not been asked in.
> Count him with the hand you cut with, not the hand you write with.

*Answer:* The mud keeps what it is given. The water owes nothing. Come back as you can.

## IV. Of the iron

> The cold tube takes. The mud takes. One of them writes it down.
> If he came before the cut, there is a pale round place on the arm
> that shows through the winter and goes out in the thaw.
> If he did not come, the raft carries the whole of him.
> We have set the two down side by side and have not made them agree.

*Here the tideline has taken two words. I knew them and have left the space the length of them. Knowing a thing is not the same as having it.*

*Answer:* The mud keeps what it is given. The water owes nothing. Come back as you can.

## V. Of the name and the knot

> Say it three times into the water: once for the cut,
> once for the board you stood on, once for the child who cannot say it.
> If the slate took it first, leave the place where the name would stand.
> A written name travels where the person does not.
> Tie the wool now and do not untie it. A knot keeps what a mouth lets go.

*Here the knot is tied through the hole in the margin, after the fourth station and before the sixth. It is tied with the hand she cuts with. No clerk has asked why.*

*Answer:* The mud keeps what it is given. The water owes nothing. Come back as you can.

## VI. Of the pouring

> One measure into one vessel: they say he comes back with his name,
> with the hand that held the cup and the price of bread.
> Many into many: they say he comes back as a stranger
> with a hankering for a table he never sat at.
> Across the Line they sell the second kind for a third of the tally
> and ask no questions, which is how we know the price of questions.
> We have bought neither. The mud has taken both and sent no bill.

*Answer:* The mud keeps what it is given. The water owes nothing. Come back as you can.

## VII. Of the loosing

> Set him on the raft with his face to the water and his boots on.
> A dry man is a dangerous man; let the water have him wet.
> Do not sweep the cut and do not fetch him back.
> A thing fetched back is a thing entered.
> Loose the reed and let the cut be quiet behind him.

*Here the raft goes out and the row says the answer to the water and not to the raft.*

*Answer:* The mud keeps what it is given. The water owes nothing. Come back as you can.

Here the leaf leaves a space the width of a board. It is not a board forgotten. It is the place where the reader stood, and the reed will grow over it before the next cutting and be cut again. Nobody fills it.

# The verse at the loosing

*Said low while the raft is pushed off, to the water and not to the person. The last line is said twice, the second time into the mud under the board.*

> Go where the water goes. Do not go where the slate goes.
> The reed goes with you and the reed comes back;
> the warm goes with you and the warm comes back
> in a child who wants a board he never stood on.
> Tie the knot. Leave the stick. Do not say whose.

# What the clerk did

A clerk of the factor came up from Greybank about a board moved on the Post. He writes narrow and upright, with the ink settled in two fingers of a cuff gone soft from washing. He borrowed the leaf while the raft was being made and gave it back with the verbs taken out, which is what they teach in the counting house.

- At *dry ground is a loan called in*, he wrote *tenure at will; no freehold entered*. He has entered the loan and left the lender out, which is the whole art of that hand.
- At *the country that keeps without asking*, he wrote *name of the sink not admitted; observation struck*. He has not argued with the water. He has struck the word and gone away. Both stand.
- At *a pale round place on the arm*, he wrote *draw completed; account current*. He has entered the draw and not the pale round place. The pale place is what the family sees all winter and the figure does not.
- At *a knot keeps what a mouth lets go*, he wrote *no documentary standing*. He is right. That is why the knot stays under the board and the leaf is not sent to Greybank.
- At *we have bought neither*, he wrote *no return entered in this jurisdiction*. True in his room. The mud has taken both and has never sent anybody a figure.
- At the space where the reader stood he put a round nought and wrote *vacancy* beside it. A nought is a number. The place was the width of a board.

# What the two hands put in the margin

The first is thick charcoal, made with a stick and not a pen, by the woman who tied the knot and has set two people down on rafts. The second presses hard at the ends of words and belongs to a man who served a term of bought breath and was let go. I have copied both as they stand.

At the fourth station, in charcoal: “He came up from Greybank in Silt and drew from my brother while he slept. The pale place showed through the winter and went out in the thaw. The raft went out in Reed. I have kept both dates and joined neither.”

At the sixth, in charcoal: “My man came back to a house three doors down and went toward a cup before the cup was shown. I have never told his mother which of hers went into him. She asks the pot instead, and the pot does not answer either.”

At the space, in charcoal: “Not a vacancy. The place where I stood to tie the knot. I stood there and he stood beside me, and the board held us both.”

At *a dry man is a dangerous man*, in the other hand: “I served eleven months and lasted fourteen. At the end I could not ask for the stone. The word was in my mouth with the hook in it. She put the stone in my hand and I did not know who had given it. I have written this standing up, which is not the manner here.”

At the answer: “I say it through the hook. I have said it wrong for three years. I have not let anybody correct it.”

She would not give a name and he would not say whose stone it was, and I have not asked twice. There is always somebody at a cut with a word they have carried a long way.

# At the head and in the gutter

The peat at the head is mine and has gone into the grain, which is how the cut signs anything it has touched. The sedge came off the bundle laid down while the knot was tied. The fish-hook nick is not mine; a man paying me for eels set his hook on the leaf and went off without apologising. The horn-shaving came off a count-stick a reeve laid on the leaf and left there. The brine ring stood from the first station to the seventh and nobody ate the eels, which is how a dish goes at a cut in Reed. The tallow is my lamp, set too near the fold. The grey along the outer fold came off a cuff that had no business lying where the reed is kept, and I have not swept it.

There is no Last Day in the Fen and nothing is gathered up in the morning and burnt and called dealt with. We gather the grey, bottle it and sell it to people who want their own dreams to stop arriving, which is a different way of keeping the same thing. I have kept the print where it fell.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

Struck once and written again underneath, very small:

> The Court returns what it has entered. The mud returns what it has kept.
> Two returns. Neither asks the other's price.

Struck again below that, and not written larger:

> We do not ask him back. We ask the water where to stand.

I asked a reeve of the Post, the woman who tied the knot and the man let go of his term, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the struck lines where they were struck. The leaf goes back under the loose board with the knot still tied and the horn-shaving where it fell. Next Reed the young will cut their first bundles in the dark and bring them back wet or dry, and I shall say seven stations to the water and tie the wool after the fourth, and the clerk will come up the marsh road with a soft cuff and put a nought in the space where I stood, and the man with the hook in his mouth will say the answer wrong, and we shall not correct him.
