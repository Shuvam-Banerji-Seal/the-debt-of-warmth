I take rats in High Kiln and along the Lead Run, and I am paid by the tail. Two bits a score, and the tails are burnt at the store door before the mixers come up in the morning, which is a thing I have never had an answer to and have long ago stopped asking for. I set wire in the glaze-stores and along the run where the old working stains the water blue-grey and nobody in Sill will drink it twice. I write this at the end of a round with the tallow down to a knuckle and the sweet edge of the glaze coming over the terrace on the wrong wind.

The blue at the head is not ink. It is the dust off a tail I laid down before I thought, and it has gone into the fibre the way lead goes into a hand. I have not knocked it off.

# What it goes to

It goes to the rim. Set a crust and a glazed cup down in the same store and it will pass the crust and gnaw the cup, and it will not come back for the crust afterward. It takes the lip of a cup and the seam of a bowl where the glaze has run thin, and it leaves the food standing, which is the part that makes a house angry, because a house can bear a thing being eaten and cannot bear a thing being spoiled for nothing.

I set a dish of sour barley-water and a dish of glaze-water together on a shelf, to settle a question I had no business asking. It went to the glaze-water on three nights out of three, and on the fourth I set the dishes the other way about and it crossed the shelf to the glaze-water again. A man who keeps a book in his pocket has no call to write a cause into it; a cause is a thing somebody with a slate will want the use of.

There is nothing grand about it. It is a small grey rat with a long nose and a habit of putting its head on one side when it listens. The grey outside tells a man nothing; the blue-grey is under the nails and in the mouth, and a catcher who has once seen it will see it on a tail laid on a clean board.

# The bite

It does not fight a man on his feet. I have knelt within a pace of one at a store corner with my hand in the wire and it went on with its own work. But let a hand go into a nest it has chosen, or let a child sit down beside a cup it has marked, and it bites. That is the whole rule, and there is no sense in testing it twice.

It puts two small holes in the skin, no bigger than the prick of a stylus, and not much blood. I have washed eleven of these in nine years, mine and other people's, with sour water and then with salt, and bound them with a rag torn from an apron. The holes close. That is the deceit of it. They close in a tend and a man goes back to the wheel or the room or the run and thinks nothing of it, and the hand goes on being a hand, only slower. By the end of a season the fingers have lost a little of their quickness, and by the next season a little more, and there is no wound on the hand to show anybody and no figure that can be set against it.

A girl of the Glaze Room came to me in Ember with her thumb bound in a fold of apron. She had put her hand into a stack of cups where one of these had been at the seam and had taken two holes on the thumb-side. I washed it. At the end of the tend the holes were shut and she was tasting the mix again. In Smoke she could not hold the tasting spoon steady, and she said it had been like that since the bite, and I said nothing. A mixer who cannot taste the mix does not keep the room or her mother's table, and there was nothing I had been paid to say that would put either back.

> *Written in slip-white along the rim of the second leaf, in the hand of a mixer of the Glaze Room who did not give her name:* It is the lead. The physician says it and we taste it every morning and call it our work. Do not tell me I have not seen the line at my own gum.

The physician at High Kiln tells a man to keep the hand warm, which is what physicians say when they have nothing else to give him. I have seen a Held man --- a man who carried more than his natural measure and could lift a glaze jar one-handed --- bitten once on the knuckle and go on working the whole season, because a Held man has a great deal to lose slowly before he notices he is losing it. A Thin household with rats in the cupboard is a household whose children go thin by degrees, and no one in it gets angry in the year it happens. No single bite matters. That is why I keep the book.

# The song

*Sung going down the terrace to set the wire, not coming back up with the tails. The second line of the second stanza is the hearths' line, and every catcher sings it wrong on purpose, because a song that gives the rat a reason is a song a man can carry home and set by his fire. I have set it down as it is sung, with the wrong line standing.*

> Three for the lip and one for the seam,
> the rat comes up where the sweet has been;
> it leaves the crust and it takes the shine
> and goes back down where the blue is thin.
>
> They say it's the sickness made small, with a nose
> for the cup and a grudge against the wheel;
> I set the wire where the glaze-water goes
> and find a rat where a rat can feel.
>
> Two bits a score and a fire at the door,
> the ash goes down with the wash to the run;
> count the tails and count the wire,
> and leave one count for the hand that won't close.

The last line is the gap where the thumb should have shut on the tasting spoon. A mixer at the Room sings it twice and has never said why, and I have let her keep it.

These are the rounds I kept a true account of. There were others I did not write because my hands were full and one I did not write because I had drunk. I have set down both sorts as they stand. The last column is the clerk's, not mine. I counted tails; he entered stock.

> *In the narrow upright hand of a clerk come up from Greybank, written along the margin:* Rats not a vessel. Petty damage to glaze stock: no deduction arises. Tails: enter as 20 to the score. Persons bitten: not entered. Account stands.

I have left his hand standing. He has put *20 to the score* over nine and eleven and fourteen, which is a true figure for a score and a false one for a night's work, and he has put *nil* against my hand and the mixer's thumb with a neat instrument and no name on either. There is no deduction against glaze stock, so far as his column goes, and the cups went on being glazed and the house went on being a house. I have written what his column cannot reach below it, in charcoal where he did not look.

> *Below, in my hand:* Two hands, gone slow. No entry arises. I have put the size of the holes in the margin of the second leaf where the slip-white is.

# The tails

They pay two bits a score and count the tails themselves before they pay, which is a thing no rat-catcher would object to if the counting were done where he could see it. The tails go into a fire kept burning at the store door, and the ash goes down with the wash-water to the run when the floor is sluiced. I have watched that done every time I have carried a score up and have never once asked why. A man who asks is told it is the custom, and a custom with no reason in it is an account with no payer, and I have had enough of those without bringing one home in my own trade.

There is no other use for the beast. The hide is too thin to sell and too small to burn for anything. A Fen man asked me once for the bodies, and I told him I had burnt them with the tails, which was not true, but a man who has been asked for the bodies once will be asked for them twice, and I did not want to find out what he wanted them for. I have set that down and will not explain it further.

# What the hearths say

They say it is the sweet-sickness made small enough to run on four legs, a punishment sent to the glaze-houses for the taste of the mix at the rim. There are songs for it on the terrace with a woman's name in them and a wrong done at a wheel. I have heard them and I have no quarrel with a song. I have opened a score of these in nine years and found a rat each time, and a rat that went looking for the sweet and found it where the glaze had been laid. I have put no cause beside that, because I am paid by the tail and not for causes.

There was a paper in the Glaze Room dated Y.H. 641, folded small, with a hand on it that had set down the lead and the glaze and the rats in three short lines. I read it one Smoke while a mixer held the door. The paper was soft at the fold and she used it next morning to wrap a pot, and I saw it go out with the broken ware at the end of the tend. I have not seen another like it in nine years, and when I asked an older man at Stonemouth about the year he said he had heard the figure and had never seen the paper, which may both be true. A thing can be known in this country and then not known, and the knowing does not leave a mark on anything that can be counted. I have set the year down because I read it, and I have not set down the three lines, because I did not copy them and will not pretend to remember what I did not keep.

# At the head and in the margins

The lead-blue at the head is mine. The green at the corner of the second leaf is off a tasting cup a mixer set down while she was reading the slip-white, and I have not scraped it, because it is the only thing on that leaf that came from the room the thumb belonged to. The press of wet slip across the first leaf came off the morning I set the book down on the rack board before the boards had dried, and it has stiffened the fibre so that the second leaf will not lie flat. The singe at the lower corner is from the tail-fire, taken while I held the leaf too near to read in the dark; the hand that pulled it back was mine and is not entered. The charcoal is thin where I carried the wrap against my hip. The clerk's two rulings I have not touched. The slip-white line is fading and will be gone by the next Smoke, and the song is as they sang it, with the wrong line standing and the last line left where the gap was.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> It takes the sweetness and leaves the dull cup. Ask the lip which one was chewed.

I asked the miller at High Kiln, a mixer who would not give her name, and a clerk who had come up from Greybank to count the glaze stock, and had three answerings and no hand. I have left the line where it was written.

There is an old store beyond the wash-water outlet where no glaze has been mixed in a great many years and the cups on the shelf have gone dull with handling. The wire I set there last Smoke came up with four tails and not one chewed cup among them. I cannot explain what the rats were doing in a store with nothing sweet in it, and I have set that down as I found it, with the verb standing and no figure at the end of the row. Next Ember I will take fresh wire and a dish of glaze-water and a dish of sour water and set them out in that store where no glaze has been, and if the rats come to the dish with no sweetness in it I will set that down too, in charcoal, in my own hand, and let the clerk take what figure he can out of it.
