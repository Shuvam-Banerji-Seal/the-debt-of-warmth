I copy the dippers' tide-counts at the pans outside Vhal, which is to say I write down what the water did and leave out what it meant, because the Council pays for the first and has never asked for the second. A man from the hill country asked me once what we say when there is nothing to say. I told him we say it to the water. He paid me two bits and went away believing he had learned something, which in Vhalen is the usual end of a conversation with a man from away.

These are our sayings. I have set them in the Grathic letters because our own hand is kept for contracts, and a contract is a thing made to be read by a man who is not in the room. Say them with the ends of the words let fall, the way we let them fall, so that the listener has to supply the last of the thought. Said looking at a man, a saying becomes an argument; said to a rope, a pan, a tide, it stays a saying. A landman will hear half of what is here and call it rudeness. He would be nearer the mark if he called it a contract with the half left out.

I have written these on salt-paper and I keep the leaves dry. That is contrary to the practice of the country and I know it. A contract left in the rain is no contract, which we call a virtue; a saying left in the rain is no saying either, and I have not yet met the man who sees the virtue in that.

# Of the current man

1. “There is no guest in Vhal. There is a man whose account is current.”

*Gloss — We have no word for guest and no word for friend, only for a man whose account is current: one who has paid for what he has taken and may remain in the room. A landman calls this hard. It is only exact. A guest is a person you cannot enter, and a person you cannot enter may cost you anything.*

2. “Don't say *kindly*. Kindly is the coin we don't take.”

*Gloss — Said to a woman who has done something for nothing and wants to be let off it. If a thing is done for nothing, say so at once, so the other person knows it was not a favour. A favour in Vhalen is a debt with no date on it, and a debt with no date is the only kind that can keep a man awake.*

3. “Count it by touch. The fingers don't care what the face is saying.”

*Gloss — A counting-house trick, and an eel-woman's trick upriver, and the same trick. You learn a coin with the hand and let the face lie over it. I have watched a factor smile at a widow while his thumb told him the figure was short. Both were true; only one could be entered.*

4. “A stranger's coin warms before his face.”

*Gloss — We say this of a man who has come a long way in cold weather. The coin has been against his skin all the road and the face has not; you can tell where a man has been by what he has been carrying. It is a saying about warmth and not, whatever a priest may make of it.*

# Of paper and weather

5. “Write it in the rain and see what's there in the morning.”

*Gloss — Half the port's fortunes have turned on this, and the other half on somebody forgetting to do it. A contract on salt-paper in wet weather is no contract at all. That is not a loophole in our law; it is our law. Ask a Grath man why he trusts a slate: it is stone. Ask us why we trust wet paper: it does not last.*

6. “A law that lasts too long has stopped earning.”

*Gloss — My mother said it before the Council made a virtue of it. A decree is repealed when it stops paying, which is honest if you consider a decree a tool and not a promise. The assessors from the Hearthseat cannot bear it. Their slates are stone, their sentences have no verbs, and both outlive the man who wrote them.*

7. “What the rain takes, nobody can enter.”

*Gloss — A clerk can only enter what he can still read. This is the whole of our theology of paper and the greater part of our law. It is also the reason we keep the tide-names in the mouth rather than on the leaf; a name said at the Salt Walk is written in salt, and the sun takes it by noon, and no Council has ever found a way to tax noon.*

# Of the draw and the tide

8. “The tide runs against the wind and doesn't ask the moon.”

*Gloss — The Sallow does as it pleases with the weather, and the dippers read what it did rather than what it ought to have done. A Grath man tells the time by a bell; we tell it by the turn of the water under the quay, which is never where the bell says. The moon is not consulted in Vhalen and would not be answered if it were.*

9. “Name the tide you haven't seen. Salt's teacher enough.”

*Gloss — At fourteen the young walk the pans barefoot at low tide and name a tide nobody has yet seen. The dippers write the name in the salt and the sun takes it. When a Vhaleni wants to say something he cannot write down, he names a tide that has not come. It is the nearest thing we have to a promise and it is not one, which is why we can say it.*

10. “Trail the net at dawn. Read what comes up.”

*Gloss — The dippers bless the nets by dragging them in the sea at first light and reading the draw. We say the Sallow has a Hearth of its own beneath the salt and that the Court's Hearth is only its chimney. I have said it all my life and no dipper has ever taken me down to see the one under the salt, and I have learned not to ask.*

11. “The draw takes what isn't kept, and the Court taxes what's taken.”

*Gloss — The Warm goes out seaward and the assessors arrive after it with deductions on the salt works, as punctual as a gull. We bless the draw and we pay for it, and have never thought the two inconsistent. It is a cold country that makes a man careful where his heat went.*

# Of gulls, salt and the dead

12. “The gull takes the warm thing and leaves you colder than you were.”

*Gloss — The brine-gull will lift a coal out of a brazier, or a loaf, or a warmed stone, and fly off with it under its crop. There is no malice in it. A bird that steals warmth is no worse than a bird that steals bread, and both are only doing what birds do. Say this of a man who has taken something from you and calls it borrowing.*

13. “Carry a barrel of rain. It's the only thing the salt-thing won't cross.”

*Gloss — The salt-thing follows boats at night and taps the hull where the store is, and fresh water stops it the way a wall stops a man. Every captain I have ever sailed with carries rain in a barrel against it, and every one of them would deny being afraid. The dippers bless the nets against it, which is a reasonable thing to do and costs the Council nothing.*

14. “Don't lick the Cliffs. They're sweet and they're lead.”

*Gloss — The Glaze Cliffs are blue-green with old lead workings and smell faintly sweet in the heat. Children are told not to lick them and some do, because a child will believe a warning about a taste before it will believe one about a poison. The sweetness is the part that stays with a man; the lead is the part that stays with the Glaze room across the water.*

15. “Seal him in a boat, push him on the ebb, watch till he's out of sight. That's enough.”

*Gloss — We do not break a pot at the door as they do across the water; we seal the dead in a salted boat and let the ebb carry them, and stand on the Quay until there is nothing to stand and look at. The watching is held to be enough. Nobody has ever said why, and I have never seen a Vhaleni who wanted a longer ceremony.*

16. “A man who dies at sea has already returned. The sea keeps its own account.”

*Gloss — This is a kindness we allow the sea. On land the collector comes with his cold tube and a slate and the Return is entered; at sea there is no slate and the warmth goes out with the tide, and we call it done. I have known women say it of a husband who never came back and mean that they are released from entering him, which is a mercy and is not one.*

# Of boats and shares

17. “The share goes through the mother. A husband's come aboard and his word's half a word.”

*Gloss — Boat-shares pass through mothers, and a man who marries into a boat-owning family is said to be come aboard, with his opinion on the boat counted at half. He knows it; everybody knows it; nobody says it before company. Captains are mostly women, because the sea does not care and the Council has learned not to either.*

18. “Thirty marks and a curse every hour. That's a boat.”

*Gloss — What our shipwrights ask for building a boat and what they say while they build it. The curse is included in the price, and a man who tries to pay less will get more of it. The curse does not sink the boat; that would be a gift of a kind, and no shipwright in Vhal has ever given anything away.*

19. “Two marks a month and keep. One mark and a binding promise. Know which you're being paid in.”

*Gloss — A sailor's share and a salt-worker's are not the same, and a promise is not a mark however binding the man who offers it says it is. I have taken the promise myself. It was binding and it was worth what I could get for it, which was nothing, and I have set the two sayings side by side because a man should hear both before he signs either.*

20. “Seven years at the pulled bench, or till your hands give out, whichever comes first.”

*Gloss — The sentence for a condemned criminal, which we say as other countries say a grace. The pulled benches are the salt galleys, and the hands generally give out first, so that the seven years is a figure written for the benefit of the man who wrote it. We do not say this to a man being sentenced. He will find out in the ordinary way.*

# What they sing at the Salt Walk

*Said by the young walking the pans barefoot at low tide, going out, not coming back. The name in the third line is the one nobody has seen. They write it in the salt and go home and it is gone by noon.*

> Out on the crust where the wind has been,
> barefoot, barefoot, and the pan holds;
> I name you a tide that has not come,
> and salt says yes and the sun says no.
>
> Rake at the turn and rake at the fourth,
> the gull takes the coal and the clerk the mark;
> what the pan keeps is what it keeps,
> and what's in the mouth can't enter the slate.

*Gloss — The fourth line is not a contradiction. Salt says yes because the name is spoken there, and the sun says no because it takes the writing. Both hands are telling the truth and neither can be entered, which is the first thing a Vhaleni child learns about a contract: there are two parties to everything and one of them is weather.*

# What the Council clerk did

A clerk of the Council borrowed the leaves at Greybank on a market morning, being a man who cannot leave paper alone. He writes in the narrow upright hand they teach at the counting house, with the verbs taken out as though a verb were a thing a man could smuggle in a cuff.

- At *there is no guest in Vhal*, he set *the party is accommodated*, and underlined it twice.
- At *what the rain takes, nobody can enter*, he wrote *the instrument is void by weather*, which is a true thing in a language nobody at the pans speaks. Mine stands beneath.
- At *name the tide you haven't seen*, he put *tide not entered* and a round nought in the space where the name would stand. A nought is a number. The tide was not a number and has not come back to be one.
- At the wage-sayings he wrote *two marks and one promise, current*, which joins two unlike payments into one figure and is the whole art of that hand.

I have left all of it standing. He takes the verbs out because a thing always true has no need of them. I put them back because a man standing barefoot on a pan at low tide wants to know who is doing the taking and whether he will get it back.

# In the margin, in the leaning counting-house hand

A tally-man of the Council wrote this below the song, in the sloped letters they use for tallies and never for decrees:

> They pay for the tide that's entered, not the tide that's seen.
> I have stopped telling them which is which.

I do not know his name and have not asked. It is the most honest line on any of these leaves, which is a poor recommendation for a line written by a Council man, but there it is.

# At the head and in the gutter

The salt bloom at the head came up out of the paper in a wet week at the pans and has eaten two letters of the word *current*, which I have not written in again, because a word about a current that the weather has taken is a truer word than one a clerk can still read. The tar thumb at the fold is mine; I turn the leaves with the hand I mend nets with. The peck-hole went through *gull* and nobody has claimed the bird. The fish-hook nick is from a basket set down on the leaf while a man paid for six herrings and was charged for five, or the other way, which he will not settle and I have not entered. The soft corner is the rain; two sayings went entirely and I do not know now which two, which is the condition I have described in sayings five and seven and have now been given in my own paper. The rope-burn is where the twine lay coiled. The grey print nobody will own came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying on a pan. I have left it. There is no Last Day at the salt pans and nothing gets swept.

# At the back, in a hand nobody will own

> The account is current until the weather says it is not. Everything else in Vhalen is entered.

I asked a dipper, a tally-man and a man come up from Greybank with road-mud on his boots, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The leaves go back in the tin with the twine looped through them; tomorrow the dippers will trail the nets at dawn and read what comes up, and I will write down what the water did, and leave out what it meant, and be paid for the one and not asked for the other.
