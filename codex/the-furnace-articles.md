I keep the pipes and the anneal at the third furnace. A house brings a child and goes away, and the child stays with the fire; a Council clerk comes with a cuff gone soft from washing and writes down what the pot was worth, and the pot has gone into a boat before he has finished writing. This leaf hangs on its wire beside the anneal shelf, where the heat of twenty years has taken the lower edge of the paper over into a clear bead of glass. The last two rules must be held up to the light. So must everything in this trade; a thing held up to the light shows both the bubble and the man who blew it, and the Council has never once asked to see the man.

I set the rules down as they stand. Where a hand has corrected me I have left both readings.

They were gathered in Y.H. 627, the year after the rising of Y.H. 626, when the masters found that men who could melt sand could also stop melting it, and put the stopping into articles so that a refusal would have a place to be entered. A rule with no heat on it is a rule nobody has had to stand. These have been stood.

# The articles as they stand at the third furnace

## Of the taking

1. The furnace takes a child, not a house. A house may bring him and a house may feed him, but from the day he first lifts a filled pipe he answers to the master at the furnace. The Council takes the profit and leaves the child alone. This is the one arrangement in Vhal nobody has thought to tax, which is why it has lasted.
2. A child is taken at twelve and set to the gather at thirteen, and not before he can lift a pipe and keep the end of it up. A child taken later is not made up the year he missed. The furnace keeps no count of the heats it did not make.
3. He is kept, not hired. He eats from the furnace dish, sleeps in the furnace loft, and what he makes goes to the furnace and comes back as bread and a place by the anneal, neither of which is entered. A master who tells a girl she is paid has told her a true thing about a figure and a false thing about a living.
4. His birth-name is said once at the taking and not used at the furnace. What he is called afterward is the heat, which is given at his first gather and kept in the mouth. A name in the mouth cannot be entered, and what cannot be entered is the last thing in this port the Council has not found a way to sell.
5. The gather comes before the name. A child gives what came up on his pipe before he gives who he is; a pipe that has gathered tells more about a boy than a boy at a gate tells about himself. A child who gives his name first is sent back to the pot and asked again, and the pot is not interested either.

## Of the sand and the lead

6. The sand is washed in seawater and the sea takes the salt out of it and leaves the sand. The same sea takes the figures off a contract left in the damp, which the Council calls a virtue and the furnace calls a day's work lost. A master who says the sea is on our side has not read what the sea does to paper.
7. The lead comes out of the blue cliffs, blue-green and sweet-smelling in the heat. No child is sent to the cliffs. A girl who has once tasted the cliff will ask to go again, and that is the whole of the answer to that question.
8. No child tastes the pot. The master tastes, with the side of the tongue, and spits it out. The pale line at the gum is lead and not a mark of skill; a master who calls it skill is selling the girl's fingers one at a time and calling it a living.
9. A girl who rubs her gum with cliff-glass and says sweetness answers sweetness is not to be argued with in front of the young. Take the glass away, give her seawater, and let the sea be the thing that has been paid for. We do not say kindly at a furnace; a kindness at a furnace is a debt with no date and keeps a man awake.

## Of the pipe and the anneal

10. Lift the end of the pipe and never point it at a man. A pipe pointed at a man has put out an eye in every generation, and the Council has never written a rule against it, which is how a man knows the rule is ours.
11. Roll on the marver and do not keep the glass on the pipe while you decide what it ought to be. Glass asked what it should be cools into what it was, and a thing that was only nearly what it should have been still goes to Sere with a figure on it.
12. The gather is taken from the pot and the pot is not left. A girl who leaves the pot to answer a question has given her heat to the question and taken it out of the glass. There is no figure for the heat that went into an answer, and no column where it would go.
13. The anneal is where a finished thing goes to stop being finished. Nothing is taken warm out of the anneal to show a buyer. A thing cooled too soon keeps its heat in the wrong place and breaks a year later in a stranger's hand, and the break has no entry, because by then the thing has crossed the sea and the hand belongs to somebody the Council has never counted.

> *Struck hard in charcoal against rule 13, in a furnace-master's hand, and written again underneath:* Do not take it warm. *Written again above, in the same hand, the second time heavier:* Nobody takes it warm. *In mine, below both:* I have left the first and the second standing. The master has struck the word *anybody* into *nobody*, which is the same rule said to a person and said to a room. A girl does better with the person.

# The table of heats

*Kept on the anneal shelf. What is written is the heat that came up, in a hand that does not change between one line and the next.*

| Year | At the furnace | What is taken |
|---|---|---|
| Twelve | Carry sand, wash it, pile lead, sweep, hold the pipe-ends up | The birth-name, said once and not used again |
| Thirteen | The gather and the marver; the pipe kept level | The habit of asking what the glass ought to be |
| Fourteen | The first blow; the first heat named at the pot | The heat-name, given by what came up and kept in the mouth |
| Fifteen | The anneal; nothing taken out before its hour | The belief that a thing finished is done |
| Sixteen | Take a pot alone through a firing and read the green at the gum | The last of a girl's account with any house |

14. Sixteen is the common end of a heat and not a short one. A girl who has been through it is not paid out and sent away; she stays where the furnace needs her, which may be at the third furnace or at the Quay or on a boat whose share goes through her mother. The master does not choose which. The pot does, and the Council after it.

# The tally of hands

*Kept by the under-master. The figure is the Council's and is set first. What stands beneath it is mine and is not a figure.*

| Condition | The Council's figure | What stands beneath |
|---|---|---|
| Girl taken at twelve | one apprentice, current, no wage arising | A child who eats from the dish |
| First heat named | one decorative appellation, not a vessel | A name said to the pot and kept in the mouth |
| Green at the gum | occupational mark; no deduction arises | Lead. Paid out of the hand. |
| Hand that will not hold a pipe steady | one hand, current; measure not affected | The pipe taken from that hand; the hand kept |
| A thing broken in a year | breakage not entered | A stranger's hand, in a house we have not seen |
| The end of the heat | discharged at sixteen | Paid out of nothing and sent where the pot needs her |

15. The green at the gum is entered as an occupational mark because an entry that says lead would want a payer, and there is no payer in Vhal who will put his name to a girl's hand. A physician came up from Greybank in Y.H. 798 and left a page in the glass-house saying the sweetness came from the lead; the word went out of the page before the year was over, as though a word could be scraped off paper the way slip is scraped off a bench. I have put it back here, where a clerk can see it and choose not to read it.

> *In the narrow upright hand of a Council clerk, against rule 15:* Occupational mark; no deduction arises. *In mine, underneath:* It is lead. I did not learn it from a page, which is the only reason I can still say it. Both stand.

# Of faults and their correcting

| Fault | As corrected at the furnace |
|---|---|
| A hand put to the gum with cliff-glass | The glass taken away; seawater given; the word *lead* said once |
| *Skill* said of the green | The word struck in charcoal and *lead* set above it, by a hand that will not sign |
| A warm thing taken from the anneal | Set back; the thing broken at the door and not sold |
| A heat-name written on salt-paper | The paper left out in the rain; the name said again to the pot |
| The pipe pointed at a man | The pipe taken away for a tend; the eye not put back |
| The Hearth beneath the salt asked about | Answered once: the Sallow keeps a Hearth under the salt and our fire is a candle lit from it. Asked twice: send the question to the dippers, who have measured the draw and tell the Council only what is profitable |
| A heat sold to the Council and the other kept | Not entered. There is no law against it, which is why the furnace does not speak of it |

16. A fault copied twice in the same firing is a fault the girl has not understood, and the master sits with her at the anneal until she has. The pot is not a rod. It is a pot with a place where a person stood and held it level.

# The gathering-song, pasted inside the cover

*Sung at the first gather, standing, with the pipe across the knee and the end up. Nobody sings it at the anneal and nobody sings it at the Council table. The fourth line of the third verse is the girls' line and the masters sing it wrong on purpose.*

> Lead from the blue and sand from the bay,
> the sea takes the ink and the lead takes the day;
> hold the end up, never point it at a man,
> and take what comes up, not what you began.
>
> Roll on the marver and into the anneal;
> a thing taken warm is a thing that will fail
> in a year, in a house, in a stranger's hand,
> with no figure against it and no one to stand.
>
> The green comes up where the pale line was;
> the master calls skill what the girl calls hers;
> a name in the mouth is a name without sum ---
> the clerk puts a nought where the heat should come.
>
> Lead from the blue and sand from the bay,
> one heat for the tally and one for the sea;
> the pot keeps the flaw and the gum keeps the lead,
> and the girl keeps the heat that was named for her.

The press of the wet gathering-cloth across the second verse came from the song being sung in the rain at the outer furnace with the cloth laid on the leaf while a girl said the middle line. I have flattened it. The words hold.

# What the borrowed hands did

A clerk of the Council borrowed the leaf on the morning after the salt boats came down, ink settled in two fingers of a soft cuff, and took the verbs out as though a verb were a thing a man could smuggle past a table.

- Against *he is kept, not hired* he set *retained at the furnace, no wage arising*, which is a true sentence about a figure and a false one about a girl who eats from the dish. I have put mine back underneath.
- Against the row where a hand will not hold a pipe steady he wrote *one hand, current; measure not affected*. I have left it standing, because it is exactly what the Council pays a clerk to write, and I have set *the hand kept* beneath it where a girl can read it.

The other hand is charcoal, and belongs to a girl come down from High Kiln with the taste of the Glaze Room still in her mouth. Against rule 8 she struck *a mark of skill* and wrote *It is lead. It is paid out of the hand*, and would not sign it. On the back of the table of hands she set a proportion in three lines, which I have copied as it stands:

| The furnace says | The clerk writes | She wrote |
|---|---|---|
| A flux, and nothing to speak of | the flux is retained | Lead, by the handful, out at the gum |
| The green is the mark of a girl who does her work | occupational mark | The beginning of the sweet-sickness |
| A pot that sings has filled its flaw | the vessel is whole | The flaw is under the glaze, which is not the same as gone |

She has put the price in the column where the proportion should have been. It is the only column in this trade that tells the truth. I have not asked her name and she has not given it, which is the way a girl from the Glaze Room keeps the one thing a clerk cannot enter.

# At the head and in the gutter

The white at the head is salt and not Ash; there is no Ash in Vhal that has not been bought and sold. A factor come up from Greybank asked me once whether the grey thumb on the outer fold was the stuff the northern houses leave in their corners. I told him it was a thumb and had not been paid for, and he laughed as though he had invented the weather. The tar at the fold is mine, off the pipe-rest I coil every dusk. The nick is from a glass-cutter's point taken to the leaf while I decided what word to leave out; the ring is vinegar, set down by a girl who would not eat until a question about the green was answered, and it has taken two letters off the last line without taking the sense. The bead in the second fold is cliff-glass, pressed in by a girl who watched me write and said nothing, and it has left a sweet smear that has not dried in two months. The lower edge of the leaf has gone over into clear glass and will take no more ink; the words nearest it are the ones that matter most and the ones the light has to be asked about. There is no Last Day on an anneal shelf. Nothing here is swept and burnt and called dealt with; the heat does its own correcting and keeps no roll of it.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The pot keeps the lead and the gum keeps the green.
> The heat keeps the name that the tally left out.

I asked a furnace-master, a clerk come down from the Council room, and a woman who sells eels on the Quay, and had three answers and no hand, as I have had before. I have left the lines where they were written. The leaf goes back on its wire beside the anneal; the heat will take another word by the next firing and I shall not write it back. Tomorrow a child will be brought at twelve with a name she will not use at the furnace, and I shall lift the pipe-end, keep it level, and say what came up before I say who came.
