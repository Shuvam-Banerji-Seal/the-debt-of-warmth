I sweep the stone step below the Vail kiln-house and mend the well-rope when it frays, and I set this down because a man came up off the clay road with a satchel and a cuff gone soft from washing and stood on the step asking what the children counted when they skipped there. I told him a turn and not a figure, which is the truth of it. He wrote it down before I had done speaking, with the doing taken out and a number put where a child was standing, and went off over the north bridge with his boots clean.

So I have set the words as the children say them, to the rope and the stone and never across the yard, and put their verbs back where the clerk took them out. What stands above in his hand is his. What lies underneath is theirs, as the step keeps the dust under the broom.

The dust at the head is my own thumb; I held the tally down with the hand I sweep with, and grey goes into the grain here and never comes out. There is no Last Day on a step. What falls on it gets trodden into the next season.

# How it is played

- The rope is the old well-rope, with the two knots still on it where it was tied to the pail-handle. The knots are called **the two ends** and nothing else. A new rope is no good to them: it is too light in the hand and does not tell the feet when it is coming.
- Two children take the ends and turn. They are called **the two that hold**, never the turners and never the keepers. A holder who lets the rope fall is not at fault; the rope is heavy and the step is narrow.
- The others stand in the row along the flagstones and **go through** one at a time, each jumping once as the rope comes under. A child who lets the rope take her heel is not marked and is not sent anywhere. The count goes back to one and she goes through again when it comes to her.
- One child, chosen with the others, is **the voice**. The voice says one line of the verse for every pass and does not jump while she is saying it. She says it to the rope and the stone. Said to a face it becomes an argument, and there is always a child who will argue.
- At the twelfth line the rope stops. The child who went through on the twelfth steps to a knot and takes an end, and one of the two that hold comes into the row. This is called **standing out**, and it means taking the rope. Twelve are counted; nobody is removed.
- If the twelfth falls while the rope is still going down, they hold it where it is rather than finish the turn. A pass finished after the twelfth word is a pass the yard did not ask for and the voice will not count it.
- Not played in the Five, when the children are made to stand and listen and there is no step for it. Not in Stone, when the flagstones carry ice and a child who falls comes up with a wet knee and a word for it that her mother will hear. In the Five they play it sitting, with a finger on the stone and no rope at all, which is the same game with the going taken out.
- The last verse is kept back for when the man with the satchel is past the gate and the step can hear itself again.

# The counting-out

*Said over the knuckles, the voice touching one fist for each word and not looking at the faces. The two it lights on take the ends. It is not a prize: the holders stand still in the cold and the others jump.*

> One for the lip and two for the pail,
> three for the cord and the knot on its tail;
> four for the stone where the children stand ---
> the two it lights on take the rope in hand.

A child who does not want an end holds out a fist all the same. They say a fist refused is a fist counted, and I have never seen them make an exception for anybody.

# The twelve passes

*One line to one pass, said by the voice to the rope as it comes down. The eleventh is said against the stopping of the rope; the twelfth is said low, to the stone, while the child takes the knot.*

> One for the step that the frost stood white on,
> two for the pail with the ice at the lip,
> three for the cord with the knot at the end,
> four for the flag with the crack running through;
>
> five for the grey that the yard will not sweep,
> six for the hen that scratches the grey,
> seven for the crust set out for the bird --- the ring took that word ---
> eight for the dust that comes up at the turn;
>
> nine for the well with the word on its lip,
> ten for the bell that is told and heard,
> eleven for the pull the fire keeps back,
> twelve for the child that the yard keeps back.

The voice leaves a space before the twelfth line, the length of a hand laid flat on the stone. The children do not fill it. Ask them what the space is and they say it is where the rope stops, and ask them why the rope stops and they say because the twelfth has come, and there the asking ends.

# The verse of the twelfth

*Said low to the step while the child takes the knot. The row says the last two lines together.*

> The twelfth stands out. She does not go.
> She takes the end and the rope goes round.
> The slate says one in twelve removed;
> the yard says one in twelve turned.
> We do not ask her where she stood.
> She stands where the count puts her.

The child who has taken the end says nothing. She turns for as many passes as it takes to come round to a twelfth again, and then she goes back into the row like anybody. They have no name for the going back and do not want one.

# The part they keep back

*Not said while a man with a satchel is on the boards, and not on an Account-day, when the bell goes four, four and a gap and the children are made to listen to it instead. I have it from the voice, with the space left before it, which is the space they leave when the rope stops.*

> One comes up from the well and stops on the stone lip;
> one goes up the clay road in a box and does not come back.
> The first is a word and the yard keeps it;
> the second's a figure and the road takes it.
>
> We count to twelve and we keep the twelve;
> the clerk counts to twelve and enters eleven.
> The knot is not a number. The end is a place to stand.
> The twelfth is the one that holds the rope for the rest of us.

The voice said this with her back to the step and made me set it down with none of my verbs in it. A verse said to a rope wants nothing added; a verse said to a man wants everything taken away. I have put no word between the two halves of it.

# What the rope-mender put in the margin

The thick charcoal belongs to a woman who has mended this rope four times and has had the pale line at her gum these three years. She writes where somebody is sleeping, in small letters.

At *twelve for the child that the yard keeps back*: *A child with a rope-end in her hand has not left the game. I have spliced that rope four times. It has never once been a figure, and neither has she.*

At *a child who lets the rope take her heel is not marked*: *There is a girl who was caught every morning of one tend and is now the best jumper on the step. I have not taught her and the step has not either. Ask the rope, not the slate, how a person learns.*

# What the clerk did

The factor's clerk borrowed the tally at Greybank, being a man who cannot let paper lie, with his cuff gone soft from washing and the ink sitting in two fingers of it and will not come out. He set his words above the children's, the way a new repair lies over an older one on a pot, and I have left his hand standing and put theirs underneath.

- At *the twelve passes* he wrote *twelve jumps recorded; one part in twelve deducted; eleven retained*, and set the printed figure above it. One in twelve is what the notice says, and he has entered the figure he read and not the child he saw.
- At *she does not go* he wrote *removal entered; subject to re-entry*. He asked me what the child was re-entered as, and I told him she was the same child with a rope in her hand, and he wrote that down too, without the rope in it.
- At *the yard says one in twelve turned* he wrote *turning is not a count; nothing arises*. A turning is not a pass, which is true, and it is where the child spends her afternoon, which he cannot write because it has no figure.
- At *eleven for the pull the fire keeps back* he wrote *deficiency at the hearth, unascertained*. The fire keeps its own fifth and has never asked him to ascertain it.
- At the space before the twelfth he put a round nought and wrote *vacancy*. A nought is a number and travels up the road in a cuff. The space is a place with a child standing in it, and she walks home to her supper.

He asked what the children learned by it. I told him the count and the turning and the taking of the end, and he said that was the deduction taught before a child could read a slate, and he entered it. The children stop when he comes on the boards with his satchel. They take it up when he is past the gate, and they never once leave the twelfth out on the ground.

# At the head and in the gutter

The grey at the head is mine and has worked into the grain, which is why this tally takes a mark and never gives one back. The pail ring came from a pail set down on the fourth knot while the voice was being chosen; it has taken the point off one word of the seventh pass and I have not written it in again, because a rhyme with a word gone is a rhyme said slowly, and all of ours are said slowly. The frayed knot through the hole at the twelfth line came off the very rope they use. I tied it back into the leaf where the line stands and I will not untie it, because a knot tied is a place held and a knot untied is only cord. The milk tooth was lost at the eleventh pass one Reed morning and wrapped in reed by the child who lost it; nobody will claim it, though three have been asked. The tallow thumb at the second corner is mine; I held the leaf down in the draught off the terrace and did not know I had marked it. The grey in the gutter came off a cuff that had no business lying on a step, and I have not swept it. There is nothing here that gets gathered and burnt on a Last Day, so it stays where it fell.

# At the foot, in a child's hand

> I was twelf and I did not mind. You get to hold the rope.

The letters are hard at the *th* and one of them is backward, which is how a child writes who has learned off a tally and not off a book. No hand will own it. I asked three of them and had three answers and no hand, and I have left it where it was written.

The tally goes back into the coil under the step-stone. Tomorrow the rope will come out with the two knots on it and the row will stand as long as the dry step gives, and one will ring clear on the stone and one will not, and which one will not is a thing nobody enters and nobody keeps. A child will stand out at the twelfth and take an end, and the voice will go back to one, and the clerk may keep his eleven. He cannot have the end of the rope, because it is a place to stand, and he has no word for a place that stays where the children put it.
