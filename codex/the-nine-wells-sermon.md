I keep the counting stone at the Nine Wells and nobody asked me to write this. I write because a man spoke to a stone and the stone gave back steam, and I did not want the words to go up with it. He spoke to the stone, the wells, the dish, and last of all to us, the order a reader uses when somebody may be listening who wants a figure. I have set his words down in Grathic, which is not the voice of the Hearthseat, and put the verbs back underneath where he left them out, because a saying said over a well is a thing a woman has to use before the child goes cold.

The lime at the head is off the whitewashed lip where I rest the dish; no wet cloth takes it out of the grain. The grit is warm-stone ground small between the cups and scratches a nail. The ring is the dish, set down at the first voice and taken up before he saw it. The bit in the fold was left in the fifth cup while he spoke of the figure and I have not spent it. The grey along the gutter came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business on a stone that is counted; I have not swept it. The milk is a woman's, from the fourth well, and I have left the ring where it dried. The lead stop in the crease came out of the sixth cup, which is stopped and has no business giving anything.

# The manner of the warming, as the counting stone actually has it

*Written small, from watching, not from any order the Court has printed.*

- Come before the steam stands too high. A man who comes at noon sees only his own breath and calls it the well's giving.
- Lay your palm flat on the stone. Five cups are worn smooth with thumbs and four are stopped with lead. A hollow that does not wear belongs to the Court, not to you.
- Warm the stone at your own wrist before you put it to a child. It is too hot the first time and too cold by the time you have decided, which is the whole of the work.
- Leave a bit in a cup that is open. Take nothing out of a cup that is stopped.
- Do not say the count of the four aloud. A number said at a well goes up the road in a clean hand and comes back as something a household owes.
- When the reader lifts his hand, stop. His hour is the Hearthseat's and the wells keep another. Begin again after.
- The ground gives back more than it takes. Do not ask what it takes, or from which quarter. The question is safe and the answer is not safe to carry.

He said the last twice, once to the stone and once to a man from the bakehouse in a clean coat who was writing nothing I could see.

# The sermon, as he said it

## Of the nine

He put his palm flat on the stone where the nine cups were cut, and the steam went up between his fingers.

“There are nine cups in this stone,” he said. “Five give and four are held. The world was a vessel, fired whole and cracked in the First Breaking. The Nine Who Kept drove the first repairs, and these wells are the marks of their hands upon the ground. Five were left for the living. Four were reserved to the Hearth.”

I asked why four were stopped and five were not. He gave me a year: Y.H. 668. I asked again where the steam did not cover the stone and had the same year. A date is the only answer this shrine gets from a man in a clean coat, given so the other question may stand.

“The ground gives back more than it takes,” he said. “That is the mercy of the Hearth, taught to every child on the Keel after the Return. Ask what it gives and you shall have a well; ask what it takes and you shall have the printed figure, one in twelve, which is a true thing said to a room that is not this room.”

> *The people answered, and the children said it twice, once for the road up and once for the road down:*
>
> The well gives back what the well got;
> it does not say from where.

> *Margin, in my hand:* He said *reserved to the Hearth* three times. The keys hang in the Hearthseat and nobody has brought one down to this stone. I have left the keys where they are.

## Of the lead

“Look at the four,” he said. “Lead does not give. Lead does not take. Lead stops. A stopped cup is a cup that has been decided about, and a decision is a mercy when the Hearth makes it and an account when a man with a slate does. Until you are told which, leave the lead alone.”

A woman beside me asked what was under the lead. He did not turn his head.

“The warmth that would have come up is kept for the Hearth, and what is kept is not lost,” he said. “The account stands. Nothing is lost that we have kept. If a child on the Keel goes cold this winter, the Hearth has counted him and found a use for him elsewhere.”

He said it in the voice they use at the Weighing, which takes the speaker out of his own sentence. A man behind me with his cap in his hands had come up from the low country and had a child at home with a dark foot. He asked nothing, put his bit in the first cup and went away with the steam in his sleeves.

> *The people answered:*
>
> The well gives back what the well got;
> it does not say from where.

## Of the grey

“On the Last Day the grey at the lip is left where it lies,” he said. “In the morning it is taken and called dealt with. The Court teaches that the grey is what remains when the Warm has gone out, and that what cannot be kept is not a loss. Do not weep over dust. The rich buy it in a phial and call it quiet ash, and a man who cannot remember his own dead sleeps the better. This also is a mercy, and dearer than a loaf.”

There was grey on the lip of the fifth cup and he did not brush it away. An old man had come up from the Hearthseat and stood in the steam with his coat done up against a cold he could not feel. When the reader spoke of the Kept as past want and to be pitied, the old man held a hand in the steam a moment, took it back and went away. I have asked four people what he wanted there and had four answers and no hand.

> *The people answered:*
>
> The well gives back what the well got;
> it does not say from where.

## Of the figure

“One part in twelve of every measure is kept back for the fire, as the notice says,” he said. “This is not a tax. A tax is taken by a man who wants a thing. This is kept by the Hearth, which wants nothing and therefore cannot be refused.”

He held up the copper bit I had left in the fifth cup.

“A bit put into an open cup is a keeping. A bit taken from a stopped cup is theft, and the wrist is marked so the account may find the hand again. The iron does not take. The slate takes. The iron only opens the way. There is a pale ring on more than one wrist here that shows through the winter, and the neighbours call it the frost. It is the account, and it is never wrong.”

> *The people answered:*
>
> The well gives back what the well got;
> it does not say from where.

> *In the narrow upright hand of a clerk of the College who came up from Slate Row on the fourth day and borrowed the leaf while I was at the dishes:* A keeping is a lawful retention. The measure is banked and augmented in the spring. Enter as retained.
> *
> *Underneath, in mine:* I have put *put in* and *taken out* back under his *retained*. His hand stands above and mine under it, as the five open cups stand beside the four.

## The warming-song

*Sung low, standing, with the palm on the stone. The children say the last four with the women. The space before those four is the length of the four stopped cups, and I have left it as it is sung.*

> Five give and four stand stopped,
> the steam goes up the Keel;
> a stone at the wrist, a dish at the lip,
> and the road comes up frozen.
>
> One for the child and one for the cup
> and one for the hand that set it down;
> the lead in the four will not sing,
> and the five are worn to the thumb.
>
> *Here the four are left as they stand. The singer makes no sound.*
>
> The well gives back what the well got;
> it does not say from where;
> the bit in the cup goes up the road
> and the water comes up here.

A girl from the bakehouse asked what the space was for. Nobody answered her. The clerk put a round nought against it later, which I have left where he set it.

# The ledger, as the stone keeps it

*A bit left in an open cup goes up the road in a clean hand and leaves nothing on the leaf. These I have set down where they stood.*

| Cup | Who leaves at it | What is left | What was entered |
|---|---|---|---|
| One, the child's lip | the drawers of the five | a bit, sometimes | nothing |
| Two, the bakehouse well | a man in a clean coat | a copper bit, put in and taken | nothing |
| Three, the stone-warmer's | the women of the Keel | warm-stone grit, no coin | nothing |
| Four, the milk well | a woman with a sour-milk jar | a pale ring on the lip | nothing |
| Five, the last open | nobody will say | a bit left while the figure was read | nothing |
| Six, Seven, Eight, Nine | the Court; the keys hang in the Hearthseat | a lead stop in each | nothing entered |
| The grey on the Last Day | the Court's men, from the four | what the fire could not hold | nil |

There were bits before these, and my mother could set a winter to each, and I have not written the winters, because a row of years is a thing a clerk can carry away and a cup is not. The four stand as they stood when the lead went into them; no figure has ever come out of them.

# What the clerk did

He came up from Slate Row on the fourth day with ink in two fingers, borrowed the leaf, and gave it back with the verbs taken out.

- At *the ground gives back more than it takes*: *the measure is retained in the spring and augmented thereby*, above and underlined.
- At *four were reserved to the Hearth*: *four wells reserved to the Keeping; purpose not entered*.
- At the old man in the steam: *subject not stated; no account arises*, true as only a man who has never stood here can say it.
- At the gap in the song a round nought and *vacancy*. When I asked what a space was worth that held four cups, he said nothing a clerk could enter, the first true thing he said all morning.

I have put the verbs back and left his hand above, as the stone keeps the worn cups under the stopped ones. He takes them out because a thing always true has no need of them. I put them back because a woman with her palm on a stone wants to know who is doing the giving, and whether it was her own hand that set the lead.

# At the head and in the gutter

The lime is mine and will not wash out of the fold; I wipe the dish on my sleeve and the sleeve goes into the grain. The grit is warm-stone ground between the cups by every hand that has warmed a child here; it scratches a nail, which is how I know a drawer from a visitor. The wet ring is the dish, set down while he spoke of the four. The bit in the fold lay in the fifth cup when I took the leaf from the spoiled slip, unspent and unentered. The grey came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business on a counting stone; I have not swept it. The pale ring is milk from the fourth well and has taken the corner off two lines without the sense. The lead stop is the sixth cup's own. The clerk noticed none of this, which is how I know he is a good clerk.

# At the foot

Struck through once and written again underneath, very small, in a hand that is not mine, not the reader's and not the clerk's:

> Five were left open by a hand. Four were stopped by another.
> The steam does not tell which hand was kinder.

I asked the reader, the woman with the milk jar, a man come up from the bakehouse and the clerk, and had four answerings and no hand. I have left the line struck through and standing, which is the only honest way to keep a sentence you cannot sign.

The leaf goes back in the tin under the stone's lip with the lead stop in its fold. Next Stone the five will give and the four stand stopped, and the road will be frozen hard enough to carry a cart, and a reader may come up and say the same four sentences over the steam. If the clerk comes I shall let him take the verbs out and put them back as I did this time, with the nought in its place and the four cups stopped the length they are.
