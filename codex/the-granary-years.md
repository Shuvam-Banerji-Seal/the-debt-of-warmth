I keep the chronicle of the granary at Greybank, which is not the same thing as the chronicle of the Court, or of Grath, or of the road that brings us the grain. The granary keeps what came in. The chronicle keeps what was entered. Between the sack and the slate there is a space the width of a clerk's hand, and it is in that space that this book is written. Where the two disagree I set both down and leave them standing, because an annalist who reconciles them has begun to write an account, and an account is a thing a factor can carry to the Hearthseat and show to a man who has never stood in a granary loft in his life.

The loft-book lies in a tin box above the second floor, where the warm comes up through the boards in winter and the dust settles into the grain of the paper and no brush takes it out. I have copied this chapter four times, and each time the loads have stood as a round figure in the clean hand, because the first keeper of this book counted sacks the way a miller counts a row of wet pots, by the places and not by what was in them. I have left his round figure standing and put his rows underneath it, where a clerk of the College will not think to look, which is the gutter.

# The years as the loft-book has them

*Copied in the tallies out of the tin box. I have put nothing in from the temple tells, which have their own years and a comfort in them.*

**Y.H. 711.** The factor's house buys the old malt-floor by the quay and lays a new floor over it. Entered as *a necessary enlargement of the office*.

**Y.H. 726.** The granary is raised two storeys. The Court's levy of grain in Grath is doubled, from one part in twenty of the harvest to one part in ten. Entered as *the establishment of the granary; the levy adjusted to the Hearth*.

> *In the narrow upright hand of the College, against the second entry:* The levy was readjusted. No burden was added. Strike *doubled*.

I have not struck it. A burden halved in the telling is still a burden carried in the cart, and the carts came down the Clay Road that autumn with the same horses and the same ruts and one more sack to a load. The College hand has never in this chapter changed a figure. It takes the doer out of the sentence and leaves the figure standing, which is the readiest way of correcting a book I have yet seen.

**Y.H. 782.** Every birth in Grath must be entered on a slate within three days, and the hearth-maids who kept the slates for nothing are paid by the Court. The granary's own roll grows by a column. Entered as *a regularity in the keeping of the birth-slates*.

**Y.H. 811, the Long Cold.** Ice came on the Ilm in Stone and did not break for ninety-four days. No cart crossed the ford and no collector crossed from the quay. The grain levy was called all the same, in a district that had no grain to give, and in Low Kiln the measure-levy was raised to one in three, which is a different figure and is printed nowhere. Entered as *a visitation of the weather; the account standing*.

> *In the margin, in a hand that is mine:* They called themselves walking thin. I have put the words here and not in the year, because a man who keeps moving because stopping is the same as cooling is not a figure and the loft-book has no column for him.

**Y.H. 812, the Thin Year.** The granary gave out in Grain, before the second harvest, and was opened again in Smoke. I have entered both dates, which the first keeper did not, because he entered openings and not closings, as a man enters what he can see from the quay.

The four loads of Y.H. 726 went into the ford in Silt, which was high that year, and the carters came up with wet boots and empty places in the string. The figure was made in this room before they left, so the four went into the book as received. I have struck the figure and written it again underneath, by touch as it were and not by eye, because a man writes differently when he is setting down what happened from what was entered.

> *In the narrow upright hand of the College, against the struck figure:* Deficit not entered. Levy discharged at the granary door. Account standing.

The hand is careful and it has never once in this chapter changed a figure. It has made four wet sacks into no sacks at all, which is a cleaner thing to carry into a counting room. There are eleven words for a deduction taught in the College and not one for a sack that came back half-full, which is the whole of the difficulty between us.

# The cart-song

*Taken down at the Greybank landing from a carter of High Kiln who had come six days on the Clay Road and would not sing until the horses were unhooked. It is sung going up and not coming down; coming down a man wants his ale. The third verse is not sung at the granary door, and the carter said a man who sang it there would not be hired again in Grath.*

> Four days to the clay and five back,
> five in Grain and six in Silt;
> the sack goes up and the slate goes down
> and neither one knows what was spilt.
>
> One in twenty the old book said,
> one in ten when the floor was laid;
> the same wheel turns in the same old rut
> and the same hand mends the same old trade.
>
> They write the figure at this end
> before the horse has set his foot;
> we carry the difference in our backs
> and enter nothing at the boot.
>
> Ninety-four days the river stood
> and the ford was a stone that would not bear;
> the levy came on the frost behind us
> and found us thin and standing there.

He would not say who made it and said that songs about roads are made by the roads, which is the custom of that country and not an answer. I have set it down as he sang it, with the unsung verse where it stood.

# What the College hand did

- Against *the levy doubled* it set *the levy was readjusted*, so that the act of doubling has no actor in it.
- Against *four loads lost at the ford* it set *deficit not entered; levy discharged*, which is true at the door and false at the ford.
- Against *they called themselves walking thin* it wrote *no designation arises*, which leaves the men standing and takes the name away.
- Against *the granary gave out in Grain* it set *stock reduced, not exhausted*, and underlined it twice.
- Against *one in three in Low Kiln* it wrote *the figure is the measure-levy and not the grain; no entry lies*, which is the only correction in this chapter I did not wish to argue with.

It takes the person out and leaves the grain standing, and grain standing by itself has never gone hungry. I have put nothing back under the last of these, because it is true. A clerk who can tell two figures apart is a rarer man than a clerk who can write one, and this one came up from the Hearthseat with the figure for the measure already in his cuff.

# At the head and in the gutter

The barley dust at the head is off the second-floor boards and is older than my copying of this chapter; it goes into the paper and no damp takes it out. The husk in the gutter stuck there in Grain of Y.H. 811, when the last of the old grain was swept up off the floor and the granary stood open and empty behind it. The weevil shut in the second fold was alive when I folded the leaf and is not now, and I have left it where it died. The lower corner is mouse-gnawed and has taken two words clean off the third leaf; I have not written them back, because a gap in a chronicle is a thing a reader can put his finger on and a supplied word is not. The white at the head is salt bloom off the Sallow and comes and goes with the wind. The tallow thumb is mine. The ring at the foot is not any man's; three men set a cup in it over one evening of copying and not one will say which, which is how small ale is drunk in this room when there is a thing to say and no proper word for it. The grey along the outer fold is not mine and is not the College's. I have had this leaf four years and have not swept it.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The floor was laid and the figure stood;
> four loads went wet and were entered dry.
> The granary keeps what the carts bring in,
> and the book keeps what the factor will deny.
>
> Ask the sack. It has a mouth and no slate.

I asked the carter at the landing, the second clerk who made up the right-hand column, and a woman who has swept this loft for thirty years, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written. The leaves go back into the tin box above the second floor, where the warm comes up through the boards and settles into the grain of the paper. Tomorrow the levy will be called again, and the notice in the room below will say one in twelve, which is the figure for the measure and not for the grain, and nobody in the district has ever been asked to tell the two apart. The carts will come down the Clay Road with one more sack than the year before, and I shall enter the rows, because that is what the loft-book is for.
