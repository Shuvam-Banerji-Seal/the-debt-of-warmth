I have walked the bank between Greybank and Sill for nineteen springs, with a three-strand rope laid across the heart of my right shoulder and a barge behind me that somebody else has packed. I did not set this down. A girl at the eel-stalls wrote it for me because I had begun to lose the fourth verse in the same year I began to lose the use of my left hand, and a verse lost at fifty is lost for good. She put my words in the order of the pulls. I have put the rubrics back, which she left out because she has never hauled.

A hauling song is not like a yard song. A yard song can be sung while the hands go on doing something else. This one is counted in the feet, and if you sing it out of time the rope takes up the slack on your own shoulder, which is the only correction a river makes. We do not sing going down. A boat going down wants a hand at the tiller and a woman at the bight, and no words.

# How it is sung

- At the first pull after the boat comes off the mud at Greybank, not before. The first pull is the one that tells you what the barge weighs, and a song sung before it is a song sung to nothing.
- Lay the heart of the rope to the shoulder, not a strand. A strand across the shoulder will roll and take the skin off; a heart lies still and tells you by its pull when you are about to be wrong.
- Put your foot where a foot went before you. The Clay Road is packed clay over corduroy in the wet places, and the corduroy is under the silt, and a man who steps beside it instead of on it spends the morning finding his ground again.
- Refrain after every verse, into the rope and not into the air. Said into the air it becomes a song for listening to, which is a different thing and belongs to the Cross.
- Do not take up the song under the sill at Sill in Ilmen. The stone gives its low note under the water when the Warm comes up, and the old of Sill set their clocks by it, and a man who pulls against a note that is not his own loses his breath. Let the stone have that stretch of river.
- Not on thin ice, and not in the Breaking. In those weeks the bank is a different country and the song does not know its roads.
- Rest on the rest, even when there is time to make up. The count is in the pull and the resting, and a man who eats his resting to get home early pays it on the next haul with his shoulder.

# The song

> Salt up from Greybank and iron up with it,
> three strands and a heart, and the heart to the shoulder;
> put your foot where a foot went before you,
> and draw.
>
> *Draw. The rope takes it.*
> *Draw. The bank gives it back.*
> *Rest. The water goes on without us.*

> The wind comes down off the Sallow and the water comes up,
> and the two never agree, and the boat goes between;
> do not ask the river which one owes the other,
> it has no slate and it will not be told.
>
> *Draw. The rope takes it.*
> *Draw. The bank gives it back.*
> *Rest. The water goes on without us.*

> The factor writes what goes into the hold,
> the silt writes what comes off it in a year;
> one hand in a clean cuff and one hand in the mud,
> and the same boat goes up in the morning.
>
> *Draw. The rope takes it.*
> *Draw. The bank gives it back.*
> *Rest. The water goes on without us.*

> [The fourth verse is given below in another hand. I have never been able to say it, and the girl wrote a line of dots where it stood.]

> *Draw. The rope takes it.*
> *Draw. The bank gives it back.*
> *Rest. The water goes on without us.*

> There's a step short at the Fishstairs and nobody mends it,
> the water put it back a hand's breadth out of line;
> step where the others step and do not look down at it,
> and draw.
>
> *Draw. The rope takes it.*
> *Draw. The bank gives it back.*
> *Rest. The water goes on without us.*

> In Stone the rope is a piece of iron with no heat in it,
> and keeps the print of your shoulder after you are gone;
> in Silt it's a snake in the mud and wants to go downriver,
> and you must want it up more than it wants to go.
>
> *Draw. The rope takes it.*
> *Draw. The bank gives it back.*
> *Rest. The water goes on without us.*

> A brine-gull took a coal off a banked fire last spring,
> warm and not yet cold, and went upriver with it;
> no slate in Sill stood one mark the poorer,
> and the fire went on taking its breath in fours and a gap.
>
> *Draw. The rope takes it.*
> *Draw. The bank gives it back.*
> *Rest. The water goes on without us.*

> The notice says one in twelve, and the clerk reads it twice,
> once for the moot and once for them that came late;
> the bank keeps a count that will not stand still to be entered,
> and I have not put it here, and neither has the girl.
>
> *Draw. The rope takes it.*
> *Draw. The bank gives it back.*
> *Rest. The water goes on without us.*

> When the rope goes slack and the barge lies at the stair,
> shake the water out of it and coil it against the wall;
> what we brought up will be spent by evening,
> and the river will be where it was when we came.
>
> *Draw. The rope takes it.*
> *Draw. The bank gives it back.*
> *Rest. The water goes on without us.*

# The fourth verse, above the line of dots, in a hand that is not mine

> There was a man went under at the Weir in the flood year,
> and the rope came back with a knot in it we had not tied;
> we did not undo it and we did not sing it,
> and the next boat went up with one less hand.

It is said once, low, when a boat goes out with fewer hands than came up, and then the rope is shaken out and the song begins again at the first line. I asked the girl who wrote it. She said she had not. The eel-women at the Weir say their mothers sang it, which is an answer that does not spoil in the mouth. I have left the knot where the words put it and have not looked to see whether the rope still has one.

# What the clerk did at Greybank

The factor's clerk borrowed the slip when the salt tally went up. He set a figure against each verse, as if a verse were a basket of tile, and struck the verbs out of two lines. *The water goes on without us* became *the current is constant*. *The silt writes what comes off it in a year* became *loss is entered annually*. Where the fourth verse stood he ruled a line and wrote *no entry* against it, which is a true thing in a place that has never had a man come back up a rope.

I have left his figures standing above and put my words underneath. A clerk can write a current that is constant because he has never had one on his shoulder. He has put a number where a man's breathing goes, and the number will travel as far as Greybank and arrive as light as salt-paper. When a rope goes slack it does not care what has been entered.

# At the head and in the gutter

The tar at the head is my thumb; I held the slip down on the bight while the girl wrote and did not know I had marked it. The silt tideline along the gutter came up over both of us on the third pull out of Greybank and took two words off the second verse, and the girl wrote them back from my mouth, which is how I know she was listening. The salt crust is from the weigh-slip itself and has eaten one of the clerk's figures half away, which I have not mourned. The rope-burn across the third verse is where the slip lay inside my coat against the heart-strand. The fish-scale came off a woman's creel at the Fishstairs when she leaned over to see the writing; she did not stay, and I have kept it where it fell. The splinter of corduroy in the fold is off the Clay Road and got there when the slip was opened in a wind, which is the only part of this paper that has walked the towpath on its own. The grey along the lower edge came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying; nobody will own it, and there is no Last Day on a river. The tarred twine through the hole at the fold is not mine and not the girl's and I have not untied it to find out who tied it.

# At the foot

The song belongs to the rope and not to me. I have lost the fourth verse and the use of my left hand, but the right shoulder still knows where the heart lies, and it will know after I have given the rope up. When that day comes another woman will take the bight and a girl, or a girl's girl, will write down what she hears in the order of the pulls. If the clerk gets the leaf again he may have the figures. The pulls will be there underneath, and the water will go on without us, as it has done since before there were slates at Greybank to write it on.
