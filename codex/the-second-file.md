I copy the second file forward. It lies in a tin beside the sealed-file room, and once a year at the Opening I take the leaves out, set them in order and copy them into a fresh hand. The names are not on the leaf I copy. They are kept in the tin in another hand, and I have never been shown which hand; the College holds that a boy who is set to copy removals has no need of the names he removes. I write the shape of each removal, the figure struck, the figure set above, and the remark. That is the whole of my office.

A removal is not a death and not a forgiveness. It is a correction to the roll. The roll is never wrong, so the person entered in it must be. I set that down because it is what the College does, not because I admire it; a clerk who admires his own work has begun to write a sermon, and sermons get copied.

# The order of the file

The name goes off the roll in the same hand that put it on. This is the first thing a junior is taught and the last thing he forgets: a hand that changes between one entry and the next is a hand that can be accused of feeling something. The figure is struck through and a figure set above it, or no figure set above and the place left standing. A reason is not entered. There is a column for the reason in the tin and it is blank from the first leaf to the last, which is a finer arrangement than a column struck out, because a struck column invites a man to ask what was in it.

Twelve bits to a mark; twenty marks to a Sere crown that does not stay in the Hearthseat. An ember is called five marks in this College and no junior has ever seen one exchanged. One in twelve is printed on the notice across the way and is the figure every boy in this room learns to write before he is allowed to write his own name. I have copied it into the removals more times than I can count and have never once set it against a removal, because a removal is not a deduction. A deduction takes a part of a measure. A removal takes the man who was carrying it.

# The leaf, as I copied it

*Figures in Ledgerhand. The remarks are those set down in the tin, in the hand that kept the file, and are not part of the account. A dash is a place where no figure was set above, not a nought.*

| Manner of removal | Figure struck | Figure set above | Remark |
|---|---|---|---|
| A boy who could not learn the hand | 19 | — | The hand was his own. No man took it from him. Entered in the same hand as those who stayed. |
| A vessel returned three times, entered whole by order | 29 | 30 | *Whole* set above the earlier figure. The earlier figure kept here. Joined to neither. |
| A number cut out of an arm and a new number cut in | 41 | 39 | The later figure entered. The earlier stands in the skin. Nobody asked which count was his. |
| A debt bought before it was due | 52 | — | Assignee entered; debtor not asked. The debt kept its figure and lost its man. |
| A whole return announced for a person not in the room | 300 | — | Room empty. Name not spoken. The file does not say where the person was. |
| A sealed file carried beyond the room | — | — | Carrier's own name set where the other stood. The first entry left standing beside it. |
| A hand greyed by cold work | 38 | — | The hand struck off the rolls; the fingers greyed. The figure is not struck. |
| Forty of Low Kiln, one slate for the batch, by lot | — | — | Names not taken. Figure entered elsewhere. Nothing carried up into this tin. |

Removals copied on this leaf: eleven. Figures struck: eight. Figures set above: two. Names set down: none. Reasons entered: none.

# What the narrow hand did

The Provost's clerk borrowed the leaf after I had copied it and before it went back into the tin. He writes upright and takes the doer out of every sentence, as though a thing with nobody in it were truer than a thing a man had done.

Where I had copied *they took his name off the roll*, he set *the name was removed from the roll* above it, and above that *the removal is recorded*. I have put my line back underneath and left his standing over it. A sentence with nobody in it is a sentence no woman can ask about, and women ask about these removals in the end, always at a door, always after the tin is shut.

Against the vessel returned three times he struck my *the earlier figure kept here* and wrote *superseded* above it. Both stand. *Superseded* is the word the Weighing uses and the word a household hears in a clean voice. *Kept here* is the word I use when I am not reading anything aloud. I have not chosen between them, and would thank any man who told me I must.

He struck my last column and wrote *No such column* over it. I drew the column back underneath. A man does not count the rows of a column before he writes that there is nothing in it unless the column has troubled him, and he counted every row.

# The column with no heading

I keep the figure that stood before the removal beside the figure the College will read. I have set no heading over it. A heading would make it an account, and an account is a thing a man may be called to answer for, and this College has answered for enough.

On the arm with two sets of numbers the older figure was thirty-nine and the later forty-one, and the file carries them in that order, which is the order in which the knife went into the skin. The roll carries forty-one alone. A surveyor would say the roll is right and the arm is wrong, because a roll has one figure and an arm can have two. I have set down both and joined neither. A clerk who joins two figures to make them agree has begun to keep an account of a man, and the College pays for those by the leaf.

The foundling has no removal. He has a figure and a stone and no name, which is a different absence from the absences in the rest of this tin, and I have kept it on its own line and not laid it across any of them. Seven absences standing in a row are seven; they do not become an account until somebody stands under them to pay, and nobody in this room has stood.

# The verse the copyists say

*Said low at the Opening, while the tin is open and the ink is still wet, never in the Weighing Hall and never with the door toward Slate Row. The last line is said to the tin.*

> Strike the figure, set the other,
> leave the reason where it stood;
> one in twelve as the notice says,
> and the roll is always good.
>
> Name goes up and name goes down,
> same neat hand from first to last;
> what the arm keeps the roll forgets,
> and the house keeps what the roll passed.
>
> Four turns the wheel and the fifth held back,
> the tin keeps what the tin was told;
> hold the empty column open,
> it is the only one we hold.

A junior who had been set three Openings to the file said the first two lines while I checked the counting and would not say the last while the Provost's clerk was in the room, which is how I know the last line is the one that matters. I have copied it as it came and put no gloss on it. A clerk who explains a verse has begun to write a sermon.

# In the tin and in the gutter

The slate dust in the gutter came off the floor of this room, where it gathers under the benches and goes into the grain of whatever is laid down. The brown at the head is iron-gall and mine, from holding the quire open through an Opening morning; there are juniors in this College who can tell a copyist by his thumb before he has spoken a word. The damp line along the foot came out of the sealed-file room, which takes the damp in every season and gives it back to everything kept beside it. The grey print on the outer edge is Ash and is not mine; I found it on the leaf when I took the tin out and have not brushed it away. On the Last Day they sweep the corners of houses and burn what they find in the morning and call it dealt with. There is no Last Day for a tin beside a sealed room.

The green bead in the lower corner is lead glaze and not slip. A girl come up from High Kiln with the sweetness still at her mouth leaned over to see the figures while I copied and touched the leaf and said nothing and went back down the road. I have left it where she put it. The small bare square is where a name was scraped off with a knife before this leaf was given to me. I did not cut it and cannot read what was there. I have not put a name back and have not tried to.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The first file keeps the name. The second keeps the place the name stood in. Neither of them keeps the man.

I asked the Provost's clerk, a woman who came to ask after a house in the low country, and a collector come up from Greybank, and had three answers and no hand, as I have had before. I have left the line where it was written.

The leaf goes back into the tin and the tin beside the sealed-file room. Tomorrow a boy will be taught to write a life with nobody in it, and a name will be taken off a roll in the same neat hand that put it on, and I shall copy the shape of it forward with the reason column standing blank. Nothing has been joined, named or closed. The empty column stays open, which is the only column in this College nobody has thought to strike.
