I count the draw at the pans. Before light the nets go over and the dippers say this with the cord still in their hands, while the salt is wet on the boards and the gulls have not yet come down for what we spill. Everything in Vhal is written on paper made to rot, and this is written on the same. The Court writes on slate and calls it kept; we write on salt-paper and call it said. In the damp the leaf goes soft, the words come up in pale islands, and we copy it again in the spring. No two copies agree. What follows is the third copy, and where the fourth station differs from the second I have put down what my mother said and left the other standing underneath, because a prayer that agrees with itself has been copied by somebody who was not there.

A tally-clerk from the tide-tally house borrowed the second copy on a morning when the pans were covered and set his correction above mine in Court ink, narrow and upright and very sure. The salt took his ink before it took mine, which is the first thing this coast taught me about the Keeping: charcoal rubbed with salt holds, and the figure they send up the road does not. I have left the holes where his sentences were. A hole where a correction stood is worth more than the correction.

# The manner of it, written small at the head

- Say it at the pans in the half-light, before the nets are trailed. A net blessed after it is out has been blessed for the fish and not for the dead, and the fish do not ask.
- Face the ebb. The draw goes seaward against the wind; a prayer said with it has a better chance of arriving. The old dippers say this. I have never tested it and neither have they.
- Do not read the draw until after the third station. A number spoken early stays in the ears of the dippers and they net by it all morning, which is how a bad figure becomes a habit.
- The answer is made twice: once to the water, which is not asked to keep anything, and once into the knot-count, which is. If you have no cord, answer into your hands and tie them when you get home.
- If a brine-gull takes a word, let it go. Gulls are the only creatures in Vhal that pray without being asked, and they get no better answer than we do.
- Never say the seventh station over a boat that has gone out and not come back. Say the place where the name would stand and leave the rest in the water, where the boat is.
- There are five days on the Court's slate that belong to no month. At the pans we keep the flood and the ebb, which come oftener and are entered nowhere.

# The litany

*The pan-master reads. The dippers answer with the cord in their hands. Between the third station and the fourth the pan-master reads the draw, low, so that the water hears it before the tally does.*

## I. Of the going out

> The world was fired whole and went in one anger.
> What ran out of it went a long way and is going still.
> The Warm goes seaward on the ebb with the silt,
> and the pan takes what the pan can hold.
> Do not tell a dipper the Sallow is the Waste.
> It is nearer, and it comes back.

*Answer:* Out with the draw. In with the tide. Name it, or leave the place.

## II. Of the Nine

> Nine hands went after what had run away.
> They drove the repairs home the way a cooper drives a hoop.
> We say them by their number and not by their houses,
> because a house in Vhal is a thing that can be denied in a season.
> Nine went. One is still abroad, somewhere over the water.

*Answer:* Out with the draw. In with the tide. Name it, or leave the place.

## III. Of the paper

> Everything here is written on what will not last.
> A contract that lasts too long has stopped earning.
> The Return is the one account we would have last,
> and we write it on the same leaf as the rest.
> It goes soft. We copy it. What is copied is not kept,
> and what is kept is not the same as what comes back.

*Answer:* Out with the draw. In with the tide. Name it, or leave the place.

*Here the pan-master reads the draw. The dippers do not repeat it. The draw-song is said under the reading, so low that the clerk at the tide-tally house has never written it down.*

> The draw goes seaward against the wind,
> the net comes up with weed and silt;
> four for the pans and one for the gone,
> and a knot in the cord that the clerk cannot split.

## IV. Of the pouring

> One into one comes back to a boat he can name,
> with the cup and the share and the side of the deck.
> Many into many come back to a deck they never walked,
> with a hankering for a hearth they never sat at.
> Both are poured. The Council will pay for one of them.
> We say the prayer over both, because a prayer is not a contract.

*Answer:* Out with the draw. In with the tide. Name it, or leave the place.

*The second copy has this station differently, in my grandmother's hand: "One into one comes back with his name and the price of bread." She had never been to Vhal. I have set her words underneath and left mine above, which is the wrong order, and the only one I can use.*

## V. Of the man from Greybank

> A man comes up the Salt Road with a tube in wool
> and a slate that keeps what the paper lets go.
> He reads one in twelve as the notice says, twice,
> and writes what the damp has taken out of ours.
> His ink goes pale in a season. Our charcoal does not.
> Do not call that an argument. It is weather.

*Answer:* Out with the draw. In with the tide. Name it, or leave the place.

*The splash that took this answer came off a bucket knocked against the leaf on the morning we copied. I have put the answer back from the second copy, which had it whole. The damp has now eaten the second copy's version too, so there is no way of knowing whether I put back the right words.*

## VI. Of the water

> Do not pray to the Sallow. It is water.
> It takes what is put into it and gives what it takes,
> on its own hours, which are not ours.
> The Court taxes the draw going out.
> Nobody thanks it coming in.
> A thing that keeps nothing cannot be entered,
> which is the end of the argument as far as the pans go.

*Answer:* Out with the draw. In with the tide. Name it, or leave the place.

*A woman who had lost a brother off the pulled benches said, when we came to this station, that the sea kept a man longer than the Court kept a name and gave him back in worse condition, which is the whole of her religion and cost her nothing to say.*

## VII. Of the name

> Say the name at the going out and again at the coming in.
> If you have no name, tie the knot and leave the place.
> Do not put a figure in the place. The clerk will do that,
> and a figure is a thing that can be denied in a season.
> Leave the place the length of a name and do not fill it with weather.

*Answer:* Out with the draw. In with the tide. Name it, or leave the place.

# What the clerk set above

The salt has eaten most of him. I have set down what can still be read, with the gaps left the width they are.

- At *the Return is the one account we would have last*, he wrote: *the measure is banked and the return recorded.* The first three words have gone. *Recorded* remains, standing alone in the gutter, which is a better account of his hand than anything he meant to say.
- At *what is kept is not the same as what comes back*, he wrote: *nothing is lost that we have kept.* The salt took it except the word *kept*, which has come up pale and sits under his line like a bone in a shallow grave. I have not covered it.
- At *a prayer is not a contract*, he wrote: *observance not entered.* This one the salt left whole, which I take as a judgment on the damp rather than on him. It is the only true line he wrote and it cost him nothing, because a nought has never had a hand in it.
- At the place where a name would stand he tied nothing and wrote *vacancy* in the figure of a round nought. The dippers tied their knot underneath it. The knot held. The nought went soft and came up as a pale island with a hole in it.

He asked me what a dipper learned by the litany. I told him the order of the stations and where to stand for the ebb. He entered both. The leaf came back with his ink already going, which is the only notice a clerk has ever given me that he had been at it.

# What the salt-wife tied in the corner

There is a knot of tarred twine through the lower corner, four turns and a bight, tied there by a woman who rakes the third pan and will not give her name to a leaf. It is the count the pans keep and the Court does not: four for the pans and one that went out on the ebb and did not come in on the flood. She would not say who. She would not say whether the five turns were for a debt paid or a debt still running, which in Vhal is the same question asked in two voices.

I have not untied it. Twine with a knot in it keeps a count without a figure, which is more than I can say for the paper it is tied to, and more than I can say for the slate at the tide-tally house, where a sheet left in the damp is a debt made nothing and a man goes out lighter in the face.

# At the head and in the gutter

The tar at the head is off the net-coil and went in when I laid the leaf down on it to keep the wind from turning the second sheet over. It has worked into the grain and no washing takes it out, which is a kindness from tar and not from anybody else. The salt bloom came up out of the paper and ate the clerk before it ate me, for the reason written above. Along the gutter there is grey in one place, off a cuff that had been near the Ash in the north country and had no business at these pans; ours comes white, and I have kept the grey to be reminded that there is another kind. The splash took the fifth answer and I have put it back from a copy that is now gone. The crust in the fold crunches when the leaf is opened and has never been brushed off, because a prayer that crunches has plainly been used. The white print at the foot is a bare foot, a girl's, come in off the pans at fourteen with the salt still on it, who stood over me while I wrote and would not say what tide she had named. I have left it where she set it.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

Struck through once and written again underneath, very small, in a hand that is not mine, not the clerk's and not the salt-wife's:

> What the tide takes, it does not keep.
> What the slate keeps, it does not return.
>
> Only the paper does both, and only by going.

I asked the pan-master, a dipper, the woman of the third pan and a tally-clerk come down from the Quay, and had four answers and no hand. I have left the struck line standing. At first light I shall say the seven stations while the nets go over, the clerk will write what he can before the damp takes it, and I shall tie the knot again in the corner, which is the only part of this that has never needed copying.
