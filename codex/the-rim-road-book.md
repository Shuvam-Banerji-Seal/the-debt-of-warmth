I keep the factor's figures at Greybank. It is a room with a slate on one table and a second slate on the table behind it, and a man is entered in the second before he is heard in the first, which is the order of that house and the order of the Keeping. I went up the Rim Road in Frost of the Thin Year with a crate of fine ware out of High Kiln, a letter the factor sealed in front of me, and nine marks of the Court's money for such charges as the road might ask. I came down in Ilmen with the letter delivered, the money mostly gone, a warm-stone that held for nine days and gave out on the tenth, and a leaf of a survey which a man turned face down in front of me and which I have never read.

I set this down because the upright hand I keep for the factor would not hold it. An upright hand is a good hand for a figure and a poor one for a man. What I could not put in a figure I put in charcoal on the backs of requisitions, which nobody in that house wants back.

# What the road charged, and what I entered

The Rim Road runs a hundred and twenty leagues from Sill to Keld Keep, and the Cold Pass lies ten beyond. A cart makes it in nine days in Ash and in twelve when the high places have shut, which is to say that the road's length changes with the weather and the slate does not. At Greybank I was told to enter the charges as the factors before me had entered them. On the road I paid what was asked. Both columns stand.

| Charge | Entered at Greybank | Asked on the road | What I set underneath |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm-stone, fist-sized | 6 bits, current | 8 bits, and no promise taken | The seller would not give his name. A stone is sold and the debt stays with the man who cut it out. |
| Lodging at the third stage | 2 bits | 2 bits, with a place by the fire extra | I paid the extra. My toes had been without feeling since the second day. |
| Letter carried past the post | entered *nil* | 1 bit for the man to state my office | He asked for the office before the name. I gave the office. He wrote *bearer*. |
| Lead, a bead, Ninth Gallery | not entered | given, and laid on my leaf | The man who laid it down went away without it. I have rolled it into the gutter rather than lose it. |
| Passage of the Cold Pass | entered *nil* | no charge, and a warning | A post and no gate. The warning cost nothing and was the dearer of the two. |

The factor's clerk at Greybank set *road figures, informal* above this table, which is his way of saying a man may copy a road but not enter one. He has not struck a word. I would rather he had.

# The courtesy of the number

At Keld Keep I gave my measure before my name at the Warden's hall, which is their custom and not a rudeness, and I had been told so three times before I went up and still felt the shame of it when I did it. The clerk wrote the figure first and asked the name second, as though a name were a thing a man might keep back and use again. My figure is two and thirty and has never been checked in red, which in that room makes me a man of no consequence and no suspicion. I found I preferred it to being interesting.

A key-woman at the mouth told me a man states what he holds before he says who he is, and that a man who forgets is sent back to the foot of the post. I asked whether a lowlander was expected to know this. She said a lowlander was expected to be told once. I have set that down as she said it, looking at the keys and not at me.

The sayings there are said to the stone, to the iron, to the lamp, and never to the man beside you. Said looking at a man, a saying becomes a bargain, and there is no bargain in any of them. Where a Keldish word would not go into the south's letters I have cut the edge with my knife and left the cut standing. A notch stays when paper has gone, which is more than can be said for the factor's slate.

# What was sent, and what came back

I carried a crate of fine ware for a house of the Warden's captains and a letter I was not to read. I came back with the letter's answer, which was two words on a strip of slate, and with such goods as a factor's house will enter without asking where the warmth went.

| Sent up from Greybank | Came down again | What the factor entered | What I entered beneath |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fine ware, High Kiln, twelve pieces | nine pieces; three cracked on the climb | *broken in transit* | The three cracked where the road was worst and the straw least. I watched them go and did not say whose straw it was. |
| A sealed letter | An answer of two words on slate | *delivered, answered* | I did not read either. I carried them both. There is a difference and the factor's book has no place for it. |
| Nine marks, Court money | A warm-stone, nine days' heat | *warm-stone, current rate* | The tenth day it was a stone. The seller's fire went out of it and nobody paid for the going. |
| — | One bead of Ninth Gallery lead | not entered | Rolled into the gutter of this leaf. It is warm when the room is cold, which I have tested and have not written down as a wonder. |
| — | One leaf of a survey, turned face down | not entered | I have not read it. A man who turns a slate down in front of you has told you something in the only manner his office permits. |

At the mouth I was offered a thin return for a third of what the Court charges, the seller asking no questions about the source, and I said no, having been sent to buy iron and not a person. I entered the refusal in charcoal; the factor's clerk wrote *no acquisition recorded* above it, which is true in his office and not what I meant.

# The weigh, and the two numbers in one arm

I stood at the weigh while the shifts went down. A woman of the count took the numbers as each pair went past, put a warm-stone into a boy's hand after the knife, and kept a cup on a nail for the men who came up. She had done it two and twenty years and had never been below, which she did not call cowardice.

A man came up with a number cut into his forearm in two sets, the later cut deeper. The mine-reeve checked the later figure in red and left the earlier standing in the skin. The clerk beside him wrote nothing against the earlier number. I asked the count-woman why two figures were allowed to stand where the Court's slate would hold only one. She said the mine-reeve kept two truths without calling either a lie, and that a clerk who can do that has a busy morning and a quiet night.

In the red checking-hand of the mine-reeve's clerk, against *the boy went down with a stone in his hand*: **State the figure given, then the figure taken. Everyone here knows the order.**

Underneath, in mine: I had written that the boy held the stone as a man holds a thing he has been given and does not yet know the price of. The clerk has struck the boy out. There is no boy in this room after the first cut, he has written further, and the rule is for the morning before, when a boy still thinks a face will do instead of a figure. I have left both. A figure is a merciful thing to a counting room and a cruel one to the hand that carried it, and the two do not meet in the middle of a sentence.

# The stones above the Keep

Five leagues higher into the Rim stand the Listening Stones, where the Vorr sit for days and come away with numbers. I went up with a surveyor who had leave to be there and I had leave to carry his lamp. He sat against the third stone from the high path and came back with four figures which he set on a leaf and would not match to anything I could see: not to the wells, not to the nine of anything that I had counted. He said the numbers were correct and then turned the slate face down, which is a courtesy in Keld and an insult in Greybank, and I have set down the turning and not the reason.

There is a word the surveyors use for the oldest marks in the seam. The Keldish translate it as Ash and the Court's copy translates it as language, and the Vorr, being asked, say only that the numbers are correct. These are three guesses and I have set them one under another without choosing, because a chronicler who chooses between two guesses has made a fact of one of them.

In the narrow upright hand of a Court survey-clerk, borrowed at Greybank, set above the four figures: **Counts of the survey. Not legible in every copy.**

Underneath, in mine: the figures were legible enough. What I could not legibly match was the ground they were said to stand for. A figure not legible has a figure somewhere behind it. These four stood where nobody had put them and I have copied them where they stood.

# The road-song

*Taken down at the post from a man who had come up with tallow and was going down empty. He would sing it only going up. He said a song of that road sung downward brings the cold into the house, and I have put it where I heard it.*

> Four leagues and a stone that sweats,
> and a hand that states the number first;
> the warm goes up in a tin lined warm
> and the cold comes in behind.
>
> The post stands up and the gate does not,
> and the man who counts will not count me;
> a bead comes out of the ninth dark gallery
> and is laid on a leaf and left to be.
>
> Two numbers sit in one cut arm,
> the later deep, the earlier light;
> the mountain keeps what the slate lets go,
> and the slate keeps what it writes.
>
> I carried up and I carried down,
> and the stone I bought went cold on the ninth;
> ask the stone whose fire it held ---
> it will tell you nine, and then no tenth.

# What the two hands did

Two hands have been at these leaves besides mine.

- The mine-reeve's clerk, in the red checking-hand, changed no figure on the whole leaf. He took the verbs out of the weigh and put the order in: figure first, then the boy. I have put the boy back under it, where a man must kneel to read him.
- The Court survey-clerk at Greybank, upright and narrow, set *counts of the survey, not legible in every copy* over the four figures and wrote *no acquisition recorded* over my refusal at the mouth. He has never been up the road and has strong views about the pass. Both his lines stand above mine, as is the custom of his office.

I have left both readings everywhere, the thin one under the neat one, as a potter leaves the old break showing under a new seam and does not pretend the first hand never touched the clay.

# At the head and in the gutter

The smut at the head is coal off the sleeve I held the sheet down with at the weigh; it goes into the grain and no wet sleeve takes it out. The warm-stone shaving in the second fold held its heat through one night on a stone floor and is cold now, which is the order of things in that country and the only argument about warmth I have ever seen a man settle by going to bed. The lead bead rolled out of the Ninth Gallery and a surveyor laid it on the leaf and went away without it; it is in the gutter and I have not entered it. The frost at the head came up out of the paper on the climb over the pass and was gone by noon; I set it down and no more, because a man who writes about frost as though it meant something has already begun a sermon. The tallow is my lamp, set too near the leaf. The grey along the outer fold came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying, and I have not swept it. There is no Last Day at a mouth or at a post, and nothing anywhere up that road gets burnt in the morning.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> I carried figures up and a stone came down;
> the stone held nine and the tenth was mine.
> The post stood open and the gate was never made;
> the slate turned down and the four stood where they lay.
>
> Ask the arm which number it carried.

I asked the count-woman at the mouth, the surveyor come down from the stones, and the clerk at Greybank, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The leaves go back into the oiled wrap and the wrap into the coat pocket the wire has worn. Tomorrow I shall sit at the second table in the factor's room and enter what the road cost in the factor's hand, and beneath it, where his hand does not reach, I shall enter the day the stone stopped being warm. That entry he will not ask for, and it is the only one in the house that anyone will want to read.
