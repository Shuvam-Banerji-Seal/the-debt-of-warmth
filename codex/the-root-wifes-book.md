I dig the bank below the Fishstairs where the silt falls into the Ilm, and the ditch along the road to Mud End where water stands when the river is high. Nothing grows the same in the two places: a root from the ditch has a white heart, a root from the bank a grey one. I have sold both for one-and-twenty years. Nobody asked me for this book. A girl came off the terraces in the lean week with the sweet at one mouth-corner and asked for the sleep draught, and I heard myself answer in my mother's short way, and did not know whether I believed it. A remedy you have to say in your mother's voice is a remedy nobody has asked of itself in a long while. I have asked some of mine. The rest I have left standing, because a woman at three in the morning wants a hand more than a correction.

I write on the back of a herb-price tally rain spoiled. A price that will not read is a price nobody can charge, which is a mercy of a kind. The brown down the gutter is root-tea from a cup a woman would not drink. Nothing here is free. On this bank a thing that costs nothing has been dug out of somebody's ground or carried on somebody's back.

# The order of the drying-room

*Written small on the first leaf, with one word gone over in green.*

- Hang every root by the stalk with the root hanging down. A root hung the other way dries from the wrong end and keeps a wet heart. A physician from Greybank wrote that a root's virtue does not know which way up it hangs. I have left both.
- Try a draught on your own tongue before you give it to a woman. If it takes the feeling out of your mouth, set the cup down. I have done this every time and it has told me nothing I did not know, and have never stopped.
- Never give the sleep draught to one who has not eaten. We say a full belly holds the sleep and an empty one lets it go wrong. The physician says the belly has nothing to do with the breath. I have seen it go wrong in both and never written which.
- Turn the slate face down before you go to a child. I have no slate-name and never shall; a midwife who writes the child before she looks at it has written the wrong one.
- When the second chill comes, count with her. Do not count for her.
- Do not sell the sleep draught to a man in a soft cuff. He will ask what it is worth and not who took it, and a cup cannot be called back out of a cuff.

I asked a woman of the Weir what she saw when she crossed two fingers over the birth-flower, and she gave me a date instead of an answer. I have put it in the margin.

# The remedies, as they are used

| Trouble | What is set to it | The manner of it | Who is not entered |
|---|---|---|---|
| The sleep that does not wake | Sleepweed steeped, three spoonfuls at the going of the light | We say a pain that cannot be borne may be put to sleep. The physician says the weed numbs and does not keep the breath, and he is right in a way I have not made right in a house. Of eleven I gave it to, six woke and five did not. I have entered six. | The five. |
| The mould on the wound | Green growth from the dark cup, laid on the wound | We say the rot in the cup and the rot in the wound are the same hunger. The physician says there is no knowing what grows in a cup. I set it on four: one closed, two took a red fever, one grew quiet and did not rise. I have not used the cup since. | The one, the two, the fourth. |
| The sweet line | Bitterroot in sour barley-water; the gum rubbed with the root | We see the sweetness come off the terraces in a girl's mouth and say a bitter answers a sweet. The physician says lead. The line went off one who left the Glaze Room and came back on one who stayed. I have not seen the lead. | The glaze room, which cannot stop and does not ask. |
| The fever that cooks | Whitebark steep; a cold cloth at the temple | We say the bark draws the head down. The physician says it eases pain and does nothing to the head of the vessel, which wants the heat stopped, not the pain counted. I gave it to nine; three said the pain had gone, and two of those did not know their children by morning. | The two, in a memory they cannot name. |
| The second chill | Pepper-root in warm barley-water, and walking | The draught makes a heat in the mouth that is not a heat in the hand. I walked twelve and held four; of the walked two went thin, of the held none. I keep the counts on birch bark and have not added them. | The household, one night's sleep; sometimes the child. |
| The birth-flower | Nothing. Two fingers crossed, and the word the mother gives | I made a tea of one; the flower kept its shape and the child came on the fourth day as it would have. The physician says it is a flowering of the blood and not a flower, and is right in a language nobody here speaks. | Nobody knows. The child's skin, perhaps. |
| The kiln's breath | Nothing. A man found quiet in his sleep is left quiet | We say the breath of a closed kiln is a merciful death. The physician says it is no mercy and that the word is put on a death so nobody will open the door. I have written his sentence above mine. | The man, and the house that will not open. |
| A cut from a pot-shard | Tallow, a clean fold, a pinch of doorroot | We say doorroot closes a cut from the outside in, as it closes green ware. I set it on three and all three closed with a white line under the skin that would not come out. The physician says a cut does not close from the outside in. | The cut, in a white line it keeps. |
| The cold that stays | Three turns of wool, a warm draught, and work | The extremity goes first and the heart holds out, and there is an hour on the far side of the warming when a body spends faster than it takes in. I have no word for that hour that is not weather. | The woman. This is the row where I have been least honest. |

Nothing in this book is given away. It is dug, dried, sold and called a cure, and the account of it is kept in no house. That is the only sentence I will stand behind.

# The note under the knuckle

The potters tap a cup before the kiln and hear whether it will hold. Sound answers full; cracked answers flat, a small dead sound that stops at the lip. I tap a root the same way: a sound one answers full, a wormed one answers flat and is light in the hand. The physician says I am hearing the wood and not the virtue. Both stand, and the root still has to be sold.

I tapped a woman once, gone thin after a return, and put my ear to the flat I thought I heard. The flat was not in her. A body is not a root, though roots are shaped like bones. I did not say so then, because a man from Slate Row stood at the Cross with a slate.

# What the draught takes

Of the five who slept and did not wake, one was a woman of the terraces who had paid me twice in barley, one a girl from the Glaze Room with the sweetness at her mouth, and three I knew only by the doors they came to. A collector came up the road the next morning and drew from one with a cold tube and a slate, and went away with his fingers grey at the tips. He asked what she had taken. I told him barley-water. There is no column in this book that would hold it.

A physician who can name the breath may say I killed five. I have said it in the dark and it did not bring one back. What I can say in the light is that I gave eleven cups and have five I cannot account for, and that the eleventh went down into the silt at the door and was never steeped. A root kept is not a root given, but it is not nobody either.

# The drying-song

*Sung low while the bundles go up, to the roots and not to the women. The eleventh is held in the singing, the length of a hand going into the silt and coming out empty. Nobody fills it.*

> Six for the bank and one for the ditch,
> one for the root that the rain has lifted;
> hang it by the stalk, root to the ground,
> and set the sound ones apart from the wormed.
>
> Bitter for sweet and sweet for sweet,
> the mould in the cup and the rot in the wound;
> a draught numbs the tongue and not the breath,
> and the cup goes round and the name goes down.
>
> Count with her, do not count for her,
> the heat in the mouth is not in the hand;
> the third night comes and the warm goes back,
> and what goes back is not what went.
>
> Ten I tied and one held out,
> the eleventh goes down where the silt keeps.

I changed one line in one-and-twenty years. *and the root keeps* became *and the silt keeps*, because a root is carried away and the bank is not. The women tying beside me did not notice. A clerk of the College put a round nought in the held place and wrote *vacancy* beneath it. A nought can be carried off in a cuff. The eleventh is a place and stays.

# What the physician pressed in the margin

He came down from Greybank with a book that had the word *lead* in it and a hand that wrote without asking. Against the sleep draught he set *numbs; does not keep the breath*, against the mould *no certainty in a wild growth*, against the sweet line *lead, as in the north*. He was right in all three and had nothing a root-wife could pay for. Where I wrote *a full belly holds the sleep* he set *no relation*, with a line that scored the leaf. I have left his above mine and my verbs underneath.

# What the College clerk did

He borrowed the leaf when the winter grain was counted at Greybank; the ink sits in two fingers of a soft cuff. He struck out my column headed *Who is not entered* and wrote *No cost to the house*, which is a true thing in a dishonest place. Where I wrote *five slept and did not wake*, he wrote *the subjects expired*, taking the hands and the cup out as though a draught could drink itself. A clerk can write without verbs because he has never stood on this bank with a woman's hand gone white in the pail.

# In the collector's hand, in grey

> Your draught is not a crime; there is no entry for it. I drew from two who had taken it and entered both as age. If you want those five back, write them yourself.

He wrote it at the Cross and set the leaf back with the fold changed, and put no name to it, because a name on a leaf is a name a clerk can carry up the road. His fingers left a grey print along the outer fold and I have not swept it off. There is a Last Day in Sill and the corners are swept and burnt in the morning and called dealt with. There is no Last Day on this bank; the silt takes what is left and asks nobody.

# At the head and in the gutter

The root-fibre at the head came off the bundle I was tying at the first word and has worked into the grain. The green bloom took two words clean off the second leaf through a wet week; I have not written them back: a remedy with two words gone is a remedy said slowly. The brown tideline is root-tea off a cup a woman would not drink. The seedpod in the fold came out of a bundle a girl carried down from Cold Acre and would not say what it was for; I have kept it where it fell. The tallow is off the lamp. The scorch is where the drying-fire caught the corner; I have not entered the burn. The bank-clay is the only thing here put there by the trade and not by a person. The grey print is not mine and is not the clerk's, and I have not swept it.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The root keeps the shape of the bank it grew in.
> The slate keeps the figure that was never drunk.
> The woman between them is not entered, and is warm or is not.

I asked a physician, a clerk of the College, and a man who came up the road with a tube, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The book goes back into the oiled reed in my string bag. Tomorrow a girl will come with the sweet at her mouth or a cup held out for the sleep, and I shall turn the slate face down, try the draught on my own tongue (which tells me nothing), put my verbs back, and leave the eleventh where it is, because a child is not an account and a woman at three in the morning wants a hand more than a remedy.
