I wash the small bowls in the lower court of the Ember house. That is my office and I am grateful for it; there are women in this house whose office is to stand where a guest may see them, and they are not permitted to sit down while the third hour lasts. I set this down because a little girl of the house asked me which of her names she was to answer to, and I, who am a bowl and answer to the name the house gave me, had no courtesy with which to reply. A courtesy is a thing said to a person who cannot answer back. She could answer back. That was the difficulty.

The name I had before I came is written on the inside of a bowl I am never permitted to look into. I have carried that bowl from the press to the court and back for eleven years and have never once turned it toward the light. The children turn theirs over. That is the game.

# How it is played

1. It is played in the lower court at the going of the light, when the third hour's bowls are washed and stacked mouth-down and the court fires are low. Not at a feast. On a feast day the children stand at the rail with the others and watch the great bowls go round, and a child who plays at the naming then is told she has not yet been told, which is the politest thing said in Sere.
2. Not when a clerk crosses the court with his tablets under his arm. If a clerk comes, the bowl is turned mouth-down and the game stops until he has gone, because a name said under a counting hand travels, and a name that travels arrives in a room the child will never enter.
3. They sit in a ring on the lowest step. One child is the pourer; she holds the small glazed bowl level at her knee and begins the rhyme. She is never the one who is kept, which the children accept as the ordinary order of things. The bowl goes from hand to hand, mouth up and level. A bowl that slops is set down, and the hand that slopped it is wiped by its owner, which is a courtesy and not a punishment.
4. One line, one pass. At each line the child to whom the bowl has come says one of her names to the bowl, with the vowels open and the ends thrown into the fire. Said to the bowl it stays a name; said to a face it becomes a claim, and a claim can be argued, and an argued name is entered. There is always a child who says it to a face, and there is always an older child to turn her chin toward the bowl.
5. The little ones have two names or one and borrow the rest from the older girls. They call it borrowing breath. There is another word for breath in this house and I will not set it beside theirs. A child who borrows must hold the name level until the bowl comes round again, the way a woman holds a full cup; she may not set it down.
6. At the seventh line the bowl is covered with the corner of the cloth. The child holding it says aloud, to the court and not to the bowl, the name the house keeps. Then every child breathes once across the covered bowl, which is how the other six names are given to the fire. The cloth is lifted, the bowl passed on, and the child who spoke becomes the pourer of the next round.
7. There is no winning and no last round. The fire is banked, the children go to their beds, and the bowl goes back on the shelf mouth-down.

# The rhyme, as it is passed

*Passed level. One line, one hand. Said to the bowl.*

> One for the name my mother sang at the well,
> two for the name my aunt kept in her mouth,
> three for the name that came south in a basket,
> four for the name the old woman would not write,
> five for the name we say when the bowl is turned,
> six for the name that goes down on the slate,
> seven for the name the house will keep.
>
> *Refrain, all together:* Pass it level, pass it full;
> say it to the bowl, not to the ear.
> Six go to the fire and one stays here,
> and the one that stays is the one you hear.

# What is said while the bowl is covered

*The cloth over the bowl. Every child breathes once. The words are said low, into the cloth.*

> Six go into the fire with the wine.
> They do not come back and they are not missed.
> The house calls one and the body answers,
> which is the whole of the courtesy.

They keep both hands on the step through this, and a child who lifts the cloth an inch to see whether the names are still there has given them to the fire twice and sits out a round with her palm over her mouth until the pourer taps her wrist. Nobody calls it by a name. I have watched them do it for eleven years and never once seen a child correct herself before the breath was out.

# The verse they do not say when a clerk is in the court

I have this from the smallest of the bought children, in the flat northern speech, said into the bowl with her back turned to the counting hand. The house children think it is a game about bowls. It is a game about bowls.

> Six go up and one stays down,
> and the one that stays is the one they call;
> I had a name before the south
> and it's wrote where I mustn't look.
>
> Turn the bowl and read me none;
> I answer the bowl and not the court;
> keep the name and keep the debt,
> and give me back the one you took.

I asked her what the sixth name was. She said it was the one she had before she came, and that she had it still, and that the fire had not taken it because it had never been given to it, which is an answer no courtesy in this house would have allowed a grown person to make. I have set it down as she said it, with the *wrote* as she said it, because a child who has learned her letters off a slate writes it that way too. I have not asked whose bowl it is written in.

# What the tally-clerk did

A clerk of the house borrowed the leaf after the third hour, being a man who cannot leave paper alone. He writes in the leaning counting-hand, with the verbs taken out as though a verb were a thing a man could spill out of a cuff. He was pleased with this game, which I have not known a clerk to be with any other.

| In the court's hand | In the clerk's hand |
|---|---|
| Seven names said to the bowl | Seven designations declared |
| Six given to the fire | Six cancelled by consent |
| One kept and called at the going of the light | One name retained; person identified thereby |
| The cloth over the bowl | No transaction |
| The bowl set mouth-down on the shelf | Balance nil |

At the head he wrote, *Names declared: seven. Retained: one. Released: six. No charge.* Against *six go into the fire* he set *six designations extinguished by consent*. Where the children cover the bowl and breathe he wrote *no transaction*. At the foot, where the bowl goes back on the shelf, he put a round nought and wrote *balance nil*.

He has put a figure where a name stood, and the house thanked him for it, which I have never seen happen in the north. There, they tell me, a clerk puts a number where a child stood and nobody thanks him, and the child's people go around the number as a yard goes around a pot set down in the middle of it. Both hands are the same hand with the cuff turned a different way. I have put their words back underneath and left his standing above, as a full bowl stands on the shelf and the shelf does not argue.

# What the factor's clerk added at Greybank

A factor's clerk added one line at the head in the narrow upright hand, above everything else on the leaf. He has never been south and has not seen the bowl.

> Seven names entered; six cancelled; one whole return.

His figure is correct. The bowl is not in it.

# What the woman at the press put in the margin

She treads the press at the third hour and does not write often, and when she does she presses the letters in small, the way a woman writes in a room where somebody is sleeping. I have copied her hand as it stands.

At *five for the name we say when the bowl is turned*: **We do not say it when the bowl is turned. We say it when the light is going. There is a difference, and the house has never asked after it.**

At *six go into the fire*: **The fire takes the wine. It does not take the name. I have poured at nine feasts and my mother's name is still in my mouth.**

At *the name the house will keep*: **It keeps one because a house can only call one. A person can answer to more. That is the whole trouble in this court, and nobody in it will hear it said.**

# At the head and in the gutter

The wine-ring at the head belongs to no one hand; three hands set a cup in it over one evening and no hand will own it. The yellow dust of the burning winds is ground into the fold and I have not shaken it out, because a leaf that has never been in the wind is the wrong colour for this court. The thumb of oil is mine; I turn the leaf with the hand I wash the bowls with. The fleck of gold came off a contract carried past on a tray while the children were at their rhyme, and one of them picked it up and laid it in the gutter where a figure should stand, which is a better place for it than the counting room. The husk of grain came north in a loaf and went south again, which I could enter if I chose and have not. The crack in the fold is where the leaf has been folded and folded again; I cannot mend it and have stopped trying. The grey print came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying among the small bowls; nobody will own it, and there is no Last Day in a lower court.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> One name is kept and six are given to the fire.
> The fire has never once taken one.

I asked the pourer, the woman at the press and a potter come up from the provincial bowls, and had three answers and no hand, as I have had before. I have left the lines where they were written.

The leaf goes back under the small bowls. Tomorrow the light will go, the children will pass the bowl seven times and keep one name, and I shall wash the bowls and set them mouth-down on the shelf, and the name I was born with will stay where it is written, on the inside of one of them, which I have never once turned toward the light.
