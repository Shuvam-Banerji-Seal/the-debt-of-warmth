I keep the mixing bench and the tasting-spoons in the Glaze Room above Low Kiln. A woman from a house brings a girl and goes back down the terrace; the girl stays with the mix. A kiln-hand comes up with a pot gone wrong and asks what was put on it, and I tell him what went into the dish and not what it cost, because the cost is not in this room. There is a clay jar on the wall that is cold under its glaze and has been cold since before I came to the bench; nobody opens it and nobody says what it held. I set the rules down as they stand. Where a hand has corrected me I have left both readings.

They are not new rules. They were gathered in the year the house set the sweet sickness into its own account and then wrote the word out of it again, which is the way this trade has of keeping a thing and not having it read. A rule with no green on it is a rule nobody has had to taste. These have been tasted.

# The articles as they stand on the bench

## Of the taking

1. The room takes a girl, not a house. A house may bring her and feed her and have her back at the end with a trade in her hands; from the day she first dips a spoon the room answers for what she mixes. A house that will not give her up for the years has given nothing.
2. She is taken at nine, or at ten if her house has kept her through a winter of sickness. Not before. A girl who cannot carry a dish of slip across the room and set it down without a rim of it on her sleeve has no business at the mix; she will spend the glaze on her clothes and call it the pot's.
3. Her name is said once at the taking and kept in the mouth afterward, the way a heat-name is kept at a furnace. A name in the mouth cannot be entered, and there is nothing in this country the Court has not tried to enter.
4. She is kept, not hired. She eats from the room's dish and sleeps under the room's roof. What the room makes goes into the house's pots and comes back to her as bread and a place at the bench, neither of which is written down. A mixer who tells a girl she is paid tells a true thing about a figure and a false thing about a living.
5. No girl is asked whether she wants the sweetness. She is asked whether she understands what is carried: a dish of lead, a spoon, a pale line that comes up where a person can see it, and the habit that grows in the mouth before the line does. The wanting comes after, or it does not come.

## Of the lead and the tasting

6. The lead comes up the Clay Road in a cask that the factor's clerk does not open and does not ask to. It is blue-grey and sweet in the mix. A girl who has once tasted the dish will ask to taste it again, and that is the whole of the answer; send her back to the slip and let the slip be boring.
7. No child tastes the mix. The mixer tastes, with the side of the thumb and the back of the tongue, and spits it into the slop-pail. The pale line at the gum is lead and not a mark of industry. A mixer who calls it a good sign is selling the girl's teeth one at a time and calling it a living.
8. A girl who rubs her gum with a green bead and says sweetness answers sweetness is not to be argued with before the young. Take the bead away, give her barley-water, and let the lead be the one that is paid. We do not say kindly in a Glaze Room; a kindness there is a debt with no date.
9. The slip is stirred down and the lead is stirred in, and the dish is covered between stirrings. A dish left open takes a skin, and a skin taken off a dish and put on a pot is a flaw that no knuckle will find and no firing will burn clean. Leave the skin where it formed; tell the pot nothing about it.
10. A pot is dipped and set up rim-down on the rack, and the rack is not crowded to make the day come out even. A crowded rack gives a thin coat, and a thin coat is a lie told in lead, which is the only lie a kiln cannot burn away.
11. The green at the mouth is entered as an occupational mark and no deduction arises. This is the house's phrase and not mine. I have set what it is underneath it, where a clerk may see it and choose not to read it.

> *Struck hard in charcoal against rule 11, in a mixer's hand that did not sign, and written again underneath:* It is lead. It is paid out of the mouth. *Above it, heavier, the same hand:* No. It is paid out of the hand. I have left both strikes standing, one over the other, because a girl who has stood at this bench knows which of them is true and does not need me to choose for her.

## The years at the dish

*Kept on the bench. What is written is the work that came up, in a hand that does not change between one line and the next.*

| Year | At the bench | What is taken |
|---|---|---|
| First | Carry slip, wash the dishes, sweep, hold the spoon while the mixer tastes | The girl's own hand, which learns to keep the spoon level |
| Second | Stir the lead in, skim nothing, dip under the mixer's eye | The habit of asking what the glaze ought to be |
| Third | Cover the dish, tend the rack, name the coat on a cup with the green still at her mouth | The belief that a thing dipped is a thing done |
| Fourth | Taste the mix once, with the side of the thumb, and spit it out | The last of a girl's account with any house |

12. Four years is the common end of a mixing and not a short one. A girl who has been through them is not paid out and sent down the terrace; she stays where the pots need her. The mixer does not choose where. The pot does, and the house after it.

## The tally of hands

*Kept by the bench. The figure is the house's and is set first. What stands beneath it is mine and is not a figure.*

| Condition | The house's figure | What stands beneath |
|---|---|---|
| Girl taken at nine | one apprentice, current, no wage arising | A child who eats from the dish |
| First green at the mouth | occupational mark; no deduction arises | Lead. Paid out of the mouth. |
| A hand that will not keep the spoon level | one hand, current; measure not affected | The spoon taken from that hand; the hand kept |
| A pot dipped thin | not entered | A cup in a house we have not seen, that comes apart in a year |
| The clay jar on the wall | not entered | Cold. Nobody opens it. |
| The end of the mixing | discharged at the fourth year | Sent where the pots need her, out of nothing |

13. The sweet sickness is entered as an occupational mark because an entry that says lead would want a payer, and no payer on this terrace will put his name to a girl's mouth. A physician came up from Greybank in Y.H. 798 with a page that said the sweetness came from the lead; the word went out of it before the year was over, as though a word could be scraped off paper the way slip is scraped off a bench. I have put it back here, where a clerk can read it and go on reading.

> *In the narrow upright hand of a Court clerk, against rule 13:* Occupational mark; no deduction arises. *In mine, beneath:* It is lead. I did not learn it from a page, which is the only reason I can still say it. Both stand.

## Of faults and their correcting

| Fault | As corrected at the bench |
|---|---|
| A child tasted the mix | The spoon taken away; barley-water given; the word *lead* said once |
| *Skill* said of the green | The word struck in charcoal and *lead* set above it, by a hand that will not sign |
| A skin skimmed off the dish | Put back where it formed; the cup dipped again and given no excuse |
| The rack crowded | The pots taken down and set up again with air between them |
| A heat or a figure brought up from Low Kiln in answer to a cost-question | Answered with the weather and the pots; no figure entered |
| The jar on the wall opened | Not entered. There is no rule against it, which is why nobody does it |
| A girl sent to taste a pot for a buyer | The buyer told the pot has no answer; the girl sent back to the slip |

14. A fault copied twice in the same firing is a fault the girl has not understood, and the mixer sits with her at the bench until she has. The spoon is not a rod.

# The mixing-song, pasted inside the lid

*Sung at the second dipping, standing, with slip on the forearms. The room says the refrain together. Nobody sings it at the First Firing, and nobody says why. The third line of the second verse is the girls' line and the mixers sing it wrong on purpose.*

> Lead from the cask and slip from the pit,
> the clerk writes nought where the green should sit;
> keep the spoon level, cover the dish,
> and give what came up, not what you wish.
>
> The green comes up where the pale line was;
> the mixer calls skill what the girl calls hers;
> a name in the mouth is a name without sum ---
> the house puts a nought where the year should come.
>
> Lead from the cask and slip from the pit,
> one coat for the tally and one for the kiln;
> the pot keeps the flaw and the gum keeps the green,
> and the girl keeps the coat that went on unseen.

The press of wet reed across the second verse came from the song lying in the rain at the Weir while a blessing was said over the traps. I have flattened it as well as I could. The words are plain enough under it.

# What the borrowed hands did

A clerk of the Court came up from Greybank with a cuff gone soft from washing and ink in two fingers. He struck *the lead comes up* out of the first rule of the mix and set above it *the material is delivered*, as though a cask could climb the Clay Road without a hand behind it. He put a round nought beside the green and wrote *vacancy* under it, which is a number where a colour stood. I have put the lead and the green back beneath and left his above. A clerk who takes the lead out of a sentence leaves the room standing by itself, and a room standing by itself has poisoned nobody and cost nobody, and there is no figure in it to argue with.

The other hand is charcoal, a mixer come down from the firing with the sweetness at her mouth, who struck *a mark of industry* and wrote *It is lead* above it and would not sign. She has put the price where the proportion should have been, which is the only column in this trade that tells the truth. I have not asked her name and she has not given it.

# At the head and in the gutter

The green at the head is mine, off my thumb setting the leaf down on the bench with the mix still on it, and it has gone into the grain so that the whole sheet smells faintly of a firing and cannot be mistaken for a Court paper. The white crust at the fold is dried slip that stayed there instead of falling through, which is how I knew which hand held the leaf. The sweetish edge in the paper is the mix itself, a year old and still sweet, which nobody here has thought to call a virtue. The ring is barley-water from a cup set down while a girl asked why the green came up and I told her the weather. The bead was pressed in by that girl with the sweetness still at her mouth; it has left a smear two months dry. The tallow is off the lamp I keep low so a girl will not know I am writing and change the account for me. The grey along the outer fold came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying. I have not swept it. There is no Last Day at a mixing bench; the green stays where it fell and goes into the next year under the cloth.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The dish keeps the green. The pot keeps the coat. Between the two a girl dips her spoon and sets it down, and goes to the next dish.

I asked the woman who brings the lead up, a girl new to the spoons, and a man who reads the notices at the Cross, and had three answers and no hand, as I have had before. I have left the line where it was written. The leaf goes back under the jar and the jar stays cold. Tomorrow a woman will bring a girl down off the terrace, and I shall tell her what is carried before I tell her what is paid, and put the lead back where a borrowed hand took it out. The spoon will go into the dish level, and the green will come up where the pale line was, and nobody in this room will have entered either.
