I keep the peat-dries east of Reedholt for the reeve, and I take the cow when the Standing Post wants a hide or a horn. It is not a trade a man puts on a slate. The reeve's counting-man writes what is paid and I write what I have put my hand to, and the two have never agreed and were never meant to. I set this down at the end of a stand in the peat-hut with a peat-fire under a pot and the tallow down to a knuckle, and the cold comes up through the boardwalk and takes a man by the ankles before it takes him anywhere else.

The brown at the head is peat. I know the difference between peat and Ash the way a man knows the difference between his own debt and another man's: both stick to the hand, and one of them stays in the house after you have gone. There is Ash enough on this beast without my bringing it into the book. I have not washed the peat off.

# Where it stands

The cow stands in the black peat east of the marsh road where the ground gives a little under the boot and comes back warm. I have laid the back of my hand on the peat where one has stood and on peat a spear-cast away, and the two are not the same. Under the beast the peat is warm as a banked coal under its skin of grey; a spear-cast off it is cold water and sedge. I have set that down and put no cause beside it. The reeve says it is the Marsh Mother keeping her own down there. A Vhaleni hide-man says warm water comes up through the bog and the beast has found where it comes. Both men said it standing on the boardwalk, which is a dry place to say anything.

It stands until only the head shows. I have watched one hold the same place through a tend and part of another, and the sedge about it flattened and the water went still, and I went away and came back and found it there with the same set of the head. It does not chew. It does not lie down. It takes in through the hide, which is a thing a man can feel at the back of the hand without seeing it, the way he can feel a fire in the next room before he sees the flame. I have no word for that in Grathic and I have not made one.

# The push

It lowers its head and pushes. That is the whole of the danger, and a man who has never stood in peat will not believe how much of a man a beast can take with a push when the ground will not hold his foot. On hard ground you step aside. In the bog your foot goes in to the calf and stays, and the beast does not hurry. I saw a Held man --- a man who carried more than his natural measure and could lift a peat-bale above his head --- go down under one because he braced his legs, and bracing was the thing that took him. The peat did the rest. The beast stood over him and held him where he fell. It did not eat him. It kept him there until he stopped wanting, and when we got him out he was a man who would not say what had been done to him and asked only for the warm.

The order of it I have set in the tally. The why I have left standing where I found it, which is in the peat. There is no anger in the push. It is the same work the tide does when it takes a boardwalk up and leaves it somewhere a man cannot reach. I have written that sentence out once and not crossed it out, which is more than I did in the last book I kept, and I am not sure it was wisdom.

A Drawn man came up from the Post in Tallow and walked round one of these with his coat open and did not quicken. To him it is an obstacle in the ground, not a fight. I have set that beside the Held man going down and left the two where they fell, because they are both true and a man must choose which one he is before he goes out, not after.

# The song

Sung by the peat-cutters going out to the dries at first light, not coming back. The third line is the Marsh Mother's line and every cutter sings it wrong on purpose, because a song that gives the cow a reason is a song a man can carry home and set by the fire. I have set it down as it is sung, with the wrong line standing.

> One for the mud and one for the stand,
> the cow keeps her head where the warm came up;
> she lows to the Mother and she lies in the peat,
> she takes through the hide and gives nothing back.
>
> Three for the push and three for the bog,
> three for the man who braced and could not;
> the Mother counts what her cattle take
> and never counts what they leave in the ground.
>
> Count the horns and count the hide,
> count the days that the beast stood still;
> the reeve takes one and the salt-man one,
> and one stays where the standing was.

She does not low and she does not lie down. I have stood within three paces of six of them and never heard a sound out of one, and I have never found a place where one has lain. The cutters know this as well as I do. They sing it that way because a beast with no reason in it is a beast a man cannot forgive for what it did to his brother, and a man who cannot forgive has to carry something home.

# The tally

These are the stands I kept a true account of. There were others I did not write because my hands were full and one I did not write because I had the fever and had forgotten the day. I have set down both sorts as they stand. The counting-man's figures are his, not mine. I counted what stood and what came walking back; he counted what the Post could sell.

> *In the narrow upright Ledgerhand of the reeve's counting-man, written along the margin:* Enter as: three hides, twelve horns. One hide and four horn to a beast; this is the figure the Post will ask for. Loss of persons: not entered. The bog is not a vessel. Account stands.

I have left his hand standing. He has set *not entered* over the boy's leg and over the man who did not come back, and *the bog is not a vessel* over the warm place under my hand, which is the neatest piece of work I have seen out of the Standing Post. A count-stick can settle a fine and a fine unpaid can become a term and a term unpaid can become a measure, so there is a kind of justice in it, if the stick is held by the right hand. I have written the leg and the man under his line in charcoal, where his column cannot reach.

> *In the pale ink of a Vhaleni hide-buyer, on a corner of salt-paper gone soft:* Three marks paid and received at Vhal. The water keeps out. --- K.

Three marks is thirty-six bits and a bit is a loaf and a quart of small ale, so a hide is most of a winter to a household that has one. K. has never asked where the stand was that gave the hide. I have sold him three. There is a fourth hide lying in the peat-hut that I have not sold, and I have not set a figure against it.

# The hide and the horn

The hide, dried over peat, keeps out water better than anything else in Sarn, which is a strange thing when you consider that it came out of water. The buyer comes up the marsh road in Tallow with his salt-paper in his pocket and his hands always warm, and he pays in marks and counts them twice, and I have never known him to short a man and never known him to overpay one either. I have never sold him the hide of a beast I found standing where a man had gone down. He has asked for it and the reeve has told me to take what is offered, and I keep the hide under the bench and the reeve's word on the boardwalk where it was said.

The horns go to the Standing Post and are cut into count-sticks. A horn takes a notch differently from bone: the reeve can tell his own stick by the feel of the notch in the dark, which he says is the reason for it and not the price. I have cut the horns and notched nothing. Who the sticks count is not a thing a hunter asks when the counting-man is standing over him with a cuff gone soft at the wrist and a hand that goes to the stick before it goes to the man.

# What the hearths say

They say it is the Marsh Mother's own cattle, set in the bog to keep the warm down where her people can lie face down in it and find it. There are songs in the Fenmarch with a woman's name in them and a kindness done at a peat-cutting. I have heard them and I have no quarrel with a song. But I have put my hand on the peat where one has stood and on peat where none has, and I have watched a beast driven off a warm place go and stand in another warm place, and if these are the Mother's cattle then she keeps them in particular places, and the particular place is the whole of what I can see. What it wants there I have left where I found it.

There was a man of Mud End went into the black stand after a hide in Tallow of Y.H. 811 and came out with the fever that burns a man and then leaves him colder than he went, each turn taking a little head. There is no physic for it but keeping warm, which is a thing the poor cannot do, and he went home and did. I have not put his name in this book. The reeve's stick knows it, I daresay, in its own way.

# At the head and in the margins

The peat thumb at the head is mine, from setting the leaf down on the hut bench and not knowing I had marked it. The wet sedge across the first leaf came off the morning the wind turned on us and we lay flat in the cut until the push had gone past, and I have left the press of it where it fell. The brown spot on the second leaf came from the boy's leg when we sat him on the bench to bind it, and I have not scraped it, because it is the only thing in this book that cost anybody anything and the counting-man's *not entered* has not taken it off. The horn-shaving in the gutter is from cutting the fifth stick. The scorch on the third leaf is a coal out of the peat-fire that caught while I was writing and went out under my hand, and the hand was mine and is not entered. The charcoal is thin where I carried the roll against my hip. The counting-man's two rulings I have not touched, and the salt-man's line I have not answered, because a debt paid and received at Vhal is a debt that has left the marsh and is no longer mine to argue with.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The peat keeps what the standing took. Ask the reeve whose stick it counts.

There is a new warm ground east of the sedge that I have not walked, and the water stands on it when it should have drained, and I have felt the warm come up there on the stillest morning of Tallow with no beast upon it. I will go in at the next Reed with a coil of cord and a fresh spear, and I will count what goes standing and what comes walking back, and if I find the peat where a beast has stood gone cold while the beast was away from it, I will set that down too, in my own hand, with the charcoal left in it and the verb standing as it happened.
