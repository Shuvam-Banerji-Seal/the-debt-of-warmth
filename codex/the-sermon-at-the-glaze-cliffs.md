kind: A sermon of the Sallow pans preached in the bead-niche under the blue-green cliff where the glass-sand is dug; the sayings in Vhaleni set down in Grathic as I heard them, the draw-song with the ebb-line left as it is sung, the beads in a tally, one correction in the narrow upright Ledgerhand of a clerk come down from Greybank, and one line at the foot in a hand nobody will own
provenance: Preached in Ash of Y.H. 812, the Thin Year, on a morning when the draw ran seaward against the wind, by an old dipper of the pans who had trailed a net at dawn for more years than he would give; written that evening by the keeper of the glass tally in the shed below the cliffs, on the back of a salt-paper contract gone damp and therefore void, folded with a green bead in the crease and tied with a tarry end of net-cord. I keep the glass tally in the shed under the cliffs and nobody asked me for this. I write because the old man spoke to the cliff, the net, the dish and last of all to us, which is the order the pans use when somebody may be listening who wants a figure. When a man asked him the year of some tide he gave the tide instead, which is the Vhaleni way of answering a question you do not mean to keep. I have set his words down in Grathic, which is not his tongue. Where he left a verb out I have left it out and put it back underneath in my own hand, because a saying said over a bead is a thing a person has to use.

The leaf is the back of a contract that went damp in the shed and is therefore no contract at all, which the Council calls a virtue and the clerk from Greybank does not. The salt at the head came off the old man's hand when he flattened it on the niche; the glass in the grain is from the sand we dig and scratches a fingernail. The green along the fold is not slip and not salt. It came off a bead left in the crease in the sun and smells sweet when the shed is warm, and I have told the girls not to put a tongue to it. The feather is a brine-gull's. The ring is the dish, set down at the first voice and taken up at the last. I have not washed any of it.

# The manner of the dipping, as the pans keep it

*Written small, from watching, not from any order the Court has printed.*

- Go down before the light and trail the net in the draw. Do not say what the draw is doing. A draw said aloud becomes an argument, and there is argument enough at the Quay.
- Salt on the hand, never on the net. The net has its own business with the sea.
- Leave a bead in the niche for every morning you dip. The sea cannot eat it and a clerk can carry it away, which is why we leave it in a hole in the cliff and not on a shelf.
- Do not lick the cliff. In the heat it smells of sugar and in the cold of nothing, and children are told in both months. A child who licks it gets a pale line at the gum and in the end cannot hold a cup, and no dipper has known a cure.
- If a brine-gull takes a bead, let it go. It has carried the keeping into the air, where it was going in any case. No slate in Vhal stands the poorer for a gull.
- If the draw runs backward, do not correct it. We are the ones who are early or late.
- When a man with a slate comes among the pans, stop, and begin again after. His figure is for another room. Do not lay the two together.

He said the last twice, once to the cliff and once to a man behind me with his hands in his sleeves. I have not put the sayings together.

# The sermon, as he said it

## Of the green

He put his palm flat on the cliff where the blue-green stain ran down like a glaze that had been poured and not stopped.

“Look at it,” he said. “Sweet in the heat. The children are told not to lick it and the glass-men not to breathe it, and still we dig the sand at its foot and make good glass and sell it to Sere, and Sere puts wine in it and empties the wine at a feast. There is no trick in this. A thing can be good to look at and bad to take in. That is the whole of my preaching and I could find no other sentence.”

There was green on the creases of his hand and he did not wipe it on his coat.

> *Margin, in my hand:* He used the Vhaleni word for *sweet* that also means *owed* when a debt is left too long. I have set *sweet* because that is what the cliff does. He would not have the other word written, and that is a word with a use.

## Of the bead

“Every morning I leave one,” he said. “A green bead in a hole in the cliff. I have left them since before you were born and never counted them.

“A priest out of Greybank told me the bead is an offering to the Hearth and that the Hearth keeps what is given. I asked him what became of a bead in a crack no priest could reach without a rope. He said the offering was received, which a man says when he has not been down the cliff.

“Glass keeps its colour. Salt keeps nothing. A man who puts a bead in a hole and goes back to the pans has done something with his hands before the day took it from him. If the Hearth wants the bead, let it come down the cliff and fetch it. It has not come yet.”

A woman beside me said the beads were only beads. He turned his head without haste. “A net is only a net,” he said. “I have mended them for years. Do not tell me what a bead is.”

## Of the draw

“The water goes out with the warm in it,” he said to the dish. “That is the draw. It runs against the wind and has since my father's father dipped, and we bless it at dawn so it will go on running and not turn and take what stands on the shore.

“They say there is a Hearth under the salt and the Court's fire is only its chimney. I have been asked if this is true and I have given a tide for an answer, because a tide does not spoil in the mouth and a man with a slate likes a thing he can set in a column. Ask me what is under the salt and I will tell you which tide. There is always one more tide than there are questions.”

He put two fingers in the dish and lifted them out.

“I have dipped thirty years and never seen a measure come back out of the sea. Salt comes back. Glass comes back. A boat comes back if the wind is kind. A person does not, and the dippers do not say it, and I am saying it to a dish that will not carry it up the Salt Road.”

> *In the narrow upright Ledgerhand of a Court clerk who came down from Greybank on the third day and borrowed the leaf while I was at the pans:* The Sallow draw is a tide of the Keeping and is efficacious thereby. The measure is retained in the salt. Enter as nil.
> *
> *Underneath, in mine:* I have put *goes out* and *comes back* under his *is retained*. His sentence is true eighteen leagues inland and has no verbs for a woman standing in salt water.

## Of the coin

A man from a Sere ship had given a boy a coin, and the boy showed it to the dipper, because Vhaleni say a stranger's coin warms before his face.

“It warms before his face,” the old man said. “So they say, and I have said it myself. But I have held a coin out of a counting-house fire and a coin that lay in a man's pocket in the pulled benches for a term, and the two are not the same warmth. Somebody paid to make that coin warm. A coin has no fire of its own and neither has a stranger.

“When a man says a thing warms before your face, ask who paid. You will not get an answer at the Quay. You will get a contract on salt-paper, and if the weather is kind it is gone by morning, and that is called a virtue here.”

The boy kept the coin.

## Of them that go out on the ebb

“We seal them in a boat and push it out on the ebb,” he said. “We watch until it is out of sight, and that is held to be enough. I have stood at eleven of these and never seen a boat come back with the man in it.

“I am not saying the Return is a lie. I am saying this coast has the least to show of it. We put no slate at the boat and read no figure over the water. A Vhaleni who dies at sea is called already returned, which is a kind sentence and costs nothing.

“If there is a mercy in this world it is in standing until the boat is small, and then going back to the pans. We do not call it a keeping. We call it enough.”

## The draw-song

*Sung low, facing the water, a bead held between the teeth. There is a line missing before the last four, the one the ebb leaves, and I have left the space the length it is sung.*

> Salt on the hand and the net in the dawn,
> the draw goes out where the cold has gone;
> the cliff tastes sweet and the sweet is a lie,
> and the bead in the niche will not say why.
>
> Three for the pans and three for the rain,
> three for the boat that comes back again;
> the gull takes one and the gull gives none,
> and the clerk writes nil when the count is done.
>
> The ebb takes what the tide would not keep —
>
> We watch till the sail is a speck and then
> say it is enough, and go to the pans again;
> the sea keeps nothing and keeps on keeping,
> and the green in the crack is not for eating.

A girl from the glass shed asked what the space was for. Nobody answered her. The clerk put a round nought against it, which I have left where he set it.

# The bead-row, as the niche keeps it

*A bead that goes into the crack is a bead no tally can reach, and a figure brought into the shed in a pocket goes out again in the glass and leaves nothing on the leaf.*

| Bead | Left by | What was asked of the dipper | What the tide gave |
|---|---|---|---|
| Green, chipped at the rim | the old man, every morning | nothing; he set it in and went | another morning |
| Clear, with a tarry fibre shut in it | a man off the pulled benches | whether his term was kept | a tide named, no year |
| Green bead melted round a coal | a girl of the glaze works | whether the sweet would kill her | *Ask the cliff*, which is no answer |
| The bead the gull took | nobody | nothing | a feather on the leaf |
| Nine beads under the loose stone | the pans, in the lean winters | one in twelve, read at the Quay | the printed figure, unchallenged |

There were beads I did not set down, because a row of beads is a thing a clerk can carry away and a morning is not. The nine under the loose stone have no names.

# What the clerk did

He came down from Greybank on the third day with ink in two fingers, borrowed the leaf while I was at the pans, and gave it back with the verbs taken out.

- Where the old man said *the water goes out with the warm in it*, he wrote *the measure is retained in the salt* above and left the old words below.
- Where he said *ask who paid for the warming*, he wrote *claim not established* and drew a small stop between two clusters of his neat dark hand.
- At the bead-row he put a round nought against the nine under the loose stone and wrote *vacancy* where the tide should stand.
- At the gap in the song he wrote *vacancy* again, and when I asked what a line was worth with no words, he said nothing a clerk could enter, the first true thing he said all morning.

I have put the verbs back underneath and left his hand standing above, as the cliff keeps the green over the sand that made it. He takes them out because a thing always true has no need of them. I put them back because a woman in salt water wants to know who did the going out, and whether it was her own net.

# At the head and in the margins

The salt at the head is the old man's and mine and will not wash out of grain this soft. The glass scratches a nail. The green on the fold is sweet in heat and I have kept the leaf out of the sun since it took. The feather lay on the niche when I came to write and I have not moved it. The ring is from the dish, which stood on the leaf while he spoke of them that go out on the ebb. The tar at the tie holds a knot the way a niche holds a bead. The damp at the lower corner is the voided contract showing through its own writing. The clerk did not notice any of these things, which is how I know he is a good clerk.

# At the foot

Struck through once and written again underneath, very small, in a hand that is not mine, not the old dipper's and not the clerk's:

> The beads are an account of warmth the Court did not take.
>
> The beads are the place where an account was refused.

I asked the old man, the girl from the glass shed and the clerk, and had three answerings and no hand. I have left it struck through and standing, the only honest way to keep a sentence you cannot sign.

The leaf goes back in the shed with the bead in its crease and the cord tied as it was. Next Ash the dippers will trail their nets and leave their beads in the crack, and the draw will run out against the wind as always. If the clerk comes I shall let him take the verbs out and put them back, and leave the nought in its place and the green where it fell.
