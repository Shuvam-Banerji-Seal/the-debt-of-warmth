I lift the baskets at the turn and keep the leading cord. There are four traps below the plank and a fifth set where the water goes quick, which nobody admits to setting. A girl came up from Low Kiln this spring who can hear a pot before a knuckle touches it and cannot tell which way the water runs under her own feet. She is our kin and she is not ours, and what the weir can give her is the old sentences. A saying said to the water stays at the water. A saying tied in a cord goes on with her without a figure.

Our counts are not written. They are tied in grey wool and reed-fibre, at arm's length, with one tied low by teeth and thumb where the hand that made it cannot see. A knot keeps when paper has gone soft, and no clerk at Greybank has ever read one, which we did not think of first and have since come to value. Where a sound has no word I have tied a knot in the margin. Do not put a word into it. A man come down from the Cross asked what we say when there is nothing to say. I told him we say it to the water. He went away believing he had learned the Weir, which is the usual end of such a conversation.

Say these to the plank, the basket, the water under the plank, and to a person last. Said looking at a person, a saying becomes an argument, and there is enough arguing at the Cross without carrying it upriver.

# Of the water under the plank

1. “Ask the water what it took. It will tell you where it put it.”

*Gloss — At the Fishstairs they ask what the water cost and are given the weather. Here we ask where it went. The leach takes the Warm out of a thing and hands it to the Sallow draw; the river is only the road. A man wants a figure. The water has a direction. Both are true, and only one can be carried into a room with a table.*

2. “The ice is a lid. Do not trust a lid.”

*Gloss — Two or three children a winter go through the skin of the Ilm. The old women say the ice-mouse is the cold made small enough to be killed, which is a comfort that kills no fewer children. We say it of the water and of a man who promises what he has not stood on.*

3. “The sill sings before it breaks. Nobody sets a clock by the song.”

*Gloss — When the Warm rises in Ilmen the stone under the falls gives a low note that carries to the Fishstairs. The town sets its clocks by it and does not explain it. We say the river clears its throat before the Breaking. A man from Greybank wanted the hour entered; we told him the hour was the note, and the note was not entered.*

4. “The Breaking comes up the same steps every year and takes a different house.”

*Gloss — In Silt the Ilm comes over the Fishstairs and leaves silt waist-deep in the lower town. The water is the same water; the door is never the same door. We say it of the river and of the levy, which comes on the same day of the month and takes a different household each time. Neither is malicious. Both are habitual, which costs more.*

# Of the eel and the basket

5. “The eel keeps its heat behind the head. Cut there and you learn nothing.”

*Gloss — The old women say glass eels are the shadows of the rivets that hold the lid on the world. There is a sac behind the head where the eel keeps a little of the Warm; a boy who cuts one open to find the heat finds it gone into the water, which is where it was going. Say this of a man who opens a thing to look for the feeling in it.*

6. “Count them going in. Count them coming out. The basket has no column.”

*Gloss — The traps are lifted at the turn and the eels counted by the hand that lifts them, not the eye that prices them. A tally at the Fishstairs takes your figure and gives another by the tide's turn. We keep the going-in count in the cord and let the buyer have the other, which is not dishonesty but an arrangement two people can live with.*

7. “A gull will take a coal and leave you colder. It will not take a wet eel.”

*Gloss — The brine-gull lifts what is warm and light enough, with no malice in it. Say this of a man who has taken something and calls it borrowing. The eel is safe because it is cold and wet and no bird wants it, which is a poor safety and the only kind the basket has.*

> *Tied in the margin, where the word for the little green eel-thread would stand: a knot low down, made with teeth and thumb.* It has come loose twice and I have tied it twice. I have not written the word in.

# Of the cord and the count

8. “A knot remembers what the hand forgets.”

*Gloss — We tie grey wool when a thing is owed and untie it when paid; the sleeve-cord has one knot tied low where the hand could not see. A knot keeps longer than a name and does not care who tied it. A factor's clerk entered our knots as nil, because a knot is not a figure. Right by his law, wrong about the cord.*

9. “Do not read a cord. Ask the woman who tied it.”

*Gloss — A man from the Court held up a reed-cutter's cord and asked what it counted. She told him to ask the water, and then to ask her, in that order. He wrote “no entry lies,” which is true of the cord and false of the woman, who had carried the count eleven years and knew every knot by her thumb.*

10. “Forty and one. The one has no name and is not a debt.”

*Gloss — In the year of the flower a woman at these traps tied forty knots for the children of Low Kiln taken in the levy, and a forty-first she cannot account for. She has never untied it. When she says the word low into a basket, the child looks at her and cannot answer, which is the answer given by people who asked the same question at their own first tying.*

> *In the narrow upright hand, written against this saying:* Figure entered as nil. Vacancy. *Underneath, in mine:* A nought is a number. The forty-first is a place. He has made the place smaller by writing in it, which is the whole art of that hand.

11. “The slate goes by edge-on. Ask the carrier, not the slate.”

*Gloss — The runner who carries the thane's slate between the Cross and Greybank holds the writing turned into her sleeve and will not read it. “I don't read it. I carry it,” she says; “there's a difference, and the road doesn't care which.” A man who wants the figure must walk to Greybank for a number that was not true when it left the basket.*

# Of the collector and the figure

12. “You hear the collector on the plank before you see his slate.”

*Gloss — His boots have never been in the water. Children here are taught to know the sound of them before their own names, which is a grim kindness and the only warning the weir gives. He comes with a cold tube and a clean figure, and the figure does not get wet on the way.*

13. “Cold iron takes. The water takes. Only one of them writes it down.”

*Gloss — The brand for a stolen cup of warmth goes on the wrist, and the district pays in cold for what the river carries off without a slate. Ask a woman which is the kinder and she will ask why a man wants a kind way to be emptied. There is no feast at the Weir in Silt.*

14. “One in twelve is printed. The other goes up the Clay Road in a box.”

*Gloss — Read twice at the Cross, once for the moot and once for those who came late. The reader says the levy is called and not called by him, taking himself out of his own sentence. What goes up the road in his locked box is a different figure, and the two have never agreed here.*

15. “A name said to the water is not entered.”

*Gloss — The slate takes a number and the house takes a name, and both travel where the person does not. What is said at the trap stays there. A woman whose child came back from another hearth keeps one name at home and one for the officer, and has never joined them, which is the only kindness her house can manage.*

# Of the dead and the plank

16. “Feet to the river. Pot at the door. Sherd on the plank.”

*Gloss — We break a pot at the door of a death-house as they do in Sill, so the heat may get out, and set one sherd on the plank afterward, facing upriver. A threshold is a thing a house can step back over; a plank has only one way off it. Nobody says who the sherd is for, and nobody takes it up.*

17. “Nothing is swept off a plank.”

*Gloss — In Sill they leave the grey in the corners on the Last Day, burn it in the morning and call it dealt with. There is no corner on a plank. What falls off goes into the water and becomes somebody else's weather, a difference no clerk has entered.*

# The basket-song

*Sung lifting the traps at the turn of the water, going out and not coming back. The fourth line of the second part is held, and the space after it is the length of a knot. Nobody fills it.*

> Four for the baskets and one set out,
> one for the cord that the thumb knows;
> the water goes where the wind isn't
> and the eel goes where the water goes.
>
> Count them going and count them back,
> a bit for the basket, a bit for the board;
> the gull takes the coal and the clerk takes the mark
>
> and the one I keep
>
> The cord goes home in a sleeve, unread.
> The slate goes up in a hand, unread.
> The river reads both and says neither,
> and the water keeps what the water took.

*Gloss — The held line is not a fifth basket or a fifth trap. Children ask and are told to ask the cord, which is the answer their mothers were given. A tally-man from Greybank put a nought in the space and wrote vacancy beneath it. A nought is a figure. The space was a place.*

# What the borrowed hand did

A factor's clerk borrowed the leaves at Greybank on a market morning, with ink settled in two fingers of a soft cuff. He writes narrow and upright and takes the verbs out as though a verb were a thing a man could carry off in his sleeve.

- At *the water will tell you where it put it*, he wrote *the loss is not ascertained*, which leaves the water out and the loss in.
- At *count them going in*, he set *two tallies entered; discrepancy nil*, joining the count I kept to the count the buyer paid, which is the whole art of that hand.
- At *a knot remembers*, he wrote *no instrument recorded*, true in his language and of no use to a woman with a cord in her sleeve.
- At *forty and one*, he put a round nought and wrote *vacancy*, which is a figure where a place stood.

I have put the sayings back under his hand. He takes the doers out because a thing always true has no need of anybody doing it. I put them back because a girl from Low Kiln wants to know whose hand lifted the basket, and there is always a hand.

# In the margin, in a slate-runner's sloping hand

> I carry the slate edge-on and never look at what is on it. The figure that goes up to Greybank does not know what came out of any basket on this river. I have walked that road eleven years and never once opened one.

Below, in mine: she said it to the plank and would not say it twice. I have not asked. A person who carries a slate is not one who reads it, and the road cares about neither.

# At the head and in the gutter

The silt at the head came off the hand that held the tally down on the rail in high water; no wet sleeve takes it out, which is why we use charcoal. The slime at the fold is off the leading cord and goes soft when the weather goes soft, which tells me when to shut the tin. The hook nick is from a basket set down by a man paying for six eels and charged for five, or the other way, which he has not settled. The gull's peck went through one word of the song and nobody has claimed the bird. The green thread in the gutter is a glass eel, fine as a hair, left where it fell. The pressed reed came from a bundle set down while a woman decided whether to sell. The grey along the outer fold is not mine and not the factor's. I have not swept it. There is a Last Day downriver; there is none on a plank.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The water takes and does not enter.
> The cord keeps and cannot be read.
> A man is what he has carried to the basket and not yet let go.

I asked a woman who lifts the traps, a slate-runner, and a factor's man out of Greybank, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written. The leaves go back into the eel-skin under the board. Tomorrow the water turns at the fourth and the baskets go down; I shall count them going in, say nothing of what came out, and tie one knot low where my thumb cannot see it, which is the whole of the trade at the Weir.
