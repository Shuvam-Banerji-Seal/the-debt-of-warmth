title: Nine to the Floor, Being a Drinking Song of the Duelling Floor in Keld Keep
kind: A Keldish floor-song in three parts, taken down by the clerk who keeps the numbers at the Duelling Floor; the verses in the hard Rim speech, the corrections in the verbless hand of a Court survey-clerk, one answer from the Listening Stones, and a lowlander's verse at the foot
provenance: Written in Bone of Y.H. 812, the Thin Year, in the room off the Duelling Floor beneath Keld Keep, on the back of a survey sheet spoiled by wet slate, the figures on the face of it gone soft; carried down the Rim Road and the Clay Road with a survey party in Ilmen of Y.H. 813 and copied at Greybank. Kept in the floor-clerk's board box among the spoil slips. It survives with coal dust at the head, a press of wet reed across the first leaf, a smear of hot lead at the fold, an ale ring, a tallow thumb, a grey Ash print along the gutter, two corrections in the narrow upright hand of the Court, one line in a hand that writes very large and very slowly, and a verse added in Grathic by a clay-man who had been levied to the Cold Pass.

I keep the numbers at the floor. A man comes down from the shift or off the stone and states his measure before he states his name, and I set the two down in that order, because that is the order the Keep uses and I have never known a man to complain of it until he has had a clamp across his arm. What follows is not part of the account. It is what they sing after, in the room off the floor, with the cups out and the irons put away. No one sings it on the stone. A man with a clamp in his hand has no breath to spare for a refrain, and a man who has just opened a way in his forearm is not in a singing mood.

I have put the words down as they came. The coal dust at the head came off the sleeve of the man who dictated the third part to me; he would not wash before he had finished, and said a song that had never been in a mine was not worth the paper. The lead at the fold is not mine. I have left it where it lies, and I have not swept the grey out of the gutter either. There is no Last Day in a board box.

# The song, as it is sung

*Sung standing. The cup in the right hand, the left palm flat on the table stone: the hand that has held a clamp does not hold a cup at the same time. The room says the refrain together. A man who has just come off the floor says the lines of his own part, and nobody says his name.*

## The first part: the floor

Nine to the floor and one to the stone,
state your measure, then your name;
the cup goes round and the cold goes round,
and the number is not the same.

The iron bites and does not let go,
the arm below goes the colour of rope;
a man says his measure, a man says his name,
and the third thing he says is a hope.

They put hot iron to the palm and ask if you owe,
and the honest come back with a hand gone dead;
I have known eleven honest men
and not one of them lied, and nine cannot lift a cup.

Nine to the floor and one to the stone,
state your measure, then your name;
the cup goes round and the cold goes round,
and the number is not the same.

## The second part: the Descent

At thirteen he goes down for a single day
and comes up with a number changed;
his mother asks what he saw in the dark
and he tells her the dark, and his hands tell her the rest.

There is a mark on the inside of the arm
where the way was cut and kept open while it scarred;
it aches before the thaw and aches after,
and he answers to whichever number the room has given him.

Some come up with the same number and some do not,
and the clerk writes the second down without a mark beside it,
which is the only honest thing a clerk can do.

Nine to the floor and one to the stone,
state your measure, then your name;
the cup goes round and the cold goes round,
and the number is not the same.

## The third part: what comes up through the stone

When the lead smells hot in the lower drift, stop singing.
The warm comes up through the stone like a river loosed in a room;
the grub in the warm-stone knew it a tend before any man did,
eating from the inside and leaving the stone standing hollow.

We go down in pairs and come up in pairs,
and where there were two at the mouth there is sometimes one.
The number stands. The number stands.
That is what we say, and we do not say it twice.

Five leagues up where the bare stones sit,
a Vorr sits still and comes down with a figure;
ask him what it means and he will tell you once,
*the numbers are correct*, and he will tell you twice.

Karric shut the Pass in seven hundred forty-one,
and the border was fixed in forty-seven by men who had never walked it;
we drink to the Warden and we drink to the road,
and the road has no name and the Warden has two.

## The cup at the end

There is a cup set at the end of the floor that nobody takes.
It stands for the one whose number was changed twice.
We set it out. We do not drink from it.
The clerk leaves the place the length of a name.

The last cup goes to the man just off the stone,
not to the one that won but to the one still standing;
he drinks with the left hand, for the right has gone cold,
and he states his measure, and after that his name.

# What the Court hand did

A survey-clerk of the Court borrowed the leaf when the survey table sat at the Keep in Ilmen. He has a cuff gone soft from washing and the ink sits in two fingers of it and will not come out. He struck out *the cup goes round and the cold goes round* in both places and set above it, *the measure is circulated and the chill is entered*. Against *the number is not the same* he wrote *the vessel was re-entered*, which is a true thing said in a language nobody at this floor speaks. At the end, where the cup stands that nobody takes, he put a round nought and wrote *vacancy* beside it.

I have put the words back underneath and left his standing above. He takes the verbs out because a thing always true has no need of them. I put them back because a man who has just come off the stone does not know he is hearing something always true, and wants to know which hand to drink with. A nought is a number and a place is not. He has put a number where a place should be, which is the whole art of that hand.

# From the Listening Stones

The leaf went up to the stones with a surveyor who had a question to ask and was in no hurry to hear the answer. It came back with one line in a hand that writes very large and very slowly, a hand like a man learning to use a tool he has no use for:

> The numbers are correct.

I asked the surveyor whether that was an answer to the song. He said it was an answer to the numbers, which is not the same thing, and that a man who wanted a Vorr to translate a thing had chosen the wrong sort of man. I have set the line where it was written and have not glossed it. In this country a sentence that can be carried away and still mean nothing is not a small thing; it is the only kind of sentence that survives the winter.

# The verse at the foot, in Grathic

A clay-man from the low road was at the Pass in the year the snow lay long, and he wrote this in the bottom margin in a small careful hand, with the *th* hard and the vowels short as they speak them down the Ilm. He had been levied and had not chosen to come.

> I do not state my measure. It was stated for me
> by a man with a clean cuff and a number already written.
> Where I come from a pot split lip to foot is a pot gone wrong
> and the yard goes around the place where it stood.
> Here they clamp it and call it a repair,
> and the repair gets carried all the same.
>
> Four for the corners and four for the wall,
> and a place where the fifth should be --- even up here.

I have not struck it out. He asked me what the fifth was, and I told him I had never known a man who could say, and he said that was the answer his mother would have given and went back to the wall.

# At the head and in the gutter

The coal dust is the Shift-man's, who dictated the third part standing and would not sit. The press of wet reed came from the board walk at the Weir, where the survey party put the leaf down while a barge unloaded and it took the weather. The ale ring is not a mark anybody owns; three men put a cup in it over one evening and no man will say which. The tallow thumb is mine: I held the sheet down in the draught that comes up the shaft and did not know I had marked it. The grey in the gutter came off a cuff I had no business putting into a corner in the room where the cups were, and I have not washed it off and have no wish to.

The board box goes back under the survey table with the spoil slips. The figures on the face of the sheet are gone soft on the wet side, and the song on the dry side is plain enough to read. A man will state his measure and then his name tomorrow as he did today, and the number of some of them will be changed by the end of the tend, and the clerk will set the second down without a mark beside it, which is the only thing he is allowed to do honestly.
