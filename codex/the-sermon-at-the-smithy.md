I bring the broken barrow-irons down from the north gate and I keep the forge's tally in charcoal, which is a thing nobody asked me to keep and no officer has ever wanted to read. Harn cuts his count into the bench with a chisel, where it stays under the grease and cannot be carried away; I write mine on the back of a slip the Cross spoiled with a wet thumb, which is paper no clerk will borrow for a column. I write because he spoke to a wall, and a wall keeps nothing by itself.

He spoke first to the niche behind the anvil, then to the tube in his hand, then to the bellows and the kiln behind the bench, and last of all to us, which is the order a smith uses when there are collectors in the yard and somebody may be listening who wants a figure. I have set his words down in Grathic, which is the speech we all use, and left out the verbs where he left them out, putting them back underneath in my own hand. A saying said over a tube is a thing a person has to use before the next draw takes it.

The blue at the head came off a bar of Keldish iron he was turning when I set my palm to the niche and got the mark on my writing hand; no washing takes it out of this paper, which is why I sign with a crossed tong and not a name. The filings in the gutter are off the rim of a tube and have worked into the scorch. The brick dust is from the kiln that breathes behind the bench. The grey came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying on a forge bench, and I have not swept it; there is no Last Day at this forge and nothing gets burnt in the morning and called dealt with.

# The manner of the mending, as the forge actually keeps it

*Written small, from watching, not from any order the Court has printed.*

- Come with the iron before the bellows are stirred. A man who brings a tube at noon has had the morning's heat counted for him by somebody else and will not know what he is paying for.
- Set the tube on the bench with the split upward. Do not lay a tube on the anvil. The anvil is for irons that have a right side; a tube has no right side and will find the one you did not mean.
- Tap it once and listen. A pot answers full or flat. A cold-iron tube answers neither, and a smith who says he has heard a third thing has been at the small ale.
- Leave the clipped end in the niche when the mending is done. Do not count the ends, do not take them home, and do not set them on a shelf where a man with a slate can see a row of them and want a total.
- When the kiln behind the bench comes round to its fourth, stop. It will stop itself. Do not put coal in for a fifth, and do not say what the fifth would have been if you had.
- If a collector comes in with the tube still warm from a draw, wrap it in wool before you take it. The iron takes from the hand that holds it as well as from the arm it is set to, and a smith's fingers are not made of anything different.
- When an officer asks what the niche is for, give him the number of pegs. A number is safe and does not spoil in the mouth. Do not give him the reason, because there is no column for a reason in the book he carries.

He said the last twice, once to the bellows and once to a man in a clean coat who had come in off the Clay Yard with a slate held inward. I have not joined the two sayings.

# The sermon, as he said it

## Of the niche

He put his palm flat on the wall where the pegs stand and left it there while the bellows took a breath.

“It has no name,” he said. “The women of the yard call it the smith's niche and the Court has no word for it at all. There are the ends of tubes in it, clipped off where I have set the new rim. I have not counted them in eleven years and I never will.

“A priest will tell you that nothing is lost that we have kept. I have kept a wall of iron ends and there is not a measure among them. It is off a tool. If that is a keeping, it is a poor sort, and I have never asked anybody to kneel to it. Stand where you like. The wall has never asked for a knee and will not begin now.”

A woman with a barrow-iron across her shoulders asked whether the ends were part of the tubes they came from. He considered this longer than he considered the iron in his hand.

“A clipped end is not the tube,” he said. “It is what the tube had to give up to be made sound again. There is a difference, and I have made a living on not saying it out loud.”

> *Margin, in my hand:* He gave the same answer to the barrow-iron and to the man in the clean coat, and I have set the two answerings side by side and not one over the other. An answer given twice to two different questions is either a doctrine or a way of not answering, and he has not said which.

## Of the tube

He took up a tube and tapped it once with the chisel, very lightly, the way the women at the racks tap a pot.

“A pot tells you the truth,” he said. “Full, or flat, and a flat one you can hear across the yard. This does neither. I have mended tubes for eleven years and I have never heard one answer, and I have learned not to want it to. It is not honest and it is not lying. It is a tool, and a tool has no mouth to tell you which end you are standing at.”

He turned the rim toward the light and showed us the new seam.

“I mend the tube. I do not mend the man it is set to. A man from Greybank asked me once whether I ever thought about the arm. I told him I thought about the rim and the heat and the price of the iron, and that I had three orders that morning and would not get the fourth out if I thought about the arm. That was true. It was not the whole of the truth, and I have never said it was.”

There was a girl from the Glaze Room at the door with the sweetness of the lead at one corner of her mouth. She touched the paper when I came to write that sentence and I have kept the green where she put it. She said nothing, and went back up the terrace.

## Of the bellows

He leaned on the bellows then and let them down slowly, so that the fire came up small.

“I held these in the year of the Rising,” he said. “I was old enough to hold them and not old enough to be told what the things I was heating were for. I learned what they were for by watching what came back. A tube came back with a grey rim and another came back with a warm one, and after a while I knew which houses had sent them and which of those houses had gone cold, though nobody told me a figure.

“They taps a pot and calls the note a mercy. I taps iron and calls the seam sound if the rim holds. These are the same hand and the same ear, and the two trades have never once agreed about what a sound thing is.”

> *Margin, in my hand:* He said *went cold* and not *was collected*. I have set down the word he used. A man who works eleven years at a trade chooses his words as carefully as he chooses his iron, and I have not put a cleaner word over his.

## Of the fifth

Behind the bench the kiln kept its four low breaths. It came to the fourth and there was the silence where the next should have stood, and he let the silence stand as long as it wanted.

“That is four,” he said. “I have not put coal to it and I will not. A man who mends for a living has to leave one thing unmended, or he will go home at the going of the light and find he has mended himself into a doctrine.”

A woman asked what the fifth would have been for. He did not turn his head.

“Ask the kiln,” he said. She asked the kiln. The kiln answered as a kiln answers, in four.

I have kept this gap on its own place, with a finger between it and the gap in the song below, and a finger between it and every other absence in this yard. There are two things not given in this forge. Two are two and are not one lesson, and I have not made a third of them.

## Of the figure

A collector come up from Slate Row held a narrow slate against his forearm with the writing turned inward, and Harn went on speaking to the tube.

“One in twelve is printed on the notice,” he said. “Read twice, once for the moot and once for them that came up the road too late. I have mended the tubes that take the twelve and the tube that takes the other figure, and the two tubes are the same iron out of the same bar and I have set the same seam in both. The iron does not know which figure it is carrying. I do. That is the whole of my trade and the whole of my trouble.”

He set the chisel down.

“The slate does not sweat and the hand does. A clerk's figure goes up the road in a coat and comes down again in the same coat and leaves no dust on the bench. My figure stays here in notches under the grease, and if you ask me what it is I shall tell you how many tubes I have mended, which is a safe answer and means nothing that can be entered.”

The man in the clean coat wrote something on his slate then. Harn did not ask what, and the man did not say. I have set the order down and not what it means.

## Of them that are drawn

A woman came in at the end with a tube wrapped in wool and set it on the bench with a copper bit beside it, not on it.

“A boy was taken from her house,” Harn said, looking at the tube and not at her. “She asked me if this was the tube that had been in his arm. I told her I could not tell one tube from another once the rim had been turned. That was true too. It is a poor thing when a man's truth is all of it about iron.”

She took the bit away again and left the tube. He did not put the bit in the niche. He set it on the shelf with the other bits that nobody has asked for, and there it stays, which is not the same as keeping it and is not the same as refusing it either.

“They read a whole return in a clean voice for a person not in the room,” he said to the wall. “Here a woman stands in my forge with a wrapped tube and asks me a question I can answer only by telling her what I know about rims. If there is a mercy in this trade it is in the wrapping of the iron before you take it from a man. It costs a length of wool and is entered nowhere.”

## Of the close

“The measure is banked,” he said. “The account stands. Nothing is lost that we have kept.”

He said the three sayings to the anvil and kept his hands on the bellows. He had them from a man in a clean coat and did not like the feel of them, as a man does not like the feel of iron that has been heated twice and cooled wrong.

“I keep the measure of a tube,” he said. “I do not keep the measure of a person. There is a third sentence in the litany that I have never been able to say with my hands where they ought to be, and I have said it here to an anvil, which is stone with a use, and will not carry it up the road.”

He crossed two fingers over the new seam on the tube and covered the act with a stroke of the chisel on the bench, which is how this forge covers a thing it has stood at and will not explain. I have written the crossing and left the covering where it was.

# The hammer-song

*Said at the anvil while the iron is worked, stroke by stroke, never at the bench and never for a man from Greybank. There is a stroke held back before the last four lines, the length of a hammer that does not come down, and I have left it as it is not struck.*

> Four to the anvil and one held back,
> the heat goes out on the other track;
> a pot says full and a pot says flat,
> and iron says nothing, and that is that.
>
> One for the hand that turned the rim,
> one for the hand the tube went in;
> three for the mending and one for the cost,
> and the fifth is the place where the stroke was lost.
>
> *Here the hammer stays up. No sound.*
>
> The shelf keeps the ends and the wall keeps none,
> the seam holds true and the trade goes on;
> ask the tube what it took, it will not say —
> set it down level and take yourself away.

A girl from the Clay Yard asked what the space was for. Nobody answered her. The clerk put a round nought against it later and wrote *vacancy*, which is a figure and is not a stroke that did not happen. I have left the nought where he set it and the hammer where Harn held it.

# The pegs, as the niche keeps them

*These I have set down where they stood. A figure that comes into this forge in a coat goes out again in the same coat and leaves nothing on the bench.*

| Peg | What was brought | What he said over it | What was entered |
|---|---|---|---|
| One, split at the turning | A collector's tube, down from Slate Row | The iron does not know which figure it carried | Nothing |
| One, with the blue showing | A damper-iron from the kiln behind the bench | Leave it in fours | Nothing |
| One, gone grey at the eye | A barrow-iron from the north gate | Iron outlasts the hand and no hand asks it to | Nothing |
| One, the wool still about it | A tube from the house that lost a boy | I cannot tell one tube from another | Nothing; the bit on the shelf |
| Under the loose peg | Four clipped ends, eleven years of them | Nothing | Nothing |
| The gap in the song | No iron | No sound | A nought, and *vacancy* above it |

There were pegs before these that Harn could set a winter to, and I have not written the winters, because a row of winters is a thing a clerk can carry away and a peg in a wall is not.

# What the clerk did

An assessor's clerk come up from Greybank to weigh the winter iron borrowed the leaf on the morning after, with his cuff gone soft at the edge from washing and ink in two fingers that would not come out. He gave it back with the verbs taken out, as though a verb were a thing a man could carry off in a cuff and sell at the next crossing.

- At *a cold-iron tube answers neither*, he wrote *the instrument is neither vessel nor subject of the full note; no entry arising*, above and underlined. Harn's words stand beneath. A tube is not a vessel in the Court's books, which is true, and which is the reason no column has ever held one.
- At *I mend the tube, I do not mend the man*, he wrote *the instrument is maintained in lawful order; no third party affected*. He had not been in the forge when the woman came in with the wool, and I have set that beside his sentence with a finger between.
- At *the other figure* he wrote *no such deduction is printed; one in twelve for every vessel of the Keeping*, and set a figure above Harn's words in the neat upright hand they teach in the College. Underneath, in mine: *I have not written the other figure. He has not either. We have both heard it.*
- At the gap in the song, a round nought and *vacancy*. When I asked what a stroke was worth that had not been struck, he said nothing a clerk could enter, which is the first true thing he said all morning.
- Against the bit on the shelf he wrote *gratuity not entered*, and against the kiln's fourth, *not a matter for the forge*, true as only a man who has never stood behind a bench with a kiln at his back can say it.

I have put the verbs back underneath and left his hand standing above, as the forge leaves the old seam showing under the new rim. He takes them out because a thing always true has no need of them. I put them back because a woman with a wrapped tube on a bench wants to know who is doing the taking, and whether it was her own hand that paid for the wool.

# At the head and in the gutter

The blue at the head is Keldish and will not wash out of grain this soft; it is the only part of this leaf that was put there by the trade and not by a person. The filings came off a rim Harn set down hard on the bench while I was asking him to say the word again, and they have worked into the scorch and stay there. The brick dust is off the kiln and comes up on every sleeve in this forge, which is how I know a man who has stood behind the bench from one who has only heard it. The scorch took the corner off one line and not the sense. The tallow thumb is mine; I held the leaf low so that the men at the anvil would not see me writing and change the words for me. The ring is from a cup that stood from the first voice to the last and was drunk by nobody, which is how small ale goes in Low Kiln when there is a thing to say and no proper word for it. The grey came off a cuff and nobody will own it. The bead is lead glaze from the Glaze Room, green and sweet in the wet, and I have kept it out of the sun since it took. The end of a tube in the second fold answers neither full nor flat when I knock it, and I keep it there to remind me what neither is. The wire at the tie has been annealed twice and will not spring; a thing that has been heated and cooled and then held still is the only kind of keeping this forge trusts.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

Struck through once and written again underneath, very small:

> The smith mends the tube and the tube takes from the man.
>
> The wall keeps the ends. The shelf keeps the bits. Neither keeps a person.
>
> A tool has no mouth, so the account is read over it in a clean voice.
>
> Four are four, and the fifth is not owed by anybody standing here.

I asked Harn, the woman with the barrow-iron, the collector come up from Slate Row and the clerk, and had four answerings and no hand. I have left the line struck through and standing, which is the only honest way to keep a sentence you cannot sign.

The leaf goes back behind the loose peg in the niche with the clipped ends. Next repair-month the bellows will go down and the kiln behind the bench will keep its four, and if the clerk comes up from Greybank I shall let him take the verbs out and put them back, and leave the nought in its place and the hammer up where it was held, and set nothing down for a fifth that nobody has asked for.
