I set bones for a living. In Nine Pots that is half a trade and a whole accusation: four kilns, one clay pit, and a road neither town will mend, and between them a man can break himself in more ways than I can count.

Nobody asked me for this book. A man came in off the feud road in the lean week with his thumb lying the wrong way, and I heard myself answer in my mother's voice, and did not know whether I believed it. A remedy you have to say in your mother's voice is a remedy nobody has looked at in a long while. I have looked at some of mine. The rest I have left standing, because a man at three in the morning wants a hand held steady more than he wants a correction.

I write on the back of a road-tally a rain spoiled. The figures went before anybody could charge them, which is a mercy of a kind: a figure that will not read cannot be entered against a hamlet that has never agreed whose road it is. The chalk at the head is mine, off the moot-post. The blue in the gutter came off the Ferr ford when I washed a man's arm in it, which I have not done since. Nothing here is free. In this country a thing that costs nothing has been paid for by whoever carried the stone.

# The order of the splint-room

*Cut small into the first leaf with the tally-point, with one word gone over in clay slip.*

- Ask which kiln before you ask which hand. A man who has carried from the fourth tells you where his back hurts; a man who has carried from none tells you where it ought to. This is not kindness. It is the order of the room.
- Lay the broken one on sacking, never on the clay floor. Clay takes the warm out of a side faster than a bed does. A physician from Greybank wrote that a cold floor draws; I have set my line above his. Both stand.
- Turn the slate face down before you go to a child. I have no slate-name and never shall. A bone I can see I can set; a name written before the face is looked at is a bone set by somebody who was not in the room.
- Put the stone in wool at the belly and try it on your own wrist. The stone came off somebody's kiln bank and somebody paid for the coal.
- Bind loose enough to see a fingernail. A finger you cannot see has begun to go the colour of old rope.
- We used to say never bind with cupworm silk, because it seals a crack in green ware and a bone is not a pot. I have bound four. Three held. I have left the saying standing and put the four underneath.
- When the third night comes, let the woman do the counting. A number counted for her belongs to whoever counted it.

Against the cupworm line the physician wrote *no tensile strength in flesh*, in a hand that scores the leaf. I have put my four under it.

# The remedies, as they are used

| Trouble | What is set to it | The manner of it | Who carries it |
|---|---|---|---|
| The crush (a hand under a stack of saggars) | Two hands, a lath of willow, a fold of wet cloth; set before the swelling takes | We say a bone dressed green takes the tool the way a block does. The physician says wet cloth cools what should be kept warm. I have set twenty and had four come crooked; one I bound too tight and it did not come back. | The hand. This is the row where I have been least honest. |
| A bone set wrong | Leave it a tend and a half; do not look on the odd days | The physician says three days, and that a bone knits when it knits and not when a woman tells it to. Our saying went downriver in a salt-wife's book and came back at Greybank with her hand under it; I had not known it had travelled. | The patient, in the holding still. |
| The four-kiln cough | Steam under a cloth; honey if the house has it | We say it is the smoke of our own fourth kiln and cannot hurt a man who has thrown all his life. A carter who has worked both yards says the Stonemouth cough is the honest one. The physician says it is clay in the lung and the only physic is to leave the yard. I sent three up the Rim Road; two came back worse, for the road has dust of its own. | The man, in the morning he coughs up a colour he will not discuss. |
| The sweet line | Sour barley-water; wood-ash at the gum | We do not taste our glaze in Nine Pots; we fire it. The green comes west in a girl's mouth from the Glaze Room at Sill, and we say a sweet answers a sweet. The physician says lead, and that sour water only troubles the teeth. I have seen the line go off one who left that room and come back on one who stayed. I have not seen the lead. | The glaze room, which cannot stop and does not ask. |
| A feud-cut | Tallow, then a clean fold; never the grey | The men of the road put grey in a cut to dry it, believing grey is clean. I have dressed three such and found the grey under the skin in all three, and could not get it out. | The cut, in a white line it keeps. |
| The cold that stays | Three turns of wool, a stone at the belly, and work | The extremity goes first and the heart holds out, and there is an hour on the far side of the warming when a man gets worse. The physician says a warmed body spends faster and walking faster still. I have no name for that hour and have left the place where the name would stand the length a name of that kind wants. | The man, who cannot stop working. |
| The second chill | Warmth, barley-water, a watch to the fourth day; we walk them | A hearth-maid come west from Low Kiln says a warmed body gives heat away faster and that the child should be held and counted with. I have walked eleven and held two; all saw the fourth morning, and one went thin. I have kept the counts on birch bark and not added them, for a count added is a count a clerk can carry. | The household, one night's sleep; sometimes the child. |
| The birth-flower | Nothing. Two fingers crossed, and the word the mother gives | A woman of the washing at Sill crossed two fingers over one and gave me a date instead of an answer. I have put the date in the margin. The physician says it is a flowering of the blood and not a flower, and is right in a language nobody here speaks. | Nobody knows. The child's skin, perhaps. |

Nothing in this book is given away. It is carried from one potter to another and called a cure. That is the only sentence I will stand behind.

# The note under the knuckle

The potters tap a green pot before it goes to the kiln and hear whether it will hold. Sound answers full; cracked answers flat, a small dead sound that stops at the lip and will not go through. This is the one thing in the four kilns that costs nothing and is still allowed to be true.

I tapped a man once, gone thin after a return, and put my ear to the little flat I thought I heard. The flat was not in him. A body is not a pot, though we make pots in the shape of bodies and put a face on the fourth. I did not say so at the time, because a moot-man was in the room with a tally. I have written it here, which is a different matter.

# The road

The feud is over a road neither town will mend. The men of Sill come west over the ford when the Ferr is low and call our road a disgrace; our men go east and say the same, and both arrive with the same cut in the palm where a stone went in. I have never asked which side a man belongs to before I set his hand. Once I did, and a man who had come six leagues for a bone went away without it.

A road takes and takes and enters nothing. A slate enters everything and takes nothing. Between the two a person stands and is mended or not, and the hamlet goes on calling the road ours and theirs and leaving it where it is.

# The setting-song

*Sung low over the splint, to the bone and not to the man. There is a place after the last line the length of the road's name, and no singer has put a word in it.*

> Four for the kilns and one for the pit,
> one for the hand that the saggar split;
> set it green and bind it loose,
> and leave the nail where the thumb can touch.
>
> A cup answers full, a cup answers flat,
> a bone gives nothing to knock at;
> the road goes east and the road goes west
> and neither has ever been mended.
>
> Two for the ash and two for the clay,
> two for the woman who counts under wool;
> the third night comes and the warm goes back,
> and what goes back is not what went ---

A clerk of the College put a round nought in the place and wrote *vacancy* beneath it. A nought is a figure a clerk can carry out; the road's name is a place and stays.

I changed one line in four-and-twenty years: *set it green and bind it loose* is now *set it green and leave the nail*, because a man cannot see a nail through a fold tied too tight. The men singing with me have not noticed, which is how I know the line wanted changing.

# What the physician pressed in the margin

He came down from Greybank in Smoke of Y.H. 811 with a book that had the word *lead* in it and a hand that wrote without asking. Against the cupworm silk he set *no tensile strength in flesh*; against the birth-flower, *a mark in the blood*; against the four-kiln cough, *the dust of the clay, and no physic but departure*, true and of no use to a house whose living is cut out of that clay. He was right about all three. I have left his sentences above mine and my words underneath, where a man at three in the morning will find them.

# What the College clerk did

He borrowed the leaf at Greybank when the winter pots were counted, ink in two fingers of a soft cuff. Against my column headed *Who carries it* he wrote *No cost entered*, which is a true thing in a dishonest place. Where I wrote *the road takes and enters nothing*, he set *no toll is recorded on the nine pots road*, the most beautiful sentence I have ever read in a hand that has never carried a stone. Where I wrote *we bind it loose enough to see the nail*, he wrote *the limb is dressed and retained*, taking the hands out as though a bone could set itself.

I have put my verbs back underneath his. A clerk can speak without verbs because he is never in the room. A man with a thumb lying the wrong way wants to know which hand will set it, and who will pay for the clay.

# In the margin, in charcoal, the moot-man's hand

> A pot tells you when it is fired. A hand does not. I have sat at this post through two feuds and counted no bones; the bones come to Talla and the figures come to me, and neither of us asks the other what the road cost. --- M.

He wrote it while I was at the ford; I have set his line above mine. A man who has counted nothing is allowed one sentence in a book of physic.

# At the head and in the gutter

The chalk at the head is off the moot-post, where I marked the days the road was impassable. The blue tideline came off the Ferr and took two words clean off the second leaf; I have not written them back. The clay in the gutter is out of the shared pit, the only thing here put there by the trade and not by a person. The cupworm cocoon was pressed in by a girl who said its silk holds a crack in green ware and would hold a bone; I have not told her about the three hands, or the fourth. The ring of barley-water came from a cup set down while I told a man to drink and he would not. The scorch is a spark off the fourth kiln, put out under my hand; I have not entered the burn. The green bead in the corner was pressed in by a girl of High Kiln with the sweetness at one mouth-corner, and I did not ask whose glaze it was. There is no Last Day in a potter's apron and nothing here is swept up and burnt and called dealt with.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The pot keeps the crack. The bone keeps the crook.
> The road keeps nothing, and is paid all the same.

I asked the physician, the clerk and the moot-man, and had three answers and no hand. The book goes back into the apron. Tomorrow a man will come in off the feud road with his thumb lying the wrong way, and I shall set it green, bind it loose enough to see the nail, put my verbs back where the clerk took them out, and turn the slate face down before I look at the child, because a child is not an account and a man at three in the morning wants a hand held steady more than he wants a correction.
