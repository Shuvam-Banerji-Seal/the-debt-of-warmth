I keep the glass-book of the furnaces below the Cliffs. It lies in a tin lined with sand in the tally shed, and the sand comes in through the seams in the wet season and stays, so that the leaves are gritty where a thumb has gone and where none has. The Council keeps its own register in the tar-smelling room up at the Quay, and the two books are not the same book. The Council's register holds what was allowed. Mine holds what was fired. Between the allowance and the firing there is a space the width of a crucible, and it is in that space that the year 626 is written.

Everything in Vhal is written on paper made to rot, and the Council calls it a virtue that a contract left in the damp is a debt made nothing. In the shed the paper goes soft in a season and the figures come up in pale islands, and we copy the book again before the next firing. No two copies agree, which is the only honest thing about them. Where the Council has taken the doers out of a year I have put them back, the way a man puts his hand back on the tongs before he lifts anything hot.

# The year as the Council's register has it

*Copied out of the leaning hand in the tin. What I have set underneath is not an argument with the register. It is the register with the men put back.*

**Y.H. 624.** Sand and lead from the Cliffs come down to the shed in the ordinary carts. Entered as *materials received; the furnaces in their course*.

**Y.H. 626.** The furnace-men of the Cliffs shut every furnace in the seventh week of Seed and kept them shut into Frost. No glass went north that year. The Council's contracts for the year's pots were suspended, by decree of the Council of Tides, for the space of one year. Entered as *a suspension of instruments; the ordinary course observed; account standing*.

> *In the narrow upright hand of the Council room, written above my line:* The several contracts were allowed to lapse. No interruption in the trade occurred. Strike *shut*.

Mine stands underneath, where a clerk of the Council will not think to look. Nine of us shut the furnaces, and we shut them with our own hands, and the men who came up from the Quay to ask us why went back down the Salt Road without an answer they could enter. A suspension of instruments is a thing that happens to paper. A shut furnace is a thing that happens to men. Both were entered; only one was entered under this year.

**Y.H. 627.** The furnaces were lit again in Ilmen and the pots went north as before. Entered as *the ordinary course resumed*. I have entered the number of men who did not come back to the shed, which is a column the register has no place for.

**Y.H. 641.** The pale line comes among the glaze-women of High Kiln upriver, and the physician writes *teeth*. I have put the year in the margin and not in the account, because I can set two sentences side by side and cannot join them, and the glass-book is not the place for a joining I have made myself.

> *In a Court hand, borrowed from Greybank on a market morning:* Lead not implicated. Glass a vessel of trade, innocently used. No deduction arises.

A thing a man cannot weigh he may still carry in a cuff. This one carried a correction in it.

# The table of pots

*The right-hand figure is made up in the Council room before the carts go out. The figure beside it is what the shed actually fired. I have kept both since the first year and have never been asked which is true.*

| Year | Pots fired below the Cliffs | Pots entered at the Quay | Entered as | The shed's note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 624 | 900 | 900 | *levy of glass received* | seven furnaces lit |
| 626 | none | 900 | *instruments lapsed; account standing* | seven furnaces shut; nine men gone to the benches |
| 627 | 900 | 900 | *the course resumed* | three furnaces lit by hands I did not know |
| 641 | 900 | 900 | *trade uninterrupted* | the sweetness went upriver and was called teeth |

The nine hundred of Y.H. 626 were entered as received. They were not received. They were written in the room at the Quay with the damp coming up under the pen, and the figure stayed where the clerk put it while the pots stayed where no pot had ever been. I have not struck the figure out. I have set the firing beside it, which is as near as a tally-keeper comes to asking a question of a room he is not allowed to enter.

> *In the narrow upright hand of the Council:* Deficit not entered. Contract discharged at the Quay. Account stands.

The hand is careful and has never once changed a figure. It has made nine hundred pots that never existed into nine hundred pots that did, which is a cleaner thing to carry into a counting room. There are eleven words for a lapsed contract taught in that room and not one for a furnace gone cold in Seed, which is the whole of the difficulty between us.

# The song of the shut furnaces

*Taken down in the shed from a furnace-man of the third mouth who had worked the lead since before the rising. He would not sing it while the man from the Council room stood inside the door, so we went out and sat on the sand where the gulls could hear. He said the song had been in the shed longer than he had, which is the custom of that place.*

> The Cliffs gave green and the shed gave blue
> and the lead came down in the sand;
> they wrote *a lapse of the paper*
> for the shutting of every hand.
>
> Seven mouths and seven cold,
> and the tongs hung dumb on the wall;
> a man entered by another man's pen
> has no mouth to answer the call.
>
> Nine went down to the pulled benches
> and the oar keeps what the slate let go;
> a debt on salt-paper is a debt the rain forgives,
> but the bench keeps the figure and the throat keeps the knot.
>
> The pots went north on the ordinary cart
> with a figure made up in the room;
> no fire had touched them, no hand had set them,
> and the Quay received them, and the account stood.

I have copied the verses as they came and put no gloss on them. When I asked him who made the song he said the furnaces made it, and when I asked him how a furnace makes a song he said a furnace makes one every time it is lit and nobody writes it down until it is shut, which is the only answer the shed has ever given me.

# What the two hands did

- Against *the furnace-men shut the furnaces* the Council set *the several contracts were allowed to lapse*, so that the act of shutting has no actor in it.
- Against *nine men went to the pulled benches* it set *certain persons were assigned to the public service*, which is true at the Quay and false in the shed.
- Against *the paper said one thing and the pots another* it wrote *no discrepancy arises*, and underlined it twice.
- Against the song it wrote *no cost entered*, which is the neatest thing I have ever seen done to sixteen lines of a man's breath.
- The Court hand from Greybank struck *lead* out of the Y.H. 641 line and put *a vessel of trade* above it. I have left both standing. A vessel of trade is a thing that can stand on a shelf for ever without touching anybody's gum.

It takes the person out and leaves the pot standing, and a pot standing by itself has never burned a man. I have put nothing back under the Council's last line, because it is true. A clerk who can tell a lapsed contract from a cold furnace is a rarer man than a clerk who can write either one, and this one came up from the Quay with the figure for the pots already in his cuff.

# At the head and in the fold

The glass at the head is dust off the third mouth and has worked into the grain; I have never got it out and have stopped trying. The green bead in the second fold was pressed in by a girl come up from the furnaces who watched me write the Y.H. 626 line and said nothing, and the sweet smear she left has not dried in two years. The white along the gutter is salt out of the paper and not salt off the sea, which is a thing only a person who has kept salt-paper knows the difference of. The scorch on the third leaf is the shed lamp catching while I wrote and going out under my hand; the hand was mine and I have not entered the burn. The lead at the foot came off the rim of a dish I set down to hold the leaf in the draught that comes up the Cliffs. The fibre in the stitch is rope off a boat I was mending when I thought of the question and put the leaf down on it. The gull took the corner and not the word, for which I have no thanks to give him. The square of bare paper is where I scraped a word away with the point of a tally-knife and put no other in its place; it is the only square in this quire that I made myself. The grey along the outer fold is not mine and not the Council's. There is no Last Day in a tin and nothing here is swept up and burnt in the morning and called dealt with. I have had this leaf six years and have not swept it.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The paper rots and the figure stays,
> the furnace cools and the count runs on;
> a year allowed is a year entered,
> and a year entered is a year gone.
>
> The sand in the tin has no tongue,
> but it is gritty where a hand has been;
> ask the third mouth why it would not take,
> and ask the bench where nine have sat.

I asked the furnace-man, the clerk who made up the right-hand figure, and a woman who has swept the shed since before the rising, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written.

The leaves go back into the tin lined with sand. At the Opening of the Slates I copied them again and put the firing beside the figure again with my own hand, because a figure the damp has not taken is the one the Council will read first. Tomorrow the furnaces will be lit and the contracts written on paper made to rot, which is a virtue in this port, and I shall enter the pots that come off the quay, because that is what the glass-book is for. The nine who shut the furnaces have no column in it. They have the oar.
