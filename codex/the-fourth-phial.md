To Oda at the birth-slates in Low Kiln, who puts the back of her wrist to a stone before she puts her hand to a child and has never once said which was warm. From Renna at the licence-house in Greybank, where the corks are kept in a tin and the ash in a drawer that will not shut in the damp.

I write on the back of a licence spoiled by a wet thumb at the signing. The paper is made to be kept, which is the opposite of salt-paper and a great deal less honest. A licence has a line for the buyer's name and a line for the seller's hand, and I have written across the buyer's line, because I have never yet met a clerk who could read a blank the way it was meant. The factor here says a licence fulfilled is a licence closed. I say a licence with a name on it is a licence with a handle, and a thing with a handle can be carried into a room you did not mean to enter.

Do not rub the second fold. There is a cork-shaving in it that has been there since the first sealing of the tend and it will take your thumb.

I have sold eleven phials since the kiln month went out. A phial is sixty marks. You know what a mark is at the racks; I know what it is at the factor's counter, and the two are the same coin with different hands. It comes to us as a grey powder in a clay bottle no bigger than a thumb. The physician's order runs one phial for a voice that comes once in a dream, two for a voice that comes often, and three for a voice that has begun to ask for something. Three is the most the order allows. I have never seen a fourth written.

There is a woman in the upper street here who has never done a day's work that anybody paid for. She came in the second tend of Grain with a green edge at one corner of her mouth from something she had tasted and set down, and asked for three phials, and asked me not to put her name on the line. I told her the line is the licence and the licence is the Court's, and a name withheld is an unclosed account, and she said her husband had been banked these nine years and she had not been asked whether she consented to any of it. I sold her three. She came back on the ninth day and asked for a fourth.

The order allows three. I told her so. She said the order was written by men who had never been woken at the fourth hour by a voice asking for a fire. I have carried that sentence nine days and found no answer I would put on a slate, which is why it goes in a letter and into a pot-carter's basket instead of into a clerk's hand.

My right hand is the colour of wet ash from the cuff to the knuckle. They grey it at the College and call the greying a qualification; a seller who has not been greyed is a seller who has never handled the stuff, and a seller who has never handled it might as well be selling salt. When I stop a phial I use the left hand, because the right has no feeling in the cork and a cork set down by a hand that cannot feel is a cork that will not hold. There is a happiness in that, of a poor kind: the hand that cannot feel the cork is the hand that earns the keeping of it.

Now the asking. You have kept the birth-slates longer than I kept my first apprenticeship. When a child comes cold and is warmed and on the third day speaks in a voice that nobody in the house owns, what do you set down? I do not want the physician's word for it. I want the word you use when there is no physician in the room and the woman who carried the child is standing there with her hands in her apron, waiting for the slate to tell her which hand held the stone.

# The corking verse

*Sung low at the drawer, to the phial and not to the buyer. The third verse is the one the licence-house taught me; the fourth is mine, and I have never sung it where a clerk could hear it.*

> One for the voice that comes to the bed,
> two for the voice that comes to the chair,
> three for the voice that has learned to ask;
> the ash is by the ounce and the ask is unpaid.
>
> Grey at the cuff and grey at the thumb,
> the drawer shuts hard and the stopper holds;
> what was a man is a pinch in a glass,
> and the buyer sleeps, or the buyer does not.
>
> Four for the woman who comes on the ninth,
> and no fourth in the book and no line for it;
> the licence has a place where her name should stand,
> and I have written across it in charcoal.
>
> The house says the guest wants another house.
> The slate says the day and says nothing else.
> Ask the stone which hand warmed it. It will not tell you,
> and you will not ask a second time.

A clerk of the factor's house borrowed the leaf on the morning the bread-basket went back, being a man who cannot leave paper alone. He writes in the narrow upright hand, with the verbs taken out as though a verb were a thing a man could hide in a cuff. Against *the voice asks for a fire* he set *auditory disturbance, not entered*. Against *a fourth phial refused* he wrote *limit observed, account current*, which is true in the manner of a shut drawer. Against my charcoal across the buyer's line he set *name withheld, per licence*, and underlined it twice. Where I put the price in marks he wrote *twelve embers, current*, which no market in Sarn has ever paid, and against *what was a man is a pinch in a glass* he put *residue, current*. I have put the verbs back underneath where I could still read them. He takes them out because a thing always true has no need of verbs. I put them in because a woman at a drawer at the fourth hour wants to know whose hand stopped the cork.

# In the margin, in a second hand, small, pressed hard at the ends of words, the letters leaning

Renna. You have written across the line where her name should stand, which is the only sensible thing in the letter. Keep it. A name on a licence is a handle, and a woman woken at the fourth hour for nine years has had enough handles.

The physician's order is a device of the College. We do not count voices at the bench. We count the third day. A child warm on the third day is entered as warm, and a child that speaks is entered as a child, and the house keeps the child and the woman keeps the question, and the slate has no column for either.

For the voice: we call it the guest, the one that wants another house. We do not write it on the slate. There is a column for the day and a column for the house and no column for the guest, and a woman who has carried a child three nights wants to know which hand held the stone, which the slate will never tell her. I have set a stone under eleven children and had a twelfth go cold while I was warming him. I have given up finding the figure that makes the two agree, which is not the same as not minding.

As for the fourth: sell it. Not because it will stop the voice --- I do not know that and you do not know that --- but because a woman who comes back on the ninth day has already paid for the coming back, and there is no column for the coming back. Put it where you put the charcoal and let the clerk strike it out; he will, and then it will be the only true figure on the sheet.

> The slate keeps the day and the wool keeps the warm,
> the stone goes in at the dark and comes out at the light;
> the guest comes in where the column is not,
> and the house keeps the child and says it is right.
>
> Four for the coming back and none in the book;
> you write across the name and I write the date.
> A date is not an answer. It stays where you put it,
> which is more than can be said of a voice.

I have put my verse under yours and left yours standing above, because a letter with two hands on it is the only kind I have ever known anybody believe.

# At the head and in the gutter

The grey at the head is not the ash we sell. It is the powder off the stopper of the phial I set down unsealed on the second day, and it has gone into the grain so that the whole sheet smells faintly of the drawer. The cork-shaving in the second fold has been there since the first sealing and will not fall out; I have shaken the letter twice and it stays where the paper made room for it. The rain-line along the gutter came from the carter's basket, which leaked onto the bread and then onto the licence, and he apologised to the bread and not to me. The ring on the third leaf is small ale from the counter, where I was counting the widow's marks in the morning and did not notice the glass sitting there. The tallow at the outer fold is mine, off the lamp I keep turned down while I seal, so that the factor's man will not come in and ask what I am doing with the drawer shut. One word has gone out of the corking verse where the damp took it clean, and I have not written it in again; a verse with a word gone is a verse said slowly, and a verse said slowly is a verse nobody can enter.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The drawer keeps the ash and gives no sleep.
> The slate keeps the date and gives no guest.
> The licence keeps the blank and gives no name.
> Sixty marks for a pinch of a man; ask who was paid.

I asked a physician of the factor's house, a hearth-maid of Low Kiln and a clerk who could not leave paper alone, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written. The leaf goes back into the tin among the corks and the grey rags, and tomorrow a woman in the upper street will come for a fourth phial, and I will sell it to her and write across her name again, and neither of us will call it what it is, because a thing called by its right name is a thing the Court can enter.
