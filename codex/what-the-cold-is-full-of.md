I keep the post. There is a post and no gate, and the surveyors who set me here count men through it: those who go up and those who come down. When the party turns back I stay with the counting. Nine winters I have heard what the people beyond it say, and this winter I put it down, because a keeper who stays long enough becomes the only man who remembers a sentence that the country it came from has let go.

I write in Grathic letters because Waste Cant is not written. It is a handful of borrowed words and a way of letting the ends fall away, and what cannot be done with the mouth is not done with a hand. Where a sound has no word I have left a blank. Do not put a word into the blank. A man come up with lime from Stonemouth asked me what they say when there is nothing to say. I told him they say it to the cold. He went away believing he had learned the Waste, which is the usual end of such a conversation.

Say these to the ground or to the stone you are sitting against, never to the person who has just said them. Said to a person, one of these sentences becomes a question with an answer owed, and there is not one sentence here with an answer owed in it.

# Of the question, which is the whole of their information

1. “Warm?”

*Gloss — Ask a Wasteling what he fears and he asks whether you are warm. I took it for evasion the first winter. In the fourth I understood that it was all the information he had, and the only question worth asking a man who has come that far. It is not asked about the weather. A man may be asked it three times; the third asking is for the child who has not yet learned to be cold, who cannot answer and is asked all the same. I have set that down as I heard it and have not made a lesson of it.*

2. “Then you have not come far enough.”

*Gloss — Said to a man who showed a warm-stone in his coat and thought it counted as an answer. Out there a stone is a thing you carry and not a thing you have. The man who sold it to him at the Keep had told him the price and not the debt, which is the whole of that trade. They do not say the second sentence unkindly. They say it the way a man at the bottom of a stair says there are more steps.*

3. “It does not change. That is not weather.”

*Gloss — There is no weather beyond the Rim. Weather is a change, and the cold there does not change: it is an even, patient cold with nowhere to go and all the time in the world to get there. A man does not freeze in it so much as come gradually to agree with it. This is the hardest saying on the leaf for a man from the clay countries, because it leaves out the thing he would recognize.*

4. “Coming to agree.”

*Gloss — What they call it when a traveller stops being warm and starts being quiet. I have put Grathic words round a phrase with no grammar in it. A surveyor come up from the shaft told me travellers go back older and thinner and cannot say what they went for, and that the Court sends no collectors past the post. He said both in one breath and turned the slate face down.*

# Of what is carried, and what is let go

5. “What is in your hands.”

*Gloss — A Wasteling owns what he is still carrying and nothing else. This makes for a short inventory and a long silence. A man of Keld states his number before his name and has the number cut into his arm; a man out here holds out his hands, and if they are empty there is nothing to state and no shame in stating it. I have seen an officer ask after a man's holding and been given the palm of a hand to look at, which is an answer in that country and an insult in every other.*

6. “A stone. A name. A day. No figure.”

*Gloss — They will give any of the three and ask nothing back. The factor's man at Greybank could not leave this sentence alone; see below. A gift that expects nothing is the one thing a lowland household cannot enter, and so it calls it theft, or folly, or a virtue done badly. Out here there is no word for a debt and no use for one. They are not the people the Court's sealed rooms argue about. They are simply the spent.*

7. “I put my hand on yours and let go.”

*Gloss — The giving of a day's warmth. A man told me he offered his palm to one of them and felt nothing come or go, and yet came back a day short somewhere he never found. I have set it as it happened and not as a bargain: a bargain is a thing two men remember, and he remembered only the hand.*

8. “Keep it. I have gone out of it.”

*Gloss — Said when a name is given. A Wasteling takes one word for a name and changes it when it stops fitting; asked for a second name he will point at the cold. When he gives the word away he does not lend it, and the man who receives it cannot return it to him, because the giver has gone out of it. I asked a woman who had given me a word what I owed her for it and she asked me if I was warm. I did not ask twice.*

9. “—”

*Gloss — There was a sound before the last sentence that I could not write, and she would not make it again. It was not Grathic and not Keldish, and it asked for nothing, which may be why I wanted it down. The blank is where it was. Men put words into blanks because a blank troubles them. A page is not troubled. Only men are.*

# Of the dead, and the road they came by

10. “He faces the way he came.”

*Gloss — The dead are set upright facing the way they came and left uncovered. A woman who had stood beside one all morning said a man who had come that far had a right to be seen off toward the road he had travelled, and that covering him would be an argument with him, and they did not argue with the dead. In Grath they break a pot at the door to let the heat out. Both are kindnesses; I have never seen two so unlike and so complete.*

11. “Do not cover him.”

*Gloss — The same saying said to the living, at a man who has stopped walking and is still sitting. It is not a refusal to help. It is a refusal to ask a question of a vessel that has no answer left in it. A hearth-maid from Sill who came up with a survey party crossed two fingers over the face and gave me a date instead of an answer; I have left the date out, because she would not have it entered.*

12. “Nothing swept. No Last Day.”

*Gloss — There is no day on which the grey is gathered and burnt, because there is no morning after out here to burn it in. The Ash of a person whose warmth was never taken is gathered by Wastelings and given away. I put my hand on such a place and it left no mark on me or the leaf; that is the stain along this fold, which nobody has owned and which I have not swept.*

13. “The cold is not empty.”

*Gloss — It is full of what nobody kept. Every traveller brings back this sentence and every clerk tries to enter it; I have heard it oftenest and understood it least. A Vorr says of his dead that they were never owed and leaves it standing; out here there is no word for owing, so the sentence cannot even be refused. What comes in is not called kept or lost. It is called there.*

# Of the thing that comes close

14. “It stands. You go.”

*Gloss — The cold-eater comes toward any vessel with a head. It has no language and no appetite a visitor can recognise; it does not kill, it takes. Lowlanders say it is the Waste come to fetch what is owed. That is a lowland sentence. There is nothing owed out here, and they do not fight the shape because there is nothing among them worth owning. A man who stands his ground against it is not brave; he has not understood the arrangement. I have left the sentence where it was said and made no rule of it.*

15. “That is what it is for.”

*Gloss — Said of a cold-eater by a woman I asked why they did not drive it off. I took it for an answer about the creature and saw it was an answer about everything: the post, the counting, the sitting dead, the things given away. I have kept it because it is the only sentence in this country that sounds like a reason and refuses to be one.*

# What they say when a man goes on ahead

*Said to the ground, walking, by a man who would not sing it standing still. The place before the last line is left as it was left; nobody fills it.*

> Stone in the hand and the road in the face,
> a name let go where the name stood;
> one for the man who goes on ahead
>
> and one for the man who does not come back.
>
> No mark, no debt, no fire to carry,
> no door to break and nothing swept;
> the cold keeps what nobody kept
> and does not ask to be paid.

*Gloss — There is a space where a third line of the first part would stand. I asked a man what went in it and he said it was not a man but the place a man leaves, and that a place has no name and cannot be carried. The surveyor who took the leaf up the Rim Road put a notch in that space, because a notch is the nearest his trade comes to a place. I have left the notch standing and the space under it.*

# What the clerk did

A survey-clerk of the factor at Greybank borrowed the leaves on a market morning. He has a cuff gone soft from washing and ink in two fingers that will not come out. He writes upright and narrow and takes the verbs out as though a verb were a thing he could carry off in a cuff.

- At *warm?* he set *the thermal condition of the party is not entered*, which is a sentence true of every man alive and therefore of none.
- At *a stone, a name, a day, no figure* he wrote *no consideration recorded; three gifts void*. He was right by his own law and has entered the three things as nothing, which is what the country does and what the country does not call nothing.
- At *the cold is not empty* he wrote *the Waste is occupied*, which is a word for a room and not a word for a sink, and is the nearest I have ever known him come to agreeing with them.
- At the space in the verse he put a round nought and wrote *vacancy* beneath. A nought is a figure. The space was a man's place, and the clerk has made it smaller by writing in it.

I have put the words back underneath and left his above, the way a newer survey line lies over an older one on the rim-seam. He takes the doer out so that a thing always true can be carried into a counting room. I put the doer back because a keeper at a post wants to know which way a man was facing when he stopped.

# Cut into the edge, in a Keldish tally-man's hand

> Ask one of them what his measure is and he will hold out his hands. Our numbers are on the arm and theirs are in what they have not let go. Both can be read. Only one can be entered.

He cut it with his tally-knife and would not write it, the manner of a man who has been up to the stones. The cut has gone grey in the fold.

# At the head and in the gutter

The frost at the head came up out of the grain in the ninth winter and pressed the first two sayings together, so that *warm?* and *not far enough* stand closer here than they were said. I have not prised them apart; they are generally said in one breath. The coal smut at the fold is mine, off the sleeve I wipe the tally with. The flake of warm-stone kept its heat through one night on the stone floor and is cold now, which is the order of things here. The grey stone in the lower gutter was given by a woman who would not take a bowl for it; I stitched it in so the account and the thing it accounts for travel together. The white hair between the third and fourth leaves is not mine, and I have left it where it lay. The Ash along the outer fold leaves no stain and I have not swept it. There is no Last Day at this post, and I have not found one since I came up.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> A man is what he has not yet given.
> When he has given it, he is the road.

I asked a Wasteling who came through with no stone, a surveyor come down from the stones, and a clerk at Greybank, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written. The sheets go back into the oiled cloth and the cloth under the board at the post. Tomorrow the party will come up and count the men, and I shall count with them, and the people beyond the post will go past it without asking what the counting is for, which is the only honest answer any post has had.
