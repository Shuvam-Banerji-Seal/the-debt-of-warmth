I keep the scullery in the southern factor's house at Greybank, and the room where the girls sit before they are sent down. There are two factors' houses here and a man must know which one he is standing in before he says a word; this one is the south's, and the other writes in Ledgerhand and does not eat with us. The girl I write for was bought down from the north in the Thin Year and cannot yet tell which end of a courtesy is the hinge. She will learn. Everybody learns, generally by closing a door at the wrong moment.

These are not the sayings of the Ten Bowls. The great houses keep their sentences in thick gold and say them at the third hour, when an act of state is announced. These are said over the washing-up, in thin gold that will not outlast a wet week. Say them with the vowels open and the ends thrown into the fire, so that the listener supplies the last of the thought. Said to a bowl, to the oil, to the door, to the dust, they stay sayings. Said looking at a person, a courtesy becomes a contract, and a contract is a thing said to a man who is not in the room. He is in the room. That is the difficulty.

# Of courtesy, and the hinge on the inside

1. “A smile is a door. Do not ask what is behind it until you are through.”

*Gloss — We are a courteous people and it costs us nothing, which is why a stranger mistakes it for kindness. The door is held open a long time here and closed very quietly. A man from the clay country came to sell a year's barley and went home believing he had been welcomed; he had been received, which is a different room.*

2. “Say yes to everything. Ask which afterwards.”

*Gloss — There is no rude word for refusal in our speech, only a long polite way of not having heard the question. The factor says yes to a debtor and asks which afterwards; the yes is for the man and the which is for the slate. Both are honest. A northerner wants one answer to cover both and goes away with a headache and an obligation.*

3. “A courtesy said twice has a figure in it.”

*Gloss — The first time a thing is said kindly and is a kindness. The second time it is a price. The third time it is entered, generally by somebody in a clean cuff. I heard a man say “we should be honoured” to one household on three evenings running; by the third the honour had a date on it.*

# Of names, and the one inside the bowl

4. “Seven names by sixteen, and the house keeps one.”

*Gloss — A child of a great house may gather seven names before she is grown, and the household keeps whichever it prefers. The others are not lost; they are folded the way a good cloth is folded with a stain in it. At sixteen the young empty a bowl of warm wine and name a debt they intend to carry, and the applause is for the debt. The girl in my room came north with one name and will be given another before she scours properly; she asked me which is hers. I told her the bowl knows and she must not look into it.*

5. “A house-name is a fine door on a thin room.”

*Gloss — House-names are grand and frequently false, which is a courtesy extended to the name rather than to the house. A woman will tell you which of the Ten Bowls she descends from, and the number changes with the company, and nobody calls her a liar. A liar is a person who has been caught; no one here has been.*

6. “There is a name written inside the bowl. The bowl does not show it to the girl.”

*Gloss — When a household takes a girl into bought breath it gives her a name of the house and forbids the old one. In the great houses the old name is written inside a bowl which the girl may never turn up. I keep the bowls here and I do not turn them up. The place where the name stood on this leaf is rubbed thin with oil, because a name written where another name was is a name the Court can enter.*

# Of the bowl and what it washes

7. “What the bowl washes is not what the bowl held.”

*Gloss — After a feast the bowls come to my scullery and the water goes out into the yard. You can wash the wine out of a bowl. You cannot wash the debt out of it, or the name said over it, or the hour it was set down. I have kept this kitchen eleven years and that is the whole of what I know.*

8. “A full bowl set before a man is a kindness. A full bowl set before a debt is a receipt.”

*Gloss — The same bowl, the same wine, the same hand setting it down. The difference is in what the man thinks he is receiving, and the factor knows before he pours. Ask the bowl which it is and it will not tell you, which is why the factor does the pouring.*

9. “The third hour puts a name to an act. The second hour puts the wine on the table.”

*Gloss — In the Bowl of Sere a public bowl of wine is emptied at the third hour and an act of the Sun-Emperor is announced. Here we do it at the second so the factor can catch the post to Vhal, a smaller empire and a punctual one. The act is no less an act for the earlier hour. The wine is warmer.*

# Of the factor and the figure

| Said at the factor's door | What the house keeps underneath |
|---|---|
| May your bowl never go dry | Sit down. You will be counted, and the count begins now |
| We are honoured by your debt | The debt is current and may be called |
| Take the crown. It is nothing here | It is worth more here than where you got it |
| The wine is still warm | The act has already been decided |
| Come back when the ash is quiet | Your name has been entered and your room is ready |

10. “Buy next year's warmth in this year's wine. It costs less warm.”

*Gloss — The factor advances a debt before it is due and takes the household's next year against it. The household that drinks the wine has sold the warmth of a child not yet banked, and drinks it anyway and calls the advance a kindness. I have served six of these tables. The wine is identical. The kindness is not.*

11. “The factor's advance is a bowl with the handle on the wrong side. He carries it away.”

*Gloss — An advance is a bowl set down for you to hold and then taken up by the man who brought it. No one in the north signs the paper because no one in the north can read it, and the factor is polite about this, which costs him nothing and buys him the crop.*

12. “Take the crown. It will not change here, and that is why we take it.”

*Gloss — Gold crowns come north and will not change at Greybank or at the Cross in Sill, so a northern household must spend them with us or not at all. The eel-women of the Fen say take the eels and leave the crown, which is the same fact from the other side of the counter. Both sayings are true. Only one is said by a person holding the crown.*

# Of women, and the door in the room

13. “The concubine sits where the light can find her.”

*Gloss — Returned women are kept openly and counted as part of a household's standing, the way a row of bowls or a good fire is counted. They are not hidden and not ashamed, and a visitor is expected to admire them. A girl from my room who thinks this is freedom will last about a tend in the south; I tell her so and she does not believe me.*

14. “At the Line the mud pays in eels. We leave the mud where it is.”

*Gloss — Our northern advance stopped at the Peat Line in Y.H. 634 and the Fen pay tribute in eels, which come north in barrels and go south in other barrels and are never once called a tax. We trade with the Fen and do not move there.*

15. “A Grath man will sell you his winter and curse you for buying it.”

*Gloss — Grath hates our factors and sells to them anyway, and the cursing is in the price. A man from Low Kiln brought pots down the Salt Road and complained the whole way about southern buyers, took our crowns, and spent them before the frost. I never knew whether he was angry with us or with the cold.*

16. “The Court weighs the ash and sends a figure. We pay the figure with grain.”

*Gloss — Our dead are burnt in a public bowl with wine and music, the warmest send-off in the world. The Court taxes the ash and the family buys it back as quiet ash, and one funeral can ruin a house that has fed a province. The grain goes north and the ash comes home, and both are entered in the same clean hand.*

# The washing-song

*Sung at the scullery after a feast, kneeling, with one hand in the water and the other on the rim. The line where the name would stand is not sung; the girls supply nothing and the bowl supplies nothing. Nobody sings it on the ebb.*

> Scour the bowl and scour the rim,
> the wine goes down and the debt stays in;
> three go round and the three go dry,
> and the water goes out where the eels go in.
>
> Oil on the hand and dust in the hair,
> the factor counts what the kitchen bore;
> a name goes in where the glaze is thin
> and the girl must not turn the bowl again.
>
> Wash it in rain and wash it in wine,
> the water goes back where the water came;
> what the bowl keeps is not what it held ---
>
> and the place where the name stood is left as it stands.

*Gloss — The unsung line is not a word the singer forgot. The girls ask what it is and are told to ask the bowl, which is the answer their mothers were given. A Court clerk put a round nought in the space and wrote vacancy beneath it. A nought is a figure. The place was a name.*

# What the borrowed hand did

A clerk of the Court's factor borrowed the leaves on a market morning, being a man who cannot leave paper alone. He writes narrow and upright and takes the verbs out as though a verb were a thing a man could carry off in a cuff.

- At *say yes to everything*, he wrote *assent recorded; qualification deferred*, which leaves both the courtesy and the hinge out of it.
- At *a courtesy said twice has a figure in it*, he set *repeated assurance, current*, and underlined current twice.
- At *buy next year's warmth in this year's wine*, he wrote *advance against future deduction*, which is true in his language and of no use to a woman who has served the wine.
- At the space in the song he put a round nought and wrote *vacancy*, making the place smaller than the name.

I have left his hand above mine everywhere. He takes the doers out because a thing always true has no need of anybody doing it. I put them back because a girl at my scullery wants to know whose hand poured, and there is always a hand.

# In the margin, in charcoal, in the girl's hand

> I have been here nine days. I know which end of the courtesy is the hinge now. I have not turned the bowl up.

Below, in mine: she told it to the bowl and would not say it twice. I have not asked her what she said the first time. A person given a name in a room where she could not answer is not a person who needs a question from me.

# At the head and in the gutter

The yellow at the head is dust off the burning winds, ground in when the leaves lay a tend on the scullery sill; no oil takes it out, which is one of the few honest things about the south. The thumb at the fold is mine, off the scourer's cloth. The wine-ring is the third hour, or what passes for it here. The husk in the gutter came off a loaf sent north and returned uneaten. The fish-scale came in with the eels from the Line; the girls say it is a coin from a country under the water and I let them say it. The green shard is a bowl bought cheap from a potter in Sill, cracked in the first frost and not mended. The place where the old name stood is rubbed thin with oil. The grey in the gutter came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying; I have not swept it. There is no Last Day in this house and nothing gets burnt in the morning except the slates.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The bowl is washed. The debt is not.
> The name goes in where no one looks.
> We are the only people who thank a vessel for being emptied.

I asked a woman who keeps a bowl-house, a clerk of the Court's factor, and a girl who has been here nine days, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written. The leaves go back into the oiled wrap behind the wine-jar. Tomorrow the factor will say yes to a man from the clay country and ask which afterwards, and the girl will be told to scour the bowl and not turn it up, which is the whole of the teaching here.
