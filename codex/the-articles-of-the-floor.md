I keep the irons and the roll of hands at the Floor, in the stone beneath the Keep where the shaft ends and the rock has been cut level for bouts. A boy comes up from the Descent with a number new in his arm and a warm-stone in his palm, and the Order takes him if he came up. The mine-reeve checks his figure in red before he is let past the post, and there is no second asking. A Court survey-clerk comes up with a soft cuff, writes that instruction is supplied and no wage arises, and goes away believing he has entered a trade. I set the rules down as they stand. Where a hand has corrected me I have left both readings.

These are not new articles. They were gathered in the year the pass was shut, when the Keep got a border and the Order got boys from both sides of it. A rule with no clamp-bite on it is a rule nobody has had to hold. These have been held.

# The articles as they stand at the Floor

## Of the taking

1. The Order takes the boy, not his house. A house may bring him to the mouth and feed him and bury him if he does not come up; from the morning he holds a clamp with the bite toward him he answers to the Floor. The family is paid in iron for the one who does not come back, which is the only price in Keld paid without a bargain.
2. He is taken the morning after his Descent, being thirteen, and not before he has come up with the number he went down with or a number the mountain gave him. A boy who comes back thin has the old numbers cut out and new ones put in. Nobody asks him which count is his; the mine-reeve enters the later and leaves the earlier standing in the skin.
3. He states what he holds before he says who he is. This is done at the Warden's hall, at the gate and here, as a courtesy, so that no boy can say he was not told. A lowlander who gives his name first is sent back to the foot of the post to begin again. I am not shaming him. A name is a thing a man can change at the next well; the number goes down with him.
4. The Order keeps the method of the first way within Keldish hands. A lowlander may be taught it for iron, and often is. He is never taught it for nothing. A thing given free is entered against the giver, and there is no house in Keld that will carry that entry.
5. He is kept, not hired. He eats from the Floor's dish, sleeps in the loft above the clamp-rack, and what he makes goes to the Floor and comes back as bread and a place by the brazier, neither entered. A master who tells a boy he is paid tells a true thing about a figure and a false thing about a living.

> *In the narrow upright hand of a Court survey-clerk, against rule 5:* Apprentice retained at the Floor. No wage arising. *Underneath, in mine:* I had written *kept*. He strikes a mouth wherever he finds one. I have put the boy back under the figure, because a figure eats no bread and sleeps in no loft. Both stand.

## Of the number

6. The number is part of the name. The name is not part of the number. Ask a Keldishman who he is and he will give the figure first and the word after, the way a man lifts a stone before he looks under it.
7. The number stands. Do not say it twice. To say it twice is to ask the mountain to make it other than it was, and the mountain keeps no second figure.
8. At the end of the Floor there is a cup that nobody takes, set there for the man whose number was changed twice. It is not a memorial and it is never filled. A boy who asks whose cup it is is told to count the clamps. There are ten, and the tenth has never been taken off its nail.

## Of the way and the two bites

9. Cut across a surface way in the forearm and hold it open while it scars. This is the first way. It aches before the thaw and after, and a man answers to whichever number the room has given him. An old cutter told me a way is a door cut in the wall of a man, and that a door once cut is not a door that can be shut. I have set that down in his words, which are fewer than mine.

> *The lamp-scorch took the last word of this rule and left the sense, which is more than the Court has ever taken from a man. I have not written the word back.*

10. A fortnight is given to the arm after the cutting. It is a useless arm and the boy is not sent to the rack with it. A master who sets him there to hurry the scar is buying a hand with a fortnight and paying for it with the whole life.
11. Tap before you cut. A sound block answers full under the knuckle; one with a flaw gives back a small dry sound. The same goes for an arm. A boy who cuts a way across a flaw he did not tap is sent to the wall to learn the note, and the wall does not answer him.
12. One bite holds the vessel whole. One bite takes the hand off a man. It is the same bite. Cold iron takes and does not give back; clamp a way past the turning of the watch and the arm below goes the colour of old rope and does not come back. The iron has not asked anybody's opinion.
13. Trial of hot iron is put to the palm when a man is to be trusted with the racks. Put hot iron to the palm and ask a man if he owes; the honest answer leaves the hand dead. There have been a great many honest men at this Floor with dead hands. I have set the number down in the tally below and have not set what it cost, because the tally has no column for a hand.

> *Struck hard in charcoal against rule 13, in a cutter's hand that did not sign:* Mine is dead and I answered true. They entered the honesty and not the hand. *In mine, below:* I have left his line struck above mine and mine standing under, so a boy can see what was said and what was done to it. The charcoal has come off on the fold and I have not brushed it away.

## The table of years

*Kept by the boy who sweeps the clamp-room, in a hand that does not change between one line and the next.*

| Year | At the Floor | What is taken |
|---|---|---|
| Thirteen | The Descent; the number cut; a warm-stone given after the knife | The habit of giving a name before a figure |
| Fourteen | Carry and sweep the irons; tap blocks and learn the full note | The birth-name, said once at the taking and not used at the Floor |
| Fifteen | The first way; the fortnight; the arm kept from the rack | The belief that a thing cut can be uncut |
| Sixteen | A first bout; the clamp held and loosed at the word | The last of a boy's account with any house |

14. Sixteen is the common end of a keeping and not a short one. Eleven months is the common end of a bought life and is not a keeping at all. A boy who has been through the years is not paid out and sent down the road; he stays where the Floor needs him, at the rack or on a pass or in a room where a man must state a figure before a name. The Floor does not choose which. The arm does, and the Warden after it.

## The tally of hands

*Kept by the under-master. The figure is the mine-reeve's and is set first. What stands beneath it is mine and is not a figure.*

| Condition | The reeve's figure | What stands beneath |
|---|---|---|
| Boy taken the morning after the Descent | one apprentice, current, no wage arising | A boy who eats from the dish |
| The first way | permanent mark; no deduction arises | A door cut in the wall of a man |
| Hot iron trial | honesty entered | A hand that cannot lift a cup |
| Clamp left past the watch | one hand, current; measure not affected | The hand kept; the boy sent up the rope |
| A boy who does not come up | paid in iron | Iron, and the number called once |
| A block that answers dry | not cut; not entered | The flaw the knuckle found |

15. The reeve enters a hand that cannot close as one hand, current, measure not affected. It is a true figure and a true figure about the wrong thing. I have set what it cost beneath it, where a clerk can see it and choose not to read it.

> *In the narrow upright hand of the Court clerk, against that row:* One hand, current; measure not affected. *In mine, underneath:* It is a hand. I did not learn it from a page, which is the only reason I can still say it. Both stand.

## Of the sweating and the grub

16. The mine sweats before it gives. In the Ninth Gallery the rock sweats where no seam was cut, the air turns sweetish and hot, and the smell of hot lead comes up before anything shows. That smell is the whole warning a man gets. No boy is sent down to a sweating to learn it; a boy who has smelt it once does not want a second lesson, and a boy who has not will never believe it matters.
17. The reeve stops a shift for one grub. This is not kindness. He counts every life that goes down and every ember of iron that comes up, and those two figures have never agreed and were never meant to. A stopped shift is a figure that does not balance. He stops it anyway.
18. Warm-stone is sold and is never lent. It holds heat longer than anything else in Sarn, and a man who goes up without a stone borrows from the next man who has one, or turns back; there is no lending here. The shaving swept from the cutting floor is sold by the half-mark and not given away. A thing that can be lent has a place in somebody's account. A thing sold is paid for, which is cleaner.
19. Read the frost-feather at the mouth before the sun takes it. It grows where the Warm has lately gone out of the stone and is gone by noon. A feather on the lip means a man is sent to find where it went, and the finding is still owed when the feather is gone.

## Of the tin and the neighbours

20. There is a tin lined with warm-stone in the survey room off the Floor. It is kept shut. No boy in his years at the Floor is shown it and no boy is told what is in it; he learns that there is a thing not to ask after, which is the only instruction in this house given without a demonstration.
21. The Vorr sit at the stones and are not asked to translate. The Deep Hand is copied and not read. When they come down with a figure they say the numbers are correct, which is not an answer to the question put. These are the Floor's neighbours, set down here so that a boy may know which questions to leave alone.

# The floor-song, pasted inside the cover

*Sung at the first opening of a way, standing, with the arm across the block and the clamp held in the teeth. Never sung after a bout and never while the mine-reeve's clerk is at the tally. The last two lines belong to the boys; the masters sing them wrong on purpose, and no boy has ever been told why.*

> Iron on the block and iron at the door,
> state what you hold and then say who you were;
> cut it across and hold it while it scars,
> a way is a door and the arm is the wall.
>
> One bite to hold and one to take,
> the hand knows which the mouth will mistake;
> four to the cut and a slack for the cup,
> and the tenth clamp hangs where no one takes it down.
>
> Do not ask the stone what it wants of you,
> the stone has no want and the lead smells true;
> hold till the watch turns, then let the iron go ---
> the arm below is the colour of rope.
>
> Iron on the block and iron at the door,
> one for the boy and one for the reeve's score;
> the honest hand leaves the cup where it stood,
> and the number stands where the number stood.

The press of a lamp-cloth across the second verse came from the song being held up to the lamp at the change of shift. I have flattened it; the words hold.

# What the borrowed hands did

A Court survey-clerk borrowed the leaf at Greybank with ink in two fingers of a soft cuff, and took the verbs out as though a verb were a thing a man could smuggle up the Rim Road in a sleeve.

- Against *the Order takes the boy, not his house* he set *Instruction is supplied. No wage arising.* I have put the boy back underneath, because a figure eats no bread and sleeps in no loft.
- Against *a way once cut stays cut* he set *the condition is permanent.* He has made a door into a state and left the man who cut it out of the sentence. I have set mine under his.

The other hand is charcoal, a cutter of the Floor who would not sign. Against rule 13 he struck *leaves the hand dead* and wrote above, *Mine is dead and I answered true. They entered the honesty and not the hand.* I have left his line struck above mine and mine standing under. A rule two men have argued over is a rule that has been held.

# At the head and in the gutter

The smut at the head is coal off the sleeve I wipe the rack with; it is in the grain and no wet sleeve takes it out. The iron-red thumb is the reeve's point, checking a boy who gave a figure that would not hold; he did not look at me and I did not look at the boy. The corner has had a clamp-bite taken out of it by a man showing his brother how the iron closes; both were honest afterwards and one cannot lift a cup. The shaving in the fold is warm-stone swept from the cutting floor, kept and not lent; it is warm on the coldest morning and I have not sold it. The bead is lead out of the Ninth Gallery, where the rock sweats and the smell gives what warning it gives; a man laid it down and did not take it up. The feather pressed flat was on the mouth stone after a warm night and was gone by the time I found the leaf to put it in. The grey along the outer fold is not mine and not the reeve's, and I have not swept it. There is no Last Day beneath this Keep and nothing here is burnt in the morning and called dealt with.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The number goes down and the name goes home.
> The clamp keeps the hand, and the Floor keeps the count.
> The house that sent the boy is paid in iron and keeps nothing.

I asked a rack-master, a Court clerk come up from Greybank, and a woman who counts at the mouth, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written. The leaf goes back on its spike and the coal takes another word by the next shift. Tomorrow a boy will come up from the Descent with a number new in his arm, and I shall put a clamp in his hand with the bite toward him, state the count before his name, and say nothing about the cup at the end of the Floor.
