title: The Want in the Count, Being the Hopping Game of the Drying Racks in Low Kiln
kind: A children's counting game, set out with its words, the scored ground, the forfeits, and the two hands that disagree over the empty place
provenance: Set down by an old woman of the drying racks in the Clay Yard, Low Kiln, Sill, in Ilmen of Y.H. 813, having no slate-name and signing with a thumb pressed into slip; written in charcoal on the back of a spoiled drying tally and kept folded in the band of a cast-off apron. It survives with the score of a knuckle in slate dust, a tallow thumb, a drop of barley grease, a green bead of lead glaze from a girl who leaned over to see the writing, a grey print of Ash, and one correction in the narrow hand of a clerk borrowed from Slate Row. The game is held to be older than the bell at the Cross.

I have no slate-name. The rack has one, and the kiln has one, and a pot has a number chalked on its foot where the thrower puts her thumb, but I have never been entered in anything that I can read. So I have set my thumb in slip at the foot of this leaf, which is how we sign a pot in this yard, and if a man come down from Slate Row wants a name for the hand, let him write *the racks* and be done.

I write it because the little ones have begun to leave the want out. They hop the eight and stop, and say the count is finished, and stand looking at the bare clay where the ninth score should be, waiting for somebody to tell them it is finished. It is not finished. A count that closes is a Court count. I have taught them the words before they had the legs, and now I set the words down, because a thing carried only in the legs goes out with the legs.

# The words

*Said while hopping. The row says the refrain; the hopper says the lines. No one says the want alone.*

One for the stone that was warmed at the fire,
two for the pot that answers full,
three for the grey that turns the corner,
four for the loaves that go on the levy.
Four and no more before the want.

Five is the bell that is longer than a church bell,
six is the step that is not at the landing,
seven the eels in the bridge's shade,
eight the pots on the rack, bone-pale.
Four and four, and a place where the fifth should be.

> *Refrain, all together, stamping:* Hop it, hop it, call it out — four and four and a want. Put your foot where the foot should be and the whole count comes to nought. Hop the want and come back warm. Come back as you can.

If you set down in the want you are gone wrong,
a flat note under the heel;
go stand in the grey where it turns the corner
and do not answer when they say your name.
Bide till the bell goes. Then come back warm.
Come back as you can.

The big ones carry nineteen out and nineteen back
from the pit to the rack and again;
we carry eight and the place where a step should be,
which is as much as our legs can hold.
When your legs are long enough
the want becomes a step
and you go on.

One in twelve, as the notice says,
four and four as the kiln breathes,
nineteen as the women tread —
three counts and never one agreement.
The Court has a figure for every place.
We have a place with no figure in it.
That is the one we keep.

*The calling at the end, which is not a verse but the way they end:*

> Who is not in the row? — One that was not in the room.
> What do we call her? — Kept.
> What do we say to her? — Come back warm. Come back as you can.

# The ground, and how it is scored

Eight places are scored in the trodden ash beside the racks, a hand's breadth apart, with the end of a broken pot or a bit of slate. The ninth is not scored. On the Last Day the ash is not swept, and there is no need to score anything, because the grey shows the places of its own accord; after it is burned the game is played on the bare clay and the want is marked with a chip of pot set on its edge, so that it can be moved aside and put back.

Where there is a cord, two of the bigger ones turn it and the hopper runs in on the first line. At the word *want* the cord is lifted over her head and comes down empty; she must call the word while no rope is under her foot, and come in again on the next line. Where there is no cord — and there is seldom a cord — the children stand in the eight places and say their lines each from her own ground, and the hopper goes from mouth to mouth. The want is then said by nobody and by the whole row at once, which is the only way it can be said.

They play it behind the racks on Account-day, when the square is full and the mothers have sent them out of it. They play it in the dark on the Long Night without any scores at all, going by memory, which is the hardest way, because a want you cannot see is a want you are likely to stand in. No one plays it on the Last Day after the bread is baked. I do not know why, and I have not asked the little ones, and they could not tell me if I did.

# The forfeits

To set a foot down in the want is to be *gone wrong*, which is what the yard calls a pot cracked from lip to foot. The hopper says nothing; the row says *flat*; she goes and stands in the grey against the skirting board, where it turns the corner without a break, and she does not answer if one of the others says her name. This is the whole of the punishment and the reason for it, which the children know as well as I do: a place with no name in it cannot hear itself called.

If she stays there till the bell goes she is fetched in and made warm, and the game begins again. If the game ends before she is fetched, she is *kept* — she stands in the middle while the others go in for bread, and is not warmed and is not called. I have put that in the words because it is in the game. I did not put it there for a reason.

I have seen a child stand in the want on purpose, to be gone wrong, because the house she goes home to is colder than the skirting. I have not struck that out either.

There is a second game played at the edge of this one, with a nail of cold iron out of the smithy sweepings and a chip of slate. One of them is the collector, and goes among the ones standing in the grey, sets the nail to a wrist and asks the figure. The one in the grey gives a number — six, or eleven, or none at all — and the collector writes it on the chip with chalk and says the account stands. I have told them not to set cold iron against a living wrist. They say it is only a game, the way their mothers say the cough is the pits, and I have let it be.

# What the narrow hand did

A clerk borrowed the leaf one account morning and struck out the word *want* wherever it stood, setting a round nought above it in the Court's small upright hand. He wrote *vacancy* beside the ninth place. He asked me what the count was for, and I told him it was for keeping the little ones from standing where they should not, and he wrote that down too. Then he asked how many stood in the row, and I told him eight and a want, and he wrote eight.

I have put the words back under his noughts and left his noughts standing. A nought is a number, and a want is a place; he has put a number where a place should be, which is what the Court does with everything it comes near. The children prefer my reading. When they come a second time they jump the nought as well, which I did not teach them and will not discourage.

He also numbered the five days outside the months as four, having left one out without appearing to notice, and wrote a figure against Kindling where there should be no figure at all. I have not corrected that one. It is not my leaf to correct a man's counting on, and besides, the five are not ours to give away.

# In the margins

The green bead at the top right is not slip. A girl with sweetness at her mouth leaned over on a morning when the glaze room had been open and the whole terrace smelled sweet enough to make a body careless; she touched the leaf and said nothing, and went back to the rack. I have not washed it, and I would not if I knew how.

The tallow thumb is the little one's who says the refrain loudest; she holds the leaf down with her whole hand when the wind comes up the yard, and does not know she marks it. The grey print at the foot came off my own thumb when I pressed it into the slip to sign, and is the only mark on the leaf that is mine. The barley grease is from a crust laid on the leaf while a child was being told to eat and would not; I left it, because a crust put down and not eaten is a thing this yard knows how to read.

In a hand I do not know, written very small between the fifth and sixth lines, there is one sentence, unfinished:

> Senn put her foot in the want and we left her till the bell went and she

The hand stops there. It is a child's hand; the letters lean and the last word is written smaller than the rest. I have asked which Senn it was, and been told three different names, and have stopped asking. It may be that no child of that name ever stood in this yard. I have left the space as it is, because it is the right length for a want.

# At the foot

The leaf goes back in the apron band after the little ones have gone in. It is soft now at the fold and the charcoal has come off on my fingers, the way charcoal will. I have not copied it onto a slate and will not. A slate is a thing that gets read aloud at a market by a man in a clean voice, and this is not a thing to be read aloud.

They are hopping it behind the racks now as I write, and getting the refrain wrong in the same place every time: they say *come back as you can* too fast, so that it sounds like *come back as you was*. I have not corrected them. There is a difference, and they will learn it soon enough without me.
