I sing this on the boards at Reedholt, to a child three nights old, in the hut my mother's mother set on a hummock so the water could take the ground back without taking the house. Tomorrow is the Hearth Drawing, and a collector comes up the marsh road with a tube, a slate and a cuff folded back twice. He may draw from the dying and the newly born on the same day, which the Court calls thorough and we call the road being short. Before he reaches the boards I must have said the child's name three times into the ground under my feet. After that he may have what he comes for. A number is a hole in the wall; a name is a door. I mean to leave him the wall and keep the door.

A lullaby here is not a comfort. It is a piece of work, like layering a peat-stack, and it is done before daylight because a man with a dry cuff will come into a wet room. Such a man stood at the peat-dry in Ilmen and asked what the women sang to their children before a drawing, and wrote the answer while I was still saying it, with the doing taken out and a figure standing where a place should stand. I have left his hand where he put it and put mine underneath, the way the boards keep both the foot that went over them and the water that came up through them.

Fen Cant is not written. It is said through the nose, slow, with the last word dropped into the mud and not finished; what must be kept is cut in a stick or tied in a knot. I learned my letters off an eel-trader who could tally but could not read what he wrote. The brown at the head is peat, not Ash; there is Ash enough in this marsh without bringing it in on my thumb. I held the tally down in a wind off the black peat and did not know I had marked it. Peat goes into the grain and no washing takes it out, which is why we sign with it here.

# The manner of it

- Said through the nose, slow, to the child and to the boards. The last word of every verse is let fall into the mud under the boards and is not finished. Said up at the sky it is a prayer, and there is no prayer in this marsh that has not been entered against somebody. Said across at a man it is an argument, and we have enough arguments without carrying one up the marsh road.
- The name is said three times: once to the ear, once to the ground under the boards, and once for the part of the child that cannot yet hear and will be the last to forget. If there is a fourth saying, it belongs to the man with the slate and not to this house. We have never made a fourth.
- Where a word will not come into Grathic I have tied a knot of grey wool in the margin and written nothing. A knot keeps and a word on paper travels. A factor's clerk entered our knots as *nil*, because a knot is not a figure. He was right. That is why we keep them.
- Do not sing it standing on dry ground. Every dry place among us is a loan from the water and the water calls it in when it pleases. A song said on borrowed ground goes back with the loan, and the singer is left with a mouth full of nothing.
- One verse for the board, one for the name, one for the peat, one for the cold that comes with the iron, and one for what the mud keeps. There is a sixth place and it is not scored and not stepped. Where the sixth should stand I have laid a thread of grey wool in the fold. The clerk has written against it, and his word is a figure and mine is a place.
- Stop before dawn on the Drawing. A song said while a number is being read aloud becomes part of the number, and after that no mother in Reedholt can get it back.

# The song

*Said low, sitting, with the hand on the board and not on the child. Through the nose. The last word of each part goes down into the mud.*

The refrain, as the boards keep it after every part:

> *Hush. The board bears and the water lends.*
> *Say it three times to the ground where the name descends.*
> *What will not write we tie and leave tied.*
> *The dark keeps warm what the day has denied.*

## I. Of the board

> You were laid on a board before you were laid on a bed;
> the board is a loan and the water calls it when it will.
> We do not ask it to carry us, only to keep
> the shape of a foot for the length of one step.
> Hush. This board has borne worse than you and said nothing.

> *Hush. The board bears and the water lends.*
> *Say it three times to the ground where the name descends.*
> *What will not write we tie and leave tied.*
> *The dark keeps warm what the day has denied.*

## II. Of the name

> I gave you the word before the man with the slate came up the road.
> Three times into the ground under my feet I put it down:
> once to your ear, once to the mud, once to the you
> that cannot hear yet and will be the last to forget.
> If he comes before the third he will give you a figure.
> Keep the word. A figure is a hole and a name is a door.

> *Hush. The board bears and the water lends.*
> *Say it three times to the ground where the name descends.*
> *What will not write we tie and leave tied.*
> *The dark keeps warm what the day has denied.*

## III. Of the peat

> The dark under the black peat is warmer than this room;
> the Marsh Mother holds it and hands it back
> to whoever asks, which here means whoever digs.
> She has no keys, keeps no slate, and asks no figure;
> no man in a clean coat opens her for a fee.
> A coal out of her was paid for by somebody's back.
> Hush. Keep your hand on the pot and not on the flame.

> *Hush. The board bears and the water lends.*
> *Say it three times to the ground where the name descends.*
> *What will not write we tie and leave tied.*
> *The dark keeps warm what the day has denied.*

## IV. Of the cold iron

> There is a pale round place on a man's arm
> that shows through the winter and was not there in Ember.
> The iron takes and the mud takes;
> one of them writes it down and one of them does not.
> When the cuff comes to the hut at dawn, let it find the number.
> Let it find the number and go away with it.
> A word that goes up the marsh road does not come back the same.

> *Hush. The board bears and the water lends.*
> *Say it three times to the ground where the name descends.*
> *What will not write we tie and leave tied.*
> *The dark keeps warm what the day has denied.*

## V. Of what the mud keeps

> What goes into the mud is kept; we do not fetch it back.
> A body goes out on a raft into the reedbed
> and the water keeps the account and sends no figure.
> You are three nights old and already owe nothing,
> which is the only thing in this house that owes nothing.
> Lie still. The debt will find you soon enough without our help.

> *Hush. The board bears and the water lends.*
> *Say it three times to the ground where the name descends.*
> *What will not write we tie and leave tied.*
> *The dark keeps warm what the day has denied.*

## VI. The place that is not a verse

*Here a thread of grey wool lies in the fold where the sixth part should stand. There are no words to the sixth place. The hand goes to the thread and not to the child, and the refrain goes round once more with the wool under the thumb.*

> *In the narrow upright hand, against the thread:* nil; vacancy; no entry lies against an unentered place.
> *
> *Underneath, in mine:* A nought is a figure and can be carried up the road in a cuff. The thread is a place and stays where it was tied. I have not moved it and will not.

The child was asleep before I came to the sixth place. That is how I know the sixth is not for him but for the woman who sings, who has stood in this hut through four drawings and kept a word behind every time, and has never once been asked what she kept. I have laid the thread there for her.

# What the factor's clerk did

He borrowed the leaf on the morning of the drawing, with a cuff gone soft from washing, and gave it back dry in one corner and damnable everywhere else. He writes narrow and upright and takes the verbs out as though a verb were a thing a man could smuggle past a weigh in the seam of a cuff. I have left his hand standing above and put mine back underneath.

- At *the board is a loan and the water calls it when it will* he wrote *tenure conditional; ground tenure not established*. True to a man who has never stood on a board, and false to the child who will learn to walk on one. Mine stands below his.
- At *three times into the ground under my feet I put it down* he wrote *designation declared; three readings witnessed*. There was no witness but the mud and the child, and the child could not speak, and the mud has never given evidence to a Court.
- At *the Marsh Mother holds it and hands it back* he wrote *peat retains measure; draw requires officer*. Right about the peat, and he has left out the hands, which is the whole art of that sentence.
- At *the cuff comes to the hut at dawn* he wrote *collection effected; no gift arises*. He was there. He knows what was taken and he knows it was not given, and he has written the one and called it both.
- Where the thread lies he put a round nought and wrote *vacancy; no claim lies*. I have left it. A knot is not a figure, which is what makes it worth tying.

# What the woman of the peat-dry put underneath

The charcoal belongs to a woman who has caught children on these boards for twenty-one years and laid out the drowned of the drained cuts. She presses hard at the ends of words and will not read them over. I have copied them where she set them.

> At *once to the ear, once to the mud*: I have said a name into the ground under these boards for every child this hut has held, and only two of them came up speaking. I have never known whether the mud kept the other two or whether they kept themselves. Do not ask me which is the kinder answer.

> At *keep the word*: In the year of the Long Cold a man came before the third saying and gave a number to a child of mine. The child answered to both from that day, and turned its face when the clean voice used the one that was not ours. A number is not a hole in the wall to a child. It is a second mouth in the room.

# At the head and in the gutter

The peat at the head came up out of the paper itself and is the only thing on this leaf that entered without a hand. The tideline has taken two words clean off the second leaf and I have not written them in again, because a lullaby with two words gone is a lullaby said slowly, which is how all of ours are said. The sedge across the third came off the bundle I set down when the child took its hand from the wool. The pale round place in the margin is where the collector laid his cuff while I found the fifth verse; it has gone the colour of the floor and I have not swept it. The scratch came off a reed-wolf that put its tooth into the dry while I reached for the tin, then went off without a word, which is more than the men from Greybank manage. The ring at the foot is small ale, set down at the first part and taken up at the last, by which time it had gone the colour of the boards it stood on. There is no Last Day kept at a peat-dry and nothing here is gathered and bottled and called dealt with. The thread lies in the fold and I have not lifted it.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> A man is a board laid across water. The water does not owe the board anything.
> What the mud keeps it keeps without a figure, and it will not give the figure back.

I asked the woman who catches children, an eel-trader come up from Sill, and a man who has been drawn twice and answers to both numbers, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written.

The leaf goes back into the tin and the tin under the boards. In the morning the collector will come up the marsh road, put his hand to the slate before his eyes go to the child, and go away believing he keeps the world alight by carrying off what is already going. I shall say the word three times and stop before dawn. The boards will keep the shape of where I sat, the thread the place where no verse stands, and the water will go on calling in the dry ground in its own time.
