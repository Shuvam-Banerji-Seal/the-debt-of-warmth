I keep the tally at the gate of a house of the Ten Bowls. The counting room is barred and no woman of this house goes through it; the figures in there are read out by a man in a low patient voice, and I write what comes out on the backs of tallies spoiled at the third hour. Charcoal will not take on prepared paper, and gold is for what a house means to keep. A market-current is for what a house means to buy, and is written on the side meant to be destroyed.

Say the figures to the hand and not to the person standing. Said looking at a person, a price in this city becomes an invitation to choose, and a man who chooses here has already agreed to both answers. I set the figure down before I look up, which is the order of the room.

Twelve copper bits to the silver mark; twenty marks to the gold crown, not changeable beyond the Peat Line, so a northern trader who carries one south has paid for his journey before he has spent it. The Court books in embers and calls an ember five marks; I keep no column for it. One in twelve is printed on the notice at Greybank and is taken on grain at home, before the sack is tied, which is why the mill price and the bowl price are the same figure with the road standing between them.

> *In thin gold, the tally-clerk's hand, above mine:* Currency recorded. Deduction remitted at point of lading.
>
> *Underneath, in mine:* He has taken *before the sack is tied* out and put *at point of lading* in, which says the same hour in a language a mill woman cannot use at her own table. I have left his gold standing and put the sack back beneath it.

# The market of the Open Court

*The left column is what is called at the third hour, when the public bowl is emptied and the act is announced. The right is what this gate took between the last market of Smoke and the first of Grain. The wheel at the mills does not stop for a figure, and the crowd does not stop for a wheel.*

| Article | Called at the court | Taken at the gate |
|---|---|---|
| Southern grain, a sack, dressed at the mills | 4 bits | 4 bits; one in twelve gone before the sack was tied |
| Warm wine, a jar, at the third hour | 1 crown | 1 crown, and the jar is not returned |
| A cup at the public bowl | no figure | poured on the stones; not sold, not entered |
| Olive oil, a jar | not called | 2 marks |
| Myrrh, a pinch | not called | 3 marks; the fire is out before the wine is cold |
| Smoked eel, the tribute basket | not sold | counted at the rail, poured at the feast |
| Northern glass, a sheet from the Glaze Cliffs | 2 marks | 2 marks; sold again as cliff-glass with the sweetness at the edge |
| Green ware, a whole bowl, out of High Kiln | 1 mark | 1 mark |
| The same bowl mended with brass, the seam shown | 2 marks | 2 marks; a mended thing fetches more here than a whole one does at home |
| Gold ink, a phial | 2 marks | 2 marks; the correction stands in the same ink and lasts as long |
| Quiet ash, a phial, bought back after a burning | 60 marks | 60 marks; the smoke goes up for nothing and what is left does not |
| Ash from a slate burned at a feast, in an unmarked jar | not called | 1 mark, from a man who will not name the house |

> *Margin, in mine:* The last two rows are the same grey in two vessels, one with the house's name and one without. I have not put that on the face. A tally-clerk who writes it down does not last a tend at this gate.

# What the house buys

*Not cried at the court. These are the figures read in the room and written here after, with the person left where the figure stood.*

| Article | Read in the room | Written at the gate |
|---|---|---|
| A bought breath, a season | 20 marks, entered in months | 20 marks; a term, and the hook is in the mouth |
| A thin return, bought at the rail | 2 marks | 2 marks, entered as breath; the figure is for the life bought, not the life lived |
| A whole return, bought for the Ember house | 300 marks | 300 marks; set on a shelf and called beautiful |
| The fourth bowl | carried away full | no figure; nobody pours it, nobody says whose debt it holds |

> *In thin gold, above the second and third rows:* Labour hired for a term. Return graded, one third current.
>
> *Underneath, in mine:* A term is a season with a hook in it, and a graded return is a stranger a house must keep. I have left the person standing under both readings.

# The tolls of the roads

Roads bring a thing to this city and roads take it away. What follows is what is asked at this end and what the carrier says he paid at the other. The two have never agreed; the difference is the whole art of travelling in the south.

## The Line road, down from the Peat Line

Two hundred leagues of drained clay and the burning wind, from the rail where Sere's advance stopped in Y.H. 634 and the eel tribute began. The South calls what is taken at the rail a tariff; the Fen pay it in eels and call it neither tribute nor tax, and north of the rail a man is a neighbour, which costs less and holds more.

| | Due |
|---|---|
| Cart of northern glass or hemp, at the rail | 4 marks, entered as a tariff |
| Basket of eels going south | 1 bit at the rail; the seventh basket holds six, and the boy who lifted it is back in the reeds |
| Sack of peat for the bowl-house fires | 5 bits; the same sack is a bit in the Fen, and the road is the difference |
| Northern trader with a crown and no marks | the crown; it will not change north of here |
| Thin return going south | 2 marks, entered as breath; no figure at the rail knows the person |
| Man going north with a figure in his head | no due; the road counts him once and the Court counts him twice |

> *In a carrier's hard charcoal:* Paid at the rail and paid at the gate and paid again for having carried it, and the crown would not change when I got home.
>
> *In mine:* All three stand. A road has two ends and both have a hand; the wind has one hand and does not write.

## The mill road

South of the city, where the grain is dressed before it comes up to the courts. There is no gate going out. There is a hand at the top of every sack coming back.

| | Due |
|---|---|
| Cart of grain going down to the mills | nothing; the mills are the city's own works |
| Sack coming back dressed | a handful off the top, lifted before sacking and never weighed |
| The Court's deduction | one in twelve, taken at home; the notice stands unchallenged |
| A woman of the mills with a charcoal correction | no figure; she will not give her name |
| The burning winds | yellow dust into every sack; no due, and no washing of it out |

## The road that counted nothing

Beyond the Line lie the roads and wells and doorways of the ground the Court calls the Empty Cantons and the Fen call the Quiet Acre. There is no gate and no due. A square of bare clay above a lintel where a house-roll hung is not a nought, and a man who puts a nought into it has made an empty room into an account. A man who comes back from that road with his number changed states the later number and keeps the earlier under the skin. I have left the column empty and written no nought in it, because a nought can be added and what that road keeps cannot.

# The carriers' road-song

*Sung going down the Line road and not coming up. The last line is said to the cart and not to a man, and the fourth place in the second verse is held. A clerk from the room put a round nought in that place once and wrote* vacancy *beneath it. A nought is a figure. The place is not.*

> Four roads bring and one takes away,
> a sack of grain and a hoop of glass;
> the rail takes its bit and the gate takes its mark,
> and the crown goes north where it will not pass.
>
> The eels go down and the seventh goes back,
> the slate goes up and the clean comes down;
> one for the bowl and one for the fire,
> and one held back where the number goes ---
> we do not sing it going down,
> we do not sing it coming home.

I have put no gloss on it. A woman who explains a song has begun to write a sermon, and sermons get copied.

# The hands that are not mine

The tally-clerk writes in thin gold and takes the doer out of every sentence, as though a sack could tie itself and a slate could burn itself without a house choosing which. Where I wrote *one in twelve gone before the sack was tied*, he set *deduction remitted at point of lading*; where I wrote *the fourth bowl was carried away full*, he set *vessel removed, contents not entered*. He has not struck a word of mine. A clerk who strikes and writes above means to be argued with; one who writes above without striking means to be done with.

The factor's clerk come down from Greybank wrote above the gold in narrow upright letters and took the verbs out as though a verb were a thing a man could hide in a cuff. He entered the crown, the term and the graded return, and left the jar, the hook and the person outside his column. I have put my words back underneath both, as an older repair shows through a newer glaze on a bowl still used at table. The carrier wrote at the foot in charcoal off a tally carried in his coat; I have left his mud above mine.

# At the head and in the gutter

The yellow at the head is dust off the burning winds, ground in going down and not shaken out; a woman who shakes a leaf wants to pretend she has not been where she has been. The wine-ring is the third hour. The olive at the fold came off the tray that carried a contract past the gate; the fleck of gold leaf in the third fold is the only bright thing in this leaf. The grain husk came down with a sack from the mills and has no business on a tally, which is why it has stayed. The myrrh at the foot came off the back of a woman's hand after the shrine, and has gone hard and smells of the feast still. The fish-scale came off the tribute basket. The bare square is a word I scraped away and have not written back. The pale round ring is where a collector set his cold tube down and took it up; he did not ask what this gate was counting, which is a kindness of a kind. The grey along the outer fold is not mine, not the clerk's, not the carrier's. I have not swept it. There is a Last Day up the mill road and none at this gate; the dust goes into the next feast under the mat and nobody calls it dealt with.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The grain goes up and the figure goes home ahead of it.
> The bowl keeps what is poured and the house keeps what is not.
> The road takes twice and the wind takes once and writes nothing.
> The fourth place is a place. Ask the cart.

I asked the woman who carries the bowls, a carrier off the Line, the tally-clerk and a man come down from the mill with charcoal on his sleeve, and had four answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written.

The leaf goes back into the tally-box and the other box stays barred. In the morning the carts come in with glass from the cliffs and the ring will be on a fresh spoil by the third hour. A man will ask what his sack is worth and I shall give him the figure I gave yesterday; by the time he has carried it up to the court it will be a thing that was true.
