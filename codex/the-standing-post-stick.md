I keep the dry stick at the Standing Post. A man comes up out of the bog with a bundle on his back and asks what it will fetch, and I tell him what the arm that carried it says, because the arm knows the weight and not the cost. I cut a notch and let him touch it. A notch is still there when the stick is wet, which is more than can be said for the paper they use at Greybank. A man from the factor's house stood here one Silt morning and asked for a figure that would stand still. I told him a figure that stands still is a man who has forgotten the water. He told me to cut it in a better stick. I have cut it in the same stick.

Twelve copper bits to a silver mark; twenty marks to a gold crown, which comes north with the Sere traders and will not change at this Post, so a man may be paid in one and go wet all the same. The Court books in embers and calls five marks an ember. No man at this Post has ever held one, and I have left no ember column on the reed. A column with nothing in it is a thing a man can see. A Court notice with nothing in it is a thing a man has to be told about.

> *In the narrow upright hand of the factor's clerk, written above mine:* Coin and reckoning entered. Ember: five marks. *Underneath, in mine:* He has written it as though an ember were a thing a man could carry in his boot across a bog. I have put *called* back. It is the Court that calls it five marks. The coin does not agree.

Say the figures slowly, and say them to the board rather than to the man asking. Said looking at a man, a price becomes an argument, and an argument on a Post is a debt with nobody's name on it, which is the one debt we know how to leave in the mud.

# The rates at the Post

*Cut on the stick, the first column what is taken here at the dry boards, the second what is said of the same thing upriver. Both were cut between the last market of Bone and the first eels of Ilmen.*

| Article | Taken at the Post | Said upriver |
|---|---|---|
| Peat, a dry barrow | 1 bit and a measure of eels in Grain; 2 bits in Stone when the stacks are wet | 2 bits in Grain; 5 bits in Stone at Sill |
| Reeds, an armful cut in the dark | 1 bit the armful, counted in the arm that carried it | half again at Reedholt, where they will not say which arm |
| Hemp, a hank | 3 bits, or a pot from the south | 4 bits at the Cross |
| Eels, smoked | 2 bits | 3 bits at the Fishstairs before the bridge shade goes off |
| Eels, salted | 1 bit | 2 bits; a third bit when the boat has been out longer than anyone says |
| Eel skin, dried | half a mark | 1 mark; it has been down the water to be sold back up it |
| Grey wool, a hank | not sold; tied, and untied when the debt is paid | not entered, being a knot and not a figure |
| Barley loaf, brown-edged | not baked here; brought in on the Tallow road, 2 bits | 1 bit in Low Kiln, where the edge is the greater part of it |
| Ash, by the thimble, in a jar with a Fen name | 4 bits | the same grey in a jar with a Court mark is sixty marks |
| A thin return | 10 marks, or the same in peat and eels by the load; the source is not asked | 30 marks, paid by the district in labour |
| Bought breath, the season | 6 marks and a knot | not spoken of in the kiln towns |
| Bought breath, the year | 18 marks and two knots | not entered, though the boys are entered elsewhere |
| Warmth in an unmarked jar | not rated at this Post; asked for among the reeds after dark, and paid in marks, never in kind | not on any board I have ever been shown |
| A bundle judged by the arm | no figure; the arm tells the weight and not the cost | no figure; the same answer comes down from the cutters |

> *Margin:* The peat row is the one I cut first and cut over. A man from Sill paid the Grain figure here in Stone and asked why the barrow had doubled going nowhere. I told him the barrow had not changed and the weather had. He said that was a robbery done by a bog, which is a true thing and not an entry I can make.

> *Margin:* A man asked why the four bits and the sixty marks stood on one reed. I told him the four bits had a Fen name and the sixty had a Court mark, and went on cutting. He wanted the likeness written down. A likeness written down is a debt with two names, and a debt with two names gets paid twice. There is a knot in the margin where the word would go.

> *In a small eel-bone laid across the bought-breath rows:* The hook is in the mouth, so a man cannot say the word he would need in order to complain of it. I did not write that on the reed. I laid the bone across instead, and it has lain there since Ilmen.

# What a fine becomes

*The reeves settle fines and feuds standing up on the Post. Nothing here is a price. A fine is a thing that stands until the mud calls it paid, and the standing is the whole of the law.*

| The thing heard at the Post | Paid in | If not paid | What the Post calls it |
|---|---|---|---|
| A board broken by a stranger | a bit, and the board mended by the man who broke it | a season at the peat-dry | a board paid for |
| A feud between two houses | a knot of grey wool tied before both | the knot stays tied and the standing goes on | standing |
| A fine left out over the tend | a term of bought breath | the term becomes a measure | carried forward, in our words |
| A measure called in | nothing said at this Post; the collector's boots were seen on the boards | no due, no guarantee | not entered |
| A wrong done in a hut and heard here | entered only where a household's account has suffered | otherwise left where it was said | not entered, which is not the same as not known |
| A child's first bundle brought back wet | the child goes again at the next cut | no figure and no name | the Reed Cut |
| A man who stands longest in the bog | nothing | nothing | reeve |

> *Margin:* A child at this Post can tell a collector by his boots before she can tell you her own name. I have never seen a collector pay a due at these boards, and I have never seen him step on the loose one. The stick keeps what men pay. It has no place for what they take.

# The dues of the ways

There are no roads in the Fenmarch. What a man pays here is for a board somebody laid, a cut somebody cleared, or a strip of ground the water has not yet called in. A road in the south is a thing built and then charged for. A way here is a thing the water allows and takes back.

## The Standing Post boards

| | Due |
|---|---|
| A man put up on the boards | nothing; nobody tells him which boards are safe |
| A stranger who steps where nobody pointed | the water to the calf; no figure |
| A peat-block set on a board overnight | 1 bit; the board bears it or the block goes in |
| The reeve's place on the Post | nothing paid; the standing is the fee |
| A barrow of grey going out by the boards | not weighed, not entered; the water takes what it wants |

> *Margin:* The showing of the boards is done by stepping first, and whoever steps first does not say which board he chose. A man from Greybank asked me to mark the safe ones on the reed. I told him the boards move after rain and a mark on paper does not. He wrote *vacancy* in the empty place. A vacancy is a thing the Court can enter. The boards are not.

## The Reed Cut

*Where the young cut their first bundle in the dark. A bundle is judged by the arm that carries it, and the arm tells what went into it and not what it cost.*

| | Due |
|---|---|
| Going in at the dark | nothing |
| Coming back with a bundle | counted in the arm; no figure |
| The dry boards shown to a man from the south | 1 bit for the showing; shown by a child who stands on them first and will not say which is which |
| A second cutting owed on a stick | one notch, untied at the next cut |
| A man who asks what the hands will be paid | the weather and the river; never a figure |

## The ways up into Grath

*East by the mud road to Mud End, three leagues downriver of Sill; the south bridge into the town; north-east by the Tallow road; and west the Quiet Acre that lies between us and Grath.*

| | Due |
|---|---|
| South bridge into Sill, loaded cart | 1 bit, paid going up |
| The same on foot | nothing; a foot sinks and pays in water |
| Barrow of peat going up to Sill | 2 bits at the bridge, and the same peat is 5 bits in Stone; the difference is the road |
| Tallow road, going up with wool | no due; a sheep's fibre from such a man is in the fold of this reed |
| The Quiet Acre | no toll; the wells have water and the doorways have no hands to open them |
| A man who stops at a well there | nobody tells him which house will answer |

> *Margin:* The Empty Cantons are what the Court calls the ground between us and Grath. We call it the Quiet Acre and leave it alone. A doorway is built for coming back through. I have not written what hung in the square of bare clay above one of them, because a thing a man can put a name to is a thing the Court can put a figure to.

## The Line

*South to the Peat Line, where Sere's northern advance stopped in Y.H. 634. We have paid tribute in eels ever since, by the year and not by the cart.*

| | Due |
|---|---|
| Tribute in eels at the Line | the same since Y.H. 634; nobody has told me it was raised |
| A cart going south with peat | 2 bits, or a pot out of the south, whichever the reeve's stick says |
| A gold crown offered at the Line | taken and not changed, which is a toll the coin pays by being useless |
| A man who goes south and comes back | nothing coming back; the Line has taken its eels and does not ask twice |
| A thin return carried south | not entered here; a man who buys one has paid somebody, and in the Fen we know who |

## Ditches and water

| | Due |
|---|---|
| A ditch between two dry places | no toll; the ditch takes what the man leaves in it |
| A peat raft out into the reedbed for the dead | no fee, and no fetching back |
| Dry ground built on | borrowed; called in when the water pleases; nothing entered |
| The water under the boards | no figure; the water keeps no stick and does not say what it took |

> *Margin:* The factor's clerk set a nought against the last row and wrote *no charge arising* above it. I have left both. A nought is a figure paid. The water has never paid anybody, and nobody has ever paid the water.

# What the borrowed hands did

The factor's clerk writes upright and narrow and takes the man out of every sentence, as though a fine could be paid by a sentence standing by itself. Where I wrote *I cut*, he set *was entered* above. Where I left the place for the water he put a nought and then *vacancy*, and numbered the ways as three, leaving the ditch and the water out, which is the readiest way I know of counting a road without counting who walks it. I have put my words back underneath and left his standing above, as a board keeps both the man's weight and the water's mark.

The other hand is a reeve's, and it is mine to know and not mine to name. He cut a notch through the figure for bought breath and cut it over again, so that the first notch shows under the second. He would not say which way he meant it. A notch cut over a notch is a correction a man can read with his thumb, which is how most things are read at this Post.

# The song said to the boards

*Said low at the Post while a stick is being cut, never to a man from Greybank, and never while a reeve is standing within hearing of the second verse. The last line is said to the water.*

> A bit for the board and a bit for the showing,
> a knot for the term and a stick for the load;
> four for the ways and a place for the wet,
> and the fifth is the water and takes what it wants.
>
> The peat goes down and the eel goes up,
> the fine stands longest and is called right;
> a man comes over with dry boots and goes back wet,
> and the Post keeps the notch and the mud keeps the rest.

> *In the factor's narrow hand, against the fourth line of the first verse:* Four ways entered. *In mine:* There are four ways and a place. A nought in that place is a figure paid, and the water has never paid. A man from the south sang this with his back to the boards and would not sing it facing them.

# At the head and in the gutter

The peat at the head is mine; I hold the reed down with my whole hand when the wind comes off the bog and do not know I have marked it. Peat goes into the grain and will not come out, which is why we sign with it. The reed-fibre in the second fold came off a bundle set down wet while a man counted his marks and went away without his change. The tide-line in the gutter is bog-water and has taken one word off the peat row; I have not written it in again, because a row with a word gone is a row said slowly, and most of ours are. The eel-bone across the bought-breath figures was laid there by a woman who would not say why and has not come back for it. The wet boot's print over the dues came off a man who stepped on the loose board while he was asking what the safe ones cost; he paid a bit for the showing and would not say whether he had stepped before he paid or after. The grey along the margin is not mine and not the Court's. I have not swept it. There is no Last Day on a Post.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The stick keeps the weight. The knot keeps the term.
> The mud keeps the man who stood longest, and none of them says what it cost.

I asked the eel-trader, a reeve of the Post, and a man come down from Greybank, and had three answers and no hand, as I have had before. I have left the line where it was written. The reed goes back into its sinew under the loose board, the stick goes back on its nail, and the notch for tomorrow's bundle waits where all the other notches are. A man will come up out of the bog and ask what his armful fetches, and I shall tell him what the arm says, and the water will take what it wants as it has always done, without a figure and without a hand to write one.
