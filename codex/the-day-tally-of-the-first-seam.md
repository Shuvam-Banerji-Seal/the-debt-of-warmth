I keep the day tally at the mouth, not the gate. The gate counts what goes up the road and what comes down it; I count what goes down the shaft and what comes back. Those two counts have never agreed and were never meant to. A cart is weighed at the post and a man is counted at the rope, and between the weigh and the rope there is a length of dark in which the Court has put its figures and we have put our dead.

The reeve checks what I set down with a point dipped in iron-red. He does not say what he is checking. A red cross on this leaf means the figure has been looked at and is to stand; it does not mean the man came up. My own hand is charcoal on the backs of spoiled seam-tallies, because a figure the damp has taken cannot be entered against anybody. Number first, then the name, then the remark. That is the order at this mouth. A lowlander who gives his name first is sent back to the foot of the post to begin again, which is not shaming him; a name is a thing a man can change at the next well, and a number goes down with him.

Coin is scarce here and iron is common. Twelve bits to a silver mark; twenty marks to a Sere crown, which comes north with the mercenaries and goes south with them, a fine thing to hold and a poor thing to spend under a mountain. The Court books warmth in embers and will tell a man an ember is five marks. I have set no ember in this book. In nineteen years at the mouth I have never seen one paid, and I have seen a man try to carry one in his boot. A life bought for the deep is entered at eleven months whether the man lasts them or not, which is a figure for the life purchased and not the life lived. The twelfth is the mountain's and nobody has found it.

> *In the narrow upright hand of a survey-clerk come up from the Court's table at Greybank:* The heads are descended; the iron is raised; lives are entered at eleven months. *Underneath, in mine:* He has taken every doer out of the sentence and left the figures standing like stones. A life entered at eleven months was entered by somebody who held the slate while the man was still breathing. I have put the holding back beneath his words.

# The tally, as it stood

*Figures in Ledgerhand. The remarks are mine, in charcoal, set down short because the reeve reads the column standing and will not wait on a sentence. A dash is a place where nothing came up, not a nought.*

Heads down: three hundred and sixty. Heads up: three hundred and fifty-five. Lives entered: six. Iron raised: fifty-nine loads by the reeve's beam. Stone cut: seventeen palms, sold never lent. Figures checked in red: two. Columns struck: one. Places held: one, which is not a figure and has never been entered.

# The note

Before a man goes down the reeve taps the stone he will cut and I tap the man's arm where the way is to be opened. A sound block answers full; one with a hair in it answers dry, a small flat stop that does not travel. The same goes for an arm. I have written *dry* against the block on Bone 8 and set it aside and will not have it cut. A man whose arm answers dry is not told why he is kept from the shift, which is a kindness of a kind and not one I would choose to receive. A low note from a vessel and a dry note from a stone are the same note, and neither is a figure, and neither has a column in any book the Court prints.

# The column the Court's man struck

Beside each row I had set down what came up in the men: the new number in the boy's arm, the name the mother said into the slate and would not say twice, what the smell of lead did to a man before the heat reached him, the colour of a hand below a clamp, and the thing a thin man said when the keys were put into his hand, which was nothing. I set no heading over the column. A heading would make it an account, and an account is a thing a man may be called to answer for.

The survey-clerk borrowed the leaf at Greybank and struck the column through from the first row to the last, and wrote *No such column* above it in his narrow upright hand. I have drawn it back underneath and left his words standing over it. He had to read every remark before he could write that there were none worth entering, and I watched him do it, standing as he stands, with his cuff gone soft from washing and the ink settled in two fingers. The College has eleven words for a deduction and not one for the colour a hand goes below a clamp, which is the whole of the difficulty between us.

# What the borrowed hands did

Where I wrote *I entered the life at eleven*, the Court's man set *the life was entered* above it, and above that *the entry stands recorded*, which is further from the man breathing on the slate than the first. I have put my words back underneath. A sentence with nobody in it is a sentence no mother can ask about, and mothers ask, always at the wall, always after the iron is paid.

The other hand is tall and leaning and comes out of the factor's house at Greybank. Against the held place at the foot of the Bone 8 row it set a round nought and wrote *no head returned* beside it. I have left both standing. A nought is a figure and can be carried down the road and added at the other end; the held place is a place where the mountain's number goes and no man has ever put a figure in it. The factor's man put one there because a hand that writes whole returns for persons not in a room has long since stopped asking whether a place and a number are the same thing. They are not. The mine-reeve set his red point against the boy's two numbers and against the under-vessed man's eleven and said nothing at either.

# What they say going down

*Said low at the mouth while the rope goes out and the boy after it, to the rope and not to the boy. Coming up, it is said backward with the held line held again. Never say it looking at a person; said looking at a person, it becomes a checking.*

> One for the rope and one for the stone,
> one for the count that the boy brought down;
> tap it and cut it and hold it true,
> and what comes up is the mountain's own.
>
> Loads to the beam and heads to the wall,
> the twelfth is kept and is never found;
> eleven they write and eleven they pay,
> and the reeve's red stays red in the ground.
>
> One for the keys and one for the hand,
> and one held back where the number goes ---
> we do not say it going down,
> we do not say it coming up.

Children ask what the held line is and are told to ask the rope, an answer given by people who asked the same at their own Descent. The factor's man put a nought in that place and a word beside it. I have left the place the length it wants.

# At the head and in the gutter

The smut at the head is coal off the reeve's sleeve; he leaned to read the Bone 8 remark standing and would not sit down, and said a tally that had never been under the mountain was not worth the keeping. The iron-red is his point and stays red in frost, and I have not wiped the margin where it touched. The shaving in the fold came off a palm's piece a man set down while he counted his marks and went away without it; it is warm on the coldest morning and has been asked for twice and never lent, and when it goes cold I shall not know who paid for the warmth I had off it. The bead is lead out of the Ninth, where the rock sweats and the smell of hot lead gives the only warning there is; a man laid it on the leaf and did not take it up. The filing in the gutter came out of the clamp-rack and I have left it where it fell. The frost-fern came up out of the paper on the climb over the Cold Pass and went to water before I had made an end of the first column; it entered itself, which is more than can be said for any figure in this book. The clamp-scratch across two columns was made by a man showing his brother how a clamp bites; both were honest afterwards and one has a dead hand. The thumb in coal dust at the foot is not mine and not the reeve's. I have not wiped it away.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The slate keeps the figure. The wall keeps the man. The rope keeps what it brought back and says which way it came.

I asked the reeve, a key-keeper come up from the mouth, a cutter of the Floor, and a woman whose son went down at thirteen and came up with a different number, and had four answers and no hand, as I have had before. I have left the line where it was written.

The book goes back into its oiled cloth and the red point back into the reeve's pocket. Tomorrow the rope goes down on the morning shift and a boy will give his number before his name, and I shall set down what came up beside what went down and put the verbs back where the Court struck them out. The mountain will sweat when it sweats; the smell will come before the heat, as it always has, and the count will try to agree and will not. The held place will stay the length it wants, and no figure will be put in it by any hand of mine.
