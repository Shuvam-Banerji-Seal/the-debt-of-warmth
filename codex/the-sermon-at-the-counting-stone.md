I keep the sitting-register at Keld Keep, in a box beside the high-path door, and nobody asked me for this. There is no word for shrine in the Vorr tongue. They call the stones the stones, and a man from below who says shrine has named something he cannot see, which is the whole difficulty of that country in one word. I write because the boy had no name to set down. A Vorr takes his name one at a time, many years apart, and is known until then by the stone he keeps, so I have entered him as the boy of the third stone, and left the place where a name would stand the length a name would be.

He spoke to the stone, the dust, the dish of meltwater, and last of all to the boy, which is the order the Vorr use when there is a season waiting and somebody below may be listening who wants a figure. He said everything twice. I have set each saying down once and put the second beneath it, in the same hand, because a man who reads the second as a correction has not understood the first, and there are enough men below ready to correct a thing without help from me.

The pale at the head is lichen scraped off the north stone when he brushed it clear of rime; it goes into the grain and no wet sleeve takes it out. The doubled print at the second corner is his, pressed into the tallow of my lamp while he turned the leaf; a Vorr's last joint is split in two and leaves a mark like a cleft hoof. The rime in the fold was on the path at first grey and was water by the time I folded. The warm-stone shaving lay on the register-box and kept its heat through one night on the stone floor and is cold now, which is the order of things here. The copper thread came off the spoiled rubbing and is not part of the sermon. The ring is the dish, set down at the first saying and taken up before the boy saw it. The grey along the gutter came off a cuff and nobody will own it; there is no Last Day above this Keep.

# The manner of the sitting, as the stone has it

*Written small, from watching, not from any order the Court has printed.*

- Come at first grey, before the seam-tide turns. A boy who comes at noon sits in another man's season and calls it his own, and the stone has never once corrected him.
- Sit facing the way you came. Nobody tells you which way that is. An elder will turn a boy around without a word if he faces the road, and the road is not the direction this shrine keeps.
- Put the dust on the palm, not on the stone. The stone marks what is kept; the hand marks who keeps it. A mark on the stone outlasts the hand and is of no use to either.
- Say each sentence twice. Once is for the stone and once is for the boy. A third saying is the fault of the sayer, not of the stone, which has heard three sayings before and will hear three thousand.
- Do not say the number before the season ends. A number said early is a number with a buyer, and there is a man come up from Greybank who would pay for it and enter it wrongly.
- The first sitting gives a number and not a name. A boy who asks for his name is given the number again, which is not anger but the arrangement of years this people keeps.
- A season is not an hour. If you cannot sit past the turning of the tide, go down and come back when you can. The impatient hear the stone scratch and bring home a number they could have had at home.
- A number goes down the road. The dust stays. If a man from the Court writes one, let him write the number and not the sitting; the two have never agreed and were never meant to.

He said the last twice, once to the stone and once toward the box where my register lay, and I have not joined the two sayings.

# The sermon, as he said it

## Of the dust

He took a pinch of grey from a fold of his coat and pressed it into the boy's palm with the doubled joint of his last finger. The dust is marked off a blighted seam and is not sold.

“This is put down. It is not given.”

> This is put down. It is not given.

“A thing given can be taken back by the giver. A thing put down stays where it was put. I have never given a boy anything in forty years and have had no cause to ask anything back.”

> I have never given a boy anything in forty years and have had no cause to ask anything back.

“The Court has a word for what is put down. Their word has a place in a column. This has no column, and if you find one on the road below, do not stand in it.”

> This has no column, and if you find one on the road below, do not stand in it.

> *Margin, in my hand:* He used the word for *put down* that the factor's clerk at Greybank translates as *retained*. I have left both, one above and one below, as the dust lies on the palm and not under it.

## Of the first sitting

“You will sit until the number changes. It changes. That is the whole of the sitting.”

> You will sit until the number changes. It changes. That is the whole of the sitting.

“Ask me what it changes to and I will tell you the numbers are correct. That is an answer to the numbers and not to you. You will be told it twice. A third asking is yours.”

> Ask me what it changes to and I will tell you the numbers are correct. That is an answer to the numbers and not to you. You will be told it twice. A third asking is yours.

“You want a name. The stone gives a number. Names come one at a time and many years apart, and a man here is known by the stone he keeps rather than by what his mother called him. I have kept this one forty years and have not yet been given the other word.”

> I have kept this one forty years and have not yet been given the other word.

A girl come up from the Keep with a slate under her coat asked him outright what the boy was to be called. He gave her the number. She asked again in a different way and he gave her the same number, and went back to the stone. I have set the two askings down and not joined them.

## Of the bone

“The count is in the bone. The tube finds nothing there.”

> The count is in the bone. The tube finds nothing there.

“They below take what is in the blood and write it on a slate. We keep ours where no iron reaches. This is spoken of below as a difficulty and never as a mercy, and they are right: a thing a man cannot take is a thing he will not call a gift.”

> A thing a man cannot take is a thing he will not call a gift.

“We were never owed. We do not ask.”

> We were never owed. We do not ask.

A miner off the shift stood at the back of the row with a pale ring showing at his wrist where something had gone through the winter, and did not come forward. When the elder said the last the miner put his hand over the ring and went back down the path before the boy had heard the saying twice. I have written the hand and not the man.

## Of what goes down

“A stone is kept. A number is sold.”

> A stone is kept. A number is sold.

A Court survey-clerk had come up with a cuff gone soft from washing and ink in two fingers, and held his slate inward to his forearm so the row could not see the column.

“You will carry the number down in a clean hand and read it in a room I have never stood in,” the elder said to him. “I have given you the number and not the sitting. Do not lay one over the other.”

> I have given you the number and not the sitting. Do not lay one over the other.

“The measure is declared and the person is identified,” the clerk said, in the voice that takes the speaker out of his own sentence. He wrote it down and put the boy in a column where no boy had stood before.

“If the Court's fire is a candle lit from our mountain,” the elder said, turning his head toward the stone and not toward the clerk, “the candle has never once been brought up here to light this one.”

> The candle has never once been brought up here to light this one.

> *In my hand, at the foot of this section:* He said that to the stone. The clerk wrote *claim not examined* against it, which is true of a man who has never climbed five leagues of scree. Both stand.

## Of them that sit no more

Higher on the path there was a body seated upright facing the way it had come, left as the Vorr leave their dead, with no fire carried up and no word read over it. The elder said the words for it while we stood below and did not go near.

“He came from there. He faces there.”

> He came from there. He faces there.

“The sitting is finished. No one goes up to bring him down.”

> The sitting is finished. No one goes up to bring him down.

“There is no slate at a Vorr death and no return, and the account below has never found a place to put one. We do not ask it to.”

> We do not ask it to.

I have kept the three sayings in their order. A man who washes bodies at the Keep asked me afterward whether they were sending the dead home, and I said no, and he wrote nothing, which is the only honest thing he did that morning.

# The sitting-song

*Said low, facing the stone, the dust still in the palm. The elder says each line twice and the boy says the last four twice after him. There is a season between the third part and the last, the length of a sitting, and I have left the space the length it is kept.*

> The stone was here before the sitting.
> The stone was here before the sitting.
>
> The dust is on the hand and the hand is on the stone,
> the dust is on the hand and the hand is on the stone;
> one for the way you came and one for the way you face,
> one for the way you came and one for the way you face.
>
> The number goes in where the bone is old,
> the number goes in where the bone is old;
> the count comes down and the stone stays up,
> the count comes down and the stone stays up.
>
> *Here the boy sits for a season. No words are said. The space is the season and I have left it the length it is kept.*
>
> The number changes. The stone does not.
> The number changes. The stone does not.
> We were never owed. We do not ask.
> We were never owed. We do not ask.

A girl from the shift asked what the space was for. Nobody answered her. The clerk put a round nought against it and wrote *vacancy*, which is the first figure of his that has ever fitted anything above this Keep, and still does not fit, because a nought is a number and the space is a season. I have left it where he set it.

# The sittings, as the register keeps them

*The number goes down the high path in a coat and leaves nothing in the box. These I have set down where they stood, without saying what the numbers count, because I do not know and the Vorr have never told me.*

| Who kept the stone | Count before | Count after | What was entered at Greybank |
|---|---|---|---|
| Iyn of the Listening Stone, at his third sitting | 41 | 41 | figures confirmed; no proceedings |
| A woman of the high path, at her second | 17 | 17 | number unchanged; no cause assigned |
| Ao of the Third Seam, before the Rim War | 9 | 14 | change entered; reason withheld |
| The boy of this morning, first sitting | not yet counted | the sitting continues | a column standing |
| One seated higher on the path facing the way he came | no count | no count | no entry; party not on the roll |

There were sittings before these that I could set a season to, and I have not written them, because a row of seasons is a thing a clerk can carry away in a cuff and a stone is not. The two that did not change stand as they stood. The Vorr call no number a rise and no season a loss, and the Court has never asked them to call it either, which is the nearest thing to restraint I have seen out of that house.

# What the clerk did

He borrowed the leaf on the morning after while I was entering the boy in the register, and gave it back with the verbs taken out.

- At *this is put down, not given* he wrote *the stone is the lawful custodian of the mark*, which is true in a room where a mark can be passed from hand to hand and has no meaning on a high path.
- At *the count is in the bone* he wrote *the measure is not deposited; party difficult to draw*, and underlined *difficult* twice, which is the only part of the sentence he has ever understood.
- At *we were never owed* he wrote *the party is not entered for a return*, leaving out the owing and keeping the party, as though a man could be kept and the debt he denies thrown away.
- At *the sitting continues* he wrote *proceedings pending the season*, and put a season into a column where no season has ever stood.
- At the gap in the song he put a round nought and wrote *vacancy*. When I asked him what a season was worth that had no number in it, he said nothing a clerk could enter, which is the first true thing he said all morning.

I have put the verbs back underneath and left his hand standing above, as a newer repair lies over an older one in the seam and the older one is still there under it. He takes the doer out because a thing with nobody in it cannot be argued with. I put the doer back because a boy at forty with dust in his palm wants to know who put it there, and whether it was his own hand that will keep it.

# At the head and in the gutter

The lichen is off the north stone and will not wash out of the grain, which is why the Vorr scrape with a thumb and not with cloth. The doubled print is Iyn's and no other hand in this Keep could leave it. The rime came up out of the path and was gone before I folded; the writing there is thin and I have not gone over it, for a word written thin is a word said quietly, and most of theirs are. The shaving held one night and is cold, which the keeper of the warm-stone calls the order of things and I have no cause to argue. The copper thread is from the rubbing and has nothing to do with the sermon, but it lies where it lay. The dish stood from the dust to the dead and was drunk by nobody, which is how meltwater goes at these stones when there is a thing to say and no proper word for it. The grey came off a cuff and nobody will own it. The pinch of dust is shut in the crease and has not been sold and has not been spent and has never once been put on a stone.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

Struck through once and written again underneath, very small, in a hand that is not mine, not Iyn's and not the clerk's:

> The stone has kept every number that was ever brought up to it.
>
> It has never once been asked what the numbers were for.

I asked the girl from the shift, a surveyor who carries the numbers down, a miner who came up and went down again in one morning, and the clerk, and had four answerings and no hand. I have left the line struck through and standing, which is the only honest way to keep a sentence you cannot sign.

The leaf goes back into the oiled cloth and the cloth into the register-box with the dust in its crease and the copper thread where it fell. Next Bone another boy will sit at a stone and come down with a number changed or a number unchanged, and the clerk will come up from Greybank and take the verbs out and put a nought in the season. I shall put the verbs back as I did this time, and leave the nought in its place and the space the length the sitting is, and the place where the name would stand the length a name would be.
