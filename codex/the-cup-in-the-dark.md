I keep the Wet Wool, a room on the south bank where the kiln hands leave their wet coats and come back for them after the firing, and where I take washing and give back names at the end of a tend when the factor's man comes up from Greybank. On the Long Night there is no fire after dusk and no names given, and the room fills with people who have come in out of the cold to say things they would not say by daylight. I have set them down this morning, while they are naming the dead up the street, because a thing said in the dark has no owner once the light comes into the room. There is no ink in this house. Ink wants a steady hand, and there is no such thing in the dark. I write in charcoal and pipe-clay, which is what the house signs with.

The white at the head is pipe-clay, not Ash. There is Ash enough in the corners without bringing it into a book, and on the morning after the Last Day I burn it as everybody does and it is back on the bench by noon. I have not swept either. The house keeps its own counsel about the corners, and I have no wish to argue with it.

# The manner of the night

- The coals go blind by dusk and the pot is set on the floor under the bench. A man who brings a lamp puts it out before he sits down.
- The cup goes round from hand to hand and nobody pours for himself. In the dark nobody can see who pours, which is the whole of it. A man who pours for himself on this night drinks alone, and the room lets him.
- A telling starts with *I saw* or *I carried*. A man who starts with *they say* is told to begin again. The house wants witnesses on this night and has no use for news.
- The cup comes back to the teller's hand and the telling ends there. No clapping, no *well said*; there is always another man sitting in his wet coat.
- No name is spoken over a telling. Names are for the morning, and the morning has enough to do with them.
- If a man in a clean coat comes in off the road, stop where you are. He will ask what was entered and the answer is nothing, and he will write that down as though it were a great deal.

# The five that were told

There were eleven coats on the pegs and five of the eleven told. I have set the tellings in the order they were said and not in the order of their worth, which the cup has no way of measuring.

The first was put by a carter come up from Greybank with clay dry on his boots.

> I was on the Clay Road when the ice set, and there were nineteen of us at the road house and one bed among us. The woman set a pot on the floor and told us to knock it, one after another, and never said what she was listening for. Eighteen answered full and one answered flat, and the man whose pot answered flat was given the bed, because she said a man who has lost his note has need of a place to lie down. He was gone by morning. The pot went back on its shelf and I have knocked at that shelf twice since and never heard anything.

He said it to the bench and not to the room. A man who has told a thing about a bed does not want ale afterward. He took his coat and went out, and the cold came in past him and went round the room looking for the place he had left.

The second was put by a girl who works the drying racks two doors up.

> In the Long Cold my brother and I slept against the bank wall. Each night I put my hand between his back and the stone to see that the stone was warm, and one night the stone was cold and he was warm. I did not tell him. In the morning the stone had its heat back and he had gone thin in his sleep, and my mother wrapped him in a coat she had kept folded for nine years. He is alive. He does not know the coat was kept for him, and I have never had the heart to say whose it was before.

Somebody asked her whose, which was against the rule, and she answered the cup instead, which is the proper way to answer in this house. She drank standing, with her sleeve over the rim, and there was a wet mark on the bench where her coat had been.

The third was put by a woman from the Fishstairs who had come up with a creel and would not set it down.

> We sold eels under the ice. A man came with a sack of salt and said the river had given him the eels, so he would take three pair for nothing. I told him the river gives nothing and takes everything, which is a thing my mother said to the bucket and not to me. He said it was the same sentence said twice. I let him have the three pair and would not take the salt. In the morning there was a thin white line on the step where he had stood, and it went when the sun came on it. I have swept that step every day for eleven years and it has not come back.

She asked for no ale and was given a cup by the man next her, which is how it goes when a person has told a thing and the room wants to put something between itself and the telling. She held the cup a moment and set it down again with the ale in it.

The fourth was put by a woman who had been a hearth-maid in High Kiln before she came down to the washing.

> I carried a coal from my mistress's fire to my sister's in a clay dish under my coat, with a fold of ash over it the way we do. There were three doors between and I lifted the lid at each, and at each the coal was there and a little less of it, which is how a coal behaves. At my sister's the fire took. When I got home my mistress asked whether I had been out and I said no, and she said nothing and set the dish back on its shelf. She knew. She had put the ash on it herself.

This one the room heard out in silence, and then the cup went round faster, as it does when a thing has been said that everybody has done and nobody has owned to.

The fifth was put by a clay-hand from the yard, a young man with the grey worked into the creases of his knuckles that no scrubbing lifts.

> In the Long Cold the pit was hard as a flagstone and we warmed the spade under our coats between strokes. There was a woman on the firing who counted the pots by knocking them as they came off, and she never knocked the last pot of any firing. I asked her why. She said the last pot belongs to the count that goes round the yard and is not asked anything, because a thing you knock has to answer and that one has not been asked yet. I have worked eleven years and I have never knocked it. Neither has anybody who learned from her.

He said the last words to the floor. I have set them down as he said them. No one in this house has ever knocked a last pot and no one here has ever claimed the habit as his own, which is two ways of keeping the same thing.

Eleven coats, five tellings, and the cup came back to my hand with a dark ring in the wood where I could not remember setting it. I have counted the rings by daylight and got the number of tellings. I counted them in the dark and got one more. Both counts are mine and I have kept them side by side, the way the board keeps what the cup forgets.

# The low verse, said at the end of the Long Night

*Not sung. Said low, to the pot, the floor and the door, in that order, and to a person last. There is no verse for Kindling in this house; the fire comes up from somewhere else and wants no help from us.*

> The coals lie blind and the coat hangs wet,
> five have told and the sixth sits yet;
> the cup goes round and the cup comes back,
> the board keeps a ring that the hand forgot.
>
> Name them in the morning. Name them once.
> The year was lit before the dark came down;
> and the thing that was said in the dark goes round
> with the ale, and stays where the ale has gone.

The second line is the one people change when they say it, putting *six* or *nine* or the count of coats on the pegs. I have left *five* where it stood, because it was five this year. Next year a hand will put another figure over it and leave mine underneath, which is how a verse gets said in this room.

# What the clerk did

A clerk of the factor came up from Greybank on the market morning, with a cuff gone soft at the edge from washing and two fingers of it the colour of the slop-pail. He laid his hand on the leaf before his eye went to me, which is the manner of that table. He set the five tellings down as *narratives of the Return, five received*; struck the sixth place and wrote *no telling tendered* against it, which is true in the way an empty chair is true; and wrote *five quarts small ale, watered; levy one in twelve*, and a figure against the clay dish as if a man could pay for a coal that way. At the dark ring he could not leave it alone. He put a nought under it and wrote *vacancy*, and a nought is a figure and a figure travels well.

I have put my words back underneath his and left his standing, the way the board keeps a dark ring under a spilled quart. He has entered the five and struck the sixth. The sixth was not struck: it sat in a wet coat on the bench and went home when the rest did, and I have never asked which of the eleven it was.

# The carter's gloss

Against the telling about the bed, in thick charcoal, in a hand that presses hard at the ends of words:

> I knocked that shelf twice. It never answered. I did not say so at the time.

He brought the leaf back himself and stood at the door while I read it, and went out without his ale. I have kept his two knocks where he set them. A man who says a thing in the morning that he did not say in the dark has paid something for it, and I have no slate on which to put the payment.

# At the head and in the gutter

The pipe-clay at the head is mine. I held the leaf down with my whole hand because the draught comes under the door and takes paper before it takes a coat, and clay goes into the grain where nothing will get it out. The tallow thumb is off the lamp I wrote by this morning, with the street full of people naming the dead. The dark ring came off a cup that stood from the first telling to the fifth and was drunk by nobody, which is how small ale is drunk in this room when there is a thing to say and no proper word for it. The press of wet wool across the first leaf is the girl of the racks leaning over to see what I was writing; I have not brushed it, because she would only lean over again. The frost along the outer edge came up out of the paper on the walk from the wringing-board and went soft by noon; the writing there is thin and I have not gone over it, because a word written thin is a word said quietly, and most of ours are. The Ash in the gutter came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying, and nobody will own it. The charcoal is rubbed thin where a sleeve has gone over the tally. There is no Last Day in a room where names are taken for ale and given back at the turning of a tend.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> What is said in the dark keeps its heat till morning.
> What is written in the morning goes cold on the shelf.

I asked the girl of the racks, the woman of the Fishstairs and a man who takes in washing, and had three answers and no hand, as I have had before. I have left the lines where they were written. The leaf goes back into the meal crock behind the door. Tomorrow the bank wall will be warm again and the room will be full of wet coats, and a year from now, when the coals go blind and the cup goes round, somebody will begin with *I saw* or *I carried* and the sixth place will be there for whoever sits in it.
