I mend nets on the Quay and I have turned the rope for three councils of children, which is a thing a woman can do without ever being asked to do it and without its appearing on any tally. A man out of the Council room came down in Smoke with a cuff gone damp at one fold and asked what the children counted when they skipped on the flags below the tide-mark. I told him four and a half, which is the truth, and he wrote four in his book and asked me what the half was for, and I told him it was for the come-aboard, and he wrote *vacancy* against it and went back up the Salt Road with salt dried white at the heel of each boot. What follows is what the children said before he came and what they said again after he had gone, with his hand set above theirs where he put it and theirs underneath where they stand, as the flags keep the water's mark under a dry foot.

The charcoal at the head is mine. I held the tally down on a bollard in a wind off the Sallow and did not know I had marked it; charcoal goes into the grain and no washing brings it back, which is why we sign with it here and not with ink. Ink on this Quay is a thing that waits for rain.

# How it is skipped

- Played on the dry flags below the tide-mark, where the last ebb has left its salt and the stone has not yet taken the next one back. Not on the wet flags. A foot that skids on salted stone takes the skin off a knee, and the Quay has no column for that.
- At the turn of the tide, stop. When the quay-crier calls the turn the turners let the rope lie and the children stand where they stand until he has done. He is counting something that is not in the game. A child who skips through his call is not bold; she is skipping through two counts at once and will go home with them mixed.
- Two turners hold a painter, an old mooring rope gone stiff with salt, and the third is the skipper. Say the counting-out to the rope and not to a face; said to a face it becomes an argument, and there is always a child who will argue.
- One line of the verse is one turn of the rope. Turn from the shoulder, slow. A rope snapped at the wrist goes over a child's head and the game is done for that day. There is a shine on this painter where the hands of the Quay have gone up it, and it will not turn fast for a stranger.
- Four turns are four whole shares. The fifth line is the half: the rope goes round slow and the skipper hops once on one foot. It is not a fifth. A fifth is a place people stand in upriver and put a nought in; this is a share that somebody holds.
- The half belongs to the child newest aboard, and nobody says which child that is. When a girl has held the rope through two turnings of the year she is counted whole and another child takes the half, which is how the Quay comes to have a new person in it without anybody holding a slate over her.
- When the verse comes round again the skipper takes an end of the painter and a turner goes into the middle. There is no winning and no last turn. A child who has never held the rope does not know what the half weighs.
- Do not skip it while a man in a clean cuff is on the flags. He can enter four. He cannot enter a half, and a game entered as four comes home wrong in the wrist.

# The counting-out

*Right hands out, fists shut. The child who says it touches each fist in turn, looking at the rope and not at the faces. The fist it lights on takes the painter, which is not a prize: the turners do the standing and the skipper gets the jumping.*

> Mother's rope and mother's share,
> aunt's rope and the mending there,
> the come-aboard's word at half again ---
> the one it lights on turns and does not complain.

# The rhyme, as it is skipped

*One line, one turn. Four whole shares and the half. The half line is said slow, with the rope going over once and one foot going down once. It is not said louder or softer than the others, and no child is told why. Said to the rope; said to a child it becomes a claim.*

> One for the painter stiff with the salt,
> two for the share that went down the mother's line,
> three for the gull that carries a coal upriver warm,
> four for the hull that was laid before the daughter;
>
> *And the half, and the half, the come-aboard's turn;*
> *the rope goes slow and the foot goes down on one.*

> One for the ebb that takes and does not ask,
> two for the dippers blessing the net at dawn,
> three for the glass that sweetens in the furnace heat,
> four for the bench where a man's word counts at half;
>
> *And the half, and the half, the come-aboard's turn;*
> *the rope goes slow and the foot goes down on one.*

> One for the pots that go down the Salt Road,
> two for the peat that comes up in a barrow,
> three for the watch kept till the fourth day,
> four for the Sallow that runs against the wind;
>
> *And the half, and the half, the come-aboard's turn;*
> *the rope goes slow and the foot goes down on one.*

When the crier calls the turn they say no verse at all. The rope lies on the flags and the turners hold the ends down with their feet, because a rope left loose in a wind off the Sallow will coil itself around a child's ankle without meaning anything by it. When he has done, they begin again at the first share. I have asked them why they do not keep the place they had reached, and they say the tide has not kept its place either, so why should they.

# The verse they do not skip while a man from the Council room is on the flags

*I have it from the child who held the painter at the third turning, with the space left before it, which is the space the turners leave when the rope goes slow. She would not say it facing the Quay and made me set it down with none of my verbs in it, because a verse said to a rope wants nothing added and a verse said to a man wants everything taken away.*

> The tally goes up and the tally comes down,
> and the damp takes a word out of every one;
> the half we keep is the half that would rot,
> and the Council calls a rotten paper saved.
>
> A man may be entered and never asked,
> a share may be held by a name not written;
> we count the half to the child come aboard
> and we do not say *aboard* in the hearing of a cuff.

# What the Council clerk did

A clerk of the Council borrowed the leaf on a market morning while the salt was being weighed, being a man who cannot let paper lie, and gave it back with his hand above mine and the damp already at work under both. He counted four children on the flags and set the fifth down as *vacant, no share arising*, which is how a person disappears from a Quay tally: first made a half, then made nothing. Against *the come-aboard's word at half again* he wrote *the vote of the incoming member is rated at one half*, which is a true thing said in a language nobody on the Quay uses to describe a child. Where I wrote *the half is a share that somebody holds* he set *fractional interest; no deduction arises*. The damp has eaten his *fractional* and left *interest, no deduction*, which is the Council's own hand written by the weather, and I have not put the word back for him.

I have put their words back underneath and left his standing above, as the flags keep the salt under the dry foot. He has put a figure where a share was held. A figure travels up the Salt Road in a cuff; a share stays with a woman and goes down her mother's line. He asked what the children learned by it. I told him the count and the turn and the holding of the half, and he wrote that the young were instructed in shares, and entered it. The children stop when a man in a clean cuff comes down the flags. They take it up when he is past the coast pass with his satchel under his arm, and they never once call the half a fifth.

# What a woman who mends nets put in the charcoal

She has mended the same nets these forty years and has never once called a rope a line. She pressed one line into the margin beside the half, small, the way a person writes where somebody is sleeping:

> A child counted half at the rope is counted half at the table when she is grown. I have watched two of them grow. The half does not come off. The boat comes to be hers, and by then she has forgotten she was ever counted at all, which is the only kindness this Quay knows how to do and will not admit to.

I have left it where her thumb put it. She said a half is not a lack of a whole but a whole thing held by somebody who has not yet been given the name of holding it, and she would not say whether she meant children or boats.

# At the head and in the gutter

The salt bloom at the head came up out of the paper itself on the two nights the sailcloth went damp; it has taken the edge off one word in the third share and I have not written the word back, because a rhyme with a word gone is a rhyme said slowly, and everything on this Quay is said slowly. The tar thumb is mine, off the painter I held down when the wind was taking everything else. The fibre across the second leaf is eel-sinew from the binding, gone soft again in the damp; when it is stiff the leaf is dry and when it is soft the leaf has been out, which is a more useful clock than the Council's. The green bead came off a girl with sweetness at one mouth-corner who leaned to see the writing, pressed it into the fold and went back up to the furnaces without asking. The brine ring at the foot came from a cup set down at the first share and taken up at the half. The white feather shut in the fold is a brine-gull's; it came down on the tally while I was writing and I closed the fold on it rather than move it. The grey along the outer fold came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying on a net-mender's leaf. Nobody will own it. There is no Last Day on a Quay: salt does not burn, it comes back with the next tide and goes on doing what it did.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The rope keeps the half. The crier keeps the turn.
> The tally goes damp and the Council calls it saved.

I asked the net-mender, the dippers, a factor come up from Greybank and a man who has been come aboard for nine years, and had four answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written.

The leaf goes back into the oiled sailcloth and the sailcloth into the tin where we keep the paper we mean to let rot. Tomorrow the children will be on the flags below the tide-mark, four whole shares and a half, and they will stop when the crier calls the turn and begin again after, and if the clerk borrows the leaf again he may have the four. He cannot have the half, because it is a share, and a share is a thing that stays where a woman put it.
