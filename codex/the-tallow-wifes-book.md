I render sheep fat and have caught the children of the lamp-women for nine-and-twenty years. Nobody asked me for this book. A girl came in off the ridge road in the lean week with her sleeve stuck to her forearm and asked what I did for the vat, and I heard myself answer in my mother's short way of speaking, and did not know whether I believed it. A cure you can only say in your mother's voice is a cure nobody has looked at in a long while. I have looked at some of mine. The rest I have left standing, because a woman at three in the morning wants a hand more than a correction.

I write on the back of a fat-tally a spill of hot fat took clean. The figures went into the lamps and burned, which is where the Works sends everything it cannot read. The brown at the head is mine and has gone into the fibre; the ink beads where my thumb was, and a leaf that smells of rendering is a leaf nobody borrows twice. The fourth cup goes under the bench and has no figure; I have made no lesson of it.

# The order of the fat-house

*Written small on the first leaf, with one word gone over in sour fat.*

- Set the slate face down before you go to a child. I have no slate-name and never shall; a hand that writes before it looks has chosen already.
- Keep the north door latched but not held. A woman watched from the door holds her breath, and a held breath is a poor thing to bring a child on.
- Try the stone on your own wrist before it goes to a belly. It came off somebody's fire; somebody paid for the coals.
- Lay nothing warm side up on the vat ledge. A stone with its warm side up takes from the room, which is spending already.
- Do not set a red branch under the sluice. Cross two fingers over it and say the word the mother gives. Sluice-water carries the ash of burnt nests, and I have not tried it on one of these and will not.
- When the third night comes, count with her. Do not count for her. The fourth cup is under the bench and is not a count.
- If a man comes up the ridge road with a cold tube and a slate, give him the mother and keep the child behind the vat. This is not refusal. It is the order of the room.

An old lamp-woman asked why I turn the slate before I look and gave me a date instead of an answer. A date does not spoil in the mouth; I have put it in the margin and left the question standing.

# The remedies, as they are used

| Trouble | What is set to it | The manner of it | Who pays |
|---|---|---|---|
| The vat-scald (a sleeve stuck to the forearm) | Fat from the same vat, then a clean fold | The fat runs at a heat no water reaches and takes the skin when it cools. We put the same fat back, believing a sweet answers a sweet and draws. A physician come down from Greybank says fat seals and water comes first. I have dressed three forearms and the white line stayed in two. Both stand. | The forearm; the household that bought the fat. |
| The sweet smoke (the headache off the flue) | A wet cloth at the mouth; the head out the north door | The flue draws from the second hour and the sweet smoke goes over the ridge into Sill on a north wind; a woman in the low houses tastes it in her pottage and calls it our trade. The physician says the flue wants mending, which no shed can afford in the lean week. I sent two down the ridge for air; both came back with the headache. | The lamp-room, which cannot stop and does not ask. |
| The wool-cough | Steam under a cloth; a spoon of rendered fat in it | We say the fat of the sheep makes a lining for its dust. The physician says it is wool and bone-dust in the lung, and the only physic is to leave the shed, which no household here can do. I sent three down to Sill; two came back worse. | The woman, in the morning she coughs up a colour she will not discuss. |
| The nest-sting (a hand into the warm crack) | A warmed stone in wool; stillness; nothing at the swelling | The tallow-wasp hangs its grey paper in the seam where wall meets flue, and a hand that goes in after the second hour comes out with a swelling that has no head to it. We say a knife lets out what is holding. The watchman says do not open it, for a knife lets out the little that was holding. A lamp-woman sat a whole night with a boy's hand on a warmed stone and he came back by morning. I set that down because it happened, not because the stone did it. | The hand. Nobody has told us what the nest keeps. |
| A cut from a vat-shard | Tallow first, then a clean fold; never the grey from the nest-fire | The women put grey from the nest-fire in a cut to dry it, believing the ash of a burnt thing is clean. I have dressed three such and found grey under the skin in all three. | The hand. |
| Milk that will not come | Another woman to hold the child while the mother sleeps; a spoon of fat in the pottage | We say a fattened woman makes milk, and the fat is spent out of somebody else's lamp. The physician says fat makes no milk and that the favour is paid out of the other woman's own house. I gave the spoon to six and stopped at the third child who cried over a dry breast. | The other woman's child, first. |
| The birth-flower (the red branch) | Nothing. Two fingers crossed at the corner, and the word the mother gives | A woman of Cold Acre crossed two fingers over one of ours and gave me a date. A woman of Low Kiln washed one with sluice-water and her child went flat by evening; I have not joined the two. | Nobody knows. The child's skin, perhaps. |
| The third-night chill | Warmth, barley-water, a watch to the fourth morning | It comes after the warming, not before. A hearth-maid from Low Kiln says a warmed body spends faster and the child should be held and counted with. I have walked nine and held four; all saw the fourth morning, and one went thin. I have left the count unadded, for a count added is a count a clerk can carry. | The household, one night's sleep; sometimes the child. |

Nothing at these works is given away. It is poured from one vessel into another and called a cure. That is the only sentence I will stand behind.

# The note under the knuckle

The lamp-women tap a clay lamp before pouring and know by the note whether it will hold a flame. Sound answers full; cracked answers flat, a small dead sound that stops at the lip. It is the one thing here that costs nothing and is still true, and no factor has thought to enter it.

I tapped a woman once, gone thin after a thin return, and put my ear to the little flat I thought I heard. The flat was not in her. A body is not a lamp, though we pour fat into lamps in the shape of cups. I did not say so with a soft-cuffed man in the door. I have written it here, which is a different matter.

The watchman says the warm crack gives back a note when a bare crock is set against it, and that he has caught nothing with fat where there was no warmth and a full crock where there was no fat. I have left his sayings where he put them. A sheep has no slate and no return, and the flue runs from the second hour.

# The fourth cup

At the turning of the year we pour three cups and set the fourth under the bench, with a heel-tap and no word. A man from the factor's house at Greybank stood in the door with his cuff soft from washing and asked what we did with the fourth. He wrote *on the floor* and went away satisfied, which is how I knew he had written the wrong thing. A thing written down is a thing a man can carry off in a cuff; a thing under a bench wants a man on his knees, which no clerk has ever done here. The fourth is a place and stays.

# The pouring-song

*Sung low at the vats while the fat is still running, to the flame and not to the women. There is a fourth place in the last verse the length of a cup going under a bench, and no singer has put a figure in it.*

> Three for the vats and one for the flue,
> one for the nest in the warm crack;
> the fat runs light and the wick runs short
> and the fourth goes under the bench.
>
> A sound cup answers, a cracked cup flat,
> a sheep goes down and gives no name;
> the hand goes in and the grey comes out
> and the ash goes down the sluice.
>
> Warm the middle and wrap the end,
> count with her, do not count for her;
> the red branch keeps what the water takes
> and the child comes back as it can.
>
> Three I counted and one held out ---
> the fourth goes down where the bench keeps.

A factor's clerk put a round nought in the fourth place and wrote *no cup entered* beneath it. A nought is a figure and can be carried away; the fourth is a place and stays. I changed one line in nine-and-twenty years: *the hand goes in and the grey comes out* was *the fat goes in and the flame comes out*, and the women singing with me did not notice.

# What the physician pressed in the margin

He came down from Greybank in Ilmen of Y.H. 812 with *fumes* underlined in his book and a hand that wrote without asking. Against the vat-scald he set *fat seals; water first*; against the sweet smoke, *mend the vent*; against the birth-flower, *a mark in the blood*. He was right in all three and had nothing a renderer could pay for: the vent wants a mason, and four marks a nest is refused at the shed door. Where I wrote *we push the warmth back*, he set *no such transmission*. I have put the warmth underneath.

# What the factor's clerk did

He borrowed the leaf at Greybank when the winter fat was counted, ink in two fingers of a soft cuff. He struck out my column headed *Who pays* and wrote *No cost to the house*, true in a counting room and false in a shed where a woman's sleeve has stuck to her arm. Against *four marks a nest refused* he wrote *no value arising*, the same refusal in a cleaner hand. Where the notice says one in twelve he wrote *the deduction is constant*. Where I wrote *the flue takes from the room and gives it to the wind*, he wrote *the measure is retained in the smoke*, taking the hands out as though a flue could carry itself. I have put my verbs back underneath. A clerk can write without verbs because he has never stood in the draught that comes up under a rendering pot.

# In the margin, in charcoal, the watchman's hand

> I have taken four hundred nests and the sting comes only out of the warm crack. A man from the coast offered four marks for one paper and the factor refused him the same four at our door. Ask the factor which country holds the heat.

He wrote it while I was at the vats and set the leaf back with the fold changed. A man who has taken four hundred nests is allowed one sentence in a book of physic; I have set his line above mine.

# At the head and in the gutter

The tallow at the head came off my thumb setting the leaf on the vat ledge in the draught and has gone into the grain, so the book smells of the shed in any room. The grey bloom came up out of the paper itself and took two words clean off the second leaf; I have not written them back, because a remedy with two words gone is a remedy said slowly. The singe is a spark the flue drew while I wrote; the hand that beat it out was mine and is not entered. The wick in the seam was trimmed to a fingernail by a lamp-woman who leaned to see. The brown spot is a crust off a loaf set down while I told a girl to eat and she would not. The grey paper in the corner came off a nest a girl carried in; she said nothing and I did not ask. The round mark is a buyer's; he set his mark-piece down and took it up again. The grey along the outer fold is not mine and not the Court's; I have not swept it. There is no Last Day in a lamp-room. The nests go on the fire at the shed door by the factor's order and the ash goes down the sluice, and still there is a nest in the warm crack by the second hour.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The fat keeps the warmth and calls it given.
> The bench keeps the cup and calls it poured.
> The child between the flue and the door is warm or is not, and neither column asks.

I asked the physician, the factor's clerk and the man who keeps the nests, and had three answers and no hand. Tomorrow a girl will come down the ridge road with her sleeve stuck to her arm or a child that will not breathe, and I shall turn the slate face down, try the stone on my own wrist, set the fourth cup under the bench and put my verbs back --- because a child is not a cup and a woman at three in the morning wants a hand more than a remedy.
