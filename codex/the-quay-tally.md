title: The Quay Tally at Vhal, Being the Rates of the Sallow Market at the Flood and at the Ebb, with the Dues of the Salt Road, the Coast Pass and the Waters of the Quay, Copied from the Board Beside the Tide-Tally House

I keep the shore-board beside the house where Kett reads the draws. A carter comes down the coast pass with salt in his load and asks the figure before he has unloaded, and I give him the figure of this water; by the turn of the tide it is not the figure any more. The factor at Greybank wants a number that will stand still, and I have told him that a number which stands still is a lie told by a man with a roof over his goods. He has told me to copy the board again in a better hand. I have copied it in the same hand and set the two waters against each other.

Twelve copper bits to a silver mark; twenty silver marks to a gold crown, which is a Sere coin and comes north because it must, and cannot always be spent here. The Court books in embers and calls five marks an ember; no man has ever exchanged one for the other. I have left no column for them on the nailed board and kept the row here, because a carter who has walked eighty leagues is entitled to know which figure has a hand behind it.

> *In the narrow upright hand of the factor's clerk out of Greybank:* Coin and reckoning entered. Ember: five marks. *Underneath, in mine:* He has written it as though a mark could be carried to the bank and poured into an ember. I have put the verb back. The Court calls it five marks. Nobody trades it, and a figure that nobody trades is a figure that gets read aloud.

# The board as it stands on the Quay

*The left figure is what a thing fetched at the head of the flood, when the boats are coming in and the buyers have not yet had the morning's bread. The right is what it fetched at the turn of the ebb, when the same boats are loading out and the buyer knows he must pay for the road as well as the goods. Both were cried under the tide-tally house between the last market of Stone and the turn of Grain.*

| Article | At the flood | At the ebb |
|---|---|---|
| Salt, a sack off the pans | 1 mark | 2 marks, and the carter does not complain |
| Glass, a sheet, green off the Cliffs | 1 mark | 2 marks after the Glaze boat has come in |
| Rope, a coil, tarred | 1 mark | 1 mark, and tar on the buyer's hands before he pays |
| Tar, a pail | 4 bits | 1 mark when the galleys are refitting |
| Dried fish, a string | 1 bit | 1 bit, or two if the gulls have followed the boat in |
| Salt herring, a basket | 2 bits | 2 bits, and a third bit when the boat has been out longer than anyone says |
| Eel skin, dried, come down the river | 1 mark | 1 mark; it has been down the water to be sold back up it |
| Brine-gull feathers, a bundle | 1 bit | 1 bit; the bird is not consulted |
| Red wine, a jar, out of Sere | 2 marks | 2 marks; salt on the rim, and no charge for the salt |
| A bed in a house on the Quay, the month | 4 marks | 4 marks, and the floor is a bit |
| A boat, new, from the shipwright | 30 marks | 30 marks, and the curse is included, which no shipwright will enter on a tally |
| Grain going up the Salt Road, by the beam | by the deduction | read twice, once for the moot and once for those who came late |

> *Margin:* The Council clerk took the two columns and made one, saying a board has no use for the weather. I put them back. Salt is not dearer at the ebb because the salt has changed; the boat must go out on the water that brought it in, and a man who has paid for the going out is in a hurry to be gone.

> *Margin, in the leaning hand:* A sailor's share is two marks a month and keep; a salt-worker gets one mark and a binding promise. A man who marries into a boat-owning family is come aboard and his opinion counts at half, which is entered nowhere because it has never been a figure. *In mine:* I asked a salt-worker whether a binding promise could be spent at this board. He said it could not, and went back to the pans.

# Paid in kind, and not entered

These stand on the board with a space where the figure should be. The Council has more than once put a nought in the space. A nought is a figure and can be entered; a boat-share is not, because it passes through the mother and a man cannot pay for what he was born beside.

| What passes | What is given | What I write |
|---|---|---|
| A boat-share going inland | paid by the lot and not the league | the lot, in kind, no figure |
| A man come aboard | half a word on the boat | nothing; the half is not mine to enter |
| A shipwright's curse | included in the thirty marks | nothing; it is the price of the boat and not the boat |
| A binding promise | one mark's worth, said binding | one mark in the promise column, which the Council has no column for |
| Seven years at the pulled bench | a condemned man rows them and is paid nothing | seven years, which the Council calls a mercy |
| A dead man sealed in a salted boat and set on the ebb | the watching, until he is out of sight | no fee, and no collector |
| A contract on salt-paper left in the rain | a debt made nothing | nothing, which is held to be a virtue |

> *In the Council clerk's leaning hand, against the last row:* Instrument void by exposure; no claim can lie. *In mine:* He is right in a language nobody at the pans uses. Half the fortunes of this port have turned on a contract going damp at the right moment, and the other half on somebody forgetting to bring it in.

# The dues of the roads

Four ways leave this Quay: the Salt Road down to the coast pass and on to Greybank, the shore road along the Cliffs, the water of the Sallow, and the road that is the tide itself. The figures below are what the Council asks or what the factor's man asks at the far end. The difference between the two is the whole art of travelling.

## The Salt Road, by the coast pass

Eighty leagues to Greybank, sixty-two of them beyond the pass, where the clay country gives way to the salt; a day's walk south-west of Vhal by the low road. Salt, glass, rope and dried fish go out; coin and contracts come back, and the damp gets into both.

| | Due |
|---|---|
| Sack of salt, at the coast pass | 1 mark to the Council of Tides |
| Coil of rope, or a sheet of glass | 1 bit |
| Cart with a load, at this end | 2 bits |
| The same at the factor's beam at Greybank | 2 bits more |
| Contract written on salt-paper | no fee; it will not survive the journey, which the Council calls a virtue |
| Man going up with the glass at the turn of the month | 3 bits, returned if he comes back with the answer |
| Passage upriver for a man | 2 bits; his boat-share inland is settled by the lot |

> *In the Council clerk's leaning hand:* *toll* is struck and *contribution freely entered* written over it. *In mine:* He has left the figure standing. A Council that cannot charge a toll has found a finer name for one, and a man pays it all the same.

## The shore road to the Glaze Cliffs

Five leagues along the coast, where the old lead sweetens in the heat and the glass comes green off the rock. There is a path and no gate, and a due at the path's end because a path in Vhalen is never left alone for long.

| | Due |
|---|---|
| Sheet of glass carried off the Cliffs | 1 bit at the quarry path |
| Pig of lead from the old workings | 2 marks; the seller does not say which workings |
| A man who licks the Cliffs | nothing, and the sweetness stays with him |
| Barrel of rain carried against the salt-thing | 1 bit; a man who does not carry it pays later |
| Winter, when the pass is shut | no figure; the road is a direction and nothing more |

> *Margin:* A child is told not to lick the Cliffs and some do, for a child believes a warning about a taste before one about a poison. The due is nothing and the cost is not entered.

## The water roads

| | Due |
|---|---|
| A keel at the Quay, the month | 2 marks, or a fish off the catch |
| A boat that comes in on the ebb and goes out on the flood | nothing; it has not stopped |
| A boat passing upriver with a share aboard | by the lot, not the league; no figure |
| A net trailed at dawn under the tide-tally house | no fee, and the draw is not entered |
| A brine-gull's coal off a brazier | nothing, and no slate in Vhal stands the poorer |
| A man who dies at sea | no slate; the sea keeps its own account |
| A man who dies on land, carried down for the boat | 1 bit for the carrying; the Return is entered before he goes |

> *Margin:* The factor's clerk wrote a figure against the net row and called the draw a taxable current. It has not been paid in eleven years. A current measured honestly gets taxed, and I have stopped telling the Council which is which.

# What the borrowed hands did

The factor's clerk strikes the verbs out, as though a verb were a thing a man could carry off in a cuff and sell at the crossing. Where I write *the carter paid*, he writes *was paid*, as though a mark could pay itself. I put both back underneath and leave his words above. A figure with nobody in it is a figure that will be paid twice.

The Council clerk numbers the roads as three, leaving the shore road and the water out, and puts a nought in the space where the verse keeps a place for the tide that has not come. A nought is a figure paid. The tide has not come and will not be entered, and I have set nothing beside his nought but the space itself.

# The song the tally-men carry

*Sung at the head of the flood while the boats come in, never at the ebb and never while the factor's clerk is within hearing of the second verse.*

> Twelve to the mark and twenty to the crown,
> the Court books an ember and keeps it in town;
> the board says a mark at the flood, two at the ebb,
> and the carter pays both and says neither was debt.
>
> A mark at the pan and a mark at the pass,
> a bit for the coil and a bit for the glass;
> the share goes down where the mother went,
> and the man come aboard writes nothing on mine.
>
> The draw takes the Warm and the tax takes the draw,
> and the rain takes the contract and calls it the law;
> I keep my leaf dry and the Council keeps wet,
> and the tide keeps the figure nobody has entered.

> *In the Council clerk's hand, against the last line:* No figure entered against the tide. *In mine:* He is right, and the song is sung with the last line held until the ebb, which is how I know the line matters.

# In the head and in the gutter

The salt bloom has eaten the figure against *eel skin*, so that I cannot now read whether it stood at one mark or two; a price the weather has taken is truer than one a clerk can still read, and I have not written it in again. The tar thumb is mine, from holding the leaf down in a wind off the Sallow. The rope fibre in the second leaf came off a coil paid for with a wet hand and goes soft when the weather goes soft, which tells me when to shut the tin. The brine-gull peck went through the row for dried fish and nobody has claimed the bird. The fish-hook nick came off a hook set down in a hurry by a man paying for six herrings and charged for five, or the other way, which he will not settle and I have not entered. The green bead is glass off the Cliffs and fell out of a girl's hair when she leaned to see the figures; she did not ask for it back. The grey print along the margin is not mine and not the Council's, and I have not swept it. There is no Last Day on a Quay.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The board keeps the figure the boat paid. The tin keeps the figure the man carried. The difference has no column, and the tide has no hand to write one.

I asked a tally-man, a carter come down from Greybank and a woman who holds a share from her mother, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written.

The leaf goes back into the tin under the counter; the board stays on the wall, where the bloom will take another figure by the end of the tend. Tomorrow the boats come in at the flood and a man will ask the morning price, and by the ebb it will be a thing that was true.
