title: Nineteen Out, Nineteen Back
kind: A work song of the slip pits and the drying racks, Low Kiln
provenance: Taken down on a market morning in Y.H. 812, the Thin Year, from the mouths of women treading slip in the Clay Yard of Sill, by a clerk in the factor's house at Greybank whose name has been rubbed from the corner of the sheet; the copy stands on the back of a spoiled Weighing notice, folded into an apron pocket, and survives with slip-stains, a green bead of lead glaze, a thumb in tallow, and the clerk's own square hand striking out one line. The song is held to be older than the Clay Road.
relates_to: [the Clay Yard, Low Kiln, Sill, the Slip Brook, the drying racks, the Ilm, the Weir, the first firing, the full note, one in twelve, the Ash, the Return]
---

I set this down because I was told to copy what the women sang and not what I thought they ought to have sung, which is a harder thing than it sounds. I am a clerk. My hand wants a verb where there is no verb, and a line where the line breaks off and goes around the pot instead of through it. What is written below is as near as I could come, with the knocked-out parts left standing so that a man reading it in Greybank may know what he is looking at. The slip at the top right is not mine. Nor the green. The tallow thumb is the woman's who sang it loudest; she put her hand on the sheet to keep it from the draught and did not know she had marked it.

# The song, as it goes round the yard

The pit is cold before the sun,
the slip is old bone under the heel;
tread it, girl, and tread it again,
it will mind your weight and keep it.

Tread, tread, the clay says more
than the potter will say before company;
a fist in the belly, a thumb at the rim,
and the crack shows where you left it.

Nineteen out and nineteen back,
a pot on the hip and the broom at the track;
four and four and a want in the Count,
and the rack keeps the dry and the pit keeps the wet.

The wheel does not care whose hands,
only that they come steady and come soon;
bone-pale slip to the bend of the finger,
and the hollow seat keeps the shape of a man.

Set it down. Let the pot speak first.
A whole one answers full under the knuckle;
a cracked one gives a little flat knock
and will not be told what it should have said.

Leave the crack showing. Let the glaze go over
the good part and the bad part alike;
a repair is a scar with a pot around it,
and the pot is the one that gets carried.

Nineteen out and nineteen back,
and a place at the end of the rack where nothing stands;
the yard goes around it, the broom goes around it,
and nobody puts a pot there, and nobody says why.

Up the terrace the glaze room breathes sweet,
sweet enough to make a body careless;
a pale line at the gum is the first of it,
and a woman with green at her mouth still sings.

One in twelve as the notice says,
three loaves to the levy and one to the dish;
the bell at the Cross goes longer than a church bell,
and nobody crosses himself at it, and the pigs go over the boards.

When the river comes up and takes the low steps,
the steps come back a hand's breadth out of line;
the yard calls it weather and goes on using them,
as a woman calls a cough weather and goes on.

Nineteen out and nineteen back,
the fire keeps the breath it will not give;
bank it with the back of a broken pot,
and leave the grey where it lies till the Last Day.

# The fourth line, and what I did to it

The women sang, *the pit is cold before the sun*. I struck this out, being used to a verb in its place, and wrote above it, *the cold is in the pit before the sun is*. Missy Clay, who sang loudest and keeps her hand on my sheet, looked at what I had written and asked me which of us had been standing in the pit, because the cold was not in it before the sun and never had been; it came up out of it when you trod, which is a different thing and not the thing on my notice. I have left my line standing above hers so that the correction can be seen. It is the only place in this copy where the Court's grammar has got the better of me, and I am not proud of it.

# A note on the counting, in the same hand

The nineteen is not a number I have ever been able to make agree with anything. It is the steps from the slip pit to the drying rack, out and back, and a woman of House Silt has walked it every day since Y.H. 806, which is six years, and will not vary it by one. I asked her why nineteen and she asked me why a tend has ten days in it, and when I told her the Court had not made the tend and could not unmake it, she said that was the answer, and went back to her tread. There is a twentieth place at the rack's end where a pot once stood. I put it in the song because they put it in the song. I cannot tell you what pot it was, and no one I asked would tell me either, so I have entered it as a place and not as a loss.

The *four and four and a want in the Count* is the kiln's breathing, or the stack's, or the bell's; they use the same count for all three and keep the three counts in separate places in their heads. I put down what I heard. I would not join them for a crown.

# The verse they would not sing before a man from Slate Row

There is one more verse. The women broke off at it when a collector crossed the north bridge with his satchel under his arm, and took it up again when he was past the Fishstairs, and would not let me write it while he was in the square. I have it now from Missy Clay after the market, and set it here with the proper space left before it, which is the space they left in the singing:

> The iron comes cold and the slate comes clean,
> and the hand that writes is not the hand that's seen;
> what goes in the pot is not what comes out,
> and the account stands, and the bowl's about.

I asked her what the last line meant. She said it was a song, and a song was not obliged to mean a thing just because a clerk had written it down. Then she put her tallow thumb on the corner of the sheet again.

# At the foot of the leaf

A grey ridge runs along the skirting in the room where they sang, near two thumbs wide, turning the corner without a break. It is not swept until the Last Day and is burnt the morning after, and called dealt with. I touched it with the edge of my notice and it came off on the paper, which is how the grey at the bottom of this leaf got there. If the sheet is ever read in the Hearthseat, let that be entered against me and not against the women: I moved it, and they would have left it.
