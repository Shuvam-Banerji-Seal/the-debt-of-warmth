I keep the birth-slates in the fold of a grey wool cloth that goes where I go. A girl comes to me at ten with her hands already red from the tubs and asks what a maid is for, and I tell her: to be the first hand a child meets and the last hand a mother keeps. Then I set her to warming a stone. There is no lesson before that one. A girl who cannot tell when a stone has given its heat back has no business holding a child who cannot tell her anything.

These rules were gathered before any of us had names worth writing down, and are copied forward in the fold and not from a book. A rule with no thumb on it is a rule nobody has had to use. These have been used. I set them down in the words we say, short words and often half a sentence, and where a clerk has put a whole sentence in their place I have left both standing. A woman at three in the morning wants to know which hand to hold.

# The rules as they stand in the fold

## Of the taking

1. A girl is taken at ten, by a maid and not by a house. She answers to the maid until she can answer for herself. A house may ask for her; a house may not keep her. We have had trouble with houses.
2. The taking is not written. What is written is the day she first held a stone for a woman in labour, in the corner of the birth-slate book where the mothers' names leave off. There is no roll of maids. A roll is a thing a clerk can carry away in a cuff.
3. A girl is taught in sayings, not in questions. Ask her what the stone is for and she will tell you the weather. Ask her what a child is for and she will put another log on the fire. Both are answers and neither is a lie.
4. She learns to keep her own counsel before she learns to keep a child. What she sees at a birth she carries in the fold and does not set on a bench at the Cross. A thing said at the Cross is a thing entered somewhere.
## Of the stone

6. Warm the stone at the fire, wrap it in wool and set it to the belly, never to the feet. A stone is a stone and not a kindness. The kindness is in the hand that changes it when it goes cold in your own palm, not when the child stops crying.
7. The stone goes back to the hearth at the end, whether the child stays or not. What was lent to one bed is not spent on it. This is a rule about warmth and not about children; a woman at a bedside will not see the difference, which is why it is written down.

## Of the slate and the three days

9. The birth-slate lies on the bench within reach before the child is. A midwife's hand goes to the slate before it goes to the fold. This is the order of the room, which keeps a woman from being asked twice.
10. Write the day and the given name within three days. Three days is the rule and not a shorter one. A child entered on the fourth is entered as a birth of the fourth, and the year will disagree with the mother all her life.
11. A foundling is named by whoever writes the slate. Give the name given and no house-name. A child with no house may be held by any hearth and inherited by none, which is how a foundling comes to be in somebody's arms and nobody's account.
12. If the mother has no name she will own, leave the place empty. Do not put a mark there. A mark is a figure, and a figure can be read at a Weighing.
13. The book of slates is kept in the maid's own house, under the cloth, and is not taken to Slate Row. The College asked for it in Y.H. 782 and was paid, and the book stayed where it was. They are paid to ask. We are not paid to answer.

> *In the narrow upright hand of the clerk of Slate Row, above rule 13:* The birth-slate is entered. *Underneath, in mine:* I had written that I enter it. Both stand. I do not know which of us the College pays.

## Of the flower

14. Some children come with a red branch across the ribs or down the arm. It is called a birth-flower. Do not wash it. Do not cover it with a word.
15. Cross two fingers at the corner of it and say nothing about the sign. If asked what it signifies, give a date. A date is an answer that does not spoil in the mouth, and the Court likes a date and does not ask what it covers. Where the mark is cut into the niche stone, give the date of the niche if you must, and give the same date a second time if asked a second way. Ask me what it means and I will tell you when it was built.

> *Crossed hard in charcoal across rule 15, in a woman's hand that did not sign:* The mark was here before the niche. The niche was here before the name. I have struck it and written it again underneath. I have not swept the charcoal and I have not put my name to it.

## Of the third night

16. A child may grow worse on the third day after a return. Watch to the fourth morning. Keep warmth and quiet and a living body under the quilt, and barley water by the spoon. Do not leave her to sleep it off.
17. Call it a graft settling if the room wants a name for it. The room wants a name for everything. What the room calls it and what the maid calls it are two things, and both are true in the mouth that says them.
18. Do not call a collector for the worsening. It is the night's own work and goes off by the fourth morning. If you call one, the figure is taken before the child has had her chance, and no slate in the district can give it back. Do not set a stone higher and call it a second chance. Warmth taken from a hand does not come back because you warmed the place above it.

## Of the collector and the Court

20. Turn the slate face down before you go to the child. The tube comes after the mother has been looked at, not before. A hand that goes to the slate before the eyes go to the bed is a hand that has forgot which room it is in.
21. When the collector comes, stand where you are standing. Do not fetch him. He has a slate already written and reads it in a soft voice, and the neighbours call it weather afterward, because there is no other word in the district for it.
22. One in twelve is printed on the notice and read twice at the Cross. We do not read it at a birth and do not correct it. There is a figure that is read and a figure that is paid, and a birth room is not where to make them agree.
23. The account is never closed. A child with no account is to be pitied, not envied, though the poor will tell you different after a bad winter. They are not wrong about the winter. On the Last Day the grey in the corner is left and burnt in the morning and called dealt with; in a birth room there is no Last Day. It is swept when there is a woman who can sweep it, and not before.

## The term and the roll

*Kept in the corner of the birth-slate book where the mothers' names leave off. A day is written there and nothing else; a maid is not entered, because an entered maid is a maid who can be called back to a house she has left.*

| Condition | What is written | What is kept | Discharged |
|---|---|---|---|
| Girl taken at ten | The day she first held a stone | The sayings, in her own mouth | When she can keep her own counsel |
| Girl who cannot write a date | The day, in another hand | The child, through the third night | Never; she is still a maid |
| Mother with no name to own | The place left empty | The day and the given name | The slate is not changed later |
| Foundling named by a collector | The name as given, no house | A coat any hearth may wear | When a hearth claims him |
| Child worse on the third day | Nothing entered as a worsening | A watch kept to the fourth morning | The fourth morning |
| The Forty gone in the levy | Nothing on the slate | Knots on grey wool, by the women | Not discharged, not explained |

24. Seven years is the common term in the yards and not a short one. A maid serves no term. She stays while there is need and goes with nothing written down, so nobody can call her back. The knots tied on grey wool for the ones taken in the levy are not entered here and are counted by no Court thumb. A knot is kept in the hand that tied it. Ask the wool.

## Of faults and their correcting

| Fault | As corrected in the fold |
|---|---|
| A stone set to the feet | Taken off; the girl set to warming water for a tend |
| A birth entered on the fourth day | Left as the fourth day; the mother told, and not comforted |
| A mark put in an empty place | The page copied again and the place left bare |
| A flower covered with a word | Two fingers crossed; a date given; the word not written back |
| A collector called for the third-night worsening | Not entered; the watch kept to the fourth morning all the same |
| A birth-slate carried to Slate Row | Brought back; the maid's own house named as its place |
| A verb struck out of a rule | Put back underneath, where the clerk can see it |
| A charcoal line swept away | Not swept; the line written again below |

# The washing-song, pasted into the fold

*Sung low at the tubs while the slates lie on the bench to dry, never at a birth and never twice in one house. The girl who is learning says the last line and the women say it with her. Nobody sings it on the Last Day.*

> A stone in the wool and a slate on the bench,
> a girl at the tub and a name not yet spoke;
> the water goes cold and the water comes warm,
> and the day goes down where the ink has a place.
>
> Hold the heat. Hold your tongue.
> Three days to write and a fourth to watch;
> hold the heat. Hold your tongue.
> What comes back comes back as it can.
>
> Cross two fingers and give a date.
> Leave the place where the name would stand.
> The wool keeps warm what the slate keeps dry,
> and the child keeps neither, and comes back warm.

The press of wet reed across the middle verse came from the cloth going with a survey party down to the Weir on a day the bench stood in the rain. I have flattened it as well as I could. The words are plain under it.

# What the borrowed hands did

The clerk of Slate Row came with ink settled in two fingers and a cuff gone soft from washing. He struck *I enter it* and set *the birth-slate is entered* above, as though a birth had entered itself; he struck *I set the stone* and wrote *the stone is set* over it, which is always true and therefore true of nobody. I have put both verbs back under his. He put a round nought in the empty place and wrote *vacancy* beside it. The place was empty before he numbered it.

The man come up from Greybank wrote *a lawful keeping* over rule 21 where I had set down what the neighbours call weather. Both stand. Lawful is true and does not mention a woman on a bed with the tube already gone. I set weather down because I heard it said and there is no column for what a room says when the slate has been read.

# At the head and in the gutter

The grey at the head came off my cuff when I measured the ridge in a house two doors down and came back to write; it does not wash out of the creases. The sour in the fold is lye under soap, older than the leaf. The white in the gutter is dried soap bloom and is never entered. The tallow at the stone clause is mine, from holding the lamp low while a woman told me to be quick and I would not. The green bead is lead glaze and not slip: a girl with sweetness at her mouth touched the leaf and went back to her terrace without asking to read it. The hollow in the wool is where a stone lay through a third night in a house I could name and will not. There is no Last Day for a fold, and nothing here is swept and burnt and called dealt with.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The slate keeps the day. The wool keeps the heat. The child keeps neither.
> Two hands went in and one came out, and the business of a maid is the one that came out.

I have asked the women at the tubs, a girl come down from the glaze room, and a man come up from Slate Row, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the two lines where they were written. The leaf goes back into the fold and the cloth goes where I go. Tomorrow a girl will be brought at ten with her hands red from the tubs, and I shall set her to warming a stone before I tell her what a maid is for. The clerk may strike what he likes; he does not hold the child through the fourth morning.
