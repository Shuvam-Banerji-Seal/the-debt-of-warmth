I draw at the yard well, four doors down the Clay Yard from the Vail kiln-house, and I have done it eleven winters. The rope wears a shine where the hands of the bucket-round have gone up it, and by that shine a woman can tell how much water the yard has taken in a year, which nobody has ever entered. The frost writes on the north lip in the cold months, in fronds the width of a finger, and the bucket knocks it grey on the second pail and the yard calls it weather. We leave the fourth pail standing where the frost stood. No hand carries it to the trough and no number is said over it where a man with a slate can hear.

A man come up from Greybank with a soft cuff and ink settled in two fingers of it stood at the trough and asked what the women said while they drew. He wrote the answer into his book before I had finished saying it, with the doing taken out and a figure put where a pail should stand, and went off up the Clay Road with his boots clean. So I have set the words down as we say them, to the rope and not to the bucket-round.

The stone at the head is mine. I held the tally down against the lip with the hand I wind with and did not know I had marked it. Stone goes into the grain and no washing takes it out, which is why we sign with it here and not with ink.

# How it is drawn

- Wind from the shoulder. A wrist brings the bucket up too fast and slops half of it over the lip, and water spilled on the flagstones is water the yard has paid to carry and does not have.
- A bucket snatched at the lip knocks the frost grey before its time, and a woman who has knocked it grey cannot call it weather with a straight face, though she will.
- Set the pail on the lip, not in the trough. A pail left standing in the trough goes cold before it is carried, and cold water taken into a house is warmth the house pays out of its own pot before the morning is done.
- Tap the pail as it fills. A whole one answers full and the note goes up the rope into the hand; a split one stops at the hoop and gives back flat, and a flat pail is set by the wall with the crack showing so nobody has to be told which hand drew it.
- Four pails to a verse and a rest at the set-down. The rest is not a pail and is not counted. It is where the fourth stands.
- The fourth pail is the well's. It stands where the frost wrote and the bucket goes round it. If a girl asks why, tell her to count the pails going up and find out; if she asks again, do not answer her, because the answer is a place and a place cannot be told to a person who wants a figure.
- Do not sing it on an Account-day. The bell goes four, four and a gap, and a drawing-song set against that gap comes home wrong in the shoulder.
- The last part is kept back. It is for when the man from Greybank has gone back over the north bridge and the kiln-master has gone up the terrace and the yard can hear itself again.

# The song

*Said low into the rope, with the pail steady on the lip. One verse to four pails going up. The fourth is not a pail; it is the standing of the pail on the stone, and it is not said aloud. The refrain goes into the well and not across the yard; said to a person it becomes an account, and there is enough account at the Cross without bringing it down the steps.*

> The rope has a shine where the hands have gone up,
> laid down one on another before any of us came;
> wind from the shoulder and let the bucket tell you,
> and set it down where the stone is cold.

There is a space before the last refrain in the singing, the length of a hand laid flat on the north lip where you feel for the frost before you set the pail. I have left it on the tally. A clerk put a round nought in it once, which is a thing you can hold up and look at; a hand laid flat is not.

# The part they keep back

*Not said while a man from Greybank is at the trough or a man from the terrace is on the steps. Said to the rope and not across the yard.*

> The well gives and the slate carries away;
> neither asks which house the water came out of.
> There's a word comes up on the rope and stops at the lip
> and we do not say it to a man with a cuff.
>
> Four pails and a rest. The rest is the well's.
> The clerk has no word for a pail that stands still.
> He can carry the figure. He cannot carry the stone.

There is no verse after it. The next thing is the rope going down, which is not a verse and has never wanted one.

# What the kiln-master set above it

He came down from the terrace for a pail of water with the sweet edge of the glaze at one corner of his mouth and read the tally with one eye on the steps. Against *the fourth stands where the frost-bloom wrote* he wrote in broad charcoal: *Frost on the lip; a weather effect. No entry arises.* He underlined it twice. Against *a split one stops at the hoop and gives back flat* he set: *Vessels to be proved at the kiln, not at the well.* He did not look at the well while he wrote.

Underneath, in mine: he is right about the frost and wrong about the pail. A pail is proved at the lip, where the water goes over the stone and somebody carries the loss into a house. He reads with one eye on the fire, as a man does who has never watched a bucket come up in the dark.

# What the clerk did

The factor's clerk borrowed the leaf at Greybank on the morning the tile was weighed, with his cuff gone soft from washing and the ink sitting in two fingers of it. He took the verbs out and set his words above mine, the way a newer repair lies over an older one on a pot.

- At *the well keeps the pail that nobody carries* he wrote *water unaccounted for; no deduction arises*, which leaves the water standing and takes the keeping away.
- At *the bucket knocks it grey and the yard calls it weather* he wrote *frost dissipated by use; no cost to district*. True in a figure and false at the lip, where the grey stays in the stone and the yard has called it weather for eleven winters without once believing its own word.
- At *a split one stops at the hoop and gives back flat* he wrote *vessel unsound; not a chargeable defect*. At this well a flat pail is a thing set by the wall with the crack showing.
- At *one in twelve is printed and read twice* he wrote *deduction current; one part in twelve; twice read*, and set the printed figure above it. He has entered the number that was read and not the number that was paid.
- At the fourth pail he wrote *three removed, fourth nil*. The fourth is not nil. It is a pail standing on a stone, which a man can carry away in a figure and a woman cannot.
- Where I had left the space before the last refrain he put a round nought and wrote *vacancy* beneath it. A nought is a number and travels. A hand laid flat on the lip is a place and stays where it was put.

# What a woman of the bucket-round put in the margin

The thick charcoal belongs to a woman who has drawn at this well since before the Glaze Wars and has knocked the frost grey on the second pail every winter she can remember. I have copied it as it stands, without making the hands agree.

At *the frost writes white on the north lip*: *It wrote in fronds the year my sister went south and every year since. I have called it weather every time and will call it weather tomorrow. A word said for eleven winters does not become true, it becomes cheap.*

At *the well keeps the pail that nobody carries*: *A girl asked whose pail it was. I told her to ask the stone, which is what my mother told me at my first drawing. Nobody has asked the stone and had a figure come back, which is why the question goes on being asked.*

At the part they keep back: *The Court takes what it can carry up the road. The well takes what the Court cannot. I have shouted at both of them, and neither has ever turned, which is the only mercy I have had from either.*

# At the head and in the gutter

The pale thumb at the head is mine and has gone into the grain; there is no Last Day at a well and nothing here is gathered and burnt, so the grey stays where the bucket left it and goes into the next winter under the frost. The bloom rose white along the gutter and went grey where my sleeve crossed it; it took a word off the fourth verse and I have not written it back, because a song with a word gone is a song said slowly, and everything at this well is said slowly. The chip in the fold came off the north lip under the rope on the second pail and is lighter in the hand than a good chip, which is the lesson and no use to a clerk. The fray across the third verse came off the heart of the rope; I have left it lying over the word it landed on. The ring on the second leaf is cold water set down while a woman told me to wind from the shoulder and taken up when I had done. The green bead came off a girl with sweetness at her mouth who touched the leaf and went back up the terrace; I have not washed it. The sour ring is small ale set down at the first verse and taken up at the last. The grey along the outer fold came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying on a well. Nobody will own it. The grey wool ties the leaf to the rope-hook, with a knot for the line I could not bring up.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The well keeps the fourth. The trough keeps the cold.
> The frost keeps the mark. The slate carries none of it.

I asked a woman of the bucket-round, the kiln-master come down for a pail, and a man with a barrow at the yard gate, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written.

The tally goes back under the broken pot on the rope-hook. Tomorrow the bucket will go down and come up, three pails to the trough and the fourth standing where the frost wrote, and if the clerk comes again with his soft cuff he may have the nought. He cannot have the fourth, because it is a place, and he has no word for a place that stays where the water put it.
