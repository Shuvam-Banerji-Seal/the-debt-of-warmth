To Orrel at the Quay, who reads the Sallow draw before he opens a door. From Vess at Low Kiln, who has never seen the sea and has mended what the sea's country sends back.

I write on the back of a charge-sheet a wet thumb spoiled. The entered figure shows through every line of it: *one in twelve, as the notice says*. I did not choose the paper. A physician who writes on a slate writes what a clerk will read later; a physician who writes on what was thrown away may write what she saw. The charcoal is thinned with barley-water and takes badly on paper that has been sized. Do not rub the fold. The salt will have it if you do, and then there will be no letter to correct.

You asked, in the matter of the girl come down from the glaze room, what we set to a pale line at the gum. I will tell you what I do and then what the Hall does, and you must keep the two apart as I have had to, or you will end by believing the second.

# What I do, and what the Hall does

The Hall says sweetness answers sweetness. They give sour wine for the sweet sickness and send the glaze hands back to the mix with a clean bill and a line at the gum the size of a thread. I have watched the line go off a girl who left that room for three weeks and come back on a girl who stayed. I have never seen the lead, which is a thing a man may believe in without seeing; I have seen the going and the coming, which is a thing a physician may enter if she has a column for it. There is no column. I have set it on a table instead, where the clerk cannot carry it out in his cuff.

For the fever that cooks they put cold iron to the temple and bleed at the elbow, and say the vessel is overfilled and must be emptied or it will cook its own contents. I bled four in the Thin Year. Two were quiet after it and did not wake. The measure went out of them and the fever stayed, and quiet is not better; I have known a man go quiet who was gone. I keep my knife in its fold now. When a household asks why I did nothing, I tell them I did the thing that lets the person stay in the room, which is not the same as saving them and is the only thing I can answer for.

The beautiful return is the hardest. They come back calm, want nothing, take the cup when given and do not ask whose child she is. The Hall writes *a beautiful return* at twelve hundred marks and forty unspent measures, and on the third day the hand shakes and the family is given a phial of quiet ash at sixty marks, and nobody asks what the first pouring took. My mother crossed two fingers and called it the quiet. A woman who does not know her own child is not quiet. She is gone. I have entered the calm because the rolls will take nothing else, and I have not crossed out the sentence, because a sentence is not a figure and can be left where a clerk will not look for it.

> *Margin, in Orrel's hand, written at the Quay with the pen still wet from the tide-tally:* The girl came down our road with the green still at her mouth. I set her three weeks at the pans away from the furnaces and the line went pale; then a week at the glass and it came back brown at the edge. So the lead is yours after all, and the sour wine is a kindness sold at sixty marks. I will not argue with you in a margin. I have put this on the back of a spoiled tide-tally where your clerk can read it if he wants.

| A collector, fingers grey at the tube | Rest near the Hearth | Entered as age | A hand greyed in one winter and it was not age |

The bankers tap a cup before a pouring and know by the note whether it will hold. Sound answers full; cracked answers flat, a small dead sound that stops at the surface. I borrowed the trick and tried it once on a returned woman gone thin, and there was nothing there for the knuckle to find. I wrote that the flat was not in her, which is true, and have not written what else might have been. A person is not a vessel of the Court's kind, and a Kept vessel is not a person, though both hold without leaking. The examination room has no word for the difference and the College has eleven words for a deduction.

# The second chill

This is the one I am writing to you about. It comes after the warming, not before. A child three days out of a thin return begins to fail at the very moment everybody has begun to hope; the fingers grey, the warmth goes, and the household says the graft is settling. The Hall walks them. A warmed body spends faster, I say, and walking faster still, and a child who is walked through a fourth night spends what she had. I say hold her and count with her, and I have said it so often that it has the sound of a proverb in my mouth, which is how a thing becomes a saying and stops being an instruction.

I held two and walked eleven and did not write which fared worse. That is a sentence I would like to be able to cross out and cannot. The physician at the Hall wrote in the margin of my book: *Hold the next one and see if you can write what you saw.* It is the only advice given to me in this trade that cost the giver nothing and might cost the child something, which is why I have kept it.

> *Second margin, in Orrel's hand:* I held the next one. A child out of a thin return, grey at both hands, on the third night. I did not walk her. I sat with her and counted with her breathing and put a stone off the pan-wall at her feet. She was warmer at the end and died on the fourth morning quietly. I have written what I saw, which is that she was warm and not better, and that holding does not buy a day. It is the only day I can account for.

> *Third margin, in the same hand:* On your bleeding. I have bled two and not bled two. The bled ones were quiet sooner. Quiet is not better; I have known a man go quiet who was gone. Keep your cold iron for the temple and your knife in its fold. The Sallow draws against the wind and a man who argues with it gets wet.

# The warming verse

*Said low to the child and never to the account. There is a place after the fourth stroke where the fifth should stand, and no singer has put a finger in it.*

> The fire gives four and keeps one back,
> the well says over and under;
> a child comes back in someone else's shape
> and the house calls it a mercy.
>
> Hold the hand. Count it through.
> There's a place where the fifth should stand.
> I have put no finger in it
> and the fire has not asked me to.
>
> What goes in slow comes out slow,
> and the warm goes where it's told;
> a woman comes back and does not know
> the child that she came back to.

The clerk at the tide-tally house struck nothing out of the verse. He could not find a verb in it to take away, which was the first thing about it that pleased me.

# What the clerk did to the letter

> *Outer fold, in the narrow upright hand of a Council clerk:* *took* struck through and *drawn* set above. Against *the lead*: *the sweetness of the trade*. Over the column headed *Who held the hand*: *No such column*. At the foot of the fold: *The Council does not enter hands. It enters what was drawn.*

He sent the leaf back with the correction uppermost, as though the top of a page were the truth and the bottom something a woman had muttered. I have left my words under his and his words over mine, which is the only honest way a corrected paper can be kept. A taking and a draw are the same heat going the same way; the slate is the difference, and the slate is a piece of stone with a neat hand on it. He has eleven words for a deduction and no word for the small intake and the hold and the letting-go of a breath that a person keeps beside a child through a night nobody will enter.

# At the head and in the gutter

The tallow at the head came off a woman's thumb when she held her child to me across the counter and would not give me her name. The slip in the gutter is mine; I did not wash it, and I am not sorry. The green bead in the corner came off the girl from the glaze room when she leaned to see the writing with the sweetness still at her mouth. The pale line in the margin is where I put my finger to my own gum and stopped, because a physician who writes about a thing may still be afraid to look at it. The grey in the creases came off the skirting of a room where a Kept vessel sat and felt nothing; it entered itself and I have not burnt it on the morning after the Last Day, for there is no Last Day for a letter.

The bell at the Cross goes four, four and a gap and nobody crosses himself. I have kept the told bell beside what I heard with my own ears and have not made them one. At the Weighing they read one in twelve in a clean voice and the figure goes out warmer than the room it was read in. Here the figure shows through every remedy on the back of the sheet, and nobody has asked me to check it against what it cost.

> *At the foot, in a hand nobody will own:*
>
> The book keeps what was drawn; the room keeps what was held.
> The child went between them and neither entered the hand.

I asked Orrel whether he had seen the lead, and he sent me back a brown edge and a child warm and not better, and the clerk sent back *No such column*. Three answers and no hand. Tomorrow I shall go to a house where a child is worse at the moment everybody says she is better, and I shall put the stone under her feet and count with her, and write down what I saw in a place the clerk cannot carry out, because a child is not an account and a woman at a bedside wants a hand more than she wants a correction.
