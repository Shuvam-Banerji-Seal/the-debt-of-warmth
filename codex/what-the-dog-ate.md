I keep the south ground at Greybank for the factor. It is not a post a man boasts of. The dead laid there are mostly such as came back thin out of the district, and they go into shallow clay with a potsherd at the head and no slate read aloud over them. My work is to see that they are not opened twice. Once is the hearth-maids and the diggers; the second is somebody else, and it is the second I am paid to stand against. I carry a billhook and a short spear with the head gone grey on the quay stone, and I write this at the end of a watch with a tallow end in a pot and the cold coming up through the soles of my boots.

The grey thumb at the head is Ash. I have tried to knock it off and it went into the fibre. That is what Ash does. There is nothing mysterious in it, except that a man who has never held a pot over a room where a promise was not kept will call it dirt and be done with it.

# The ground

The ground lies south of the town beyond the Ilm, a short way past the winter ford, and in Stone you may cross on the ice if you know where the ice is sound. There are two sorts of grave. The old ones are long and set in rows with a headboard gone soft at the foot. The new ones are small and close under the south fence, dug by Mott, who is paid by the grave and not by the hour, so that he has an interest in their being shallow. I have no quarrel with him. A deep grave in this clay costs a man his winter.

What I noticed first, and wrote down before I knew what to call it, is that the new small graves do not take the frost like the old ones. On a morning in Bone I laid the back of my hand on six mounds in the row. Four were cold clay and two had a little softness under the crust, the way a banked coal is soft under its skin of grey. I have no name for that and I have set no cause beside it. Mott says it is warmth rising off the river. The diggers say the clay was turned late. Both may be true. I have kept the shape of it, which is the only work a watchman has.

# What comes

It is a dog. I will set that down first, because the hearths will not. It has a dog's teeth, a dog's neck, a dog's way of putting its head on one side when it listens, and the long narrow muzzle of a dog that has been fed out of a ditch. It is bigger than the yard hounds at the Tallow Works and thinner. Its coat is the colour of wet clay and carries no grey on the outside; the grey is in the mouth and under the nails, and a man who has opened one will know where I mean.

It comes out of the low ground south of the fence in the second watch, which is when the tallow in the hut is down to a knuckle and the men on the quay have stopped shouting at one another. It does not bark. The diggers have a song about the barking which is wrong in every particular, and I have set the song down below with the wrong part standing, because a thing sung wrong for twenty years is part of the ground as much as the graves are.

It does not attack a man. I have stood within two paces of one with my spear down and it went on with its work and never looked at me. But put a man between it and a grave it has chosen and it will bite. That is the whole rule, and there is no courage in testing it twice.

# The bite

Mott put his foot in a hole at Ash 5 and came down on one knee with his hand in the clay, and the dog was already there. I struck it across the back with the spear-shaft. It went away three paces and stood, which is what it does; it does not run off and it does not come back at a man who has hit it. Mott's bite was two small holes on the thumb-side of the hand, grey at the edges, and not much blood. We washed with salt and water and bound it. At the end of the tend it was still open, with the grey where the edges had been.

Salt does not hold that bite. The physician at Greybank told Mott to keep it warm, which is what physicians say when they have nothing else to say, and Mott kept it warm and the hand went on being a hand with a hole in it. He has a rag on it now and will not show it at the market. I put the size of the holes in the margin because a man cannot close his hand and I have had to watch him try. The clerk has written over them, which I did not ask him to do.

> *In the narrow upright hand of the factor's clerk, written along the margin:* Wound: not entered. No deduction arises.

I have left his hand standing. A clerk's hand is an iron thing and has its use: it keeps a man from arguing with what he cannot change.

# The song

Sung by the diggers going out to the south ground in the dark, not coming back. The second line is their error and not mine. I have not corrected it, because Mott sings it loudest and would take it ill.

> Two for the big and three for the small,
> it barks at the wall and it comes to the wall;
> it puts its nose to the new-turned ground
> and takes what the ground has not yet made cold.
>
> Count them going and count them back,
> a horn for the reeve and a phial for the jar;
> the grey in the mouth and the grey on the hand,
> and one that is kept and not entered.

# The tally

These are the nights I kept a true account of. There were others I did not write because my hands were full and one I did not write because I had drunk. I have set down both sorts as they stand. The figure in the last column is mine, not the clerk's.

| Night | Place | Opened | Grey gathered | Lost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bone 4 | Small row, south fence | one | one | none |
| Bone 13 | Small row, west end | two | one | none; wind off the river |
| Bone 22 | Old grave under the alder | none | none | spear lost in the clay |
| Ash 5 | Small row, third mound | one | one | Mott's hand |
| Ash 16 | Two graves, the old ground | two | two | none |
| Ash 27 | New ground beyond the fence | three | one | dog stood and did not go |

> *In the narrow upright hand:* Openings: six. Loss of property: nil. No entry arises.

There is no property in a grave, so he is right, and he has written *nil* where I wrote Mott's hand. I have set my line under his and left both where they fell, the way a digger leaves the clay on top of the pot and knows what is underneath it.

# The jar and the horn

There is a man comes up the south road at the dusk of Account-day with a jar under his coat and no horse. He is of the fen people and speaks little. He pays five bits a phial and asks no questions, which is the price of quiet ash in the Fenmarch and the reason a man asks no questions at it. I do not know what dog it was that gave the grey, and I have never asked him what house will buy it. I have held the jar while he filled it from the thing we scrape out of the beast when one is taken. I have never taken the bits. A man may say that and still know it is not enough.

The horns go a different way. A reeve of the Standing Post came in Frost and paid two bits a horn, and the horns went south to be cut into count-sticks. I asked him what a stick counts that a slate cannot. He said a stick cannot be read by a man who is not standing over it, which seemed to him a virtue and to me a great deal of trouble. But the diggers like them. Mott keeps one in his belt and turns it when he is deciding which row to open, and it has more notches than there are graves, which I have not asked him to explain.

# What the hearths say

The hearths say the dog guards the graves. I have stood and watched it go to the small new ones first and pass the old ones by, and I have seen what it leaves in the clay. A thing that guards is not a thing that eats; but those are words too fine for me, and I have written only the order in which it went. From the south fence to the third mound, then the west end, then the old rows if the watch is long and the tallow is low. That is the whole of what I know. I have not written why.

# At the head and in the margins

The Ash thumb at the head is mine; I held the docket down in the draught that comes under the watch-hut door and did not know I had marked it. The brown spot on the second leaf fell there from Mott's hand when he sat down to have the rag changed, and I have not scraped it, because it is the only entry on this leaf that cost anybody anything. The boot-print across the foot came off the morning I crossed the ford after the ice went thin and came up to the calf in silt; it has dried into the grain and will not rub out. The nick at the fore-edge is the billhook, from the night the dog stood at Ash 27 and I struck at it and missed. The charcoal is rubbed thin where I carried the leaf in my pocket against my thigh. The clerk's two lines I have not touched. The song is as the diggers sang it, barking and all.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> What goes into the ground is kept by somebody else. Ask the jar.

There is new low ground beyond the south fence that I have not walked, and the dog comes out of it with clay on its chest that is not the clay of these graves. I will go in next Bone with a fresh spear and Mott's counting-stick, and I will count them going and count them back, and if the grey comes from a grave nobody laid I will set that down too, in my own hand, with the verb left in it.
