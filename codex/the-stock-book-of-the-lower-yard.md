I value a house's goods before I value the people under its roof. This is the milder half of the trade and I have never come to like it. The thane's clay levy falls on pots and tile and fuel; the Court's warmth levy falls on the vessels that made them, and a man may be counted twice in one morning without ever being asked to stand. I come down from Slate Row with a board cover under my arm, and the woman of the house sets a charcoal tally on the table for her own figures, and we do not look at one another while we work.

Twelve copper bits to a silver mark; twenty marks to a Sere crown that comes north and will not spend here. The Court books warmth in embers and calls an ember five marks, which no man has exchanged, and I have no row for embers in this book, because no ember has ever sat on a kiln-house shelf. One in twelve is printed on the notice and is the Court's figure; it does not touch these pots, which is the mercy of the clay levy and not of the Court. A whole pot answers full under the knuckle and a cracked one flat, and neither answer is a figure, and I have kept both answers out of the column.

> *In the narrow upright hand of the College, above my second line:* Goods valued for the levy; vessels valued by the collector; the two not joined. *Underneath, in mine:* They are joined in the house, by the same hands and in the same morning. I have set them on two leaves and left them side by side.

# The stock, as I valued it

*Figures in Ledgerhand. The second figure is the house's own, in charcoal, copied from the back of a slip-tally I was allowed to hold but not to keep. The remarks are mine. A dash is a place where the house set nothing, not a nought.*

| Article | My figure | The house's | Remark |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coarse pots, the score, green from the third firing | 4 bits | 5 bits | The fifth bit is for a pot set aside for a death-house and not sold. Not entered at the Cross. |
| Pots mended with brass, the score | 2 bits | — | A mended pot fetches less and holds as long. The house will not write the second half. |
| Tile, the hundred, stacked by the gable | 9 bits | 9 bits | The only row on this leaf that agrees. I have marked it and cannot say why. |
| Slip, the pit, worked and standing | 3 marks | 2 marks | They count the clay, I count the afternoon it took to mix. Both are honest. |
| Lead glaze, a jar, mixed up the terrace | 2 marks | — | Sweet at the mouth. The mixer would not stand still for the weighing. |
| Coal, the barrow, banked behind the damper | 5 bits | 5 bits | Stone price, not Grain. The house has kept the winter figure in summer, which is a kindness to me and a lie to itself. |
| Barley, the dressed sack, under wool at the rack end | 1 mark 4 bits | 1 mark 4 bits | Turned for damp twice while I stood there. No figure for the turning, and none asked. |
| Eels, smoked, a basket from the Fishstairs | 3 bits the eel | 3 bits | The eel-woman's figure and mine agree, which may be the only thing in this house that does. |
| Salt herring, a barrel | 2 bits the fish | — | Barrel not counted. The barrel is the woman's mother's and has outlived two levies. |
| Tallow, a stone, rendered at the works | 2 marks | 2 marks | Smell carried up from the north-east on Tallow 6. The figure did not carry. |
| Peat, a stack against the north wall | 5 bits | 2 bits | She has written the Grain price in the Stone month and says the stack came cheap. I have left both. |
| Pig, alive, in the strip behind the wheel-shed | 6 marks | 6 marks | cried twice at the Cross and a bit paid to have it cried. The bit is in my own pocket and not on this leaf. |
| The well, with bucket and rope | not valued | 1 mark | I set *not valued*. The house sets a mark. Water is the Court's if it is under a town, and the house's if a woman draws it. |
| The kiln, with lid, damper and back room | not valued | not valued | Neither of us has found a figure and neither of us has said so aloud. |
| The loaf shelf, the drying racks, the boards | not valued | — | A thing a house stands on is not a thing a house sells. |
| The ash in the corner | not valued | not valued | Left where it lay all of the dark and burnt on the morning after. Both figures stand at nothing. |

Draws valued on this leaf: sixteen. Rows that agree: four. Rows the house left blank: five. Rows I left blank: four. The four are not the five.

# The things that would not take a figure

There is a charcoal mark under the front edge of the loaf shelf, made by somebody who knelt to write it and then covered it with the shelf again. I saw it because the woman moved the shelf while I was weighing the tallow and did not know I had seen. I did not ask her what it counted. A tally kept where a slate would tax it is a tally with a reason, and a reason asked after in a kiln-house gets carried up the terrace and comes back down as weather. So I have put it on no row and given it no place in the columns, and it is the one thing on this leaf that I have entered nowhere at all, which is a different thing from entering nothing.

The pot on the third row has a brass seam across its belly, set by a hand that knew the note before it set the staple. I knocked it to hear, as I knock every vessel before I value it, and it answered full, and the woman went on with her charcoal as though a pot could not speak in front of an assessor. I have written *holds as long* in the remark because it is true and because she would not write it. The green ware beside it gave a small flat sound that stopped at the surface, and I set the figure down all the same. A figure is entered whether or not the thing can hold what it is said to hold. That is the whole art of this trade, and it is not a difficult art to learn.

The fifth pull of the fired coal was not supplied while I stood there. Four low pulls came up under the pot and the place where the fifth should have stood was silent, and the woman put her hand to the mark by the kiln mouth before she lifted the lid, for one breath, and at no shelf and no basket after it. I did not enter the fifth as nought. A nought is a figure and can be added; a silence cannot. There is no row on any levy sheet for the breath a woman holds before she opens a kiln, which is perhaps the most sensible thing the College has ever left out.

# What the borrowed hands did

The man from the College came down on a market morning while the bell was going four, four and a gap below the Cross. He did not sit down, and he took the leaf up with two fingers as though the charcoal on the back might come off on him.

Where I wrote *I valued the slip, the afternoon it took to mix*, he struck out *I valued* and set *the slip was valued* above, and above that *the valuation is entered*, which is further from the pit than the first. I have put my words back underneath. A sentence with nobody in it is a sentence a woman cannot ask about, and she will ask, always at the door, always after the board cover is shut.

Against the pot with the brass seam he struck *holds as long* and wrote *serviceable* above it. Both stand. *Serviceable* is the word the notice uses and the word the moot reads in a clean voice; *holds as long* is what I heard the pot say under my knuckle. I have not chosen between them and would thank any man who told me I must.

He struck the column of the house's charcoal figures and wrote *No such column* over it in the same upright hand he uses for one in twelve. He had counted every row before he wrote it, which is how I know the column troubled him. I drew it back underneath and left his words standing above. A man does not count what he has not looked at.

# The song the kiln hands keep at the boards

*Sung low, going down from the terrace with a basket on the hip. Never at the Cross, and never while a man from Slate Row is on the road.*

> Nine on the board and eight in the rack,
> one in the ash and none coming back;
> the clerk writes ink and the house writes coal,
> and neither one tells what the firing cost.
>
> Four bits the score and a mark for the pig,
> the pot keeps the heat and the shelf keeps the rig;
> count what you can and leave what you can't —
> the well has a figure, the mark has nought.

A woman at the drying racks sang the first two lines to me while she turned the dressed grain with the back of her hand and would not sing the last while her neighbour stood within hearing, which is how I know the last line is the one that matters. I have copied it as it came and put no gloss on it.

# In the margins and in the gutter

The iron-gall at the head is mine, from holding the board cover open through a morning in a room with no draught worth the name. The slip in the gutter came off a thrower's forearms when she set her tally against mine to wait for a figure; it is bone-pale and goes into the grain of the paper and stays. The green bead in the lower corner is lead glaze and not slip; the girl who mixed it leaned over to see what her jar had fetched, with the sweetness still at one corner of her mouth, touched the leaf, said nothing and went back up the terrace. I have not washed it off. The tallow thumb against the foot is from holding the lamp low while the woman of the house answered a question I had not quite asked. The grey in the outer fold came off my own cuff, going down past a corner I had no business putting my hand into. The charcoal dust along the back is from the spoiled slip-tally laid against this leaf and is the only mark on it that was never made in this room. The pale ring in the lower margin is where a wet pot stood for a moment while I wrote; it dried and left its own small circle, which I have measured and not entered. The bare square is a figure I scraped away with a knife, which was the figure the house kept and would not have read aloud. I have not written it back. On the Last Day the ash in the corners is swept up and burnt in the morning and called dealt with. There is no Last Day for a stock book.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The Court values what can be carried away. The house values what keeps it standing. The kiln is in both columns and in neither.

I asked the woman of the house, a carter who takes the steep street, the man from the College, and a girl come up from the glaze room, and had four answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written.

The leaf goes back into the board cover and the cover under my arm. At the next levy the thane's man will ask for the figures and I shall read him the ones the notice allows, which are the only ones that travel. The house will go on keeping its charcoal on the back of spoiled tallies, and the pot with the brass seam will go on answering full under any knuckle that cares to ask, and the fifth pull will go on standing silent with nobody to pay it. I shall set down what I can carry away, and leave the rest where it stood.
