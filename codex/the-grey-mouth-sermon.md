I keep the niche under the fallen arch at the mouth of the Old Kiln, and nobody asked me for this. I write because the old woman spoke to a dead kiln and the kiln gave back nothing, and I did not want the words to go the way the smoke goes. She spoke first to the ash, then to the jars, then to the road that goes north to Sill, and last of all to us, which is the order a kiln-woman uses when there is a watch upon the road and somebody may be listening who wants a figure. I have set her words down in Grathic, with the verbs where she left them.

The soot at the head is mine and comes off the arch; the grey under one nail is this place's clay, not Ash, though a clerk would call it so. The ring is barley-water set down at the first voice by a woman who did not drink it. The green bead is not slip: a girl of High Kiln leaned over with the sweetness at her mouth, touched the corner and went back up the road. I have told her what that sweetness does to a gum, and she has told me her mother had it and held a cup to the end. The frost took two words off the fore-edge and I have not written them back, because a song with two words gone is a song said slowly. I have moved none of it.

# The manner of the leaving, as the mouth actually has it

*Written small, from watching, not from any order the Thane has printed.*

- Come before the watch's round and be gone before it comes back. The throat is the Thane's. No niche is set beyond the fallen arch and no jar is carried past it.
- Set your jar on the lip with its mouth uppermost. Mouth to the sky is for a thing done with giving; mouth to the ground is for a thing still asked to hold.
- Do not glaze a jar of this clay. It will not take. A woman who glazes grey clay spends good lead on a thing that has refused it, and then wonders why the colour went wrong.
- Tap the jar before you set it. A sound jar answers full under the knuckle and a grey one answers flat, a little dead sound that stops at the surface. Do not say what you heard where a man with a slate is standing.
- Leave what you brought: a crust, a bit, a sherd, a knot. What is set down at a dead kiln stays at a dead kiln, which is more than can be said of the living ones.
- When the bell comes up the south road from the Cross, stop. It has counted something that is not your jar. Four, four and a gap, and the gap is not one of yours to fill.

She said the last twice, once to the ash and once to a man up the road with his hands in his sleeves, who was not from Sill and was writing nothing.

# The sermon, as she said it

## Of the grey

She put her palm flat on the fallen arch, where the firebrick had gone the colour of a pot fired and left.

“This clay will not take a glaze,” she said. “I have watched women carry good lead up from High Kiln and spend it on a jar dug here, and the jar comes out grey as it went in, the lead sitting on it like a promise made to a person who has gone away. A thing can be made and still not take what is put upon it. That is the whole of my preaching, and I have had seven and twenty winters to find another sentence and could not.”

She turned a grey jar so the row could see the lip, which had no colour on it.

“Do not tell me it is a bad jar. It was asked to be something it was not made to be. That difference has a place on this lip where nobody sets a jar and nobody sweeps.”

> *The people answered, and said it twice, once for the mouth and once for the road:*
>
> The clay will not take a glaze;
> it has not forgotten the fire.

## Of the ash

“The ash in the throat is deeper than a man,” she said to the arch. “I have never been in and never will, and the watch is set on the road to see nobody does. A man came this morning with his coat done against the cold and went a pace past the arch, and the watch turned him, and he came back and left his crust on the lip and went off with his hands in his sleeves. I have set the crust on the sill where the birds may have it.”

There was a silence after this, the kind that has four in it and a gap where the fifth would stand.

“They tell us the kiln's breath is a merciful death,” she said. “A man sleeps before a banked kiln with the damper shut and does not wake, and the district calls it merciful because no slate goes up to Sill over it and nobody has to be sent for. I have known three men go that way and said the word at two of the graves, because it is a word people want at a grave. Here I will not say it. A man lay down. That is what I know. Whether he was tired or cold or wanted something he could not ask for, I do not know, and the ash does not, and the district has entered nothing.”

A woman near me put her thumb into the grey at the rim of a jar and left it there and did not say whose jar it was. I have put no name to it, and there is no name on the leaf.

> *The people answered:*
>
> The clay will not take a glaze;
> it has not forgotten the fire.

## Of the figure

A factor's clerk come out from Greybank to measure the winter clay stood at the end of the row with a slate to his forearm. He asked what a jar was worth that would not take a glaze.

“Nothing at the Cross,” she said. “No carter will carry it and no market has asked its price. I have set one here every Bone for seven and twenty years and never wanted a figure for it.”

“Then it is nil,” he said.

“It is worth nothing to you,” she said. “Those are two sentences and I will not have one put over the other. One in twelve is printed on the notice and read at the Cross and is not a lie. It is a true thing said to a room that is not this one.”

“The measure is retained,” he said, in the voice that takes the speaker out of the sentence. “Nothing is lost that we have kept.”

“I keep a jar,” she said. “I do not keep a person. If the Hearth wants what went up the throat of this kiln, let it come down the south road and fetch it. It has not come yet.”

> *In the narrow upright hand of the clerk, set above the old words on the borrowed docket:* Grey clay is inert; the jars are unsaleable and unentered. No measure arises. Enter as nil.
> *
> *Underneath, in mine:* I have put *worth nothing to you* back under his *is nil*. His sentence travels well and arrives empty, which is what a man wants who has to carry it eighteen leagues to a table. I have left his hand above and mine below.

> *The people answered:*
>
> The clay will not take a glaze;
> it has not forgotten the fire.

## Of them that went into the ground

“They teach that the dead come back warm,” she said. “I have stood at four returns in this district. Two came back as other people, with a hankering for a table they had never sat at. One said a word in a tongue I have forgot. One came back thin and wanted nothing and sat by the door until somebody put a crust in her hand. The Court calls each a settling and reads a figure over it.”

She turned the grey jar mouth down on the lip and left it so.

“I am not saying the Return is a lie. I am saying I have not seen the thing they say it is, and the difference sits on this lip where no slate is set. The rich buy the grey in a phial and call it quiet ash, and a man who cannot remember his own dead sleeps the better. I have never bought one and never will. Not from piety. I have no use for a sleep that must be bought.”

A girl of the Clay Yard asked what lay under the firebrick at the back of the niche, and the old woman gave her a year: Y.H. 668, the Rising. The girl asked again in a different way and had the same year. A date is the only answer this mouth gives, and the Court likes a date and does not ask what it covers.

> *The people answered, and the children said it twice, once for the moot and once for them that came late:*
>
> The clay will not take a glaze;
> it has not forgotten the fire.

# The grey-song

*Sung low, standing, with the palm on the fallen arch and a jar set mouth-up on the lip. The row says the last four with her. There is a place where the fifth line should stand, the one the watch leaves, and I have left it as it is sung.*

> Grey on the lip and grey in the throat,
> the jar came up out of the clay;
> a glaze will not take and a name will not stay,
> and the watch goes by in the grey.
>
> Four for the jars and four for the road,
> and a place where a fifth would stand;
> the bell keeps a gap and the ash keeps a word,
> and neither is entered by hand.
>
>
> The clay will not take a glaze;
> it has not forgotten the fire.
> Take up what you brought and go home by the south road,
> and leave the throat to the watch and the Thane's desire.

A girl asked what the space was for. Nobody answered her. The clerk put a nought against it later, which I have left where he set it.

# The row of jars, as the mouth keeps it

*A jar set on the lip stays on the lip and no tally from Sill has ever counted one. These I have set down where they stood.*

| Jar | Set by | What was asked of her | What the mouth gave |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grey, unglazed, lip chipped | the old woman, every Bone | nothing; she sets it and goes | another Bone |
| Grey, with a pale line at the rim | a girl of High Kiln | whether the sweet would kill her | the clay, which would not take the glaze |
| Grey, with a copper bit shut inside | a man off the south road | whether his deduction was lawful | the printed figure, read at the Cross, unchallenged |
| A sherd with a branching mark | nobody will say | nothing | kept, and not turned up in company |
| The crust on the sill | the man the watch turned | nothing | the birds, before noon |
| The fifth place | the watch | — | not set; left the length of one |

There were jars before these and my mother could set a winter to each, and I have not written the winters, because a row of years is a thing a clerk can carry away and a jar is not. The sherd with the branching mark I have not turned up, for an answer given to no question is a kind of theft.

# What the clerk did

He came out from Greybank on the morning after with ink in two fingers, borrowed the docket while I was at the lip, and gave it back with the verbs taken out.

- Where she said *a thing can be made and still not take what is put upon it*, he wrote *the vessel is incapable of retention* above and left hers below.
- Where she said *a man lay down*, he wrote *a life was voluntarily resigned* and underlined it twice. He did not ask which man, which was the first true thing he did all morning.
- At the fifth place he put a round nought and wrote *vacancy*, and when I asked what a place was worth that held no jar, he said nothing a clerk could enter.
- At the gap in the song he wrote *vacancy* again, and at the branching sherd *mark of no significance; not a figure*, which is true, and the only true thing on his slate I could have said myself.

I have put the verbs back underneath and left his hand above. He takes them out because a thing always true has no need of them. I put them back because a woman with her palm on a cold arch wants to know who did the refusal: her own clay that failed, or somebody else's lead.

# At the head and in the gutter

The soot at the head is mine and will not wash out of the fold; I rest the docket on the arch and do not notice I have marked it. The grey under the nail is the mouth's own and comes up however I wash. The frost took two words off the fore-edge and I have left them gone. The ring is barley-water set down by a woman who stood through the speaking of them that went into the ground and did not drink it, and took it up before her daughter saw it. The green bead is not slip, and I have kept the leaf out of the sun since it took. The grey in the gutter came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business on a niche; I have not swept it, and there is no Last Day at this mouth. The charred end is from my dip, which burned down while the clerk wrote *nil* over a jar that will not take a glaze.

# At the foot

Struck through once and written again underneath, very small, in a hand that is not mine, not the old woman's and not the clerk's:

> What the glaze would not take, the grey kept.
>
> What the grey kept, no slate has entered.

I asked the old woman, the girl from High Kiln, the man off the south road and the clerk, and had four answerings and no hand. I have left the line struck through and standing, which is the only honest way to keep a sentence you cannot sign.

The docket goes back in the tin under the loose firebrick with the bit in its fold. Next Bone the jars will be set mouth-up and the watch will go by with its spear gone blue at the head, and the bell will come up the south road four, four and a gap, and the clay will refuse whatever is put on it. If the clerk comes again I shall let him take the verbs out and put them back, and leave the nought and the fifth where they stand.
