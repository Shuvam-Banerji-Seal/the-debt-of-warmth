I sink wells. There is one at Cold Acre that has never frozen, and I have kept it from the ice nineteen winters by knowing where the water goes under the ridge. A man who knows where water is knows where it is not, which is why the factor at Greybank sent for me. Three of us went south in Ash: a surveyor out of the factor's house with a slate and a new coil of rope, a Fen man who would take us as far as the first doorway and no farther, and me. We went to measure the old wells of the Cantons. I had never measured a dry one.

I have measured six now. I have come back with nothing to tell except the lie of the ground, which is what men come back with from that place, and I had better set the ground down plain before it turns into a story.

# The road in

The Cantons lie sixty leagues south of Sill, and there is no road a cart can make in a day. We walked. On the third day the ruts came back under the turf, deep as a cart's wheel and empty of anything a wheel had drawn through them in eight hundred years. A road is a thing somebody meant to come back along, and the ground had kept that much and let everything else go.

There is no gate and no due. The Fen man stopped where the first doorway stood and would not go past the shadow of it. He is a man of few words and gives them to the ground before he gives them to a person. He said a dry man is a dangerous man, and that he had stood on the boardwalk long enough to know one, and then he sat down on a stone and took no part in what followed, though he watched us the whole while with his back turned.

The first thing a well-sinker notices in a strange country is where the water would be if there were any. In the Cantons the low ground holds no damp, the ditches that were cut to drain it are dry cuts, and the clay underfoot has gone the colour of a pot fired too often.

There was no smoke. That is the thing every man writes down first, and I have written it down too, because it is true and because there is nothing else to write at that hour. In Grath a man can tell the hour by the smoke over a roof. Here the sky went on being a sky with nothing said in it, and the surveyor and I ate our bread standing up, though there was nowhere to sit that was not a step of somebody's house.

# The six wells

*Measured in the order we came to them. The rope's figure is what my line brought up; the eye's figure is what I saw at the mouth; the last column is what the surveyor wrote. I have set his hand above mine and left mine under it, which is the only way a man reads a slate in this country without lying.*

| Well | What the rope said | What the eye saw | What was entered |
|---|---|---|---|
| First, by the road | full length down, dry coming up, iron on the wet end | stones laid round the mouth in the old order, every stone where a stone expects to be | dry; no entry |
| Second, in the doorway-yard | dry; the line came up with white dust on it | no mark of a bucket on the rim, and yet the rim worn smooth on one side | dry |
| Third, under the fallen wall | the rope fouled below and would not come back till I let the coil go | the mouth worn deep where a hand would rest and a hand would pull | dry; obstruction below, no value |
| Fourth, by the last standing hearth | dry | ash in it that is not grey | dry |
| Fifth | dry | a square of bare clay set into the stonework above it, where something had hung | dry |
| Sixth, beyond the doorways | dry | the stones laid so tidy that a man's first thought is to ask who laid them | dry |

A dry well is not a hole. A dry well is a statement made in stone, and I have no trade in answering it. In Cold Acre a well that fails is a well with a cause: a seam that has shifted, a taint, a nothing that the water used to do. These six had none. The water was down out of sight, which is a different thing from being gone, and the stones were laid round the mouths in the old order, which a man does not do when he is leaving in a hurry.

The surveyor wrote *dry* against each and *no entry* against the first. I asked him why the first was entered and the others not. He said the first stood nearest the road and a figure had to begin somewhere. Then he turned his slate face down, which is a thing men do here when they have seen enough and do not want to be asked what.

# The doorways

We counted nine doorways standing where the walls had gone. In three of them there was a square of bare clay above the lintel where a house-roll had hung, and there was no nail in any of the nine. A nail would have been pulled out and carried away, or would have rusted where it stood; there were no nails and no marks where nails had been, only the bare square as clean as the day the clay was laid.

In one doorway a slate still stands in its notch. The figures on it are not figures I could read, and I have set them down as shapes and not as numbers, because a man who calls a shape a figure has begun to explain it. One groove goes deeper than an iron point would cut, and I did not write how it was made, and would not know if I had.

A Vorr came through while we stood there, going north with a bag of nothing and a stick. The surveyor asked him about the slate. The Vorr looked at it a while and said the numbers were correct, and went on. He would not say what they counted. I have put his saying in the margin below rather than in the body of the account, because a sentence a man can carry away and still know nothing of is not an answer and should not be given the room of one.

> *In the margin, in a small hard hand I took for the Vorr's, four figures set down one under another, correctly made. I cannot match them to anything on the road: not to the wells, not to the doorways, not to the nine. I have left them standing where they were written.*

# The hearths

The hearths are cold, and the ash in them is not grey. It is a white dust like bone meal, fine and dry, lying in the hollow where a fire would have been. I have put my hand into four of them. In the fourth there was a little warmth still in the stone under the dust, no more than a stone keeps when the sun has been off it an hour, and I wrote that down and then crossed it out, because a well-sinker who writes a warmth where there should be none will be asked to find it again.

The Fen man said, from his stone with his back turned, that the white is what is left when a promise has been kept too long. I have set that down as it was said and have joined nothing to it. A man who joins a saying to a hollow full of white dust has made a story, and stories are what that country has no need of. In Grath we leave the grey in the corners on the Last Day and burn it in the morning. Here there is no Last Day that anybody keeps, and the white lies where it lies, and nobody sweeps.

# The song of the dry road

*Sung by the Fen man going in, not coming back. He would not sing it within sight of the first doorway, and sang it on the third morning with his face to the north. The fourth line is his; there is a place in his singing of it where a word should stand, and I have set down what I heard rather than fill it.*

> Six wells and never a splash,
> the rope goes down where the water was;
> the stones keep house and the lintel keeps bare,
> and the road keeps the place where the step was.
>
> Four for the corners and one for the nail
> that was never driven and never pulled;
> the Fox has the hearth and the dust has the name,
> and the well keeps the white that the hand never drew.
>
> I went in dry and I came back dry,
> and the dry of the two is not the same;
> one is a man and one is a ground,
> and only the ground has a name for it.
>
> Ask the rope what it brought back ---
> and the rope said nothing, and went on that way.

He said the last line was about the song and not about the wells, and when I asked him what the difference was he said a man who needed it explained would not have sung it. I have copied it as it came and put no gloss on it.

# What the surveyor entered, and what I set under it

At Greybank a clerk of the factor's house borrowed the leaves while I was buying rope. He writes upright and narrow and leaves the doer out of every sentence, as though a well could dry itself and a man could walk sixty leagues without a pair of feet. He did not strike a word of mine. He put his words above and left mine where they stood, which is the readiest way of correcting a man I know.

- Against *the stones were laid in the old order* he wrote *masonry, derelict*, which is a true description of stones and leaves out the order.
- Against *the rim was worn where a hand would pull* he wrote *wear, cause unknown*. I have set *cause: a hand* underneath in charcoal, which does not keep, and am content it should not.
- Against *the white dust in the hearths* he wrote *residue, unclassified*, and against the Vorr's saying, *statement not entered*. He was right in the language of his office. A statement the office cannot enter is a statement the office need not answer.
- Against my six rows he wrote *six dry wells; no population; property unoccupied*, and underlined it twice. The wells are dry and the place is empty. I have never argued with either figure. What I cannot agree to is the word *unoccupied*, which is a word for a house from which somebody has gone, and not for a house that has gone from somebody.

I have left both hands standing, his above and mine below, as a repaired vessel keeps the old break under the new staple and does not pretend the first hand never touched the clay. A clerk who takes the hand out of a well leaves the well standing by itself, and a well standing by itself has never dried anybody.

# At the head and in the gutter

The white in the fold came off the hearths and is bone meal fine; it will not wash out and I have stopped trying. The grey in the gutter is silt off the south road and came honestly by its place, worked in on the walk back when the thaw had begun under the frost. The tallow at the head is my own lamp, set too near the leaf on the last night before we turned north. The rope-fibre in the sewing came off the coil that fouled in the third well; I cut it out with a knife and sewed it back, because a rope that has been down a well is not a rope to throw away. The chip in the lower corner is off the slate in the doorway. I prised it with a nail and it came away more easily than a chip should, and the mark on it is not a figure and I have never called it one. The square of bare paper is where I scraped a word away with a knife-point and put no other in its place; it is the only square in this account I made myself. The grey along the outer fold is not mine and not the clerk's. I have not swept it. There is no Last Day in the Cantons and I have not found one since I came back.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> Six wells and never a drop to count,
> nine doorways and no one to come through;
> the rope keeps the shape of what it didn't bring up,
> and the slate in the door keeps a figure for you.
>
> Ask the dust what it kept so long.
> Ask the stone why the water went.
> Ask the road whose wheel it was.

I asked the surveyor, the Fen man, and the Vorr who came through, and had three answers and no hand, as I have had before. I have left the line where it was written.

The leaves go back into the tin, and the tin into the coil, and the coil onto the shelf beside the rope I use at Cold Acre. Tomorrow I sink a well for a household on the ridge, and it will give water, and I shall be paid in marks for finding what the ground was willing to give. In the thaw I mean to walk south again with a longer line. I have not told the factor and do not know what I shall ask the well when I get there. A man who writes why before he goes has already decided what he will find, and I have made that mistake once, below the Old Kiln, and do not mean to make it twice.
