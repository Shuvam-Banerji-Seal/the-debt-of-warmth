To Luli, who is to be married on the flood. From Vessa of the Quay, who holds the share through her mother and has never held it through a man.

I write on the back of a Council draw that was left out in the rain. The figures have gone where the Council's figures go, which is nowhere a man can put his hand on, and what is left is paper enough for a letter. It is not a sheet I would choose. There is no paper at the Quay that is mine, and a letter written on a man's paper is a letter a man may read if he has a mind to, so I have written the parts that matter in charcoal and gone over them in iron-gall, and the ink beads where the salt has got into the grain. Do not hold this to the fire to read it, as your aunt does with everything. It goes soft and smells of the pans, and a letter that smells of the pans is a letter the whole Quay will want to hear.

You are to be married on the flood. I have no word for a gift and we have a word for a share, so I will put it the way we put everything else: this is not a gift. It is the cord. Your grandmother tied it when she went aboard and carried it nineteen years in a tin kept dry against the practice of the country, and I have kept it dry for nineteen more. A cord kept dry is not a cord kept from use; it is a cord that has not yet been asked. You will be asked on the flood.

Three things, and then a question. Read the three first. A woman who reads the question before the instructions will answer the question and go away thinking the instructions were the courtesy.

The first is the share. It goes through you and not through him. Your mother held it, I hold it, you will hold it. A man who marries into a boat-owning family is come aboard, and his opinion on the boat is counted at half. Half is not none, and I have known men who could bear it and men who could not; the ones who could not were the ones who said *half* loudest at the table. Daro has stood at the rail for two seasons and waited for whoever was speaking to finish before he said anything about the boat. That is not the same as being counted at half. It is the nearest thing to it I have seen in a man come off the coast road, and I have not once said so in a room where he could hear me, and I am not saying so now. This is a letter. It is different.

The second is the knot. It is tied the way our knots are tied, left over right with the tail tucked and a half-turn taken in it that no tally-man has ever counted, because it does not look like a figure. Keep it that way. A contract written on salt-paper in wet weather is no contract at all, which is the virtue of it, and every fortune in this port has turned on that fact at least once. A cord tied in dry weather is still a cord when the paper has gone. I have never entered the cord anywhere and the Council has no word for the thing it holds. Do not teach them one.

The third is the salt. Eight sacks go with you, one for the pan and the others for the house, and the house sacks are the ones to watch, because a sack set down inside a door has a way of becoming a figure before the week is out. If a man from the tally house writes against the sacks, ask him which tide settled it. He will say the figure settled it. Ask him again after the turn. He will have gone. This is not rudeness; it is the whole of our dealings with the tally house and it has kept us fed.

Now the question. When they ask you at the table whose share you hold, will you say your mother's name or your own? I am not asking which is the true answer. I am asking which one you will say, because I will not be at that table, and a name said at a table is a name that can be written down, and a written name keeps better than a cord does in the rain. Say the one you can say twice.

# The shares, as they are kept in the tin, not as they stand on the Council's paper

*The first column is the order of the women. The last is what the tally house has entered, which is not the same thing and was never meant to be.*

| Who held | How she came by it | What was tied | What stands on paper |
|---|---|---|---|
| Your grandmother | Through her mother, before the Council put the boats to contract | A bead of cliff-glass pressed into the knot | vacancy |
| Your mother | Through her mother, in a wet year when the paper went | Left over right, tail tucked | share, one |
| I | Through my mother, and not through my husband | Two half-turns taken where no thumb rests | current |
| You | Not yet. The cord is in the tin and dry | The half-turn your grandmother left in it | — |

A dash is a place where nothing stood to be entered. It is not a nought, and the difference has kept more than one woman out of the lower room.

# The share-song

*Sung going out on the flood, never at the tally house. The third line is the old line. The girls at the Quay sing it wrong on purpose, because a song that says what a share is worth is a song a man can carry to a table and set down there.*

> The share goes down and the share comes in,
> the cord keeps what the paper spins;
> half for the man who comes aboard,
> and the whole for the woman who kept the board.
>
> Sea runs against the wind and the moon gets none,
> the dipper reads the draw before the sun;
> salt on the sack and salt at the seam,
> and the knot is the only thing that means what it means.
>
> We sing the old line wrong and leave it so,
> the place where the mother's name would go;
> count them going and count them back ---
> the cord stays dry and the debt stays black.

I asked a dipper what the third line ought to be and she said it was none of my business and went on with her net. I have left it as the girls sing it.

# In the margin, in a younger hand, set close to the writing

I will say my own. You taught me to keep the count and I am not giving it to a table. Do not tell me again that half is not none; I have heard it since I was nine and I know what it costs.

You have Daro wrong. He stood at the rail because he was waiting for you to finish, not because he knew his place. There is a difference, and you of all people should know it. He has never once said *half* in this house. I would have told you if he had.

When I want to say a thing I cannot write I will name you a tide that has not come, which is the only honest way we have of saying it.

I saw the other cord in the tin. You thought I had not. I have not touched it and will not. Ask me nothing about the half-turn and I will ask you nothing about the bead.

# What the clerk did

A clerk of the tide-tally house borrowed the leaf on a market morning, being a man who cannot leave paper alone. He writes in the leaning hand the damp has nearly taken, so that of his four corrections only two verbs and a figure survive. Where I wrote *I hold the share through my mother and not through my husband*, he set *share passing by women; consideration current* above it, and the two of us went out of the sentence as though a woman were a thing a man could carry off in a cuff. Against the cord he wrote *no instrument*. Against the share-song he wrote *currency not entered*, and against my daughter's answer, *declarant's name recorded*, which is the Court's own art in a small space: she said it, and now it has happened to her.

I have put the verbs back underneath where I could still read them. He takes them out because a thing always true has no need of verbs. We put them in because a woman at a table wants to know which hand to lay the cord with. I have left his figure standing on the outer fold and my daughter's correction standing over my own, and neither of us has struck the other out.

# At the head and in the folds

The bloom at the head is salt out of the paper and not out of the sea, which is a thing only a person who has kept salt-paper knows the difference of. The tar at the second fold is off the oar I held down while the draw was read and went soft under me; it has taken two words of the first paragraph and made them slow, which is no great loss. The fibre across the third crease is wet net-cord, from the net I was mending when I thought of the question and put the leaf down on it. The green bead caught in the stitch is cliff-glass off the Glaze Cliffs, five leagues along the shore, and a girl pressed it there and said nothing, and I have not taken it out. The nick at the fore-edge is my fish-hook and went through when I folded in a hurry at the going of the light. The ring of brine came from a cup set down while I was telling Luli to eat and she would not. The grey print along the outer fold is not mine and not the Council's, and I have not swept it. There is no Last Day for a letter and there is no broom in a tin.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody has claimed

> A share is a thing a woman holds against the water. The water does not ask who paid.

I asked a dipper, a tally clerk, a man come aboard, and a woman who has held a share forty years, and had four answers and no hand, as we have had before. I have left the line where it was written.

The leaf goes back in the tin among the share-cords. On the flood she will be married and the cord will go with her, dry, and nobody at the tally house will know that it has left the country, which is how a thing is kept in Vhalen. The Sallow runs against the wind and is said to follow the Warm rather than the moon, and we have stopped asking it why; a question put to a tide is a question paid for twice. That is the whole of the letter, and it goes on.
