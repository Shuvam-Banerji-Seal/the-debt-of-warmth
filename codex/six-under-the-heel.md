I tread the pit in the mornings before the wheel is warm, and I have done so eleven winters. There are four of us when the water stands high and two when it falls, and the work is the same: foot after bare foot into the slip until the lumps are gone and the clay closes over the instep the way water closes over a stone. You do not see the air go. You feel it come up between the toes and break, and the old women call it the pot's breath and say that a pot which keeps its breath will burst in the firing. I do not know whether this is true. I have seen a pot go flat in a kiln that had been trodden by women who knew their work, and I have seen a pot come out singing from a pit any child might have trampled.

This is not a song for a fire. It is said to the clay. A caller stands at the lip of the pit and says the first line down into it; the treaders say the second with the foot, and the last line of each round is not said aloud but is the lifting of the foot, which is the rest. Say a line to a woman and it becomes an argument. Say it to the clay and the clay tells your foot when to come up.

Six rounds to a tread and the seventh held. The count is not ours. It is the count of a clay-bound year, six that the yard will speak of and one that the household enters into a slate; a man may serve six years in a pit and be in his eighth before the yard will say his name in the same sentence as his debt. We do not sing the seventh. We hold it the way the kiln holds one breath in four, which is to say we leave it where it is and go around it.

I set the words down on the inside of a pot that had gone wrong, because there is no slate in a yard that a pail of water will not spoil and no paper that will last a morning among the tubs. The charcoal went into the clay and stayed there. What follows is what the women said before the man from the counting house borrowed the leaf at Greybank, and what they said again after he had gone back over the north bridge.

# The song, as it is trodden

*Called at the lip, low, into the pit. One line, one round of the foot. The last line of each part is said to the clay and not to a person.*

## The wetting

> The slip is cold and the pit is deep,
> put in the foot where the others keep;
> the clay comes up over the ankle bone,
> takes the print of the foot and keeps its own.
>
> The water lies where the water lay,
> and mud goes down to the mud by day;
> the pit has a lip and the pit has a floor,
> and the seventh stands at the lip and comes no more.

> Six under the heel and the seventh held in;
> the flaw goes shallow and the note comes back full.
> Six under the heel and the foot comes up free;
> what goes out of the clay comes home to me.

## The six rounds

> One for the lump that the heel finds first,
> two for the bubble that breaks and is not;
> three for the water that climbs and goes,
> four for the bone-coloured slip on the toes;
> five for the print of a thumb in the wall,
> six for the seam where the thumb let it fall;
> and the seventh stands where the seventh has stood,
> with the cold of the pit and the print in the mud.

> Six under the heel and the seventh held in;
> the flaw goes shallow and the note comes back full.
> Six under the heel and the foot comes up free;
> what goes out of the clay comes home to me.

## At the wheel

*Not sung at the wheel itself. Said under the breath by the thrower while the pot goes up, with the thumb kept steady and the knuckle kept for afterwards.*

> The wheel goes round and the seat is worn
> on the left where a man leaned, and the lean stays on;
> put the pot where his thumb was put
> and listen to what the pot has got.
>
> The wall goes up and the thumb goes in
> at the one place where the wall is thin;
> tap it once with the knuckle bone ---
> full is a note that a house may own.
>
> Tap it once and it answers flat;
> sweep it around where the potter sat.
> A crack once made is a crack kept true;
> leave the crack showing and the yard goes through.

> Six under the heel and the seventh held in;
> the flaw goes shallow and the note comes back full.
> Six under the heel and the foot comes up free;
> what goes out of the clay comes home to me.

## At the kiln

> The kiln draws breath and the kiln lets go,
> four for the breath and a place in the row;
> the fire takes what the fire will take
> and leaves a brown edge on the loaf we bake.
>
> The glaze runs sweet where the lead has been
> and a girl at the cup has a pale at the gum;
> she sets the cup down on the bench and goes
> out to the racks where the dry slip shows.
>
> What the fire takes out of the clay
> the house finds in the corner at dawn;
> we leave it there and we leave the pot
> with the crack showing, and carry on.

> Six under the heel and the seventh held in;
> the flaw goes shallow and the note comes back full.
> Six under the heel and the foot comes up free;
> what goes out of the clay comes home to me.

# The verse they do not tread when a man from Slate Row is in the yard

I have it from the caller, with the space left before it, which is the space the treaders leave in the singing. She would not say it to the clerk and would not say it within sight of his cuff. She said it to the pot I had set by the wall, with her back to the road.

> Six for the years that the slate will name,
> and one for the year that the household keeps;
> the figure goes in and the man goes round,
> and the grey in the corner goes up a thumb.
>
> Six for the pot and one for the hand
> that put the thumb in and could not command
> the place where it failed. The yard says weather.
> The legs say nothing. The slate says never.

I asked her what the seventh was and she said a man who had never trodden would not know where the foot comes up, and went back to the pit. This is the answer the yard gives to everything it does not want entered, and I have set it down as she gave it, without mending the sense.

# The old woman's correction in the margin

Against *two for the bubble that breaks and is not*, a woman who has kept the racks thirty years wrote in a small hard hand: *Not air. Water. My mother trod this pit forty years and it is water that comes up between the toes, not air, and water will not burst a pot in the firing. The pot that went flat in our kiln had been dried too fast against the east wall.*

Under it, in mine: *Both stand. I have felt the bubble break and I have known the east wall. A song that has been corrected in the margin is a song that has been used, and a song nobody has argued with is a song nobody has trodden to.*

# What the clerk wrote above the seventh round

The factor's clerk borrowed the leaf at Greybank, being a man who cannot leave paper lying. He has a cuff gone soft from washing and the ink sits in two fingers of it and will not come out. He struck out *the seventh held in* and wrote above it, *one part in twelve withheld from the tread; the deduction is entered.* Against *what goes out of the clay comes home to me* he set *labour retained in the vessel; no residue arises.* At the foot of the last round he wrote *six turns performed, rest nil,* and drew a line under it that went a finger's breadth past the margin.

I have put the women's words back underneath and left his standing above, as the floor keeps the ash and the foot that raised it. He has made a rest into a deduction and a deduction into a year. A rest is when the foot comes up; a year is what a clay-bound man does not get back. He has set one where the other stood, which is the whole art of that hand. The treaders stop when they see the soft cuff come into the yard. They take it up when he is past the bridge, and they never once sing the seventh.

# At the head and in the gutter

The bone-pale slip at the head is off the hand that drew the line in the pit; it goes into the grain and will not come out, which is why the yard uses it to mark a place and not to write a name. The wet-clay thumb at the second corner is mine. I signed in slip because I have no slate-name and the pot was mine before it was a leaf. The green bead came off a pot broken by the Glaze Room and rolled to the gutter while a woman held the tally down in the draught; I did not taste it and have not put it in my mouth, whatever the children say of that sweetness. The brown crust is a barley one with the edge burnt, given to a child who would not eat it and laid down here instead. The tallow print came off a lamp set too near the leaf at the Long Night. The tideline took two words off the back of the tally and I have not written them in again, because a song with two words gone is a song said slowly and all of ours are said slowly. The grey in the gutter came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying in a yard; I have not swept it. The Last Day is for corners, and a folded leaf has none.

The tally goes back into its fold of oiled paper and into the pocket, and tomorrow the pit will be cold again and the four of us will be there or the two, according to the water. The caller will say six and the feet will answer six, and the seventh will stand at the lip where it stood this morning, and the clay will close over the instep the way it always does, and whatever goes out of it will come home to somebody.

# At the back, in a hand nobody will own

> The flaw is where the thumb was. The thumb was somebody's.
> Six go into the pot. The seventh goes home in the leg.
> Ask the yard what it counts and it will tell you the weather.

I asked the caller, the old woman of the racks and a carter waiting on the road, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written.
