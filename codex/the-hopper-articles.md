I keep the hopper and the roll of hands at the meal mill on the Ilm. A house brings a child and goes away; the child stays with the dust. When a man comes in off the ridge with a sack frozen hard I tell him what the road took before I tell him what the mill will give, because the first is a thing I have seen and the second is a thing the Cross has said twice. The toll-bag hangs on a nail behind the door, and a man must turn his back on the wheel to reach it. That is the first lesson and I never have to teach it; the bag teaches it.

I set down what stands. Where a borrowed hand has corrected me I have left both readings, the way old grinding shows under the new on a dressed stone.

They were first written in Y.H. 726, the year the factor raised his granary and doubled the levy, when a mill that had kept its rules in the dust was made to set them down so a due could be argued with. A rule that has never had dust on it is a rule nobody has had to grind. These have been ground.

# The articles as they stand at the mill

## Of the taking

1. The mill takes a child, not a house. A house may bring him and answer for his bread until the first grind is done; from the day he lifts a peck he answers to the hopper. A house that will not give up the years has given nothing.
2. A child is taken at ten, or at eleven if his house has kept him through a winter of sickness. Not before he can carry a peck of meal the length of the bench without setting it down. A child who sets it down has spent the meal on the floor and will call it the wheel's doing; the wheel does not answer.
3. His birth-name is said once at the taking and not used again at the mill. What he is called afterward is the dust-name, given the first morning he comes up white to the wrist and does not know he has marked himself. A dust-name cannot be entered, which is the only thing in this country I have found that cannot be.
4. He is kept, not hired. He eats from the mill dish, sleeps in the loft above the sacks, and what his hands earn goes into the meal and comes back as bread with the brown in the fold. He is paid in the only coin this trade uses: a crust at the door and no figure.
5. No child goes under the great wheel, and no child sleeps in the dust loft when the sacks are new. New sack-dust takes a man's breath the way the kiln's breath takes a man in sleep, only slower, so that nobody thinks to carry him out.

## Of the two stones

6. There are two stones under this town doing the same work and not agreeing. One turns and grinds and gives me a measure I can carry to the Cross. One lies under the water and gives one note and never gives a measure at all. The millstone feeds the body; the hand-stone feeds the day. A boy who lays one over the other will grind at the wrong hour and blame the mill.
7. Come to the hand-stone at the hour the note is due, not before. Stand below the ledge with the palm flat and wait until the note comes up into the roots of your teeth, before the ear places it out in the water. Hear it first and you were late, hearing the river go over instead.
8. Do not say the hour aloud. An hour said aloud becomes an argument, and there is argument enough at the Cross without carrying it down to the bank. When the bell comes down from the town, stop. It goes four, four and a gap, and the gap is not one of yours to fill. Begin again after.
9. Leave on the ledge behind the stone what you brought --- a crust, a bit, a knot, a sherd --- and take nothing back. The river takes what is set on its bank and gives back silt, which is an honest exchange and entered nowhere.
10. If the note comes before its hour, do not correct it. In the Thin Year it came under the ice for ninety-four days and the town kept its hours another way. A note is not gone wrong because we were early. Say what it gave and not what it meant.
11. Say the day to the hopper and not to the man asking. Said looking at a man, a figure becomes a price, and a price carried out with the meal comes back with a second figure on it. Twelve bits to the mark, twenty marks to a crown; an ember is what the Court calls five marks, and no ember has ever come through this door.

> *In the factor's narrow upright hand, against rule 10:* The tide arrived in advance of the reckoning. Entered: nil. *Underneath, in mine:* I had written that the note came early. He takes the doer out and leaves a nought where the hour stood. Both readings stand, because the boy will meet that hand again at the Cross and should know its writing before he has to argue with it.

## Of the sacks and the hopper

12. Lift the handful off the top before the sack is tied and never weigh it. The handful is for the stone, and a thing weighed stops being a handful and becomes a charge, and a charge wants a figure put against it.
13. The cup on the nail beside the hopper is for the woman who carries, and she does not ask. The bran goes to the pig at the door, the crust to the child, and neither is entered. A mill that enters its crusts will soon be asked to enter its children.
14. A foundling left at the mill in a fold of grey wool gets a stone warmed under him and no name until a hearth claims him. He is not a sale and not a charge. A boy who names a foundling has made a slate where there was a fold of wool, and there is no unmaking it.
15. The toll-bag hangs behind the door. Reach it with your back to the wheel. What you take here, you take without looking at the work.
16. There is a figure spoken in the yards which is not the one on the notice. I have heard it under the wheel and set no sack beside it. A boy who asks after it is told to ask the hopper, which has never once answered, and is the better for it.
17. No boy is set to dress a millstone until he has carried a sack through a Silt flood and come back dry enough to be trusted with a pick. A sharp tool in a wet hurry takes the furrow off the stone and the skin off the hand; only one grows back.

# The table of grinds

*Kept on the hopper board, in a hand that does not change between one line and the next.*

| At the mill | The work | What is taken |
|---|---|---|
| Ten | Carry, sweep, mind the sacks, set the cup on the nail | The birth-name, said once and not used again |
| Eleven | The peck carried level; the handful lifted off the top | The belief that a thing lifted is a thing known |
| Twelve | The hand-stone at its hour; the bell's gap left standing | The boy's own hour, which the town keeps after him |
| Thirteen | The first sack in a Silt flood; the dressing-pick held dry | The habit of asking what the mill ought to grind |
| Fourteen | A grist taken alone and given out without a figure | The dust-name, said to the wheel and kept in the mouth |
| Fifteen | Stand at the wheel when the year's barley comes down frozen | The last of the boy's account with any house |

18. Fifteen is the common end of a grind and not a short one. A boy who has been through the years stays where the mill needs him, at this mill or at Stonemouth or on a barge going up with salt. The mill does not choose. The wheel does, and the road after it.

# The faults and their correcting

| Fault | As corrected at the mill |
|---|---|
| A figure said to the man and not the hopper | Said again to the hopper; nothing carried out |
| An early note corrected | The note left standing; the boy sent back at the due hour; nothing entered |
| The bell's gap filled | Stop and begin after; the gap left the length it is |
| A foundling named | The name taken back; the fold of wool left as it lay |
| The handful weighed | The scales put away; the handful lifted off the top |
| A dust-name written in the meal-book | Rubbed out with meal-dust and said again to the wheel |
| A birth-name used at the mill | The roll read aloud once; the boy sent to the ledge |
| The bag reached with the face to the wheel | The toll said again with the back turned |

19. A fault copied twice in one tend is a fault the boy has not understood, and the miller sits with him on the hopper board until he has. The dressing-pick is not a rod, though men who have never dressed a stone have called it one.

# The dressing-song, pasted inside the meal-book

*Sung low, kneeling, while the pick goes round the furrow and the dust comes up white. The last verse is said to the stone and not to the company. There is a gap before it the length of the bell's.*

> Pick to the furrow and dust to the knee,
> one for the stone and one for me;
> the wheel goes round and the sacks go round
> and the note comes up out of the ground.
>
> Four for the boards and four for the sill,
> and a place where the bell has not said its fill;
> the clock takes the note and gives out an hour,
> and the mill takes the note and gives out the meal.
>
> A boy comes in with a name on his sack
> and goes out as meal with the name ground back;
> the handful goes first and the road goes twice,
> and the clerk writes down what the river said once.
>
> Pick to the furrow and dust to the knee,
> the stone takes the boy and the boy takes the dust;
> leave the place where the answer stood
> and come back white. Come back as you can.

The wet reed across the third verse came from the leaf lying on the ledge while a girl from the Fishstairs sang it into the stone. I have flattened it.

# What the borrowed hands did

A factor's clerk came up from Greybank with a soft cuff and ink in two fingers, and borrowed the leaf while I was at the hopper.

- Against *he is kept, not hired* he set *retained at the mill, no wage arising*, true about a figure and false about a boy who eats from the dish. I have put the crust back underneath.
- Against *the handful is for the stone, never weighed* he set *milling charge, one in twelve, as the notice says*. The notice is read in the square; the handful is lifted here before the sack is tied.

The other hand is a carter's, thick charcoal added at the bridge: *Paid going over and paid coming back, and the sacks lighter every time.* Beneath it, smaller, a girl who carries meal set: *The cup on the nail belongs to the woman who carries, and she has never once asked.* Neither has signed. I have not rubbed either out. A rule two hands have argued over is a rule that has been used.

# At the head and in the gutter

The white at the head is mill-dust, not salt; my hands are white to the wrist and I did not know I had marked the leaf until I opened it. The brown line along the foot came up from the Fishstairs in an apron and stayed when the water went. The awn in the second fold is stiff where the crease kept it from the damp. The barleycorn was shut in the crease when I took the leaf from a spoiled quire; it has not sprouted and I have not moved it. The ring is not ale; a woman set a cup down while I spoke of the hour and took it up unseen. The nick is from the dressing-pick, which I use to open the book when my hands are wet. The tallow is off the hopper strap. The thread of grey wool caught in the sewing came from the fold a foundling lay in one Silt; I have not cut it away. The green bead came off a girl come down from High Kiln with the sweetness still at her mouth; she leaned to read the faults and asked nothing. I have left it where it fell. The grey along the outer fold is not mine and I have not swept it. There is no Last Day at a mill; the dust goes out with the meal and nobody calls it dealt with.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The clock takes the note and the Cross takes the figure;
> the stone takes the handful and the bag keeps none.
> A boy comes in with a house's name on his sack
> and goes out as meal with the name ground off,
> and the space between is not entered.

I asked the miller, a carter off the ridge, and a clerk come up from the Greybank beam, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written. The leaf goes back into the bag and the barleycorn into the crease. Tomorrow the sacks will come down frozen hard from Cold Acre, and I shall give the figure I gave today; by the time the wheel has turned it will be a thing that was true.
