I sing this in a kiln-house two doors up the Clay Yard, and I have set it down because a man come up from Slate Row asked a woman at the well what the mothers sang when a child would not settle, and wrote the answer in a book before she had finished saying it. A lullaby is not a prayer. A prayer can be said in the clean voice, which takes the speaker neatly out of his own sentence; this has to be said in the voice the child has heard since it had ears, low, with the last word falling into the fold of the shawl. I have written it as we say it and put the verbs back where the clerk took them out.

I had a child come back to me from the bank in the Reed, warm and quiet, and he turned his face toward the pot when I set a cup down, which my first did not do at that age. I have never known whether that was the bank or the child, and I have not put either answer in the margin, because a margin is where a woman writes what she can bear to have read aloud.

The tallow at the head is mine. I held the slate down in the draught under the door and did not know I had marked it.

# How it is sung

- At the third watch, after the fire has been turned with the back of a broken pot and the house has given its last instruction to the wind. Not at the first watch; a child at the first watch is still arguing with the day.
- Warm the stone on your own wrist before it goes to the belly. A stone cold in your own hand is a stone and not a kindness, and no house here owns a Keldish one.
- Set the child on the bed and not by the fire. Sing with your face turned toward the wall. Said looking into a face, the song becomes a question, and a child who has come back from the bank cannot answer it.
- If a slate lies open on the bench, turn it face down first. A name said in the same room as an open slate travels, and a name that travels arrives somewhere the mother will not be told of.
- Say the last line twice. The second saying is for the child who cannot say it yet, and the third, if there is one, is for the one who has forgotten how.
- Do not sing it at a death-house door. That is a different song and belongs to the pot.

# The song

*The stone goes to the belly before the first line. The hand that holds the child does not hold the slate. Refrain after every verse, said into the fold.*

> The stone came out of our neighbour's fire,
> I tried it on my wrist before I tried it on you;
> a stone that's cold in the hand is a stone,
> and a warm one is a kindness somebody paid for.
>
> *Lie still. The dark has no slate and no hand.*
> *Lie still. The name goes in where the number can't.*

> The pot on the shelf answers full to the knuckle,
> the pot by the door gives back flat;
> I tap the one by the door because it's ours,
> and leave the crack showing so the neighbours can see it was handled.
>
> *Lie still. The dark has no slate and no hand.*
> *Lie still. The name goes in where the number can't.*

> There's grey in the corner where the promise went,
> don't put your hand there, and don't ask me whose;
> the broom goes around it, the bucket goes around it,
> and the house goes around it, which is the same thing said kindly.
>
> *Lie still. The dark has no slate and no hand.*
> *Lie still. The name goes in where the number can't.*

> You came with a red branch from the third rib down;
> I crossed two fingers at the edge of it and gave a date;
> a date is an answer with nothing in it to argue with,
> and the edge of the branch is not to be washed.
>
> *Lie still. The dark has no slate and no hand.*
> *Lie still. The name goes in where the number can't.*

> The tube went in at the arm and the slate took the figure,
> the iron only opened the way, as they tell us;
> you came back warm and you did not come back knowing,
> and which of them kept the knowing, nobody says.
>
> *Lie still. The dark has no slate and no hand.*
> *Lie still. The name goes in where the number can't.*

> The loaf has a brown edge and the brown goes to you,
> the crust is the kind part and the crumb is the rest;
> eat what they give you and leave the place at the end
> of the row where a loaf stood and nobody says why.
>
> *Lie still. The dark has no slate and no hand.*
> *Lie still. The name goes in where the number can't.*

> So lie still. I'll say it where no clerk can hear.
> The name's in the fold and the fold's in the bed;
> the number stops at the door and the name goes in,
> and the dark keeps it over till you want it again.
>
> *Lie still. The dark has no slate and no hand.*
> *The child cannot say it. The dark says it twice.*

There is a space before the last refrain in the singing. I have left it on the slate. It is the space where a name would be said if the child could say it, and it is the right length for a name and for nothing else.

# What the hearth-maid put in the margin

The thick charcoal belongs to a woman who carried words between bedsides in the district and would not have them on a slate where a collector might see them. I have copied it as it stands.

At *I crossed two fingers at the edge of it and gave a date*: *She gave the date I had given her mother. Two women gave the same date in one room and neither looked at the other. A date is a door that shuts.*

At *you did not come back knowing*: *Said over a child of House Silt who came back in the year of the flower. The child lay warm and wanted nothing. The mother sang the old words to it every night for a tend, and the child never once turned at them, and she never once missed a night. Both stand.*

At *the dark has no slate and no hand*: *There was a hand in the room that night and it went to the slate before it went to the child. I have put no name to it. It was not the mother's hand.*

At the space before the last refrain: *The third saying is not for the child. I have asked whose it is and been given a date.*

# What the clerk did

A Court clerk borrowed the leaf on an Account-day, with a cuff gone soft from washing and ink sitting in two fingers of it that would not come out. He took the verbs out of the verses and set his words above mine.

- At *a warm one is a kindness somebody paid for*, he wrote *the stone was transferred by consent; cost to district, nil*. It is true in the figure and false in the room I sang it in. I have left it.
- At *the pot by the door gives back flat*, he wrote *the vessel failed, having reached its term*, and underlined it twice.
- At *the house goes around it*, he wrote *no entry required*, which is what the Court says when it wants a thing left alone.
- At *which of them kept the knowing, nobody says*, he wrote *thin return, entered*, and put a figure against it that was not in my song.
- Where I had left the space before the last refrain, he put a round nought and wrote *vacancy* beside it. He has put a number where a name should stand, which is the whole art of that hand.

I have put my words back underneath and left his standing above. He takes the verbs out because a thing always true has no need of them. I put them back because a child at the third watch does not know he is hearing something always true, and wants to know which hand will hold him.

# At the head and in the gutter

The crust-grease on the second leaf is from a browned edge laid down while I was telling a child to eat and it would not. I left it where it fell. The ring is barley water, set down at the first verse and taken up at the last, by which time it had gone the colour of the pot it stood on. The green bead came off a girl with sweetness still at her mouth who leaned over to see the writing and touched the cloth and said nothing and went back to the rack. I have not washed it and would not if I knew how. The grey along the gutter came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying; nobody will own it, and there is no Last Day behind a rack. The thread drawn through the hole in the fold is grey wool, tied for the word I could not write down. I will not untie it.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The pot keeps the crack. The bed keeps the name.
> The slate keeps neither and is read aloud all the same.

I asked a woman at the well, the clerk and a hearth-maid who came through with a bundle, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written.

The crock goes back behind the rack. Tomorrow the fire will be turned with the back of a broken pot and I shall sing the same words into the same fold, and a child who cannot yet say them will hear them for the third time. If an officer finds the leaf, let him take the verbs out. The next hand will put them back, and will warm the stone on its own wrist before it reads.
