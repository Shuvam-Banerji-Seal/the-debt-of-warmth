To Ovia Gilded, keeper of the bowls in the House Ember at the Bowl of Sere, from Corven Dells at the counting house in Greybank. Written on the back of a barley tally that spoiled in the damp and was given to me for nothing, which is the only paper in this house that has not already been told what to say.

I write in the southern hand because a northern one would be taken for a petition, and a petition in this country is a thing a man spends a mark to have lost. The damp got at the tally and took two figures clean off it. In Vhal that would be called a mercy and here it is called a waste of good paper. I have written over them. A figure that has gone soft is not a figure that has been forgiven; it is a figure with the number rubbed out and the debt standing all the same. Do not wet the fold. The seal is red lead and comes off on the thumb.

You write asking for a whole return before the first Frost, with the memory kept and the shape whole enough to be shown at a feast. I answer plainly, because the courtesy we use at this end of the trade is a courtesy with a bill inside it. It can be done. Twelve hundred marks and forty unspent measures, as the Court books them; the marks go north to a household and the measures go south in a cradle, and nobody at this counter has ever seen the two things arrive at the same door. Your house wants remembering. What the north has to sell is remembering. That is the whole of our trade, and there is not a kind word in it that has not been priced.

The district is walking thin. The levy in Low Kiln is one in three and the notice in the Court room says one in twelve; I stood under the notice at the Cross last Account-day and heard the figure read twice over the basket, once for the moot and once for those who came late, and I know a figure read aloud is not the figure paid. I could not tell you which of the two goes into your cradle. That is not my department. In my department we write down what the Court gives us and do not ask the slate where it has been.

# The measure as it comes to us, set against what goes south

| In the district | On the collector's slate | In our letter to the Bowl | In the cradle |
|---|---|---|---|
| A widow of the lower bank. Two loaves | one in twelve | a whole measure, freely returned | the shape of a woman who wanted something |
| A glaze-room girl, sweet at the mouth | one mark, current | a vessel of good remembering | a mouth that turns toward the cup before the cup is shown |
| A clay-bound hand, term served out | term, not measure | a measure, term fulfilled | a hand that has forgotten the trade but not the reach |
| Six and twenty of the lower yard, by lot | one line for the batch | forty measures, purchased | forty, and no name among them |
| A man found in the yard at morning, no figure taken | not entered | nothing | nothing, and the slate shut |

I keep the four columns apart because a man in a counting house can forget what a cradle is for if he writes the four in one line. The College would put them in one line. A College clerk borrowed this very leaf before it went south and put them in one line and took the verbs out of the whole of it, and I have set the verbs back underneath and left his line standing above, so that both may be read and neither mistaken for the other.

You ask why forty. I asked the same of a man who had stood at this counter longer than I had been alive, and he said *because the account requires it*, which is a phrase that means *because we have decided it*. A whole return wants one vessel that holds and remembers; it wants forty measures that will never be a vessel at all, that go into the account and stay there and are called unspent. They are not found in this district, lady. They are made. There are households below the sill with nothing left in them but the breathing, and out of those households the forty come, one line on a slate and a hand at a wrist that leaves a pale ring the neighbours call weather. I have written *made* here and I will not strike it out. The borrowed hand above has written *were banked*, which is true in the way a butcher's floor is true.

# The song the pulled benches keep

*Sung by the condemned at the oars of the salt galleys out of Vhal, to the stroke; put down here because it is the only account in this trade that counts the rower. The last line is said twice, the second time for the man whose hands give out before the seven years.*

> One for the clay and one for the chalk,
> one for the hand that the salt took;
> pull and the measure goes over the side,
> pull and the measure stays inside.
>
> Four on the bench and four on the shore,
> and a place where the fifth oar was before;
> seven years is a long, lean tide ---
> the lamp goes out and the hands abide.
>
> We carry the warm that we never kept,
> we row the road that the slate has cut;
> ask the bowl what it wants of us ---
> it wants the shape, and it wants it shut.

You ask in the same hand after a fourth pouring for the woman of the green bowl, who was shown in that year and twice before, and you ask whether the shape will remember. I have seen the shape. It holds wine and does not spill, and it has not spilled at three feasts, and the applause for it is the cleanest applause in the empire because nobody in the room has to believe it. Whether there is anybody inside it on the third day is a question for your bowl-surgeons, not for a clerk at this counter. We can send the forty. We cannot send the wanting. That is the one part of the order the north cannot fill, and it is the part your house is buying.

# In the gutter, in her hand, set close and small

*She writes on the diagonal, as the bowls write when they are crossed, and uses the plural of houses even where a single woman must have stood at the writing.*

We emptied the bowl on the first night of Grain and the debt-slates were burned by lot and new ones made, as you know, having once been at a feast in this room and applauded with the rest. Your four columns amuse me. We do not keep accounts of that kind here; we keep the bowl and the applause and the shape that comes back. Send the forty and send the cradle. As for the wanting, my house has never found it in the north, though we have bought north for nine generations and have had good value out of every district.

*Below, in the same hand, a line she did not cross out:*

You were asked at sixteen what debt you meant to carry. I am asking you the same thing now, in a letter, because a debt named in a room is applauded and a debt named on paper has to be paid. Answer me on the back and not in the gutter, where the salt has got into everything.

# What the clerk did at the Council room in Vhal

A factor's clerk borrowed the leaf in the tar-smelling room where the Council writes its decrees on salt-paper that goes soft in the rain and calls the softening a virtue. He writes in the narrow upright hand, with the verbs taken out and everything in the passive, as they teach it at the College and as the Council pretends not to need. Against *they are made* he set *forty unspent measures were banked*, and under that *were lawfully purchased*, and underlined it twice. Against the rowing song he wrote *no cost entered*, which is the neatest thing I have ever seen done to nine lines of a song. Against the woman of the green bowl he set *a beautiful return, third pouring*, struck out *wanting* wherever it stood, and wrote *no want recorded* above it, because a thing a house has bought cannot be wanting. Where I left the place of the fifth oar he put a round nought and wrote *nil* beneath it.

I have put my verbs back underneath and left his figures standing over them. He is right in the language he writes. In the language of the benches a nought is not a place where an oar was.

# At the head and in the fold

The barley dust at the head came off the tally when I turned it over, and there is more of it in the gutter than there should be, mixed now with her sloping hand, which is a thing I cannot separate and would not if I could. The round ring is wine from the feast at which the woman of the green bowl was shown, or from another feast; I did not mark which and will not guess at it. The red at the outer fold is the seal, lead and something else, and it comes away on the thumb with a sweetish taste if a man is fool enough to touch his tongue to it, which I have not done and the clerk from the College did, and was sick behind the tar-room door while the Council went on with its counting. The pale line along the lower margin is not the Ash, whatever a northerner would call it; it is the salt that gets into everything in this coast country, and I have not brushed it off. There is no Last Day on this counter. Paper goes into the box and the box goes south, and nobody burns it in the morning and calls it dealt with.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The north counts what it lost. The south counts what it kept.
> The bowl goes round and the account stays open.

I asked a factor, a clerk who cannot let paper lie, and a woman who rowed the pulled benches and now sells eels at the Peat Line, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written.

The leaf goes back into the box of southern contracts. In the morning the grain goes south by the Peat Line and a tally goes up to the factor's table, and the forty go where they go, which is not a place I have ever been allowed to name. Tomorrow another household below the sill will find it has had a good year and that the account has stood. That is the whole of the letter, and it goes on.
