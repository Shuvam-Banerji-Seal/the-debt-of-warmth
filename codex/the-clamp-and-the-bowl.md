I was hired in Keld Keep to walk south and stand where a man might have put a third. The factor who hired me was courteous. His hand went to a slate before it went to me, and he asked for my measure before he asked for my name, which is the order at home and a courtesy I had not expected below the Rim. He wrote the figure first. Then he wrote the name. I have thought about that order since, and I have set it above the rest of what I brought back. A man who writes the figure before the name has decided what he is buying. A man who writes the name first may still be talked out of it.

I carried an iron clamp at my belt, a letter in my coat that I could not read, and a warm-stone the size of a plum that had come up out of the Ninth Gallery and was giving less heat each morning. The letter was for a house of the Ten Bowls. The clamp was to keep the letter and the man carrying it from being parted in the dark, which is the whole of a hired clamp's learning. I had stood twice at the Duelling Floor and had never been quick enough to open a way in my own forearm before the other man opened one in his, so I was not a master and was paid as a man who is not a master. This is worth saying first, because most of what follows is a soldier's account and soldiers are expected to say they won.

# The order of the name

At thirteen the number goes into the forearm and a warm-stone is put into the hand after the knife. From that day a Keldish man gives the number before the name, because the number is the thing the Warden can check and the name is the thing a man can lie about. I gave this factor forty-eight and Veth, in that order, and he wrote forty-eight and Veth, in that order, and neither of us was troubled.

In the south a child may have seven names by adolescence and keep whichever the household likes, and a slave has no measure in law at all. I asked a clerk of the house how a person is governed who has no figure. He said a person is governed as a vessel is governed, by the hand that pours it. I asked who pours the hand. He said I had asked two questions and that one of them had been answered. In Keld an answer like that gets a man a clamp across the knuckles, not because it is rude but because it wastes the morning. Here it was given with great courtesy and I was thanked for asking.

I saw a house-slave at the third bowl who was dressed better than I was and was exhibited for the generosity of the house. She had been given a whole return and a clay-name, and the name she had been born with was forbidden in that house, and written, I was told, on the inside of a bowl she was never allowed to look into. I have tried to picture a person with no measure. I can picture a clamp with no bite: it lies on the bench and nobody lifts it, and in the end the iron goes to the fire and is worked into something else. I could not make the picture stay. A northman's whole mind is built the other way round, and the road down is a long way to carry it.

# The road that counted nothing

The Peat Line is where the clay gives up and the reeds begin, and a reeve took a bit off me for stepping on a board he had not pointed to. This was within his right and I said so, which is a thing a Keldish man says when he has paid and does not wish to argue. The Fenfolk say a dry man is a dangerous man, and put him up on boards and do not tell him which will bear him. We learned.

Beyond it lie the roads and wells and doorways of the ground the Court calls the Empty Cantons and the Fen call the Quiet Acre. The temple tells children that the people were taken up in the first Returns and are whole somewhere, paid for by a line of figures. Nobody says where the line went. I counted what could be counted and left the squares alone. There is a square of bare clay above a lintel where a house-roll hung, and a square is not a nought, and a man who puts a nought into it has made an empty room into an account. I have carried a number in my arm for twenty years and I know the difference between a figure that stands and a figure that was taken away.

The hearths there have white dust in them and no grey, which the surveyors set down last and speak of least. I did not write down what a Fen man told me it meant, because I do not know it for a lie and do not know it for the truth, and a soldier who writes what he cannot check has begun a sermon. The burning winds came yellow off the east past the Line and took the skin off my lips and left the grain standing. My lips went first. The grain stood. That is a thing I have no learning in and several reasons to believe.

# The four bowls

The Bowl of Sere has no wall worth the name. Each court has a fire in the middle and men sit in the heat with their coats on the ground beside them and the yellow dust down in their hair, and nobody brushes it off. I brushed mine off on the first day and was charged for the hour. I let it lie after that.

At the third hour a public bowl of wine is emptied onto the stones and that is how the acts of the Sun-Emperor are announced. I asked a man beside me whether a debt had been forgiven or a province ruined. He said, “Yes.” I asked which. He said, “Measure for measure,” and went back to his wine. In Keld a phrase like that would be settled with iron before the wine was cold. Here it settled itself and the wine went on being drunk.

At the feast of the house I counted four bowls on the board. Three went round and were poured. The fourth stood where it stood, and was carried away full when the feasting was done, and nobody poured it and nobody said whose debt it held. I asked three times and got three silences, the last of which was the kindest. A Keldish man cannot leave a thing standing that has no use and no payer. I have set the fourth bowl beside the empty place on the bench at the Floor where a clamp is kept that nobody lifts, and I have joined neither to the other, because a soldier who joins two things that the country keeps apart has made a story, and stories are what that country lives on.

Valen Openhand preached the Spending in the court while I was there: that a vessel was made to be emptied and a life hoarded is a life wasted. He was not a thin man, and neither were the priests who said it after him, which a traveller notices after standing in a court all morning. At the end of the feast the debt-slates of one household, chosen by lot, went into the fire. A woman whose slate burned wept and the company was kind to her, and then the new slate was brought out and the company applauded that too. I have not got used to the second applause.

# What the factor gave, and what the road took

*The left column is the figure as it was given to me. The right is what I set on the back of the leaf when I got home. The left hand is mine, number-first. The narrow hand above came from a clerk who borrowed the leaves at Greybank. Both stand.*

| Thing | Given, in the south | Set on the back, at the Keep |
|---|---|---|
| Hire, forty days, at the shaft-mouth rate | 2 crowns and a half | 2 crowns and a half, and the days are mine |
| Keep and a bed in the clamp-yard | 1 crown | 1 crown |
| Warm-stone, fist-sized, bought at the gate | 3 marks | 3 marks; cold on the ninth day |
| Clamp, iron, Keldish, filed at the gate | 2 marks, the filing charged | 2 marks, the filing charged twice |
| Wine, a jar, at the third hour | 1 crown | 1 crown; the ring on the leaf is mine |
| Resin of the tallow-wasp, a nest | not taken | a bowl-surgeon offered 4 crowns for it |
| Gold crowns, changed at Greybank | 4 crowns | would not change at the Keep |

> *In the narrow upright hand of the factor's clerk at Greybank, against the last row:* Crowns entered as four. The stone and the days are not entered.
>
> *Underneath, in mine:* The stone was mine before the hire and went cold on the ninth day. The days were mine all along and I gave them. He has entered what he can carry and left what I carried.

Twelve bits to a silver mark and twenty marks to a gold crown. The Court books in embers and will tell a man an ember is worth five marks; no man I have met has ever changed one, north or south. I left the embers off. A column with nothing in it is an honest column.

# The clamp-song

*Said on the road going down and not coming up. The words in the hard Rim speech, set in Grathic letters with the endings struck off. Taken down from a man who had been hired twice and had come back from both, while he filed a clamp at the gate. He would not sing it with a factor's clerk in the yard.*

> Nine doorways and no man to come through,
> a well with the rope and no hand at the pail;
> I gave my number and kept my name,
> and the south said *bowl* where the north said *man*.
>
> Three go round and the fourth goes up,
> the iron holds and the iron takes;
> a clamp that has no bite lies on the bench ---
> ask who will lift it. Ask it twice.
>
> I carried the north in the seam of my coat
> and came back with dust that the rain will not move;
> ask the bowl what it poured. It will say it was full.

He said there was a fourth verse and that it was not a verse but a sayings, and he would not give it. I have left the place for it at the head of the leaf, scored with the knife and empty.

# What the borrowed hands did

Two hands have been at these leaves besides mine.

The factor's clerk at Greybank, in the narrow upright Ledgerhand, took the verbs out. Where I wrote *I was asked for my measure before my name* he set *identification in two parts*. Where I wrote *they burn a slate and write it again* he set *instrument discharged by lot and renewed*. Where I wrote *the fourth bowl was carried away full* he set *vessel removed, contents not entered*. I have put my words back underneath. A verb is the difference between a thing that happened to a person and a thing that happened in a counting room.

The bowl-house clerk, in thin gold on prepared paper, wrote *market rate, current* against the table and *ceremony observed* against the burning. He did not strike a word. A clerk who strikes a word and writes his own above it means to be argued with; a clerk who writes above without striking means to be done with. The gold has worn thinner than the charcoal in the fold, which is one honest thing about that country that the rain did not undo.

# At the head and in the gutter

The yellow in the sewing is the burning wind, ground in going down and not shaken out, because a man who shakes a leaf is a man who wants to pretend he has been somewhere else. The wine-ring is small and belongs to the third hour. The olive at the thumb is mine; I turn the leaves with the hand I carried the bowl with. The curl of burnt paper came off a slate that went into the court fire, and a woman put it into my hand before the new slate was brought out, and I have kept it folded in the second leaf where the heat of it is gone. The filings are off the clamp I had filed at the gate, charged twice. The shaving in the gutter was warm-stone once and is cold now, and I have kept it because a man should be able to see the price he paid after the price has gone out of it. The grey along the outer fold is not mine and not the factor's. I have not swept it. There is no Last Day on the road I came, and I have not found one since I got back.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> Forty-eight went down and forty-eight came back;
> the south kept the figure and gave me the dust.
> Three were poured and the fourth went up;
> I asked for a debt and was answered *yes*.
> Ask the slave what her number is. Ask the door.

I asked the factor's clerk, a man of the Floor come south on other business, and a bowl-surgeon who sold me nothing, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written.

The leaves go back into the tin, into the pocket the strap has worn. There is iron going up the Rim Road in the first tend of Silt and a factor who has not yet asked me whether I will walk it again. I have not said yes. A man with a clamp and no house says yes when the factor asks, which is why I have put off answering him and will put it off until the morning. The warm-stone in the tin is cold and the number in my arm is where the knife put it. Those are the two things I brought back that nobody has tried to enter.
