I keep the fire at the Grey Eel and mend the nets in the afternoons when the quay is shut, which is a different work from keeping an account, though both begin with the hand on the thing before the eye goes to the person standing there. In Ash the river goes grey under its skin and the road up to Sill is a road nobody takes, and of an evening there is nothing to do but sit where the fire is and ask one another what we already know and call the asking a question. A question whose answer nobody knows is a wager, and there is enough wagering in Greybank without ale in it.

A man came in the first tend of Ash and asked who had won the Eel's contest fourteen winters back. I could not tell him. There was a prize, and there was a night, and there were men who would have remembered both, and I had kept the fire nine years; the only man left who had been there was Varr, who kept the quay tally before I kept the fire and now sits by the door with his hands on his stick. He told me the night as he tells it when he has told it twice and would rather not tell it a third time. I have set it down on the back of a tally leaf a wet thumb spoiled, because a prize nobody can claim is a thing that ought to have a story standing where the name would be.

# The house and the night

The Grey Eel stands at the lower end of the quay, where the road from Sill comes down to the water and the salt road goes out the other way. It is a low room with a hard clay floor and a bench of black ash worn hollow at the fire end by quay sleeves and the backs of men who have carried salt. I score nothing on it. A man who wants a thing entered can go up the street to the factor's house and sit with people warmer than himself, and he will not get a riddle there.

The contest was in Stone of Y.H. 799, the winter the Ilm froze at the ford and the quay was shut for nine days. Four sat down and a fifth stood. The prize was a warm-stone out of Keld, no bigger than a fist, which the Eel kept in a pot of ash behind the bar and lent to whoever won, to sleep with through the dark. A warm-stone is a thing somebody has paid to keep warm, and a man who wins one and carries it home may sleep the warmer without ever knowing whose cold went into it. That is the sort of prize a quay gives, and the sort a quay argues about afterwards.

Varr says the night went badly from the first question, because the factor's man answered before the verses were finished, which is the way of a man who has spent his life being read rather than reading.

# The four that were put

The first was put to the factor's man, who had a soft cuff and a nail of salt-paper stuck to his sleeve from the morning's contracts.

> I am signed in a room that smells of tar;
> the wetter I get, the truer I am;
> the Council lays its name on me
> and the sea takes the name away.
> What am I?

“Salt-paper,” he said, before the last word was out. “And the wetter it gets, the less there is left to argue with.” He laid a bit toward the pot though nobody had asked him to pay, which is a habit of men who have been read rather than reading. Varr scored him one and did not say why.

The second was put to a carter come down from Sill for the winter lease, with ice still in the creases of his coat.

> I have more marks than the things I count;
> no man can read me unless he stands over me;
> a reeve keeps me in his belt and turns me
> when he is deciding which row to open.
> What am I?

“A count-stick,” the carter said. Then, because he was a carter and could not leave a thing alone: “And a reeve with more notches than graves is either a good reeve or a man with a claim.” There was an argument about the second half of that, which Varr stopped by setting the cup down. There is no winning an argument about a stick. It has the marks it has, and the reeve turns it where he stands, and a man at a bar is not standing over it.

The third was put to a Keldish surveyor come down the Rim Road with a survey roll under his coat and no word to say about the pass. He had the habit, common to his kind, of stating his measure before his name, and had done neither.

> I am heard in the square and told on a slate;
> I go four and four and then a thing that is not a number;
> nobody crosses himself when I come,
> and the clerk reads straight past where I stood.
> What am I?

“The bell,” the surveyor said. “No. The gap in it.” He said the second answer low, to the fire, which is the proper way to give the answer to a thing that is not a number. A Keldish man will say the figure and not the space after it, being paid to survey seams and not silences; that he said the space at all cost him something, and Varr gave him the score for the cost and not for the word.

The fourth Varr put himself, being the only man in the room who had kept a tally long enough to know what a figure costs and still be able to say it.

> I go up the road in a box and come back in a cup;
> I am read twice over the basket and paid once;
> I am the same in every mouth in the district
> and a different thing in every house that pays me.
> The space between me and my price has nobody's name on it.
> What am I?

Nobody answered for a while. Then the woman who mends the nets, who had sat at the end of the bench all night with her hands in her lap and had not taken her cup up, said it to the fire.

“A figure,” she said. “One in twelve, as the notice says. It goes up in a box and comes down in a cup, and the space has no signature.”

Varr passed her the pot. She set it down again without drinking and said she had mended a net for a man who carried a figure up the road and came back with a coat she did not know, and that she had never asked him what it cost because a net-mender who asks about figures does not keep nets for long. That is the best answer I have heard in nine winters and the only one that cost the giver something.

# The wager, as it stood at the fire

| What was laid | What the house kept | What the factor's clerk wrote over it |
|---|---|---|
| A bit from the factor's man | One, given early; not drunk | Query entered; commercial interest nil |
| A bit from the carter | One, argued and allowed | Response entered; no charge arises |
| A bit from the surveyor | One, given to the fire | Party unidentified; interest not entered |
| The net-mender laid nothing | One, given to the fire | No answer tendered; struck |
| The warm-stone in the pot of ash | Not taken; it went back into the ash | Prize unappropriated; no entry arises |

The figure read at the Cross that morning was one in twelve, as the notice says, and it went over the bench with the ale and nobody looked at it twice. There is a pause in that figure the length of a held breath, which the clerk reads past and Varr does not, but that is not a thing for a riddle night.

# What the clerk did

He came down from the factor's house on the first market after the thaw with a cuff soft at the edge from washing, and borrowed the leaf when the quay was full. He set the riddles down as *queries entered* and the answers as *responses entered*, and put a figure of marks against each of the four, which was more than Varr had scored, because the bench scores in whether a man sits down again and he does not.

Against the net-mender's answer he wrote *no answer tendered; struck*, because she had said it to the fire and not to him, and a thing said to a fire is not a tender. He asked who had put the fourth. Varr told him a man at the bench. He asked for the man's name, and Varr said the man had not given one, which is a true thing and not the thing he wanted, and he wrote *party unidentified*. He asked what the warm-stone was worth. Varr told him a warm-stone is worth what the cold is worth and changes with the weather, and the clerk wrote *unappropriated; no entry arises*, which is a word for a thing nobody has claimed and a thing nobody can have.

He took the stone away and left the answer where it had been said. I have put his hand above mine and left mine standing under it, the way the fire keeps its heat under a pot and is not asked where it got it.

# In the margin, in a surveyor's sloping hand

The leaf went up the Rim Road with a bundle of survey sheets and came back with a line in a hand that is not mine and not the clerk's. I have copied it as it stands.

> At *the gap in it*: I was in the square at the Cross on the tenth of Stone and heard the bell go four, four and a gap, and nobody crossed himself. The told bell and the heard bell are two bells and neither is the gap. A man who has stood in the Rim knows the difference between a number and the space after it, which is the whole of surveying. Do not let the clerk carry the space away. He will write *nil* over it and it will be a true entry and a lie besides.

I have not answered him in the margin. A thing written in a book follows a man home, and a surveyor's home is a room with a slate in it and nowhere to put a second opinion.

# At the head and in the gutter

The tallow thumb at the head is mine; I held the leaf down in the draught that comes under the bar door and did not know I had marked it. The press of wet wool across the first leaf came off the carter when he held the door for the surveyor and did not let go when he was through, and I have not written down which carter, because he would stop holding doors if I did. The dark ring is small ale that stood from the first question to the fourth and was drunk by nobody; two men have claimed it and one woman has denied it and I have given it to none of them. The barley grease came off a crust the surveyor set down on the leaf and took away uneaten. The grey in the gutter came off a cuff that had no business in the corner where I keep the tin, and I have not swept it. The frost came up out of the paper on the carry up from the quay and is the only thing in this house that entered without a hand. There is a Last Day at the Grey Eel and the corners are swept on the morning after and burnt and called dealt with.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The fire keeps the heat. The slate keeps the space.
> The stone went back into the ash and nobody slept warmer.
> The answer stood where the name should have been.

I asked the carter, the surveyor and the woman who mends nets, and had three silences and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written. The leaf goes back into the tin and the pot goes back behind the bar. Tomorrow the quay may open and there will be more to do of an evening than ask one another what we already know, and the bench will be wanted for cups again. If a man comes in and asks who won, I shall tell him the woman at the end of the bench answered and did not take the stone, and let him make of that what a quay makes of everything, which is a wager and a figure and no name on either.
