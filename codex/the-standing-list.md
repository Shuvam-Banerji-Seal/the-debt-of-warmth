I keep this house on the Clay Road, a day's walk downriver of Sill, where the road from the south bridge comes up and the cart road goes on to Greybank. Twice a tend I copy the standing list and hang the fresh leaf by the door; the old leaf goes into the book behind the meal tub. A man comes in with wet legs and asks what a bed will cost him and what the roads will take, and I tell him out of this book. A figure made up in the middle of the night is a figure that will be argued with at daylight.

Two and twenty years I have kept this house, and I have learned that a price has a man on one side of it and a road on the other, and the board at the Cross sees only the man. I buy what I sell at the Cross and carry it back on my own back, so there is a column for the figure cried and a column for what this house lets a thing go at. Travellers read the second.

Twelve copper bits to a silver mark. Twenty silver marks to a gold crown, which is a Sere coin and does not stay in these parts. The Court books in embers and will tell a man an ember is worth five marks; no man has ever exchanged one for the other, and there is no column for them here. A bit is a bit that left somebody's hand.

> *In the narrow upright hand of the factor's clerk, written above my first line:* Coin and reckoning entered in the ordinary way. *Underneath, in mine:* There is nothing ordinary about a bit that has walked eighteen leagues in a man's boot. I have put the hands back in.

# The market, as it is cried at the Cross

*Copied on Account-day, when the bell goes four, four and a gap and nobody crosses himself, and the slate is read twice. The left column is the figure cried under the moot-post. The right is what a man pays at this fire.*

| Article | At the Cross | At this house |
|---|---|---|
| Coarse barley loaf, brown-edged | 1 bit | 1 bit, and the crust to whoever is waiting |
| Quart of small ale, watered | 1 bit | 1 bit; watered, as the house requires |
| Sour milk, a quart | 1 bit | 1 bit, and sourer in Stone |
| Salt herring | 2 bits | 2 bits; 3 when the boat has been in |
| Smoked eel | 3 bits | 3 at the Fishstairs; 4 once it has lain at my fire |
| Turnips, two | 1 bit | 1 bit, and the greens over |
| Coarse pots, the score | 4 bits | 4 bits; a broken one is the seller's |
| Peat, a barrow | 2 bits in Grain; 5 in Stone | 5 bits, the year being what it is |
| Salt, a sack off the pans | 3 marks | 3 marks, and a mark again at the pass |
| Tallow, a stone | 2 marks | 2 marks; it ends in the candle |
| Rope, a coil | 1 mark | 1 mark, and it leaves tar on this leaf |
| Iron, a bar, Keldish | 2 marks in the lowlands | 2 marks; half a mark in Keld |
| Pig, at the fattening before the Five | 6 marks | 6 marks, and nothing wasted |
| A kiln hand, the day | 1 mark | 1 mark; a journeyman 2 |

> *Margin:* The factor's man asked why I keep a column for a house. I told him a man can sleep here and cannot sleep at the Cross. He said the Cross had no beds. I said that was the price of it.

> *Margin:* The peat row stood at 2 bits through the warm weeks and went to 5 when the cold came, and I have struck neither figure out. A man who buys in Grain and pays the Stone figure does not complain, which is how I know a board is not a list of what a thing is worth.

# What the house takes for itself

*These are not cried anywhere. A man learns them at the door.*

| For | The figure |
|---|---|
| A bed in the common room | 1 bit |
| A bed with a fire in the room | 2 bits |
| A night's feed for a beast | 1 bit |
| A place at the table for a carter who has not paid | nothing; he sits down anyway |
| A bed for a woman with nowhere to go | not entered, and not refused |

> *Margin, against the last two rows:* Who pays the fire? A man warms himself at it and goes out colder than he came in, and the fire has kept him all night and has had nothing entered for it. I ask the pot and the pot does not answer.

# The tolls of the roads

*Four great roads leave this country and a fifth which is the river, and a handful of little ways that no board has ever heard of. What follows is what I tell a man at the door before he puts his boots on again.*

## The Clay Road

Eighteen leagues from the Cross at Sill to the quay at Greybank, packed clay over corduroy in the wet places. A cart takes five days in Grain and six in Silt. It is a Court road since the treaty of the Blue Cliff, so an assessor may stop a cart on it without asking the thane, and does.

| | What the house tells him |
|---|---|
| Cart with a load, at the Sill end | 2 bits |
| The same cart at Greybank, by the factor's man | 2 bits |
| Beast on the hoof | 1 bit |
| Foot passenger | 1 bit; nothing on Account-day |
| Barrow of grey going out through a north gate | not weighed, and not entered |

> *Margin:* The carters say a man pays twice to travel one road, once to the moot and once to the Court. I have seen the same cart pay at both ends and come back carrying nothing but figures.

## The Salt Road

Eighty leagues to Vhal by Greybank and the coast pass. Salt, glass, rope and dried fish go out; coin and contracts on salt-paper come back, and the damp gets into both.

| | What the house tells him |
|---|---|
| Sack of salt, at the coast pass | 1 mark to the Council of Tides |
| Coil of rope, or a sheet of glass | 1 bit |
| Boat-share passing inland | paid in kind, by the lot and not the league |
| Contract written on salt-paper | no fee; it will not survive the journey, which the Council calls a virtue |

> *The Council clerk's leaning hand struck* toll *and wrote* contribution freely entered *above it.* In mine: *the figures stand. A council that cannot charge a toll has found a finer name for one, and a man pays it all the same.*

## The Rim Road

West-north-west from Sill to Keld Keep, a hundred and twenty leagues of improving road and worsening weather; the Cold Pass ten leagues beyond. There is a post at the pass and no gate. Only fools and factors travel it without a warm-stone, and the factors charge the stone to the account.

| | What the house tells him |
|---|---|
| Cart, at the pass | 1 mark, or iron to the value |
| Beast on the hoof | half a mark |
| A man without a warm-stone | the stone off the next man who has one |
| Traveller going farther into the Rim | no due, and no guarantee |

> *Margin:* A surveyor come down off the road said the Warden's men do not promise to bring a man back. I asked him what the promise was worth if it was not given, and he said that was the point of it, and went on with his lead.

## The Hearth Road

South-east from Greybank to the Hearthseat, a hundred and forty leagues, the merchants' road to the Court. Grath folk speak of it as if it were a fever. There is no barrier on it and no figure at any gate.

| | What the house tells him |
|---|---|
| Toll at the barrier | none; there is no barrier |
| Grain going south, weighed at Greybank | by the deduction, entered at this end |
| A man who asks what he has paid | the figure is read to him twice |

> *Margin:* A carter from High Kiln asked me what the Hearth Road cost and I showed him the empty column. He said it was the most honest figure in the book and the dearest. The Court takes nothing at the gate because it has already taken it at home. I have not struck the column out. An empty column is the only part of a list a carter can read twice.

## Bridges, ford and the little ways

| | What the house tells him |
|---|---|
| South bridge at Sill, loaded cart | 1 bit |
| North bridge at Sill | nothing; the Fishstairs folk argued it and won |
| River road to the Weir and Stonemouth, a beast | 1 bit; passable on foot in Silt |
| Plank walk at the Weir | 1 bit; the boards are counted and nobody says which will bear a man |
| The Nine Pots road, west from Sill | no gate; a pot set down at the feud post |
| Winter ford at Greybank | 1 bit, when there is water to cross |
| The ford when the Ilm bears ice | nothing, because no collector can cross |

> *Margin:* In the Long Cold the Ilm bore ice for ninety-four days and no collector crossed. I have left the figure standing, though it is the one figure in this book that has never been paid.

# What stands outside the list

These are not dues. No slate takes them and no factor enters them, and a man pays them all the same.

The Ferr runs lead dust off the western workings and a man who drinks it through a season comes back thin. The sweet sickness from bad glaze shows first as a pale line at the gum and ends with a man who cannot hold a cup. Beyond the Cold Pass the cold takes a year and gives back a thinner man who has forgotten what he went for.

A man may pay every figure on this leaf and go back thinner than he came, which is a thing no board has a column for. I have set it under the roads and not among them.

# The verse they sing coming down the Clay Road

> Twelve to the mark and twenty to the crown,
> and the Court books five where no coin goes down;
> the loaf has an edge and the ale has a head,
> and a bed at this house is a bit and a bed.
>
> Clay takes twice and Salt takes twice,
> Rim takes iron and gives no advice;
> Hearth takes nothing at any gate ---
> it took it at home before it was late.
>
> Four roads out and a river beside,
> and a board at the door with the figures I hide;
> ask the man at the beam what a thing is worth ---
> he weighs it, and I know what it cost to bring it here.

> *In the factor's clerk's narrow hand, against* Four roads out and a river beside: *Four roads entered. Underneath, in mine:* There are five, and I have put nothing in the place of the fifth. A nought is a figure. That line is a place.

# What the two borrowed hands did

A surveyor from Keld came down with a bar of iron and struck *2 marks in the lowlands*, writing *half a mark in Keld, and the mountain sweats besides*. I left my figure under his. It is the same bar either way, and the difference is the road.

The factor's clerk wrote *dues of the Keeping* above *tolls of the roads*, and set the printed one in twelve in the margin where I had left a space. There is a number spoken in the yards of Low Kiln which is not the one on the notice; I have heard it and have not set it here. I have no beam to weigh it on.

In the bottom corner a neighbour's girl set down her sums crooked, two bits going into a mark where four should have stood. I have not rubbed them out. They are the only figures in the book that nobody will ever be asked to pay.

# At the head and in the gutter

The ale rings are mine; I set my cup down while telling a man what his bed will be. The scorch came of a candle tipped in the night. The silt in the gutter came off the boots of every carter since the thaw and I have never got it out. The Ash at the head is off my own thumb turning the leaf toward the light; it comes back after I sweep. The green bead is lead glaze from a woman who leaned to see the prices with the sweetness still at her mouth and touched the paper and said nothing. The tar is off a Vhaleni coil; the reed I pressed on the road to the Weir; the bit gone verdigris has lain in the fold since the Long Cold and is the only coin here never spent. There is no Last Day behind the meal tub.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The board keeps the figure the beam held. The road keeps the figure the man carried.
>
> Who pays the fire?

I asked a carter, a neighbour come down from Low Kiln, and a clerk off the Greybank beam, and had three answers and no hand, as I have had before. I have left the line where it was written.

The leaf goes back on the nail by the door and the book behind the meal tub. Tomorrow a man will come in off the clay with wet legs and ask for a figure, and I shall give him this one, and by evening it will be a thing that was true.
