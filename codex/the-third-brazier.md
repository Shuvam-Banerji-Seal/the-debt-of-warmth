I keep the third brazier at the head of the shaft and count what goes into it. There are three braziers in this room. The first went out in the Long Cold and the second was emptied to feed it, and the third has not gone out since, which is a thing people say of the Hearth-fire and never of a room, though the room is nearer to most of us. In Stone the cold comes up the shaft and lies on the floor like water, and there is nothing to do of an evening but put questions down the hole and wait for what comes back.

The questions go down and the answers are said into the mouth of the shaft, not to the person beside you. In Keld a man states his measure before his name, and in this room he states it twice, once to the reeve's book and once to the cold, because the cold will not be told who he is and will take the number anyway. A riddle here is a number with the person left out, which is the sort of sentence the mine-reeve writes all day and the sort no man will own at night.

I have set this down because a surveyor came the next morning and asked whose coal was lying on the ledge, and I could not tell him. Four coals were laid down and three taken up, and the fourth lay where it had been put with nobody's name on it, which is a thing a room ought to be able to explain. I have written the night as it was said and left the red cross where the reeve set it.

# The manner of a measure-night

1. A question is put to the shaft and not to the man beside you. Put to a man it becomes a demand, and a demand in Keld is a thing with a figure on it and no face.
2. State your number before your name, and answer in one breath, and give the number first. A man who names the thing before he has counted it has not answered; he has introduced it.
3. Four are cut on the ledge. A fifth may be put from the floor and is not cut, being a thing said by the room to itself. A sixth may be written, and is not scored at all, because a thing written has already been carried somewhere.
4. The judge is the room. I say whether an answer serves and I do not say why. A reason given is a question given away for nothing, and the giver can carry it down the Rim Road and sell it at the next post.
5. A coal is laid on the ledge before the first question and taken up by the one who serves. Coals are counted by the flat of the thumb and not by the eye, because the eye reads the face and the thumb does not.
6. No slate lies on the floor while the questions are going down. If a slate comes in, the guessing stops for that night, because a thing entered is not a question any more; it is a figure with the warmth taken out of it. I have known this to fail once and would not say so before the mine-reeve.

# The four that were cut

The first went to a surveyor come down from the Stones with grey seam-dust in the creases of his thumb.

> I am said before the face is said;
> I go into the dark and do not come up with the man;
> I am checked once in red and there is no second asking.
> What am I?

“My number,” he said. Then, because he had been taught the order: “Thirty-nine. Then my name, if the room wants it.” The room did not want it. I gave him the coal. He held it in his palm and looked at it as though it were a figure he had not been entered for.

The second went to a man come up from the Ninth Gallery, with the smell of hot lead still in the fold of his coat.

> I am cut out of the deep where the rock sweats;
> what I hold was paid for in another district;
> I am sold and never lent, and a man going up without one
> borrows from the next man who has one.
> What am I?

“Warm-stone,” he said, and put his hand on the piece in his own pocket, which is how a man answers when the answer is keeping something warm for him. A boy at the fire asked who had paid for it. The man said the mountain had. The boy said the mountain kept no account in this room. Nobody scored the boy's question, because it came from the floor and not from the ledge.

The third went to a man with two sets of numbers cut into his forearm, one over the other.

> I am cut at thirteen and cut out again if the man comes back thin;
> the later cut lies deeper and the earlier stays in the skin;
> nobody asks which count is his.
> What am I?

“The numbers,” he said, and held out the arm without being asked, which is not a thing men do here. He said the later set was the one the reeve had entered and the earlier was the one he had gone down with. “They are both mine,” he said. “The reeve keeps the later and the skin keeps both.” I scored him and set the coal on the ledge before him. He did not take it up, and I have not moved it since.

The fourth went to a carter who had been through the pass twice.

> There is a post and no gate;
> a count stops there and the road does not;
> the surveyor does not promise to bring the man back.
> What am I?

“The Cold Pass,” the carter said. “A post and no more.” He said the second going through was not the same as the first, though the post stood where it stood and the figure at it was nothing either time. There was an argument about whether nothing is a price or the absence of one. I stopped it by touching the ledge, because there is no winning an argument about a post that has no gate.

# The fifth, which the room put to itself

> I go into the brazier and am gone by morning;
> the reeve counts me and my giver does not;
> I warm a man who does not say where I came from.
> What am I?

An old woman who washes bodies at the Keep said “a coal,” and said it to the shaft and not to the room. She said she had washed a man that week who had gone down at eleven months and come back in his fourteenth, and that the reeve had entered him at eleven and the three besides had gone nowhere at all. “That's the one I was warming,” she said, and set her hands back in her lap. There was no coal to give her, being the room's own question, and nobody offered her one, which is the custom here and not a kindness.

# The sixth, which was written and not said

At the end the mine-reeve came in from his room with a narrow slate under his arm and set it on the ledge, which stopped the night as a door would not. He had come to check the coals and stayed to ask a thing of his own. He did not put it to the shaft. He wrote it down, which is his manner, and read it out in a flat voice.

“Entered at eleven months. Lived fourteen. What is the figure?”

Nobody answered. It was not a riddle, and the room knew it; a riddle has the person left out, and this one had the man left in. The woman who washes bodies said, “There is no figure. There is eleven and there is three, and the three are not entered.” The reeve set his red cross against her words on the slate and wrote *measure correctly entered; no second asking*. He did not look at her while he wrote, which is how a man writes a thing he has heard before.

“I wasn't asking,” she said.

He said, “Then nothing arises,” and took the slate away, and the fourth coal lay where it lay.

# What the survey-clerk did

A survey-clerk of the factor at Greybank borrowed the leaves on the morning the winter coal went down the Rim Road. He writes narrow and upright and takes the doer out of every sentence, which is easier than carrying him up five leagues of scree. Against *my number* he set *measure declared, current*. At *what I hold was paid for in another district* he wrote *source not ascertained*, which leaves the district standing and takes the somebody away. Against *nobody asks which count is his* he put *two entries; later controls*. At the sixth he wrote *no riddle tendered; struck*, which is true in a counting room and false on this floor: the sixth was not a riddle and was not struck, and the woman said so to the slate and not to him.

I have put what was said under what was written and left his hand standing, the way a later cut lies over an earlier one in the skin and does not take the earlier out.

# In the margin, in a boy's charcoal

A boy of twelve who has not yet Descended borrowed the leaf while I was turning it. He wrote in the charcoal we mark hands with, slowly, with the ends of the words left in the air:

> At thirteen they will cut mine in.
> I want to know which way to hold the arm.

I have not answered him and I have not rubbed it out. In a year's turning he will go down and come back with a number, and nobody will ask him what he wanted to know at twelve, which is the whole of the Descent in this keep.

# At the head and in the gutter

The smut at the head is mine; I held the leaf down on the ledge with the hand I count coals with and did not know I had marked it. It goes into the grain and no wet sleeve takes it out, which is why this room keeps its figures on slate and its stories on paper nobody wants. The warm-stone shaving in the fold came off the second man's piece when he set it down to answer and took it up again warm-side inward; it is colder now than the ledge and I have kept it where it fell. The lead bead rolled out of someone's cuff and has lain in the gutter since; the rock sweats it and the smell gives what warning it gives, and nobody has claimed the bead. The frost along the lower edge came up out of the paper on the carry from the reeve's room and is gone by noon, which is the only thing in this keep that goes. The red thumb at the fold is the mine-reeve's; he set his slate down on the leaf before he wrote and did not wipe his hand. The grey along the outer fold is not mine and not the clerk's. I have not swept it. There is no Last Day at the head of a shaft, and nothing here is burnt in the morning and called dealt with.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The coal keeps nothing. The slate keeps the eleven.
> The skin keeps both counts and does not enter either.
> The mountain keeps what goes down and calls it a figure.

I asked the surveyor, the woman who washes bodies and the carter, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written. The leaf goes back into the oiled cloth under the third brazier, and the fourth coal goes back on the ledge where nobody has taken it, and in the morning the reeve will come for the count and find four coals given and three received and will write the difference in red. The shaft will go on breathing cold onto the floor, and I shall set questions down into it as I have set them every Stone since the second brazier went out, and if a man asks me what this room is named for I shall tell him: for the one that is still burning, and not for the two that are not.
