I keep the drying racks in the Clay Yard, and I have set this down because the youngest girl who used to clap here went up the clay road in the spring and came back with a number where her name had been. She stands now at the end of the racks and watches the hands go round and does not put hers in. I am not writing it for her. A leaf of charcoal cannot teach back what has been taken out of a person. I write it for the ones who come after, who will want to know why the hands stop together on the tenth, why nobody claps in the gap, and why the ninth line is said to the rack and never to a face.

The tally this is written on counted forty pots and lost the figure in the damp before I wanted it. That is how I came by the back of it. A thing spoiled for the Court is the proper stuff for a children's game; the factor's man was going to throw it out and I asked him for it, and he gave it without looking, which is the only generosity I have ever had from that house.

# How it is played

1. Two girls sit facing at the end of a rack, with the pots standing dry behind them. They clap their own hands on the odd lines and strike palms on the even, so that the pattern is *own, together, own, together* all the way to the tenth. It is played while the racks are turned in the last of the light and not at any other time, because the racks want both hands and the game wants them too.
2. The count is said to the rack, not to the girl across from you. Said to a face it becomes an argument, and there is always a girl who will argue. The rack has no ears and will not carry the count up the road.
3. One line, one clap. On the tenth line the two palms meet and stay met, without pressing, while the words *Read the count* are said. This is the reading. A girl who claps the tenth instead of holding it has read aloud where she should have kept quiet, and sits with her hands in her lap until the other turns her palm upward and says, *The count begins again.* That is the whole of the bringing back. Nobody's name is said in it.
4. After the tenth there is the gap. The hands part and lie flat on the knees for one breath and nobody claps. A girl who claps in the gap has banked it, and sits out as above. The gap is not a day and has no clap in it; they will not be told what it is, and if you ask they ask you why a man wants to put a number on a breath.
5. When ten has been held and the gap kept, the hands go back to Firstday and the whole count turns again. There is no last round and nothing is won. The girl who has kept her hands still through the most gaps gets to say the ninth line, which is the one about the name. She does not get to say it to anybody.
6. The game is not played in the Five, when the children are made to stand and listen, nor on a day a collector has been seen on the Clay Road. It is played in the thaw on the dry boards and in Stone on the trodden ash inside the kiln-house, and the words are the same in both places.

# The count, as it is clapped

*Own hands on the odd lines, palms together on the even. On the tenth the palms meet and do not move. Say it to the rack.*

> Firstday, and the fire is turned with a pot,
> Secondday, and the grey goes around the cot;
> Thirdday, and the pot answers full,
> Fourthday, and the eel comes up through silt;
> Fifthday, and the loaf shows a browned edge,
> Sixthday, and the bit goes into the house;
> Seventhday, the wheel that will not stand,
> Eighthday, the broom that goes around;
> Ninthday, the name we say at home,
> Tenthday, the hands meet. Read the count.

# What is said in the gap

*Hands flat on the knees. One breath, no more. The words are said low, to the floor, and not across the space between the two girls.*

> No clap in the gap. The gap is not a day.
> The hands lie down. The hands come back.

They keep both hands in their laps through this, and a girl who lets one hand go up an inch to begin the next count early is banked, though nobody calls it that but me. I have watched them do it for nineteen springs and never once seen a girl correct herself before the breath was out.

# The verse they do not clap while a man from the factor's house is in the yard

I have this from the girl who says the ninth line, with the proper space left before it, which is the space the hands leave in the clapping. She would not say it while the clerk had his pen out and would not say it to him at all. She said it to the rack with her back turned and made me set it down with none of my words in it.

> They gave her a figure and sent her home,
> and she stands at the end where the racks run out;
> we clap to the rack and not to her,
> and the count goes round with a place kept warm.
>
> Six bits a day and the crust is free;
> the grey in the corner is not for the pot;
> nine in the hands and the tenth in the house,
> and the eleventh is not a day of the tend.

I asked her what the eleventh was. She said the factor's man would know, and that he had put it in his book while she was still saying the tenth, and that she had let him because a child who argues with a man in a soft cuff gets no crust at the end of the row. I have set it down as she said it, with the letting left in.

# What the woman at the shrine put in the margin

The charcoal here is hers, pressed hard and small, the way a woman writes who has kept birth-slates for nineteen years and does not want the words read over a cradle.

At *the fire is turned with a pot*: **She says pot. It is the back of a broken pot. A girl who has never turned coals should not say this line, and will say it all the same.**

At *the grey goes around the cot*: **The broom goes around it. The house goes around it. It is the same sentence said kindly. Do not let them make it a proverb before they have swept.**

At *Ninthday, the name we say at home*: **There is a name for the house and a name for the slate. Keep them on two places of the tongue, and do not let the second one travel.**

At *the gap is not a day*: **We sat with a covered pot through a Long Night and did not clap. That is the only gap I have kept, and it was longer than a breath.**

# What the clerk did

A factor's clerk borrowed the tally at Greybank on the morning the pots went down, being a man who cannot let paper lie still. He has a cuff gone soft from washing and the ink sits in two fingers of it and will not come out. He numbered the ten lines in the margin and wrote against the tenth, *the account is read and the parties are identified*. Against *no clap in the gap* he wrote *five days outside the tend; no observance; no charge to the district*. Against *the name we say at home* he wrote *the name is entered where the house requires*. At the foot, where the two hands lie on the knees and nothing at all is counted, he put a round nought and wrote *eleven* beside it, and under that, *vacancy*.

I have put their words back underneath and left his standing above. He takes the hands out of it because a thing always true has no need of them. I put them back because a girl up to her wrists in slip does not know she is hearing something always true, and wants to know when to clap. A nought is a number. The Five have names and no number. He has put a figure where a breath should be, which is the whole art of that hand.

He asked me what the children learned by the game. I told him the count and the stopping and the bringing back, and he said that was an account of the Return taught before a child could read a slate, and he entered it. The girls stop when a man with a soft cuff comes into the yard. They take it up when he is past the wheel-shed. They have never once changed the tenth.

# At the head and in the gutter

The slip at the head is dried pale in the creases and came off the hand that held the tally down on the rack rail; it goes into the grain and no damp takes it out, which is why a rack-woman uses charcoal and not ink. The tallow thumb is mine, from the lamp I held low while the girl said the verse with her back turned and I did not want her to see me writing. The chalk score along the fore-edge is a line one of them drew to count on and rubbed out with her sleeve before she was told to, which is the way lines go in this yard. The small-ale ring is from a cup set down by nobody in particular and claimed by three girls and denied by one. The green bead in the fold came off a girl come up from High Kiln with the sweetness still at her mouth, who leaned over to see the writing, touched the leaf and went back up the terrace without asking. The grey in the gutter came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying among the racks; I have not swept it, and there is no Last Day in a rack-shed.

# At the foot, in grey chalk, in a child's hand

> I kept my hands still and she did not come back.

The letters are hard at the *th* and one of them is set backward, which is how a child writes who has learned off a slate and not off a book. No hand will own it. I asked the girl who says the ninth line, the woman who keeps the shrine, and a potter going down with a barrow of wet ware, and had three answers and no hand, as I have had before. I have left it where it was written.

The tally goes back under the loose board. Tomorrow the racks will be turned in the last of the light and the girls will sit at the end of them and clap ten and keep the gap, and the one who came back will stand where she stands and watch the hands go round without putting hers in, and I shall not call her over. I have kept their words and I have not joined them to anything.
