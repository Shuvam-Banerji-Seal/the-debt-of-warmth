I went north with a letter of credit and forty gold crowns, and came back with iron, lead, and a stone that was warm in the morning and would not say who had warmed it. This is the whole of my errand and I will not dress it as a pilgrimage. The Ten Bowls buy iron and warm-stone from the mountain, and wool and grain go north to pay for them, and a clerk who can count is a clerk who gets sent. In Sere we call a debt carried gracefully an ornament of a house. In Keld a debt is a number cut into the forearm, and a man gives it before he gives his name. I set that difference down first because it cost me more than the iron did.

I write in the courtesy of my country, which is elaborate, and I have left the hard words hard. A favour done to a reader is a debt the reader did not ask for; I have done none. Where I could not learn a thing I have set down the shape of the not-learning, which is a thing a clerk can carry home and a priest cannot.

# The road up

The Clay Road ends at Greybank and the north begins somewhere past it, though not all at once. At Greybank a factor sold me a fist of warm-stone in a fold of wool for three marks and would not say whose fire it had come from. This was the first honest answer I had in that country. The road says: only fools and factors go up without a stone. I was a factor's clerk, which in the high country is a fool with better boots.

From Greybank the Rim Road runs a hundred and twenty leagues of improving road and worsening weather. There are inns at the first three stages and after that a man lodges with whoever will have him, and pays in a figure or a promise, and finds out in the morning which one was current. On the fourth day the water in the bucket had a skin of ice before I had carried it ten paces. On the sixth I could see the Rim, which is not a mountain but a wall of cold with a sky leaning on it.

At Keld Keep a man asked my measure before my name. I gave him the courtesy of Sere: Joren Brightbowl, of the Ten Bowls, in the service of the house, most honoured to be received. He waited. When I had finished he asked again. I told him I had no number cut in my arm, being a lowlander and uncut, and he wrote me down as a lowland figure and told me it would not carry me ten leagues beyond the pass. He was right. It did not.

I asked him why the figure went first. He said a name was a thing said in a doorway and a number a thing said in a mine, and that the mine had the longer memory. I have set that as he said it. It is a hard saying and I have not softened it with any of our words.

# What they count

In Keld a man's number is cut into the forearm at thirteen, on the day they call the Descent, and when a man comes back thin the old numbers are cut out and new ones put in, so that a smooth place on the arm is not a blank but a thing that happened. I saw three such places in the tally-room and asked a man with one of them what number he gave now. He gave the old one.

“But the skin has been corrected,” I said.

“The number was not,” he said, and went back to his counts.

The deep mines are worked by men bought for a term, whom the Grathic buyers call under-vessed. Their lives are reckoned at eleven months. I asked the mine-reeve what became of a man at the end of the eleven and he said the term was expiring, which is a phrase that lets a man go on writing. In the Bowl we empty a bowl at the feast and call a debt forgiven. Here they cut a number and call it a man. Both are figures in a clean hand, and neither hand washes.

A term. *Underneath, in mine:* I have left his correction standing above my row, as a repaired vessel keeps the old break showing. A term is the only figure in that country that I have seen a man refuse to call a number.

# The stone, the iron and the hand

On the third morning the mountain sweated. There was no cry and no bell. A man came up from the Ninth Gallery with the smell of hot lead on his coat and said only that the gallery was giving back more than it took. The mine-reeve sent the men up before he wrote anything down, which is the first time in that country that I saw a figure wait for a man. I had been told the smell was the whole warning and had not believed it until I smelled it. It is a sweetish smell, like a glaze room in High Kiln, and a man from the south might think it a kindness. It is not.

I did not go down to the Duelling Floor, but I heard a clamp set across a way. It is a small sound, a staple going into green wood, and then a boy's breath let go in two pieces. A clamp-master told me that the first man to open a way usually wins, because the other is busy bleeding, and he said it as another man might say the price of an axe. He had a hand that would not close and held the clamp with the other.

A Keldish man told me the true Hearth lies under his mountain and that the Court's fire in the south is a candle lit from it. I said nothing, being a guest. A Court clerk standing near said nothing either and did not argue, which is the whole of the Court's answer in that country.

The warm-stone has kept a little heat for eleven days under my coat. Every morning I have asked whose fire it came from, and every morning I have had the same count of words: none at mine. A stone from the Ninth is a kindness bought by somebody whose name is not written where a traveller can read it, which is a generosity of the same order as ours in the Bowl and perhaps a more honest one, because it does not ask to be applauded.

# The people who sit against stone

I climbed five leagues higher into the Rim to the Listening Stones, where the Vorr sit against the stone for days and come away with numbers. One of them, a tall grey man with the last joint of his fingers doubled, let me stand beside him through a morning and would not let me look at the stone he kept. I asked him what the oldest marks meant. He said the numbers would be found correct when the season had turned, which is the answer they give and is not a translation. I have set it down because a courteous man owes his reader the exact shape of a refusal.

He told me, which was more than I expected, that the Vorr do not undergo the Return and are never collected. When I asked why, he said they had never been owed. He said it without pride, the way a man says that a door is locked, and I have not added to it.

# The road-song

*Taken down in Grathic letters from a carter going up, not down, on the sixth day out. He sang the middle verse twice, the second time for a man with a smooth place on his forearm. The Keldish vowels are long where ours are short; I have put them back as I heard them and left the hard consonants standing.*

> The road goes up and the name goes down,
> the number goes on the arm;
> three for the axe and two for the lead,
> and one for the stone that keeps a fire.
>
> Eleven months at the seam, they say,
> and a warm-stone sewn in the coat;
> the mountain keeps what the mountain took,
> and the carter carries what it wrote.
>
> Give me the count, I'll give you the cold,
> and the figure stays where the man went;
> a name in the mouth is a door in the wall,
> but a number is cut in the bone.

He would not sing going down. He said a song sung coming down was a debt with no date, and a man who had carried a load up the Rim Road had paid enough for the one he had carried up.

# What the clerk did at Greybank

A clerk of the Court borrowed the leaf on a market morning, being a man who cannot leave paper alone. He set his hand on the top sheet before he looked at me, and said it was entered as it stood. He writes in the narrow upright hand with the verbs taken out, as though a verb were a thing a man could smuggle in a cuff.

- Against *a man gives his number before his name* he wrote *the vessel is identified by measure*.
- Against *a life reckoned at eleven months* he set *term: eleven; value retained*.
- Against *the mountain sweated* he wrote *an increase in the seam-draw was recorded*.
- Where I had left the place where the warm-stone's payer should stand, he drew a small circle and wrote *unaccounted* beneath it.
- Against the Vorr man's saying that they had never been owed, he wrote *no charge lies against this party*, which is true in the same way that an empty cup is clean.

I have left every line of his standing above mine. He takes the verbs out because a thing always true has no need of them. I put them back because a clerk writing home wants to know who did what to whom, and the answer is in the sentence if there is someone in it.

# At the head and in the gutter

The gold at the head is mine; gold ink creeps into the grain of the paper and leaves a yellow edge that no washing takes out. The dust of the burning winds came north with me in the seams of my coat and has ground into the fold, a southern thing in an account of the north. The grey along the lower margin is stone dust from the First Seam; I set the leaf down on a bench where a surveyor had laid his slate and did not brush it off. The small round mark by the song is where I set a cup of wine on the first night back at Greybank and went to find the word I wanted; the wine went cold and has left a ring the colour of old lead. The bump in the third leaf is where the warm-stone lay under the quire for a night and kept the paper faintly warm until morning. The hard crumb in the gutter is Keldish barley bread, given to me on the road by a man who would take neither a coin nor a promise, and I have kept it because I could not enter it.

> At the foot, in a hand that answers to no name:
>
> The number goes on the arm and the name goes home;
> the stone keeps the fire and the fire keeps no date.
> Ask who paid for the warmth in a stranger's pocket ---
> the mountain does, and the mountain does not say.

I asked the mine-reeve, the Court clerk and the man with the smooth forearm, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The quire goes back into the factor's tin at Greybank. Tomorrow the grain goes north and the iron goes south, and the number in my own arm --- which is none, for I am a lowlander and uncut --- is still the only thing about me that nobody in Keld found worth entering.
