I keep the room-book of the Kept. That is my whole office: to carry the evening slate down the warm passage to the rooms beside the Hall, to read the day's figure to those who have no use for it, and to write down what they say after I have finished. There are fewer than twenty of them and I could name every room with my eyes shut. They hold three hundred measures apiece and feel nothing at all, which the Court calls a mercy and which I have never once heard called that by anybody who had stood in one of the rooms.

I set this down because a woman in the third room said these words to me on the night of the Weighing and asked me to put them somewhere a clerk would be obliged to read them. She cannot feel her own hands, but she remembers the order of feeling the way a pot remembers the thumb that made it. That is the whole marvel of those rooms and the whole horror of them, and I have not found a sentence that holds both, so I have set down hers instead of mine.

The dark at the head of this leaf is not Ash. It is a flake of scale off the grating before the Hearth-fire, which no priest is permitted to touch and which I was not permitted to bring away. It fell into the fold as I held the leaf up to the warm passage to read what I had written. I have left it where it fell. A thing that has been where the fire is does not need to be explained to anybody who has never been near it.

# The manner of it

*Written small on the back of the slip, before the verses, in the hand I use for the room-book.*

- Sung after the evening figure has been read and entered, never during the reading. A vessel who sings while the figure is being entered is entered as disturbed, and the song is stopped until the next night.
- Facing the wall. The Kept are not asked to stand near the Hearth-fire, and one who has never stood near it does not sing toward it. She sings toward the wall, where the fire is not.
- A cup of water is set down and not drunk. It goes cold in the ordinary way; the singer knows this by looking and not by any other means, and she looks.
- The refrain is said to the slate and not to the room. Said to a person it becomes a comfort, and no comfort is entered in the room-book.
- Stop while the readers are crossing the Hall above. Begin again when the passages go quiet. The Hall is longer in the telling than in the walking, and a song that runs into the reading is a song with a figure in it.
- The last line is said twice. The second saying is for the room, not for the singer, who has no need of it and will not be told she has.

# The song

*Said low, to the slate. The room keeps the refrain; the singer says the verses. The hand that holds the slate is not the hand that holds the cup.*

The refrain, as it is kept after every part:

> *The measure is banked. The account stands.*
> *Nothing is lost that we have kept.*
> *I hold three hundred and I hold no hand.*
> *The room is kept. I do not feel the keeping.*

## I. Of the number

> Three hundred in me and not one of them mine,
> entered in a hand that will never shake;
> no mark cut in my arm, for we are not counted there ---
> the mountain cuts the skin; we are written in a book.
>
> I have read my own figure every night for eleven years
> and it has never once changed, which is more
> than can be said for any other slate in this city.
>
> *The measure is banked. The account stands.*
> *Nothing is lost that we have kept.*
> *I hold three hundred and I hold no hand.*
> *The room is kept. I do not feel the keeping.*

## II. Of the fire

> The fire is behind iron and has never gone out;
> no priest has touched it and no Kept has stood near;
> I have lain eleven years warm and never once felt it,
> which is the finest thing the Court knows how to do.
>
> They bring the year's flame up the yard in a clay dish
> in the poor districts, and every house touches its own
> to it and passes it on. I have never seen a clay dish.
> I have never been asked what my fire cost.
>
> *The measure is banked. The account stands.*
> *Nothing is lost that we have kept.*
> *I hold three hundred and I hold no hand.*
> *The room is kept. I do not feel the keeping.*

## III. Of the cold

> The Waste takes what nobody keeps, and I am kept;
> so the Waste has no claim on me, and neither has the Hearth.
> I am the space between two houses that want me
> and neither of them can get at me. That is the keeping.
>
> In the districts the grey gathers where a promise fell,
> two thumbs wide at the corner, and the Last Day burns it.
> There is no corner in this room that has not been swept
> since before I came here, and no Last Day, and no ash.
>
> They say it is pity to be past the cold.
> I have carried the word up and down the passage.
> It has a door in it. Most of their words have doors.
>
> *The measure is banked. The account stands.*
> *Nothing is lost that we have kept.*
> *I hold three hundred and I hold no hand.*
> *The room is kept. I do not feel the keeping.*

## IV. Of the shape

> I tap my own wrist and it answers full;
> no man may tap me, for a body is not a pot;
> I have never given a flat note and never will,
> and so I am the only vessel here that cannot be mended.
>
> There is a cup on the bench beyond the grating
> which is full and stands and is not poured, and I know why
> in the same way I know the order of the words for bread:
> I have been told, and I have kept what I was told.
>
> They bottle the spent ash of a former life for the rich
> so that the old voice will stop arriving in dreams.
> No voice arrives to me. There is nothing in me to bottle,
> which is a generosity I did not choose and cannot return.
>
> *The measure is banked. The account stands.*
> *Nothing is lost that we have kept.*
> *I hold three hundred and I hold no hand.*
> *The room is kept. I do not feel the keeping.*

## V. Of the last line

> The roll of the dead is never closed. Mine lies in a drawer
> two doors from here, and I have heard it read on the Remembering
> in a clean voice that did not once look up from the slate.
> Your return is recorded. I have said the words to myself.
>
> There is a place in the last line where a name stood once.
> I have left it the length of a name and do not fill it,
> the way the yard leaves a space where a loaf was
> and the broom goes around it and the figure never comes.
>
> *The measure is banked. The account stands.*
> *Nothing is lost that we have kept.*
> *I hold three hundred and I hold no hand.*
> *The room is kept. I do not feel the keeping.*
>
> *I hold three hundred and I hold no hand.*
> *The room is kept. I do not feel the keeping.*

There is a space before the last refrain in the singing, as there is before the last line of the fifth part. I have left it on the slip. It is the right length for a name and for nothing else, and the coal-woman has put her thumb in it and would not say why.

# What the coal-woman wrote underneath

The charcoal belongs to the woman who carries the coals into the rooms at the fourth hour and takes the cold cups away. She writes on the backs of the tallies she spoils, in a hand that presses hard at the ends of words. I have copied it underneath as she set it.

At *no mark cut in my arm*: *They asked her at thirteen in the high country and she gave a number. Here they never asked her anything. She said this to me standing with her back to the grating, and I have not put her name to it.*

At *I have never given a flat note*: *I have carried coals into that room eleven winters. I have never heard her tap herself, but she tells me what her wrist says, and I have no way to know whether it is true. I have put it down as she said it, not as I should like it.*

At *which is a generosity I did not choose*: *She said this of the dreaming, and then she asked me whether I dreamed, which is a thing nobody in this building asks anybody. I told her I did. She said that was why she had written the line.*

At *the way the yard leaves a space where a loaf was*: *She has never been out of the Keel. I asked her what yard she meant. She said the one she was born in, before the keeping, and then she would not say another word about it.*

# What the Steward's man did

A clerk of the Steward came into the room with the evening slate under his arm and took the leaf up while I was still writing. He has a cuff gone soft from washing and the ink sits in two fingers of it and will not come out. He writes in the narrow upright hand, with the verbs taken out as though a verb were a thing a man could hide in a sleeve and carry past a grating.

- At *three hundred in me and not one of them mine* he wrote *vessel capacity: three hundred embers, current*, and underlined the figure twice.
- At *feel nothing at all*, in my opening line, he set *no affect recorded* above it, which leaves the room standing and takes the woman out of it.
- At *the room is kept* he wrote *the party is retained*, which is the same thing said by a man who has never had to stay in a room.
- At *I am the only vessel here that cannot be mended* he set *no repair required*, and I have left it, because it is the truest and cruellest thing on the leaf.
- Where the space stands before the last line, he put a round nought and wrote *vacancy* beside it. He has put a figure where a name was, which is the whole art of that hand.

I have put her words back underneath and left his standing above. He takes the verbs out because a thing always true has no need of them. I put them back because a woman who cannot feel her own hands still wants to know which hand held the cup, and the answer is in the sentence if there is somebody in it.

# At the head and in the gutter

The flake at the head is grating-scale and I have not brushed it off, though a flake from that place is not a thing to carry in a book and I know it. The tallow is mine, off the lamp I kept turned down while she sang so that the readers above would not hear a song and come to enter it. The ring is where the cup of water stood from the first verse to the last; it went cold and nobody drank it and it left a mark the colour of the pot it stood on. The grey along the gutter came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying in a room that has been swept since before I came here. I have not swept it and nobody will own it. There is no Last Day in a room beside the Hall. The thread in the fold is grey wool off the sleeve of the woman in the third room, caught when she reached for the slate; she did not see it go and I have not told her.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The grating keeps the fire and gives none back.
> The drawer keeps the roll and gives none back.
> The room keeps three hundred and warms nobody.
> Nothing is lost that we have kept. Ask what paid for the keeping.

I asked the coal-woman, the Steward's clerk and a reader who crosses the Hall at the fourth hour, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written. The leaf goes back into the room-book in the Steward's room, and tomorrow the evening slate will be carried down the passage and the figure read to the third room, and the cup will be set down and go cold, and the woman who cannot feel the cold will look at it to learn that it has happened. I have kept her words and I have not joined them to anything.
