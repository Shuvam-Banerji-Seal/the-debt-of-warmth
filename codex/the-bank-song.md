I dig the face above the Slip Brook, two doors below the wheel-shed, and have done it nine winters. The clay comes out in beds, the way bread comes out of a batch; cut across the grain and you get not a lump but a handful of the bank's own wall. There is a grey thread runs through the face a hand's breadth below the top. We leave it standing. When a girl asks why, we tell her to cut her own cut and find out; if she asks again we send her to the pot, which has no answer.

A man come up from Greybank with a soft cuff stood at the lip and asked what the women said while they cut. I told him. He wrote it down before I had finished, with the doing taken out and a figure put where a place should stand, and went off up the road with his boots clean. So I have set the words down as we say them, to the face and not to the bridge, and put the verbs back where he took them out.

The clay at the head is mine. I held the docket down with the hand I dig with and did not know I had marked it. Clay goes into the grain and no washing takes it out, which is why we sign with it here and not with ink.

# How it is cut

- Cut from the top down, never from the bottom up. A face undercut at its foot will come down on the next woman who stands before it, and the bank has no slate and sends no word ahead of itself.
- Cut with the grain, not across it. The beds lie one on one the way silt lies on a step. A blade along them takes what the face was holding; a blade across them takes the face as well and calls it a lump.
- Do not stand under the lip. Before a man stands under it, it is clay and the bank's own business. After he stands under it, it is a room, and it is a room the bank did not ask him into.
- Leave the grey thread where it lies. Do not put it in the barrow and do not put it on the rack. If a coarse pot comes out of a barrow of that clay, it goes flat under the knuckle, and the pot is set by the wall with the crack showing so nobody has to be told whose cut it was.
- Knuckle a lump before it goes up with the rest. A whole one answers full and the note goes through; a hollow stops at the skin and gives back flat. The fire finds what the spade let go, and does not ask who let it.
- Count the cuts going up, never the barrow coming down. A cut that has reached the barrow has left the face; a count coming down is a count of empty barrows, which a clerk can enter and nobody else wants.
- Say the words down into the face, not to a woman beside you; said to a person they become instruction, and instruction at one bank is instruction she will unlearn at another.
- Do not sing it at the Cross, and do not sing it on an Account-day. The bell goes four, four and a gap, and a digging-song set against that gap comes home wrong in the wrist.
- The last part is kept back. It is for when the kiln-master has gone up the terrace and the man from Greybank has gone back over the north bridge with his satchel under his arm and the yard can hear itself again.

# The song

*Called down into the face. One verse to three cuts; the fourth cut is the lifting of the spade and is not said aloud. The refrain goes into the clay and not across the bank.*

> The face has a grain the way bread has a grain,
> laid down in the bank before any of us came;
> cut with the grain and the lump comes away,
> cut across it and you break the bank's own wall.
>
> *Cut it, lift it, lay it by,*
> *three to the barrow and the fourth let lie;*
> *the grey seam stands where the spade won't go,*
> *and the bank keeps the roof that the clay lets go.*

> The bank sweats in Ember and rings in Stone;
> a spade in the frost tells you where it has been;
> there's a trickle of cold at the root of the face
> that runs for the Brook and never comes back.
>
> *Cut it, lift it, lay it by,*
> *three to the barrow and the fourth let lie;*
> *the grey seam stands where the spade won't go,*
> *and the bank keeps the roof that the clay lets go.*

> Don't stand where the lip hangs over the cut.
> A roof is a room the moment a man stands under;
> before that it's clay and the bank's own business,
> and the bank has not asked anybody in.
>
> *Cut it, lift it, lay it by,*
> *three to the barrow and the fourth let lie;*
> *the grey seam stands where the spade won't go,*
> *and the bank keeps the roof that the clay lets go.*

> There's a grey runs through the face like a thread through a sleeve;
> leave it where it lies, don't send it up in the barrow.
> Ask the pot what a grey cut costs set down there ---
> it has no answer, and it will not be told.
>
> *Cut it, lift it, lay it by,*
> *three to the barrow and the fourth let lie;*
> *the grey seam stands where the spade won't go,*
> *and the bank keeps the roof that the clay lets go.*

> Knuckle a lump before it goes up with the rest.
> A whole one answers full and the note goes through;
> a hollow stops at the skin and gives back flat,
> and the fire finds what the spade let go.
>
> *Cut it, lift it, lay it by,*
> *three to the barrow and the fourth let lie;*
> *the grey seam stands where the spade won't go,*
> *and the bank keeps the roof that the clay lets go.*

> Three to the barrow and the fourth to the bank,
> and one in twelve is the figure they read;
> the figure you hear is not the figure you pay,
> and the grey in the seam is the part they don't take.
>
> *Cut it, lift it, lay it by,*
> *three to the barrow and the fourth let lie;*
> *the grey seam stands where the spade won't go,*
> *and the bank keeps the roof that the clay lets go.*

There is a space before the last refrain, the length of a hand laid flat on the face where you feel for the grey before you cut. I have left it on the docket. A clerk put a round nought in it once, a thing you can hold up and look at; a hand laid flat is not.

# The part they keep back

*Not sung while a man from the terrace or a man with a satchel stands at the lip. Said down into the face and not across the bank.*

> There are places in this yard the foot goes round
> with a look and no number and nobody's name;
> the face is one of them, and the grey thread another,
> and the bank keeps both and asks no rent for either.
>
> The barrow goes up and the figure goes up
> and the face stays here with its roof and its thread;
> we do not say the fourth to a man with a soft cuff,
> and we do not stand where the roof is.

There is no verse after it. The next thing is the spade going back into the face, which is not a verse and has never wanted one.

# What the kiln-master did

He came up from the terrace on the wrong wind with the sweet edge of the glaze at one corner of his mouth and read the docket with one eye on the bank. Against *leave the grey thread where it lies* he wrote in broad charcoal: *Overburden at the face; rejected at source. Sound clay only to be removed. No loss arises.* He underlined it twice.

Underneath, in mine: He is right about the clay and wrong about the loss. What is left standing is not nothing even when it is not entered, and a bank without its own roof is a hole with a woman at the bottom of it. He reads with one eye on the kiln, as a man does who has never stood under the lip and listened.

# What the clerk did

The factor's clerk borrowed the leaf at Greybank on the morning we unloaded the tile, being a man who cannot leave paper lying. His cuff was gone soft from washing and the ink sat in two fingers of it. He took the verbs out and set his words above mine, and I have left his hand standing above ours, as the face keeps the old bed under the new cut.

- At *the bank keeps the roof that the clay lets go*, he wrote *unworked face; residue unentered.* It is true in a figure and false at the lip, where a roof is the one thing the bank will not let go.
- At *a hollow stops at the skin and gives back flat*, he wrote *vessel unsound; quality not a figure*, and underlined it twice. He thinks a flat note is a defect entered. At this bank a flat note is a lump set by the wall with the crack showing.
- At *the figure you hear is not the figure you pay* he struck the line through and wrote *deduction current, one part in twelve; statement erroneous*. He has entered the number that was read and not the number that was paid, which is the whole distance between a cuff and a wrist.
- At the three cuts he wrote *three removed, fourth nil*, and drew a line under it that went a finger's breadth past the margin. The fourth is not nil. It is the spade coming up, which a man can carry away in a figure and a woman cannot.
- Where I had left the space before the last refrain he put a round nought and wrote *vacancy* beneath it. A nought is a number and travels. A hand laid flat on the face is a place, and stays where it was put.

# What the woman at the tubs put in the margin

The thick charcoal belongs to a woman who scours at the tubs four doors down and has carried water past this bank since before the Glaze Wars. I have copied it as it stands, without making the hands agree.

At *don't stand where the lip hangs over the cut*: *A girl went in under the old face in Silt and came out with clay to her knee and her bodice full of it. The face did not kill her. It kept her a while. I have not stood under since, and I have not told her what I think of the place she stood.*

At *ask the pot*: *I asked. It gave back flat. It was a pot out of a bank like this one, and a woman had carried it two years before it went. I have not told the kiln-master and I have not set it by the wall, which is a thing I will answer for somewhere else.*

At the part they keep back: *The yard goes round its places with a look and no number. I have never asked which number this face would have if it had one, because a number is the one thing the bank has not got and the only one we have never tried to give it.*

# At the head and in the gutter

The clay thumb at the head is mine and will not come out of the grain, which is why I use it to sign. The pale line along the gutter came out of the Brook while the docket lay on the bank and has taken the edge off one word of the sixth verse; I have not written it back. A song with a word gone is a song said slowly, and everything here is said slowly. The grey flake in the fold came off the thread itself under the spade and is lighter in the hand than a good flake, which is the whole of the lesson and no use at all to a clerk. The root-hair across the second leaf came out of the face with a cut and caught there; I have left it lying across the word it landed on. The green bead came off a girl with sweetness at her mouth who touched the docket; I have not washed it. The sour ring is small ale set down at the first verse and taken up at the last, by which time it had gone the colour of the face it stood on. The grey along the outer fold came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying on a bank. Nobody will own it. There is no Last Day at a face; the grey stays where it fell and goes into the next year under the wool. The nick is a spade caught in the fold, and I have kept the fold open at it.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> *The face keeps the grey. The barrow keeps the count.*
> *The slate keeps neither, and goes up the road.*

I asked the woman at the tubs, a girl who has this year come to the bank, and a carter waiting with his horse at the yard gate, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written.

The docket goes back under the flat stone at the lip. Tomorrow the spade goes into the face above the Brook, three cuts and the fourth where the bank put it, and the grey thread standing where no spade has set it; and if the clerk comes again with his soft cuff, he may have the nought. He cannot have the hand laid flat, because it is a place, and he has no word for a place he cannot carry up the road.
