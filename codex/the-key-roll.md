I keep the key-slate in the Steward's room. Nine springs rise in the Keel within twenty leagues of this house, and five of them stand sealed. The keys hang on a nail behind the slate, and when a key goes out I write the hour and the hand and the spring it opens, and when it comes back I write the hour again and nothing else. The slate lies flat under the fingertips while a person is still speaking, which is the Steward's own order and not mine. What follows is Title the Eighth, copied out so that a keeper may know what is being entered against him before it is entered. The ordinance is in Ledgerhand, with nobody in any of its sentences. I have put the verbs back underneath, because a key with nobody to carry it is a key that cannot be lost, and the Court has never yet kept a key that could not be lost.

> *At the head of the first leaf, in the same hand, written very small:* Copied by the room and for it. If this is read beyond the passage, let it be seen that the verbs were restored here and struck out again above. Let both stand. A leaf with two hands on it is the only kind a keeper believes.

# Title the Eighth, as it stands in the Steward's room

*From the Ordinances of the Keeping, Title the Eighth: Of the Nine Wells, their Sealing, the Keys, and Drawing Without Authority. Four springs sealed in the first hundred and twenty years; a fifth sealed in Y.H. 618; added to in Y.H. 782 and in the Thin Year, Y.H. 812.*

## I. Of the springs and their sealing

1. Nine springs rise in the Keel. The ground there gives back more than it takes, as the ordinance says. Four were sealed in the first hundred and twenty years of the Keeping; a fifth was sealed in Y.H. 618. The others stand open and are not the Court's to keep.

2. A spring sealed is covered with a stone and a ring of cold iron. No priest blesses the stone. No name is cut upon it. The name goes into the sealed files and is not read at the Weighing.

3. The keys are kept in the Steward's room upon a nail. No key goes out without a figure, an hour and a hand entered against it. A key that goes out and comes back with the hour unentered is a key that has been out too long, whether or not the spring was drawn.

4. A key is carried in a wool sleeve, never bare against the stone. The wool is not entered and does not belong to the account.

> *Margin:* The ground gives back more than it takes, as the article says. I have watched the level in the second spring fall a hand's breadth through a dry winter and come back in the thaw. I have entered neither level. The ordinance has no column for a level, and a clerk who makes a column has begun to write a doctrine, and doctrines get copied.

> *Margin, in a second and narrower hand:* The fifth spring was sealed in Y.H. 618 and the keeper of it was hanged for drawing without a key. The roll gives the office and not the name. I have copied the year into the margin and left the article as it stands, which names neither the spring nor the man.

> *Margin:* A reeve come up from the Fenmarch says the spring sealed in Y.H. 618 is not under the Keel at all but under the Empty Cantons, and that the Court's survey sets it twenty leagues from this room so that no collector need walk the south road. I have set his saying beside the figure and joined neither. A man who joins the two has drawn a map, and I have no licence to draw one.

## II. Of the drawing and the key

5. Drawing from a spring without a keeper's key forbidden. Drawing from the Hearth without a keeper's key forbidden. The Hearth-fire stands behind a grating of cold iron; no priest has ever been permitted to touch it; even the Kept are not asked to stand near it.

6. Where the drawing is lawful, the Warm taken is booked in embers at five marks the ember, and the level of the spring is not entered. Where the drawing is unlawful, the key is withheld and the draw is entered as taken, which is the same figure with a different hand beside it.

7. A keeper who draws without authorisation is corrected. The correction entered in Y.H. 618 was a hanging, and no correction of that kind has been entered since. The account of that keeper stands open and is carried forward.

| Offence | As entered | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Drawing without a key | The draw entered as taken | The key withheld; account carried forward |
| A key out beyond the hour | The nail noted empty | No second key cut |
| A key laid bare against the stone | Not entered | Nothing |
| A level falling through a dry winter | Not entered | A mark in the margin, which is not a figure |
| The name of the keeper corrected in Y.H. 618 | The office, not the name | Carried forward |

> *Margin:* I have carried one keeper's account forward for eleven years. Nobody has come to close it and I have not closed it. A name carried forward is a different weight from a name bought, though the hand that writes them is the same.

> *Margin, in a leaning hand with a little gold in it, a factor's from Greybank:* Against *was hanged*, set *was corrected in the ordinary manner*. Both stand. A phrase with nobody in it costs less to copy, and the factor does not have to carry the key back to the passage at the end of the day.

## III. Of who may stand near

8. No priest touches the grating. A keeper of the third watch reads through the bars and the keepers answer; what is answered through the bars is not entered. The Weighing is lit by the fire behind them, and the figure read in that light is one in twelve, as the notice says. There is no other figure in the Hall.

9. The Kept are held near the fire and are not asked to stand before it. Fewer than twenty, three hundred measures apiece, and spoken of with pity. The pity is entered nowhere.

> *Margin:* One of the Kept passed the door while I was entering a key and did not turn toward the fire. A clerk come up from Slate Row wrote *no vacancy in the roll* against the space where it stood. He had looked at the space and put a figure there. I have not rubbed his figure out and I have not turned the Kept toward the fire.

> *Margin:* A keeper of the third watch told me the bars are cold on the fire side and warm on the room side, which is the order of things turned about. I have not put it in an article. An article has no room for a thing a man notices at three in the morning.

## IV. Of the keeping of the roll

10. The key-slate is kept in the Steward's room and is not shown to a household. The nail is not covered and is not explained. Where a keeper dies, the key is hung back upon the nail before the name is spoken in the room.

11. An account is never closed; it is only carried forward. A spring sealed is not called ended. A key lost is not called given.

> *Margin:* There are nine keys on the nail and I have counted them every morning for six years without once writing the count down. A count I write becomes a figure and a figure can be carried off in a cuff and entered at the next crossing. The nail keeps its own.

# The form of a key-receipt

*Set out at the end of Title the Eighth. I have copied it with the blanks as they stand. In six years nobody has filled the last line in the hand the Steward requires.*

> Entered this day, being ---day of -------, in the year of the Hearth ------, the key to the ------- spring taken from the nail at the ------- hour by ----------------------, for ----------------------, and returned at the ------- hour, the level not entered; the hand that carries not the hand that writes. Taken by ----------------------, who is not the man who keeps the nail.

# The verse the tellers say in the passage

*Said low between the Steward's room and the Hall, while the key is still in the wool. Never while the slate lies open on the desk. The last line is said by all.*

> Four stand open and five stand shut,
> and nine are told upon the slate;
> a key goes out and a key comes in,
> and the level is not entered.
>
> The fire is barred and the bar is cold
> to the hand that has not kept the watch;
> a keeper drew and the roll kept still,
> and the man went out without a name.
>
> Wool to the hand and iron to the ring,
> a nail with nine and a slate with one;
> what the spring gives back the spring remembers,
> and the account stands where the key stood.

> *In the narrow upright hand, against the first line:* Nine wells: five sealed, four current. *In mine:* He has put a figure where I put a count. A count of springs is a thing a man can say in the dark; a figure is a thing he can hand to another man in the light. I have left both.

# What the two borrowed hands did

The College clerk came down from Slate Row with a cuff gone soft from washing and ink in two fingers. Against article one he struck *the ground gives back more than it takes* and set *a measure is drawn and recorded* above it, which is true of every spring and therefore of none. I put the words back underneath. Take the giving out of a spring and there is nobody left to ask what it gave, and the question comes in the end, always in the passage, always after the key is back on the nail.

The other hand is the factor's, leaning and a little gold. He set *was corrected in the ordinary manner* above the hanging and underlined it twice. Both stand. The roll gives the office and not the name, and a correction with nobody in it is the kind the Court has always liked best.

# At the head and in the gutter

The tallow at the head is off the lamp I keep low so that a man speaking at the slate cannot see me writing down the hour. The warm-stone dust in the gutter came off a stone a keeper carried in from the passage; warm-stone holds heat longer than anything else in Sarn and gives none of it back to the paper. The green at the fold is a stain off the lip of the second spring, which I touched with a wet finger while the key was out. It comes back every damp month, which is more than the level does. The scratch in the lower margin is the key itself, laid there while I counted the nails on the frame and did not look at the slate; it is the length of a key and is not an entry. The iron-gall is in two fingers and will not come out, which is how the Steward knows I have copied a thing before he asks. The tide-line along the foot comes up from the sealed-file room, which is colder than the rest of the Hearthseat. There is no Last Day in this room. The key hangs on the nail and the nail is not swept.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The spring keeps its level. The nail keeps its key. The roll keeps the office, and the man who held it is carried forward.

I asked a keeper of the third watch, a factor come up from Greybank, and a woman who brought a cup of water from one of the open springs, and had three answers and no hand, as I have had before. I have left the line where it was written.

The leaf goes back under the key-slate. Tomorrow a key will go out in a wool sleeve and come back before the hour, and I shall enter the hour and not the level, and put the verbs back where another hand has struck them out. The figure on the notice is one in twelve, and there is no other figure in this room.
