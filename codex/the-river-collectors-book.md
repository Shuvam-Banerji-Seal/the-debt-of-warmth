I draw the measure on the water. There are slates in this country that touch and slates that do not. The man at the Court's table at the Cross touches the top of his before he looks at the woman waiting; the collector of the clay towns keeps a book I have never seen open; the clerk at the Fishstairs holds his slate to his forearm and writes before he looks at the stalls. I keep mine under oiled paper in the stern of the boat. The four of us enter the same year and our entries never meet, which is the nearest thing this country has to an arrangement.

The boat has six oars and a man at the stern who never touches them. He steers with one hand laid on the tiller and keeps the other in his coat, and when a house comes down to the bank he does not look up. I have drawn with the oars shipped and the boat standing still, and with the water slapping under the rowlocks, and the note is harder to hear in the second case, which is why I set a knuckle to the gunwale before I set the tube to a way. Write first. Look after. That is the order on the river, and the man at the stern has made a trade of never looking at all.

Twelve copper bits to a silver mark; twenty marks to a gold crown, a Sere coin that comes north with the factor and does not stay in Grath. The Court books an ember at five marks and no man on this water has exchanged one. A newborn vessel holds twelve to fifteen, a labourer in good case forty to sixty. One in twelve is printed on the notice and read twice at the Cross, once for the moot and once for those who came late. A second figure goes around the hamlets, comes aboard, and goes off again, and I have no column for it, not being a figure I can weigh.

> *In the narrow upright hand of a clerk come down from the College:* Coin and reckoning entered. Ember: five marks. *Underneath, in mine:* He has written the ember as though a mark could be poured into it at this gunwale. I have put the verb back. A mark is a coin and an ember is a word the Court uses for warmth it has already counted, and a boatman who has pulled six oars against the current is entitled to know which of the two he is being told.

# The rates, as I carry them

| Entry | As the book has it |
|---|---|
| Copper bit | twelve to a silver mark |
| Silver mark | twenty to a gold crown, a Sere coin |
| Ember | five marks, by the Court's book; no man has exchanged one |
| Newborn vessel | twelve to fifteen embers |
| Labourer in good case | forty to sixty embers |
| Deduction | one in twelve, as the notice says |
| Thin return | many measures pooled and divided; thirty marks, paid by the district |
| Whole return | one measure into one vessel; twelve hundred marks and forty unspent measures |

I have set the returns with no second figure beside them. I have heard what is paid in the hamlets and have not entered it. A clerk who writes down a figure he cannot weigh at the beam is a clerk who will be asked to read it aloud, and I do not read anything aloud on this boat.

Draws on this leaf: seven. Lines with no draw in them: three. Figures struck: none. Columns struck: one. Oars shipped: twice.

# The note

Before the tube I set a knuckle on the gunwale or the rim of the cup the household hands me across the bank. A whole vessel answers full. One cracked through answers flat, a small dead sound that stops at the surface, which is the sound a peat-cutter made at Mud End and a quarry hand made at the Ferr, though neither house would have it said of them. A vessel with air under the note answers nearly full, and the hamlets have no word for it, so I have set the phrase down as I heard it in the yard, which is not Grathic and will not be read at a Weighing. A vessel turned toward in expectation may give nothing at all; I have entered that as *none* and not as a nought, because a nought is a figure and a silence is not.

The kiln-masters of Sill know the full note and keep it to themselves, which is their right and their trade. A collector who cannot hear a vessel draws blind. He will still bring back his figure. The figure will still be entered. That is the trouble with figures.

# The column they struck out

Beside each row I had set down what was said at the water's edge while the oars were shipped. Against the peat-cutter, *the thaw will tell*; against the woman of the eel-boards, a date and the river; against the render, *ask the pot*; against the quarry hand, nothing but the weather; against the foundling, the name a woman said into the ground before she saw me. I had put no heading over the column. A heading would make it an account, and an account is a thing a man may be called to answer for.

The man from the College borrowed the leaf at Greybank and struck the column through from the first row to the last, and wrote *No such column* above it in his narrow upright hand. I have drawn the column back underneath and left his words standing over it. He had to count the rows before he could write that there were none worth entering, and I watched him do it, standing as he stands, with his cuff gone soft and his ink settled in two fingers. A man does not count what he has not looked at. The College has eleven words for a deduction and not one for a date, which is the whole of the difficulty between us.

# What the two borrowed hands did

The College man struck the verb out of the order of the draw. Where I wrote *I drew*, he set *the measure was drawn* above it, and above that *the warmth was entered*, which is further from the bank than the first. I have put *I drew* back beneath both. A sentence with nobody in it is a sentence no woman can ask about, and women ask about these draws in the end, always at the door, always after the tube is put away.

The other hand is tall and leaning and comes out of the factor's house at Greybank. Against *a man the river took* it wrote *no return arising, instrument not presented*, and against the empty figure column it set a round nought. I have left both standing. A nought is a figure that can be carried away and entered at the other end; the river took a man and left a place, and a place is not a nought. The hand put a nought in it because a hand that writes whole returns for persons not in the room has long since stopped asking which way a man went.

# The song the boatmen carry on the lower Ilm

*Sung with the stroke, never while the oars are shipped at a house, and never twice in one tend.*

> Six at the oar and one at the stern,
> and the one at the stern does not pull;
> the knuckle goes to the gunwale first,
> and the figure comes up with the silt.
>
> One in twelve, says the notice, read twice at the Cross,
> four and four and the gap uncrossed;
> I hold the tube where the oars dip in
> and write what the bank said before the face.
>
> Ask the pot, ask the weather, ask the river, they say;
> the Court has a figure for each of the three.
> The stern keeps a hand that has never held oar,
> and the boat keeps a place that the count cannot fill.

A boatman sang the first two lines dropping down to Greybank and would not sing the third while the factor's clerk stood on the quay, which is how I know which line is the one that matters. I have copied it as it came and put no gloss on it. A collector who explains a song has begun to write a sermon, and sermons get copied.

# In the head and in the gutter

The silt in the gutter came off the peat bank at Mud End and I have never got it out. The white bloom at the head is salt off the Sallow wind, which comes up the water on a flood tide and leaves a rime on everything aboard; it has eaten one letter of the Reed 30 date so that I cannot now read whether the barrow went down on the thirtieth or the twentieth. The eel slime along the fore-edge is not mine and is not the Weir woman's either, but came off a board laid across the stern while she told me the date and would not tell me the cost; it dries pale and comes again whenever the damp gets into the fold. The pale round ring was made by setting a cold tube down on the leaf while I reached for the tiller. The tube was not used in the stern and the ring is not a wound, but it is the thinnest paper on the page.

The tallow thumb at the foot is mine, from holding the lamp low while a woman at the Tallow Works said something I wanted the end of; she did not tell me the end. The green bead in the lower corner is not slip. It is lead glaze from a girl come down from High Kiln who leaned over the gunwale to see the writing with the sweetness still at her mouth, touched the paper, said nothing, and went back up the terrace. I have left it where she put it. The grey along the margin is not mine and not the factor's, and I have not swept it. There is no Last Day on a boat. The fold goes back under the stern seat and the silt stays in the gutter.

The small bare square in the lower margin is where I scraped a word away with a knife. I had written *sixth* there, meaning the sixth morning I had shipped the oars and set the tube to a way nobody called, and then I counted and could not say which mornings were the same and which were not, so I took the word off. A figure that cannot be checked is a figure I have no right to leave standing, even in the margin.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The slate keeps the figure. The river keeps the place where the figure stood. The stern keeps a hand that has never been asked to pull.

I asked a boatman, a clerk come down from the College, and a woman who sold eels at the Weir, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written.

The book goes back under the stern seat and the tube into its wool sleeve. Tomorrow the oars go out on the flood and a house will come down to the bank, and I shall set the knuckle to the gunwale before the tube, and write the note before the face, and put the verbs back where the College struck them out. Downriver the salt gets into the fold and upriver the silt does. The four of us will enter the same year and our entries will not meet. The boat goes on.
