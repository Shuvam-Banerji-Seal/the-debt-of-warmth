I throw pots. That is the whole of what I am, and I went up the Rim Road because a man who throws pots is always short of iron and never short of a reason to go looking for it. I took nine coarse bowls and a letter from the factor at Greybank. A pot does not travel well on that road, which is the first thing I learned and the last thing I entered. Four came back whole. Two came back in pieces. Three I sold at the Keep for less than the cord they were tied with, and the cart came home lighter than it went and heavier by a thing I have never been able to enter honestly.

The mountain is a kiln with the door shut. I have said that to nobody, because a man who says it in Keld is taken for a spy and a man who says it in Nine Pots is taken for a fool. I write it here where the factor's clerk will find it, which is perhaps the same thing. Put a fire under clay and it holds. Put a fire under stone and it keeps. The Keldish have a word for the keeping and no word for the heat, which tells you which of the two they have learned to trust.

# The road, and what it took

Nine Pots stands eight leagues west of Sill, and the Rim Road passes north of it at the Ferr ford, so a man can set out after the morning's firing and sleep at Stonemouth with his pots unbroken if he wraps each in straw and does not let the ass choose the ford. I let the ass choose. One bowl went in the Ferr and came up in three pieces the colour of the water, blue-grey off the old working, and I entered it at Stonemouth and not again, because a bowl that goes into the Ferr does not come home, and a bowl that comes home in pieces has cost the journey its only honest figure.

Up from Stonemouth the road improves and the weather worsens, which is the order of the whole north. A loaded cart makes three leagues in a day on good road, and the Keldish stretch it to four when the horse is fresh and the driver cruel. My ass was neither. We made the Keep in nine days going up and seven coming down, and the seven were colder, which I did not expect and have not found a clerk who will enter.

I carried a warm-stone in my coat on the advice of a lime-man at Stonemouth who had been up twice and come back both times with all his fingers. The stone was the length of my palm and cost six marks at the Keep, which is a great deal of pots. The man who sold it told me the price and not the debt, which is the manner of that country and is not a rudeness. It kept my hand warm on the climb and was cold the morning I got home. I have kept the shaving of it in the fold of this book, where it has been cold since the day I set it there.

A survey post stands ten leagues beyond the Keep, where the road goes through into the cold. I walked out to where the ground begins to lose the track and stood there a while and came back. I have set that down plainly, because a man who writes that he went past a post he did not go past has begun a different book, and I have no wish to be paid for a road I have not walked. There is a post and no gate. I did not ask why. I have learned to leave some questions standing where they are.

# What the road took, and what it gave

*The left column is what I entered at Nine Pots. The next is what stood at the Keep. The last is what I wrote on the back of the docket at Greybank, where the factor's man puts a figure against a thing whether or not the thing has a figure in it.*

| Article | Entered at Nine Pots | Given at Keld Keep | What I set on the back |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coarse bowl, straw-wrapped | nine, at four bits | three sold, four whole, two broken | A bowl that breaks has no price and still cost the road. |
| Iron clamp, small | — | 1 bit and a favour | The favour is not current. I have entered it anyway. |
| Iron clamp, for the wheel-shed | — | 2 marks at the Keep, 2 marks again at Greybank | The second was for the road. The road has no slate of its own. |
| Warm-stone, a palm's length | — | 6 marks | Sold, not lent. Cold by the seventh day home. |
| Lead, a blue-grey chip | — | given, not sold | Off the Ferr, not out of the Ninth Gallery. I have kept the two apart. |
| A number, from a Vorr | — | 1 bit | The numbers are correct. I have not entered what it means. |

Twelve bits to the silver mark and twenty marks to a crown, if you can find a crown in Keld, which I could not. The Court books in embers and tells a man an ember is worth five marks, and nobody up there has ever exchanged one and nobody down here has either, but the figure travels faster than the coin, which is the whole art of that house.

# The weigh, or the number before the name

At the Warden's hall I gave my name, and the clerk looked at me until I understood that I had begun wrongly. He said nothing. A man beside me said, “State what you hold, then say who you are,” in the manner of a man repeating something he has said to fools before. I went back to the foot of the post and came again with two-and-forty, which is what I judge I carry, and the clerk wrote the figure first and asked the name second, as though a name were a thing a man might keep back and sell later.

A name is a thing a man can change at the next well. That is what the beside-man told me when I asked why the order was the order, and it is the only explanation I was given on the whole road that I could carry home and use. The number goes down the shaft with a man. The name stays in the room where he said it.

The mine-reeve checked me in red. He set his point to the slate where I had given two-and-forty and drew a small stroke through it and wrote the same two-and-forty above, which is how a clerk in that country tells you he has found no lie without saying anything a man can thank him for. The red is at the second corner of this leaf now, and I have not wiped it off and have not entered it.

# What is sold above ground

Iron is common in Keld and dear in Grath, and the difference is the price of the road, which is a thing the road itself will never enter. A good axe is half a mark up there and two marks at Stonemouth, and a man who carries one down is paid for the carrying and not for the iron, though the buyer will tell you otherwise and believe it. Coal comes out in lumps the size of a loaf and burns with no smoke, which I did not trust until I watched a potter at the Keep bake a small dish in it, glaze it badly, and blame the clay.

Warm-stone is sold and is not lent. I have set that down as it was said to me, twice, by two men who had no reason to agree. A thing that can be lent has a place in somebody's account. A thing sold is paid for, which is cleaner. The shavings swept from the cutting floor go by the half-mark, and a potter from Nine Pots will buy a shaving before he buys a stone, because a shaving will keep a cup warm through one night and that is as much warmth as a cup needs.

There is a Vorr at the weigh who sells numbers. I bought one for a bit and asked what it was for, and he said the numbers were correct, which is a sentence with a great deal in it and nothing I can translate. He would not say it twice. A man who asks a Vorr twice has been told the answer twice and has learned only what sort of man he is.

# The mouth, and the boy going down

The Descent is at thirteen. I stood at the mouth with the old women who count and watched a boy go down who had a number cut into his arm and a warm-stone put into his hand after the knife. His mother said nothing. One of the old women said the last part of a saying with him, and the boy said the first part before he went down and the women said the second, and nobody explained it to me, and I did not ask, because a saying explained to a visitor is a saying that has been emptied so that he can carry it away.

Two go down and the count comes two. That is what they say at the mouth, and it is not a promise that two come up. It is a promise that the count will be made. I have written it exactly as it was said, with the thing that is not a promise standing in the middle of the sentence, which is where the Keldish put their mercies, in a place a lowlander will not think to look for one.

A man at the mouth had two sets of numbers in one arm, the later cut deeper. The mine-reeve entered the later and left the earlier standing in the skin. I asked which of the two was the true one and was told that both stood, and that a man who comes back thin has the old numbers cut out and new ones put in, which is a kindness done with a knife and entered as a correction.

I asked if I might go down. They said a lowlander goes down as tribute or as a spy, and often as both, and I was neither, so I stood where I was and counted the boys. This is the whole of what I know of the First Seam, and I have set it down as a thing told to me and not as a thing I saw, which is the only honest way I know to write of a place a man has not been into.

The dead are walled into the mine facing the deep seam, and the surveyors add numbers to a slate nobody reads aloud. They do not fetch them back and do not say they have. The mountain keeps its own, which costs less than a kindness and lasts longer. There is a cup at the end of the Duelling Floor that nobody takes, set there for a man whose number was changed twice. I did not see the cup. I have written it where I heard it and not where I stood.

# The song said at the mouth

*Taken down from the old women who count, said once to the boy going down and once to the ground after he has gone. The second part is said with a line left out, and the space has stood empty in every telling I heard.*

> One for the arm and one for the stone,
> one for the pair that goes down together;
> the knife goes in and the warm goes home,
> and the dark keeps what the mother never entered.
>
> Hold the rail, it is iron and it takes;
> hold the stone, it is sold, not lent;
> count them going and count them back.
>
> Two go down and the count comes two;
> the figure stands though the arm may not;
> the mountain keeps what the mountain took,
> and the slate writes once what the mother forgot.

The fourth line of the second part is left out. I have set the space where it should stand and have not filled it from the other sayings, though I know what word would go there. A song that tells a boy what the count is for is a song he cannot carry down with him.

# The Sweating, and what they reckon a life at

In the deep seams the rock sweats and the Warm comes up through the stone in a flood, and the only warning is the smell of hot lead. I did not smell it. I have set that down first so that no reader of this book thinks I have been where I have not. A shift-man at the mouth told me he had smelt it once and come up, and that men who had smelt it twice did not come up, and then he asked whether I had a stone and went away.

Below the floor they work the stone with under-vessed slaves, condemned men and bought breath, and reckon a life at eleven months. Eleven is a year with the month of the buying taken off, which is a piece of arithmetic I have thought about more than I have thought about the shaft. A man who sells a life by the month will tell you the number of months and not the month that is missing, which is the same trick as the price of a warm-stone, and the same answer if you ask where the debt went.

The Keldish claim the true Hearth lies under their mountain and that the Court's fire is a candle lit from it. A surveyor come up from the shaft, with drink in him and no officer of the Keeping in the room, told me the rim-seam shows repairs laid one over another and that the oldest of them are not stone and not clay, and then he turned his slate face down and would not say what he had written. The Keldish call those oldest repairs Ash. He said the Court's copy has another word for them and that I would not be shown it, which I have set down as I heard it and not as a thing I know.

# What the borrowed hands did

A Court survey-clerk at Greybank borrowed these leaves on a market morning. He writes upright and narrow and takes the verbs out as though a verb were a thing a man could carry off in a cuff.

- Against *I gave my name and was sent back to the foot of the post* he wrote *the person was identified after the measure was declared*, and underlined it twice.
- Against *two go down and the count comes two* he wrote *two persons entered the shaft and two were recorded*, which is true in a counting room and says nothing about the coming up.
- Against *they shut the Cold Pass in seven hundred forty-one* he wrote *the frontier was closed by treaty in seven hundred forty-seven*, which is the agreement and not the shutting, and is the figure the Court likes because a treaty has a date a clerk can copy.
- Against *the mountain keeps its own* he wrote *interment underground; no return entered*, which leaves the mountain out and keeps the entry in.

The mine-reeve's red stroke stands at the second corner where he put it. It is a check and not a correction. He found the figure I gave stood, and I have kept his mark where it fell, which is more than I can say of any mark the factor's house has set on this book.

I have left his readings above mine everywhere, and the red where it fell. A clerk who takes the doer out of a sentence leaves the mountain standing by itself, and a mountain standing by itself has kept nobody, cost nobody, and has no count in it to argue with. I put the doer back because a potter who has carried nine bowls up a road wants to know whose hands they were in when they broke.

# At the head and in the gutter

The coal at the head is mine, off the sleeve I wipe the pots with; it goes into the grain and no wet cloth takes it out. The iron-red at the second corner is the mine-reeve's point, set where I gave two-and-forty and was found honest. The chip in the gutter is Ferr lead, off the bowl that went in the ford. The bead in the fold rolled out of a shift-man's pocket at the mouth and came from the Ninth Gallery. I have never joined the two, because a lead chip out of a river and a lead bead out of a gallery are two different debts, and a man who joins them has drawn a conclusion, which is a thing I have no licence to draw. The shaving held its heat one night on the stone floor of the Keep and is cold now, which is the order of things up there. The frost-feather lay on the well lip at the mouth and was gone before I folded the leaf; the yard at home calls that weather and the Keldish call it a map of the leak. The tallow print is my own lamp set too near the leaf, because the light goes early in that country. The square of bare paper is the word I scraped away with a knife-point and put nothing in its place. It is the only square in this account I made myself. The grey along the outer fold is not mine and not the clerk's. I have not swept it. There is no Last Day above this Keep, and I have not found one since I came home.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The cart went up with nine and came back with four;
> the stone went in warm and came home cold;
> the figure stood and the name went nowhere,
> and the count came two where the cup stood still.
>
> Ask the mouth what it counted.

I asked the beside-man at the Warden's hall, a woman who counts at the mouth, and a Vorr who sells numbers, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The leaves go back into the docket, into the pocket the strap has worn. Tomorrow I take the four bowls that came back whole down to Stonemouth and sell them for what they fetch, and when the weigh-clerk asks me what I carry I shall say four and not my name, which is a habit I have brought home from a country that does not care what a man calls himself.
