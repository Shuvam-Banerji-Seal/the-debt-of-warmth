I set the worth of vessels in this house. It stands in Slate Row at Sill, built in Y.H. 776, with the Court's room next door and the Waiting Room behind, and the slate on my counter was bought the year the house was raised and has outlasted three assessors whose names I cannot now read. A figure comes to me off the collector's slate and I set a worth against it and a return due; once a tend a man comes down from the College at the Hearthseat and strikes the verbs out of what I have written. I put them back. It is an arrangement of the kind that lasts because neither of us writes in the other's hand.

The brown thumb at the head of this leaf is iron-gall and mine, made by holding a quire open through a market morning. It will not wash out of the creases. There are clerks in the Hearthseat who can tell an assessor by his thumb before he has spoken a word, which is one of the small mercies of the trade.

What follows is one leaf of the valuation book, being the figures set in the last tend of Stone and carried forward into Ilmen. I have set the rates first, so that a man may see what an ember is said to be worth before he sees what I set against a life. I have not added the column. Adding is a College matter, and the last time a hand in this house added a column the total would not agree with the printed one, and the factor at Greybank sent word that the printed one was to be copied again in a better hand.

# The rates of the house

| Entry | As the house has it |
|---|---|
| Copper bit | twelve to a silver mark |
| Silver mark | twenty to a gold crown, a Sere coin that does not stay in Grath |
| Ember | five marks, by the Court's book; no man has exchanged one for the other |
| Newborn vessel | twelve to fifteen embers |
| Labourer in good case | forty to sixty embers |
| A Held vessel | a hundred and upward |
| One of the Kept | three hundred; no figure any household can spend |
| Deduction | one in twelve, as the notice says |
| Whole return | one measure poured into one vessel, booked at the figure |
| Thin return | many measures pooled and divided, figure entered by lot |

An ember is worth five marks in this room. In the yard a woman sells six eels for five marks or five for six and makes nothing of it, which is a fact about eels and not about embers. I have set both figures down and joined neither.

# The remarks

A remark is not part of the account. The College has never thought to strike the remark column out of this book, which I take for an oversight and not a mercy, and so I keep what I please in it. *Figure smaller than the last* is the only line on this leaf that I have had to write twice. The first time I set it down as a cause and then scraped the word away, which is the bare square in the lower margin; the second time I set it down as a likeness, which is all it is. I have seen three entries of one vessel and three figures going the same way, smaller each time, and the deduction standing at one in twelve between them like a stone that has not moved while the water went down. I do not say what it means. A clerk who explains a figure has begun to write a sermon, and sermons get copied.

*Asked after the weather. Pity entered.* There is no column for pity and no figure for a courteous vessel. I put it in the remark because a man who has sat across this counter for nine and thirty years should be allowed one line that is not a number. The College left it. I have not decided whether that was the oversight.

# What the College hand did

The man from the Hearthseat has a cuff gone soft from washing and ink settled in two fingers of it. He does not sit down. He borrowed the leaf on a market morning and did three things to it.

Where I wrote *the first entry stood higher*, he struck out *stood* and set *was higher* above, and then struck out *was* also, leaving *higher* with nothing to stand on. I have put *stood* back underneath and left his *higher* where he set it. A figure with a verb in it can be argued with, which is why he takes the verbs out; but a woman at my counter wants to know who did what, and a word with nothing to stand on will not tell her.

Against the vessel returned three times I had written, in the remark, *with memory*. He struck it and set *whole* above it. Both stand. *Whole* is the word the notice uses and the word the household hears read in a clean voice at the Weighing. *With memory* is the word I use when I am not reading anything aloud. I have not chosen between them, and I would thank any man who told me I had to.

He struck my last column also and wrote *No such column* above it. I have struck his striking out and left the column where I drew it.

# The column I drew and they struck out

I keep a place beside the figure the notice gives for the figure the household actually gave. I have set no heading over it. A heading would make it an account, and an account is a thing a man may be called to answer for, and this house has answered for enough this year.

The last time I was asked to set a worth against a house of Low Kiln, the man who came did not bring the figure in his mouth. He brought it in his thumb, the way they do down the yard, and wiped that thumb twice across his slate before he touched nothing and said the figure the notice says. I entered the figure the notice says, as I am required to, and in the place beside it I entered his two wipes and the pause between them, which is not a figure and cannot be added. When the College man struck the column he wrote *No such column* in the same upright hand he uses for the deduction. He had counted the rows before he wrote it, which is how I know the column troubled him. A man does not count what he has not looked at.

A place is not a debt. The College teaches eleven words for a deduction and has none for a place, and the clerk who came down from the Hearthseat gave himself away by putting a number in one.

# In the margins

The streak of slip in the gutter came off the forearms of the thrower whose figure stands second on the leaf. She held her slate away from her coat while she waited and set it against this quire for a moment, and the grey came off on the edge. The green bead in the lower corner is not slip. It is lead glaze from a girl who leaned over to see the writing with the sweetness still at her mouth, and touched the paper, and said nothing. I left it where she put it. The tallow thumb at the foot of the second leaf is the foundling's, or the mark of a woman holding him who used her whole hand to keep the leaf still in the draught under the door; I do not know which and have not asked. The Ash in the outer fold came off my own cuff going down to the Cross on Account-day, where I put my hand into a corner I had no business putting it into and the grey came away on the cloth. They sweep the corners of the houses on the Last Day and burn what they find in the morning and call it dealt with. There is no Last Day for a valuation book.

# At the foot

The line below was not written by me and is not in Ledgerhand. I found it under the counter when I came in on the morning after the last market, and I asked the factor's man, and the College man, and a woman who had stood at the counter about her house, and had three answers and no hand. I have left it where it was written.

> The book keeps the figure. The yard keeps the place where the figure stood.

The leaf goes back under the counter. Tomorrow the factor's man will come up from Greybank with a slate whose upper line is in a neat dark hand, with a small stop between two clusters that I have seen before and joined to nothing, and I shall set a worth against it and put the verbs back and leave the column where I drew it. A woman will stand where women stand and ask what her house is worth, and I shall read her the figure the notice says, which is the only figure she can carry away. The other one stays here. It has nowhere else to go.
