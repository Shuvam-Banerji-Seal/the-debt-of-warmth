I buy glass for a house of the Ten Bowls. In Sere we burn a debt-slate in the open court at the end of a feast and write a new one before the wine is cold, and call it generosity: a debt the whole court has seen die is a debt the whole court can agree is dead. In Vhal they write a debt on paper made to rot and leave it out in the damp, and call that a virtue, and no man has seen anything. I went up the Salt Road in Bone of the Thin Year with three gold crowns, a jar of myrrh and a commission, and came back in Ilmen with one sheet of green glass, a coil of rope that has outlasted two winters, and an offer I am glad I refused. Where I saw one thing and was told another I have set both down, the told above the seen, which is the only honest arrangement a buyer can make.

# The road, and the change in the smell

Eighty leagues by Greybank and the coast pass. I carried the crowns in a belt and the myrrh in a wooden box; the glass rode in straw on the return, which is the order of the world: a man goes up with what he cannot break and comes down with what he cannot keep. Past the pass the clay gives out and the road goes white and hard. The smell of wet wool goes out of it and the smell of salt comes in, and no clerk will enter that exchange, though it cost me a cold I carried three months.

At the last stage I asked a man walking down what the sea was like. He said, *Which tide?* I said, *The sea.* He said there was no such thing in Vhal, only the one going out and the one coming in, and that a man who called them the same had never owned a boat. I told him I had never owned one. He said nothing to that, which was the first kindness I met with there and cost him nothing.

The Sallow is the colour of milk gone yellow at the edge. It runs against the wind in both directions, which a man from the south cannot accept on the first day and accepts on the third because the boats do. In Sere a river goes where the ground falls, and a man who wants water uphill builds an account. Here the water follows the Warm, they say, and the moon is for the fish. I asked a dipper what the fish wanted with the moon, and he said the fish had not put the question to him.

# What they write on

Everything in Vhal is written on salt-paper. A Council clerk told me this with a straight face and a cuff soft from washing. In the damp the writing comes up in pale islands and the debt is made nothing, and the Council calls it a virtue, because a law that lasts too long has stopped earning. I have kept the leaf where that happened to a contract of mine; there is no longer a figure on it for a clerk to enter.

In Sere we burn the proof before everybody and clap. Here they let it perish in a drawer and nobody claps. The two arrive at the same place by opposite roads, and I have set them side by side without choosing, because a buyer who chooses between two ways of losing a debt will soon be asked to choose between two ways of losing a person.

A man at the counter held my crown up to the light and would not take it up until I had said what I wanted. In Sere a man names his price first and his want afterwards; here the want comes first, and on that morning I felt myself a man whose account had not yet been opened.

# The table, with what stands above it

*Figures as they were given at the Quay, set above what I entered on the warrant. The hand above the last two rows is the factor's clerk at Greybank, who has never been to the coast.*

| Article | Carried out of Sere | Asked at Vhal | What came back | What the clerk entered |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gold crowns | three, twenty marks to the crown | three | none; two went thin at the edge | *currency, current* |
| Myrrh, a jar | 2 crowns at the Bowl | not taken | the jar, half spent | *not entered* |
| Cliff glass, green sheet | commission from the house | 4 crowns | one sheet, tested at the rim | *glass, as entered* |
| Tarred rope, a coil | — | 1 crown | one coil, two winters in the wet and sound | *rope, current rate* |
| Sallow salt, a sack | — | given, not sold | the sack, gone damp on the pass | *not a vessel* |
| A thin return | offered to buy | 10 marks, no questions | refused | *no acquisition recorded* |

> *In the narrow upright hand, above the rope row:* The figure is current. The article is preserved against exposure by the tar. Warming retained in the fibre.
>
> *Underneath, in mine:* I carried the coil and it kept. Somebody paid for that keeping, and the price is not in the fibre and not on the slate. A figure that tells what a thing did and leaves out who it was done to is the sort my house would applaud.

The factor's clerk wrote *currency, current* above the crowns, and it is true in his office. Two of the three came home thin at the edge, not by exchange but by four thumbs in four hands. That is not a rate. It is four thumbs. The two columns have stood here a year without touching.

# What they would not sell

I asked for a thin return, as my house instructed me: a third of what the Court charges, and the seller asks no questions about the source. I said the words to a woman on the Quay, and she looked at me the way a woman looks at a man who has asked the price before asking what the thing is.

She said Vhal will not pay for a thin return and will not house one. I said that in Sere a thin northerner is shown at feasts for small coins and a beautiful house-slave given a whole return as proof of generosity. She said that was the south, and went on mending her net. I asked whether it was kindness. She said it was a contract with the last line left out, the only answer I got in nine days.

In Sere a slave has no measure in law and his warmth is his owner's holding. Here condemned men row the pulled benches seven years or until their hands give out, and a bought person sold for a term cannot be returned dead to the Council, which will not pay for him and will not keep him. A man is a bowl in one country and a current account in the other, and in neither does anybody ask what he called himself before he was entered.

The woman offered me a phial of the ash they gather in the corner and bottle and sell, for a man who wants his dreams to stop arriving. I have carried it eighty leagues and not opened it. My house buys quiet ash at sixty marks for men who can afford to forget; here it stands in a jar and sells for two bits. I have not entered it, for I could find no figure to make it honest.

# The boats, and the shares

Boat-shares pass through mothers in Vhal. A man who marries into a boat-owning family is said to be come aboard, and his opinion on the boat is counted at half. I asked a salt-wife whether half an opinion kept a man from being wrong. She said it kept him from being believed, which she said was a better thing. There is no word in Vhal for a guest or a friend, only for a man whose account is current. I was current for nine days, and felt the change on the tenth in the way a cup was set down.

The dead are sealed in a salted boat and pushed out on the ebb, and the boat is watched until it is out of sight, which is held to be enough. A man on the Quay said that whoever watches till the boat is gone has done all anybody can ask, and that there is no court on the Sallow to enter either. In Sere we burn a slate for the household and keep the ash. Here they give the whole boat to the water and keep nothing, not even a name to figure against.

# The song of the salted boat

*Taken down on the Quay from men caulking a new hull. They sang the first two parts while the tar went on and would not sing the third while an unfinished boat lay on the stocks. The last line of each part is said to the boat, not to the man working on it.*

> Four for the hull and one for the seam,
> the tar goes on where the salt has been;
> the tide comes in where the wind blows out
> and the warm goes seaward all the same.
>
> They seal a man in a boat of salt
> and set him out upon the ebb;
> the boy who watches is told to watch
> till the boat is gone and the watching's done.
>
> We do not say whose warmth it was,
> we do not ask what the boat will keep;
> the paper rots and the rope holds,
> and the sea keeps no court and burns no slate.

I asked why the third part was not sung before a boat was finished. The man with the tar said a song about letting go, said over a thing not yet let go, might be taken as a wish. Here a thing is said to the object and not to the person, because said to a person it becomes a bargain.

# The cliffs

The Glaze Cliffs stand a few leagues along the shore, blue-green with old lead, and smell faintly sweet in the heat. Children are warned not to lick them. My house buys glass from those furnaces, and I know the sweetness at the rim of a well-glazed cup and the pale line it leaves at the gum, and what that line comes to at the end. I put my hand on the cliff and took it away and did not lick it and did not say why. The sheet I brought home is green at the lip and has the note a good sheet has. I have never once drunk from it.

# What the borrowed hands did

Two hands have been at these leaves besides mine.

- The Council clerk at Vhal, to whom I showed the contract that had gone soft, wrote *instrument void by exposure; no claim lies*, in pale ink on the paper itself. He is right in the language of the pans. The debt is gone and there is nobody to ask, and his line has perished along with the debt it described.
- The factor's clerk at Greybank, upright and narrow, set *figures of account, not of price* above my table and *no acquisition recorded* above my refusal. He has never been south and has strong views about salt. He has taken the verbs out: where I wrote *the rope kept* he wrote *warming retained*, and where I wrote *they would not sell me a person* he wrote *no acquisition recorded*, which leaves the person and the refusal both out.

I have put my words back underneath. A verb is the difference between a thing that happened to somebody and a thing that happened in a counting room. My house would approve of his sentences and not understand mine, which is one more thing the two countries share without knowing it.

# At the head and in the gutter

The white in the gutter is salt bloom off their paper and comes and goes with the weather, and I have stopped brushing it off. The gold in the second fold is a flake off a crown rubbed thin by counting; it is the only gold in this book nobody has entered, and it will not change on any Quay. The green ground into the head is Cliff glass, off the sheet I tested at the rim. The tar at the thumb is mine, from the coil. The myrrh in the fourth leaf came off the jar I did not sell; a woman at a counter touched it and then her own wrist before she would name a price, and I have not written that down as a wonder. The soft square is where a contract of mine perished and the writing came up in pale islands; a man can hold a debt up to the light here and see that it has gone. The bare square is a word I scraped away with a knife and have not written back. The grey along the outer fold is not mine and not the clerk's. I have not swept it. There is no Last Day in Vhal and nothing anywhere on that coast gets burnt in the morning.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> I carried gold that the damp could not touch
> and bought a sheet that the damp could not take;
> they would not sell me a person
> and the boat went out on the ebb.
>
> Ask the rope what it kept. Ask the paper what it forgot.

I asked the woman on the Quay, the Council clerk and a factor come down from Greybank, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written. The leaves go back into the waxed tin, where the salt has eaten two of the stitches. Tomorrow I tell the house I bought glass and could not buy the other thing, and they will say measure for measure and mean whatever they have decided, and by evening a slate will be burnt and a new one written before the wine is cold. I have carried two countries' ways of losing a debt in one coat and never made them agree, which is the only account in this book that balances.
