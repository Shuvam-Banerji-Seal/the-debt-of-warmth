I keep the keys above ground. My mother kept them before me and her mother before her, and none of us has been down the shaft. The mine-reeve checks a man who has lied about his number and the checking is done in red; I hand him the slate and do not watch. What is below is what men say waiting at the weigh and what the old women say counting at the mouth. A man come up from Greybank asked me what we say when there is nothing to say. I told him we say it to the stone. He went away believing he had learned the mountain.

Keldish is cut in bone and stone, not written. A notch stays when paper has gone and cannot be read by a man from the factor's house, which is a virtue we did not think of first. I have set the sayings in Grathic letters and struck the endings off them. Say them to the stone, to the iron, to the lamp, never to the man beside you: said looking at a man, a saying becomes a bargain. Where a word would not come I have cut the edge with my tally-knife and left the cut standing. A notch is not a letter. It keeps better.

# Of the number before the name

1. “State what you hold. Then say who you are.”

*Gloss — At the Warden's hall and at this gate both. A lowlander gives his name and waits to be asked the rest, and is sent back to the foot of the post to begin again. I am not shaming him. A name is a thing a man can change at the next well; a measure is what he carries into the dark, and the dark does not care what he calls himself. There is an order to a man and the order is not the one he was taught at home.*

2. “The number is part of the name. The name is not part of the number.”

*Gloss — A man's measure is cut into his forearm and counts as part of who he is; ask a Keldishman who he is and he will often give the number first. After the Descent some have the old numbers cut out and new ones put in. I have seen a man with two sets in one arm, the later cut deeper. The mine-reeve entered the later and left the earlier standing in the skin, which is how an honest clerk keeps two truths without calling either a lie.*

3. “The number stands. Do not say it twice.”

*Gloss — What men say when a pair goes down and one comes up. It is not a denial and it is not a hope. To say a number twice is to ask it to be other than it was, and the mountain keeps no second figure. At the Duelling Floor a cup stands at the end that nobody takes, for the man whose number was changed twice.*

# Of iron, and the two bites

4. “Cold iron takes. It does not give back.”

*Gloss — The collector's tube, the clamp, the blade laid across a fever: one iron and one hunger. A salt-wife at the Quay told me it draws the heat out of a wound and takes the measure with it. We say it of a man who has taken something and calls it borrowing. He hears the insult, which is what the saying is for.*

5. “One bite holds the vessel whole. One bite takes the hand off a man. It is the same bite.”

*Gloss — The clamp. There is no word in Grathic that holds both mercies and none in Keldish either, which is why the saying has two halves and a silence between them. A lowlander asked which of the two the Order meant. I told him the Order means the iron, and the iron has not asked anybody's opinion.*

6. “Put hot iron to the palm and ask a man if he owes. The honest answer leaves the hand dead.”

*Gloss — Of the Duelling Floor. I have known honest men come back from that question unable to lift a cup, and not one lied. It is said so that a man going up knows what he has paid before he is told he has been trusted.*

7. “Open a way in the arm. Keep it while it scars.”

*Gloss — The first way: a surface way cut across and held open until it heals that way. It aches before the thaw and after, and a man answers to whichever number the room has given him. An old man of the floor told me the way is a door and the arm the wall it is cut in. I have put that where he said it.*

# Of the stone, and what it keeps

8. “The mountain keeps its own.”

*Gloss — The dead are walled into the mine facing the deep seam and the surveyors add numbers to a slate nobody reads aloud. We do not fetch them back and do not say we have. A lowland priest calls this a kindness done by a mountain. A mountain is not kind; it is retentive, which costs less.*

9. “Warm-stone is sold. It is not lent.”

*Gloss — It holds heat longer than anything else in Sarn. A man goes up without a stone and borrows from the next man who has one, or turns back; there is no lending here. The shaving swept from the cutting floor is sold by the half-mark and not given away. A thing that can be lent has a place in somebody's account. A thing sold is paid for, which is cleaner.*

10. “The rock sweats and the lead smells hot. That is the whole of the warning.”

*Gloss — In the Ninth Gallery the smell of hot lead gives what warning it gives, and no man has asked for a better one. It is said quietly. A man who has smelt it does not say it again, and a man who has not will never believe it matters.*

11. “Frost-feather grows where the warm has lately gone out of the stone.”

*Gloss — Lowlanders call it frost-bloom and the yard at Sill calls the whole business weather. Here it is a map of the leak, read at the mouth before the sun takes it. A feather on the well lip means warmth went out of that stone in the night, and somebody is sent to find where it went. Gone by noon, and the finding still owed.*

# Of the Rim, the pass and the Long Want

12. “There is a post and no gate.”

*Gloss — Karric VII shut the Cold Pass in seven hundred forty-one and the agreement of seven hundred forty-seven fixed the border. There is a post there and nothing else. The Warden's surveyors keep the record of the rim-seam and do not promise to bring a man back who goes past it. A clerk at Greybank put a nought in the place where the gate should stand. A nought is a number. A post is a place, and the two are not the same.*

13. “The Long Want had nowhere to put what the Cold took.”

*Gloss — Before the Hearth the temple calls those years the Broken Years and we call them the Long Want. The temple says the world was cracked once. The seam shows layers, and a survey man will say only that the repairs lie one over another like a pot mended until the first pot cannot be seen. He will not say it in front of an officer. I have set his words where he said them and have not joined them to anything.*

# Of the Vorr and the stones

14. “We were never owed.”

*Gloss — A Vorr death has no slate, no deduction and no return. The others seat the body upright facing the direction it came from and leave it. It is the only sentence I know in Sarn with no account in it, and the Court has never found a way to enter it.*

15. “The numbers are correct.”

*Gloss — What a Vorr says after sitting against a Listening Stone and coming down with a figure. Ask what the figure means and he will say it twice. He means it is an answer to the numbers and not to your question, which he will tell you if he is in a patient mood. They sell nothing but numbers and have never translated a slate in full.*

16. “A Vorr keeps the count in the bone, not the blood.”

*Gloss — It makes them difficult to collect and impossible to bank in the ordinary way; a tube finds nothing where they keep it. This is spoken of as a difficulty, never as a mercy. A thing the Court cannot take is a thing it will not call a gift.*

17. “Forty years is a short sitting.”

*Gloss — The Long Sitting is at forty: a Vorr sits still against a stone for a season and comes away with the numbers changed. They take their names one at a time many years apart, and divide work by whether a person has sat long enough to hear the stone, which is not permitted to the impatient. In seven hundred fifty-eight they stopped selling warm-stone to the Court and gave no reason. The Court entered it as a cause with no effect, which is the kind of entry it can bear.*

# What the boys say going down

*Said at the mouth on the morning of the Descent, going down and not coming up. The mother does not say it. The old women at the count say the last line with him, and one of them puts a warm-stone into his hand after the knife.*

> State what you hold, then the name you bring;
> the number goes on the arm, the stone in the palm;
> down one day and up the next, and the dark between;
> the dark tells the mother, the arm tells the rest.
>
> One for the shaft and one for the seam,
> one for the pair that goes down with him;
> two go down and the count comes two,
> and the figure stands where the figure stood.
>
> Hold the stone. It is sold, not lent.
> Hold the rail. It is iron, and it takes.
> Come up with the number you went down with,
> or come up with the number the mountain gave.

*A gloss, because a boy of thirteen will ask. The third line of the second part is not a promise that two come up. It is a promise that the count will be made. No old woman at this mouth will explain the difference to a boy before he goes down, and none will say it after.*

# What the Court hand did

A survey-clerk of the Court borrowed the leaf at Greybank on a market morning. He writes in the narrow upright hand and takes the verbs out as though they were things a man could smuggle past a weigh.

- At *state what you hold, then say who you are*: *the measure is declared and the person is identified*, underlined twice.
- At *the number stands*: *the vessel was re-entered*, true in a language nobody speaks at this mouth.
- At *there is a post and no gate*: *the frontier is unguarded*. There is no gate to guard and never was, but a sentence with a guard in it can be entered.
- At *we were never owed*: *no account arises*. The nearest he has come to saying what the Vorr said, and I have not helped him.
- At the cup nobody takes he put a round nought and wrote *vacancy* beneath it. A nought is a number. The cup is a place.

I have put the sayings back underneath and left his above. He takes the verbs out because a thing always true has no need of them. I put them back because a boy at thirteen with a knife at his arm does not know he is hearing something always true, and wants to know which hand to hold the stone with.

# In the margin, in a carter's sloping hand

> Paid for the bar here and charged two again at Greybank, and the factor's man said the second was for the road. I have stopped asking which of the two was the true figure.

Below it, in mine: *Both stand. He paid for the iron and for having carried it, and the road has no slate of its own. The factor's man has joined them into one figure, which is the whole art of that house. I did not collect the second and cannot cross it out.*

# At the head and in the gutter

The smut at the head is coal off the sleeve I wipe the keys with; it goes into the grain and no wet sleeve takes it out. The iron-red at the second corner came off the mine-reeve's point while he checked a man who had given a figure that would not hold; he did not look at me and I did not look at the man. The lead bead in the fold rolled out of the Ninth Gallery; a shift-man laid it on the leaf and went away without it. The shaving kept its heat through the night and is cold now, which is the order of things here. The feather pressed flat was on the well lip after a warm night and was gone by the time I found the leaf to put it in. The tallow print is my own lamp set too near the tally. The grey in the gutter came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying in a room where the keys are kept; I have not swept it and there is no Last Day above ground.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> We say the number before the name because the name goes home and the number goes down.

I asked a shift-man waiting at the weigh, a surveyor come down from the stones, and an old woman who has counted here thirty years, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The leaf goes back into the key-pouch and the keys on the nail. Tomorrow a boy will state what he holds before he says who he is, the mine-reeve will check the figure in red, and the number will go on being called after the arm that carried it has come up or has not.
