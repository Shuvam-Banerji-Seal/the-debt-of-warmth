I keep the stone-shelter at the Listening Stones and count out the warm-stone to sitters, and nobody asked me for this. I write because Om spoke to a stone and the stone gave a number, and he would not say what the number counted. He spoke to the stone, to the snow in its joint, to the number itself, and last of all to us, which is the order the Vorr use when they have been asked a thing they do not mean to keep. I have set his words down in Grathic, which is not his tongue, and where he left a verb out I have put it back underneath in my own hand, because a saying said over a stone is a thing a person has to carry down the mountain and use.

The pale at the head is lead dust off the survey-sheet; no brushing takes it out of the grain. The grit is stone ground small by forty winters of palms against the Fourth, and scratches a nail. The chip in the fold is warm-stone from the ration, still faintly warm though it has lain here since the Hearth Drawing. The grey came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business on a counted stone; I have not swept it. The ring is the tin of snow, set down at the first voice and taken up before he saw it.

# The manner of the sitting, as the Stones actually have it

*Written small, from watching, not from any order the Keep has printed.*

- Come up in the dark before the wind. A man who comes at noon sees his own shadow on the stone and calls it the stone's answer.
- Keep the warm-stone in the pocket against the thigh, not in the hand. The hand must be the cold thing. A warm hand reads its own heat and calls it the number.
- Lay the flat of the palm to the stone. Do not knock. A pot answers the knuckle; a stone answers the sitting.
- Sit until the cold is ordinary. The first hour belongs to the stone, the second to you, and after that to neither.
- Ask one question. The stone will give a number. Do not ask what it counts or who paid for it; the stone allows one question.
- If the number is correct, say *The numbers are correct* and stop. That sentence is true and not a translation. Anything more is a man hearing himself.
- Do not cut your measure into the stone. The arm is for that. The stones are older than the arm and need no telling.
- If the stone sweats and the smell of hot lead comes up, go down without counting the hour. The mountain gives warning once.
- When a man with a slate comes among the sitters, stop, and begin again after. His figure is for a room below. Do not lay the two together.

He said the last twice, once to the stone and once to a man in a Keldish coat who was writing nothing I could see.

# The sermon, as he said it

## Of the number

He had his left palm flat on the Fourth Stone, and the snow went on standing in the joint beside his fingers as though the hand had told it to.

“I sat from my fortieth year,” he said. “I have come away with a number.”

A man from the duelling floor asked what the number was. This is what every visitor asks, and the Vorr have one answer for it.

“The numbers are correct,” Om said.

“Correct of what?”

“Of the counting,” he said. “That is the whole sentence. If I add a name to it, I have made the number a debt, and there was no debt at the stone.”

He drew the hand back and looked at the palm as a man looks at a tool set down in the wrong place. There was no mark on it. The Vorr hold their measure in the bone, and there is nothing on the skin to read.

> *The people answered, and the Keldish among them said it with the flat short vowels of the Keep:*
>
> The stone keeps the number; it does not say of whom.

> *Margin, in my hand:* He used the Vorr word for *correct* that also means *unmoved*, as a stone is unmoved when the wind goes over it. I have set *correct* because that is the word the Keep uses in its answer. He would not have the other word written.

## Of the arm

A young duellist of the Tenth Clamp stood with his sleeve up, showing the numbers cut into his forearm at thirteen. A scar crossed them where an old figure had been taken out and a new one put in after a thin return.

“There is the honest way to keep a count,” Om said, and nodded at the arm. “A knife put it there. A knife can take it out. A man can see his own number and know which of it is his and which was pressed in by somebody else's account.”

He put his palm back on the stone.

“This one cannot be cut out. It does not lie under the skin. It lies where the sitting put it, and the sitting was mine.”

The duellist said that a number a man could not show was no use in a duel, and several of the younger ones laughed, though not loudly.

“No use in a duel,” Om said. “That is true. I have never fought one. You would win and I would sit, and we should both have what we are good at.”

> *The people answered:*
>
> The stone keeps the number; it does not say of whom.

## Of the oldest repair

A surveyor of the seam stood at the edge with his slate turned face down against his forearm, which is how surveyors carry a thing they will not have read over in the wind.

“He will tell you,” Om said, without looking at him, “that the rim-seam shows repair laid over repair, that the oldest are not stone and not clay, and that in the record they look like language. Then he will stop and turn his slate face down, because the man who wrote the entry before his was taken out of the shaft.”

The surveyor said nothing. That is the answer the Keep gives when a thing is true and not to be carried further.

“You cannot translate a repair,” Om said. “You can only lay another over it. A mended thing keeps the break under the patch. Ask the pot, not the potter. The pot knows where it was opened.”

A woman beside me asked what the oldest repairs were made of, which is what the surveyor had been asked and had not answered.

“A question with a use,” he said. “I have sat at four stones and none has given me a word for it. They have given me numbers. A number is the shape of a thing with the thing taken away. If you want the thing, sit longer than I did, and be colder.”

> *The people answered:*
>
> The stone keeps the number; it does not say of whom.

## Of never owed

He turned then from the stone to the company, which he had not done until that hour, and spoke in the short sentences they use at the Stones.

“The Court says a vessel is emptied and pressed into another and the account stands. It says this to the dying so they may go away quiet. It is a sentence with a slate at the end of it.”

He held up the palm that had been on the stone.

“We are not collected. Our measure is in the bone and the tube has nowhere to take hold. When a Vorr dies the others seat him upright facing the way he came and leave him. We say: we were never owed. Say it to a collector and he goes away, because there is no account to draw and no infant to press it into.”

A man come up from the low country asked whether that was a mercy or a refusal. There is no Grathic word that holds the two together, and Om would not choose one.

“It is what we say,” he said. “I have never called it mercy. Mercy is a word with a giver in it.”

He put the palm back for the last time. The snow in the joint had not moved while he spoke.

> *The people answered, and the Vorr among them said it once and did not say it twice:*
>
> The stone keeps the number; it does not say of whom.

# The sitting-song

*Sung low, seated, with the palm on the stone. The Keldish take the second and fourth lines; the Vorr take the last two. The number is not spoken at any sitting, and I have left the space the length of its not being spoken.*

> Sat from forty and the snow in the joint,
> the stone gave a number and keeps its own;
> the arm takes the mark and the knife takes it out,
> and the slate puts back what the knife took out.
>
> Three for the sitting and one for the cold,
> one for the hand that would not be told;
> the surveyor turns his slate to the ground
> and the oldest repair is not clay, not stone.
>
> *Here the number is not spoken. The sitter makes no sound.*
>
> The stone keeps the number; it does not say of whom;
> we were never owed, and the snow fills the print;
> the count is correct and the count is not ours,
> and the hand goes down with the number in it.

A girl from the shelter asked what the space was for. Nobody answered her. The clerk put a round nought against it on the third day, which I have left where he set it.

# The stone-tally, as the shelter keeps it

*A warm-stone goes out with a sitter and comes back with him, or does not. These sittings I set down where they stood. The numbers are not entered, because no number has ever been given out at these stones.*

| Stone | Who sat | What was asked of the stone | What the stone gave |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Fourth, from his fortieth year | Om of the Long Sitting | nothing; he sat and came down | a number, kept, not spoken |
| The Fourth, one winter | a duellist of the Tenth Clamp | whether his arm would hold through the season | the figure at thirteen, still standing |
| The Second | a surveyor come up from the First Seam | what the oldest repair was made of | *the numbers are correct*, which is not a translation |
| The First | a woman whose son went down to the bank | whether he had been entered | a figure; she carried it down and said nothing of it |
| The Third | the mine-reeve's clerk, taking the winter count | nothing entered | nil, written above the four |
| The place under the Fifth | nobody | nothing | no stone, and no sitting |

There were sittings before these, and the old keeper could set a winter to most of them; I have not written the winters, because a row of years is a thing a clerk can carry away and a stone is not. The place under the Fifth has no stone, and I have left it in the tally so the row may be read the way it stands.

# What the clerk did

He came up from Keld Keep on the third day with ink in two fingers, borrowed the leaf while I was at the ration-tin, and gave it back with the verbs taken out.

- At *the stone gave a number*: *a figure was returned to the sitter*, above and underlined.
- At *the numbers are correct*: *sentence affirmed; referent not stated*.
- At *we were never owed*: *declaration of non-liability; not recognised*, with a small stop between two clusters of his neat dark hand.
- At the number kept and not spoken: *no figure produced; enter as nil*.
- At the gap in the song a round nought and *vacancy*, as though a space where a number is not given were an empty column waiting to be filled.

I have put the verbs back underneath and left his hand standing above, as the stone keeps the sitting under the number. He takes them out because a thing always true has no need of anyone to have done it. I put them back because a woman with her palm on a cold stone wants to know who sat.

# At the head and in the fold

The lead at the head grates between the teeth if a person is foolish enough to test it; the glass-men do that once and do not do it again. The grit is stone dust ground by every palm that has sat here, and I know a sitter by it. The chip in the fold has not lost all its heat, which is what warm-stone does. The tallow is mine; I held the leaf down in the wind and did not know I had marked it. The iron in the gutter is filings off the wire at the tie. The grey came off a cuff and I have not swept it. The wet ring is the tin of snow, set down while he spoke of the arm. The lichen lay in the crease when I took the leaf from the spoiled sheet, and I have left it. The clerk noticed none of these things, which is how I know he is a good clerk.

# At the foot

Struck through once and written again underneath, very small, in a hand that is not mine, not Om's, not the surveyor's and not the clerk's:

> A correct number is not a true sentence.
>
> The stone keeps both, and will not say which is the mercy.

I asked Om, the surveyor, the duellist and the clerk, and had four answerings and no hand. I have left the line struck through and standing, the only honest way to keep a sentence you cannot sign.

The leaf goes back in the tin with the warm-stone chip in its fold and the wire tied as it was. Next Stone the sitters will come up the five leagues from Keld Keep with snow in the joints, and a Vorr will lay a palm to the Fourth and come away with a number he will not spend. If the clerk comes I shall let him take the verbs out and put them back, and leave the nought in its place and the number where it was not spoken.
