I write on the backs of birth-slates spoiled by a late entry, which is paper enough for a book that nobody has asked to see. I am a hearth-maid of this district. I have set down what I do and what the women before me did, and where the two disagree I have left both standing. A remedy is a thing a body has already paid for. A woman who strikes a line out of a book of physic is answerable for the next body that takes it.

My hand is not a clerk's. I put verbs where a clerk would take them out, and I have not corrected this, because a direction without a verb is a thing a man can read aloud at a table and a woman cannot use at a birth. The tallow print at the top came from a mother who held the sheet down in a draught and did not know she marked it. The slip thumb is mine. I signed in slip because I have no slate-name, and a thumb in slip is how this yard signs a pot.

# The order of a birth room

*Written small at the head in charcoal, with one word gone over in tallow.*

- Warm the stone first. A common river stone out of the neighbour's fire, in a fold of wool, tested on your own wrist before it goes to a belly. It is not a warm-stone and I will not put that word on it; the Keldish stone is another thing and no house here owns one. A cold stone is a stone, not a kindness.
- Bank the fire but do not cover it. A birth needs somewhere for the heat to go.
- Set the slate face down on the bench before you go to the child. The hand that writes is not the hand that holds. If there is nobody else to write, write after, and enter within three days as the ordinance says. Do not ask why the third day is a bad day to choose. It is a bad day to choose and that is the whole of it.
- Wash in this order: face, neck, groin, knees, cord. Do not hurry the cord. It knows its own hour better than you do.
- At the red flower, cross two fingers at the corner of it and say nothing. If someone asks, give a date. Dates are safe. The Court likes a date and does not ask what it covers.
- Leave the grey in the corner. Sweeping it into a pail does not make it gone, and the Court has a word for gone that I have never seen happen.
- When the bell goes at the Cross, stop and start again after. It is counting something that is not your room.

I have done four hundred births and I still lay my hand on the slate first. I have tried to stop doing it. It is a trick the room plays on a maid, and after the first hundred you stop calling it a habit and start calling it the order of things.

# The second chill

The third day is a bad day. A child that has taken a chill will grow worse on it, and if the entry was made on that day the worsening goes down as a birth and stands as a birth for the rest of the child's life. This is entered nowhere in the ordinance and the ordinance has no place for it. We call it the second chill. The physician at Greybank calls it a settling of the graft, which is a kinder phrase and costs less.

In Y.H. 651 a physician at Greybank struck a word out of his own report and paid the hearth-maid who had written it for her trouble. The word has not come down to us. I have asked three older women and had three dates and no word. I have left the place where the word would stand, which is the right length for a word of that kind.

> *The narrow hand of the Court clerk wrote above this paragraph:* The worsening is a settling of the graft. No chill is entered. Entered as a birth, not a worsening.
>
> *Under it, in mine:* Worse before dusk. Warm and breathing was all I set down. Both stand.

# Remedies, as they are used

| Trouble | What is set to it | The manner of it | Who pays |
|---|---|---|---|
| A newborn gone cold | A river stone warmed at a neighbour's fire, in wool | To the belly, never the feet. Change it when it goes cold in your own hand, not when the child stops crying. | The neighbour's fire. Repay with a crust, and do not call the crust a gift. |
| The second chill | Warmth, quiet, and a slate turned face down | Skin to skin under the shawl, barley water by the spoon, watch kept till the fourth day. Do not send for the collector because the child has got worse; that is what the day does. | The household, one night's sleep and a loaf. |
| Cough off the pits | Barley water steamed under a cloth | Steam to the mouth, not the face. A hand over the mouth when it comes up, so the wall does not get it. | The pot and the fuel under it. |
| A burn from the kiln | Tallow first, then a clean fold | Cool it with tallow and keep the air from it. The old women lay wet slip on a burn: the slip cools while it is wet and cooks when it dries. I have left their line standing under mine. | Whoever opened the kiln, if the house can bear to ask. |
| A wound the salt will hold | Salt from the herring barrel on a feather | A brine-gull's feather is better if you live near the coast and can bear to take it. In Low Kiln we use what the barrel gives. It is a brute method and it often works. | The barrel. |
| A bite from a bone-hound | Nothing I know | Keep him from the orchard. Salt does not hold that bite and neither does tallow. A dog that has eaten the little graves puts the grey into a wound and the wound will not close. | Nobody. This is the honest column. |
| The sweet sickness | A cup set down and not taken up | There is no physic for it. The glaze is sweet and the sweetness is the sickness. Stop the cup, if the house can afford to stop the cup. Most cannot. | The glaze room, which cannot pay and does not ask. |
| Kiln's breath in a closed room | An open window | It spoils the glaze. The potters leave the window shut and call a death in sleep a merciful one, which is a phrase for people who have not had to identify the body. | The man who sleeps. |
| A child emptied by leeches | Nothing. Do not set them. | A silt-leach out of the shallows draws as the collector draws and without a slate. A child can be emptied by a handful in an hour. If you find them on a child, take them off and count them, and do not put them back. | Whoever put them there. |
| Milk that will not come | A warm stone and another woman to hold the child | Let the mother sleep while the child is held. The other woman's own child goes a little colder for it. Say so. A kindness that has no payer is a kindness somebody else has paid for already. | The other woman's child. |

A physician from the College wrote in Y.H. 641 that the sweetness came from the lead in the glaze, and the copy that came back to us had the word taken out. I have seen the pale line at the gum. I have seen the hand that cannot hold a cup. I have not seen the lead, and I do not set down what I have not seen; but I have set down that a man wrote it and that it was taken away, because a thing taken away twice is a thing worth leaving a place for.

# The full note

Tap a sound pot with the knuckle and it answers full. Tap a cracked one and it answers flat, a small dead sound that stops at the surface and will not go through. This is a thing the kiln-masters keep and the rest of us borrow when a pot is set down on a bench.

Once, in a birth room, a cup across the room gave the flat note while nobody touched it. I did not say what it was. There was a woman in labour and a slate on the bench and no advantage in adding a third thing to the room. I have written it here because a thing heard in a birth room and never mentioned is still a thing heard.

Do not knock a person to try it. A body is not a pot and the note of it is not ours to take. I knew a woman who tapped the chest of a child gone thin and said she had heard nothing back, and she said it as though the child had refused her. The child had not refused her. There was simply nothing there for her to hear.

A woman of the drying racks told me of a pot set down that answered nearly full --- a note between the two, almost the full one and never quite. I have not heard it and I have written it down because she was not a woman who made things up, and because a note that is almost full may be a thing a household could live with, if it came to that.

# The song for the third night

*Sung low, to the child, while the watch is kept. The room says the last line with her.*

> The stone goes cold before the milk,
> the milk goes cold before the bread;
> a body is a pot with a flaw
> and the flaw is where the heat has fled.
>
> Warm the flaw and warm the pot,
> warm the place where the heat is not;
> four for the corners, four for the wall,
> and one that does not answer when you call.
>
> Three days, three nights, a watch kept true;
> what comes back is not what went;
> sing to the child, sing to the cold,
> and come back warm. Come back as you can.

There is a space before the last line in the singing. I have left it on the page. It is the same space the kiln keeps and the bell keeps, and I have never filled it and never will.

# What the narrow hand did

A clerk borrowed the leaf on an Account-day and struck out my verbs. Where I wrote *the heat went out of him*, he set *the measure was drawn* above and left mine under it, saying that a book of physic is not a slate and need not be true in the same way. He wrote *deduction* against the warmed stone and *one in twelve, as the notice says* against the night's watch. He put a round nought in the space before the last line of the song and wrote *vacancy* beside it. I have put the words back under his and left his standing. He has a figure for everything and a place for nothing.

There is a line at the foot in a hand I do not know, struck through and written again underneath: *Nothing is lost that we have kept.* It is not a remedy. It has no business in a book of physic. It is in the book now, and when I copy the leaf in Ilmen I shall copy it again, in that hand and not my own, as it stands.

# At the foot

The grey along the gutter came off my own cuff when I measured the ridge in a room two doors down and came back to write. The barley grease is from a crust laid on the leaf while a child was being told to eat and would not. The green bead is not slip. A girl with sweetness still at her mouth leaned over to see the writing and touched the paper and said nothing and went back to her work. I have not washed any of it.

The book goes back behind the meal bin. A slate is a thing that gets carried into the square and read in a clean voice; this is a thing that gets carried under an apron and taken out when the room is quiet. If an officer ever finds it, let him strike out what he will. The next woman will put the verbs back, as I did, and warm the stone before she reads.
