I keep the second file. It holds the names taken off the rolls, and I copy them in the same hand as the names that stayed, because a hand that changes between one name and the next is a hand that can be accused of feeling something. The College takes a boy with a name and teaches him to write a life with nobody in it. These are the rules by which that is done. I have set them down as they stand, and where a hand has corrected me I have left both readings.

The assessors are a guild of a sort, though the word is a clay-country one and is not used in Slate Row. These articles are not new. They were gathered in Y.H. 776, when it was found that a method could be kept longer than any man who used it. A rule with no thumb on it is a rule nobody has had to use. These have been used.

# The articles, as they stand in Slate Row

## Of the taking

1. The College takes an apprentice, not a house. A house may bring a child, or a collector, or the Temple's poor may be sent, but from the day of entering the child belongs to the College and to no house. He is given no kin. Graduates are given none either, which is the same rule said at the other end.

2. A child is taken at twelve, or in the year after, and entered for three years. A child taken later is entered for the same three. This is a kindness the College does not advertise and does not enter.

3. The birth-name is written once on the roll, in a careful hand, and then turned face under. A slate-name is entered in its place. The birth-name is not spoken in the teaching room. A boy who is called by it there writes his slate-name fifty times, and the fifty sheets are burned before he is let back to the copying.

4. An apprentice is kept, not hired. He eats from the College's dish, sleeps in the College's room, and what he earns goes to the College. The College does not pay him, and does not call him paid. What comes back comes back as bread and a bed, and neither is entered.

5. A boy who cannot learn the hand is taken off the rolls and entered in the second file. The file lies in its tin beside the sealed-file room and is not read at the Weighing. His entry is made in the same neat hand as an entry in the rolls, so that no man can tell, by looking at the writing alone, which boy stayed.

> *In a narrow upright hand, the verb struck from rule 4:* The apprentice is kept. *Underneath, in the copyist's hand:* I had written that he is fed. The Provost's clerk strikes a verb wherever he finds one, even in a rule about food. I have left *fed* standing under *kept*; a boy can be kept and hungry in the same sentence, and the passive voice has room for both.

## Of the hand

6. Ledgerhand is Grathic with the verbs removed, written upright and neat, all in the passive voice. The first exercise is to write a true sentence about a man and then take the man out of it. What is left is always true and can be read at the Weighing.

7. The passive voice is the College's own. *The measure was drawn* is true of every man who ever drew a measure, and a sentence true of everybody can be argued with by nobody. An apprentice who writes *I drew* is told to write again. An apprentice who writes *he drew* is told twice.

8. There are eleven words for a deduction. They are spoken in the teaching room in the second year, by the Provost or the officer he sends, and are not written in these articles. A boy who writes them on a slate copies the page again without them, and the slate is scraped clean in front of him. The words are not a secret. They are a trade.

9. No apprentice adds a column alone. Addition is done in the Weighing Hall, where figures are read aloud and not inspected. A column added alone will not agree with the notice, and what will not agree with the notice is copied again. An apprentice who needs to add is told that a man who can add is a man who can accuse, and is sent back to the copying.

> *In the Provost's narrow hand, above rule 7:* The passive voice is used. *Underneath, in mine:* I had written that it is chosen. A rule with nobody in it is a rule nobody can be thanked for and nobody blamed. I have left my word where he put his above it.

## The three years

| Year | The exercise | What is taken |
|---|---|---|
| First | The upright hand; copying notices from the moot-post at the Cross | The boy's own hand |
| Second | The passive voice; the eleven words, spoken only | The boy's own name in a sentence |
| Third | The Weighing; the reading of a figure in a clean voice | The boy's own voice, which he gets back only as the College's |

10. At the end of the third year the apprentice reads a figure aloud in the Weighing Hall, in a clean voice, with the Hearth-fire behind the grating and no priest or boy allowed to touch it. A reader who puts himself into the sentence is set back. The figure is the one the notice prints: one in twelve, as the notice says. There is no other figure in the room.

11. A whole return may be announced at the Weighing for a person who is not in the room. The apprentice does not ask where the person is. The College keeps a second file for the answers to questions of that kind, and the second file is not read aloud.

12. At the end of the three years the officer is given no house and no kin and is sent where the College sends him. His slate-name goes on the rolls in the same hand as his betters'. He does not take his birth-name with him, and the College does not take it from him; it is already face down.

## Of faults and their correcting

| Fault | Correction |
|---|---|
| A verb in the roll | The page copied again, in the same hand |
| A column added alone | The slate taken up for a tend |
| A birth-name spoken in the teaching room | Fifty sheets, burned before the boy |
| A figure read with feeling | The reader set back one year |
| A sealed file carried out of the room | The carrier's own name entered in the second file |
| A slate lost | Paid in kind; a slate is a piece of stone and a name is not |

13. The College keeps its rolls, its Weighing notices, its sealed files and the only right to write a lawful deduction. It keeps them in the Hearthseat. An apprentice who carries a sealed file beyond the room has his own name carried into the second file instead.

14. The Vorr sit at the survey table and are not asked to translate. The Deep Hand is copied and not read. These are the College's enemies, set down here so that an apprentice may know which questions to leave alone.

# The copying-song

*Sung at the benches in the first year, under the breath, while the hand learns the upright stroke. Nobody sings it at the Weighing.*

> Ink in the crease of the thumb,
> slate dust along the sleeve;
> write the man, then take him out,
> and leave the part believed.
>
> One for the roll and one for the file
> and one for the name turned down;
> the words go out, the figures stay,
> and the house keeps its own.
>
> Eleven words and none said aloud,
> a clean voice for the sum;
> the boy who copies all night long
> is the boy it comes from.

The grey at the head of this leaf came off a collector's thumb laid there while the song was being copied; he had the colour at his fingers and did not know he had marked it. I have not rubbed it out. The tin is not swept on the Last Day, and the dust stays where it fell.

# What the borrowed hands did

The Provost's clerk struck the verb out of rule 7, as he strikes every verb he can find, and set *is used* above my *is chosen*. I have left both. Another hand, narrow and not the Provost's, struck *the birth-name is turned face under* in rule 3 and set *the writing is turned* above it, as if a name were a piece of writing and not a boy. I have put the name back underneath. A name turned face down is still a name, and a clerk who forgets that is a clerk who will one day find his own in the tin.

The damp along the foot came from the sealed-file room, which is colder than the rest of Slate Row and always has been. The green bead in the fold came off a glaze-mixer come up from High Kiln with the sweetness still at her mouth; she leaned to see the eleven words and did not ask to read them. I have kept the bead where it fell.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> A hand that can write a life without a person in it will write one for anybody.

I have asked the copyists, the Provost's clerk and a man come down from the Weighing Hall, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The leaf goes back into the tin beside the names that were taken off the rolls. Tomorrow a boy will be entered at twelve and will learn to take himself out of his own sentences, and by the third year he will read a figure in a voice that nobody can thank and nobody can blame. The figure will stand as read.
