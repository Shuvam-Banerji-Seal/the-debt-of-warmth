I keep the birth-slates at the shrine and I have written this because a man from Slate Row stood in the yard and asked what the children said when they jumped. He wanted a figure. I told him there was a count in it and no figure, which is the truth of the thing, and he went away and sent me a clerk instead. What is below is what the children said before the clerk came, and what they said again after he had gone back over the north bridge. I have set the clerk's hand above mine where he put it, and the children's words under it where they stand, as the floor keeps the ash and the foot that raised it.

They play it in Stone when the yard will not bear a foot, on the trodden-ash floor of a kiln-house where the fire is banked and the boards hold a little warmth. They play it in the thaw as well, on the dry boards under the racks, and not during the Five, because in the Five the children are made to stand and listen and there is no floor for it. Some twist a plait of grey wool and call it the eel and hold it between two hands; the words are the same words and the twelfth is the same twelfth. A mother will tell them not to knot the wool, because a knot of grey wool is an account and a child who ties one in play will be asked what it counts.

# The counting-out, said before the floor is taken

*Right fists held out, knuckles up. The child who says it points at each fist in turn and does not look at the faces. The fist it lights on at the last word keeps the crack, which is to say she draws the line on the floor and calls the fault. She does not get to hop first. She is the one who has to be fair, which the children accept as a punishment and the rest of the yard accepts as practice.*

> Crock and dish and crock and dish,
> the potter's thumb and the eel's wish;
> pit and rack and wheel and sill,
> and the one it lights on keeps the crack.

*Say it to the floor. Said looking at a child, it becomes an argument, and there is always a child who will argue.*

# The rhyme, as it is hopped

*One line, one hop. The line drawn in slip or in the grey is thumb-wide and must be crossed, never touched. A child who touches it has gone wrong and sits on the ash at the edge of the floor while the others go around her. At the twelfth line the hopper must cross twice without setting a foot down between; that second crossing is the dish, and the kept one. If she puts a foot down between, she is banked and sits with her arms folded until she is brought back. The last thing said before they stop is the bringing back: three children say her name, and she comes in again. A child whom nobody brings back is thin until the next round. The children will not be told why the twelfth is the kept one. If you ask them they say it is the dish, and if you ask what is in the dish they ask you why a man wants to know what is in a dish.*

> One for the pit where the slip comes up,
> two for the bench and the hollow seat,
> three for the rack where the wet ones stand,
> four for the crack that you must not meet.
>
> Five for the thumb on the birth-slate's rim,
> six for the grey where the skirting turns,
> seven for the bell that wants one more,
> eight for the eel that goes in and stays.
>
> Nine for the step that comes back askew,
> ten for the broom that goes around,
> eleven for the word we say at home,
> twelve for the dish, and the one kept out.

# A note on the several hands in it

The fifth line is not mine. I wrote *the thumb on the birth-slate's rim* because that is where the hand goes at a birth, and a woman beside me at the shrine put her mark in the margin against it and wrote, *not the thumb on the rim — the thumb over the writing*. It is Alda Reed's hand, I think, though she has never said so. She crossed two fingers over a child's birth-flower on the seventh day of Stone and would not say the word for it, and she has kept her own account of this game in her head for nineteen years without once setting it down. Her reading is better than mine. A hand on the rim is a hand that might lift the slate; a hand over the writing is a hand that has already decided what the slate will say. I have left both lines standing, hers in the margin and mine underneath, as a kept thing is left where it fell.

# What the clerk did

The factor's clerk borrowed the leaf at Greybank, being a man who cannot let paper lie. He has a cuff gone soft from washing and the ink sits in two fingers of it and will not come out. He numbered the twelve lines in the margin, struck through *the one kept out* and wrote above it, *the deduction is one in twelfths, the residue is nil, the young are instructed*. Against *twelve for the dish* he wrote *the twelfth is retained for the Hearth*. At the foot of the count he put a round nought and wrote *absence* beside it, where the children's game ends with a name said three times.

I have put their words back underneath and left his standing above. He takes the verbs out because a thing always true has no need of them. I put them back because a child up to her ankle in the grey of the yard does not know she is hearing something always true, and wants to know which foot to lift. A nought is a number. The dish is a place at the table. He has put a figure where a place should be, which is the whole art of that hand.

He asked me what the children learned from it. I told him the count and the fault and the bringing back, and he said that was an account of the Return taught before the child could read a slate, and he entered it. The children stop when a man in a soft cuff comes into the yard. They take it up again when he is past the bridge, and they never once change the twelfth.

# The verse they do not say while a man from Slate Row is in the square

> One for the lean and two for the thin,
> three for the loaves that go on the dish;
> the slate says one and the yard says none,
> and the name said at home is the only one.
>
> The dish goes round and the dish comes back,
> and what was kept is counted as lack;
> four for the corners and one for the sill,
> and the place where the twelfth stood is standing still.

I have it from the child who keeps the crack, with the proper space left before it, which is the space they leave in the hopping. She would not say it while the clerk had his pen out, and would not say it to him at all. She said it to the floor with her back turned and made me put it down as I have it, with none of my verbs in it, because a verse said to the floor wants nothing added, and a verse said to a man wants everything taken away.

# At the head and in the gutter

The slip at the head is off the hand that drew the line; it goes into the grain and will not come out, which is why the yard uses it to mark a place. The green bead came off a pot broken in the glaze room and rolled as far as the fold before anybody saw it. The tallow thumb is mine: I held the notice down in the draught under the shrine door and did not know I had marked it. The grey in the gutter came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying; I have not swept it and there is no Last Day in a slate-box. The tideline took two words off the back of the notice and I have not written them in again, because a rhyme with two words gone is a rhyme said slowly, and all of ours are said slowly. The twist of grey wool through the hole is mine, tied for the word I could not put down. I will not untie it.

# At the foot, in grey chalk, in a child's hand

> I kept the crack and nobody came.

No hand will own it. The letters are set down as they were made, hard at the *th* and one of them backward, which is how a child writes who has learned off a slate and not off a book. I asked whose it was and had three answers and no hand, as I did with the grey print. I have left it where it was written.

The leaf goes back into the slate-box with the birth-slates that have been answered. Tomorrow the yard will draw its line again, and one in twelve will be read in a clean voice at the notice-board, and the twelfth will be kept out of the count and brought back at the end of it, which is more than can be said for the district's own twelfth. The children will go on saying the name three times. I have kept their words and I have not joined them to anything.
