I keep the tally in the survey room off the Duelling Floor. The tin stands on the shelf above the survey sheets, and the key to the room hangs on a nail beside the clamps, but the key to the tin does not hang there and I have never held it. That is as it should be. A clerk copies what is put in front of him and asks for nothing that is kept from him; the floor has room for one answer and the room has room for one silence.

The tin is lined with warm-stone. On the Last Day, when the ash in the corners is left where it lies and nobody sweeps, the master takes the paper out and reads it standing and puts it back. He has never once said what he thinks of it, and I have never asked. I have copied it four times. On each copy the same thing happens: the seven propositions go in neatly, in a hand that wants to be read, and the hand that wrote them will not go in at all.

This is the chapter the Court clerk cannot find. He has asked for it twice, once at the survey table and once at Greybank with a soft cuff and a slate already open, and both times I have given him the roll of hands, which is a true account of every boy who came down, and is exactly what he came for under another name. What follows is not the roll of hands. It is what the tin keeps.

# The years as the annals have them

*Copied from the floor's roll and set beside what came down from the Hearthseat in a hand that writes with the verbs taken out. The entries are in the number-first hand. I have put nothing in from the temple tells, which have their own dates and a comfort in them.*

**Y.H. 702.** A keeper of the Hearthseat named Haldre set down a paper called *The Sealed Vessel*. It was read by nine persons. The Hearthseat enters it as *a paper concerning the retention of vessels*. The floor's roll enters it nowhere, because the floor counts measures and a paper holds none. Entered here as *nine readers*, and the nine set out below by seat.

**Y.H. 704.** The keeper disappears. The Court records that he died and was banked. No completed return is entered. The Unbanked say he crossed first. Entered as *the keeper's account carried forward*, and beneath it, in the same hand, a nought. There is no grave.

**Y.H. 710.** Nine copies ordered burnt. Eight went on the grate in this room and the ninth went into the tin. The Court's entry stops at *nine copies ordered burnt*, because an order is a thing a clerk can enter and a fire is not. Entered as *order discharged*. The ninth is not entered, and the roll goes on to the next boy.

> *In the narrow upright hand of the Court's survey-clerk, against the entry of Y.H. 704:* The keeper's return was not completed. Strike *crossed first*.
>
> *Underneath, in mine:* I have not struck it. The keeper's return was not completed, which is true, and the Unbanked say he crossed first, which is also true, and the two have different shapes. A clerk who strikes the second has left the nought standing by itself, and a nought on its own is the readiest answer to a man there is.

# The seven propositions, as the paper has them

*Taken out of the tin on the Last Day and copied while the master read from the other side of the table. The paper is in a hand like Ledgerhand and is not Ledgerhand: every verb in it is in the present, which is the way a man writes of a thing he has seen done and not a thing he hopes for. I have set the Court's correction above each proposition and left the proposition standing beneath, as the old break is left under a new keying.*

| No. | As the paper has it | What the Court's hand set above |
|---|---|---|
| One | The world is a vessel and every vessel leaks. Leakage is a flaw in the making and not a law of it. | The world was made whole and cracked once. Enter as the First Breaking. |
| Two | A flaw may be stopped. The seam can be made to hold so that nothing passes through it, and the vessel need not be mended again. | The Return is the appointed repair. Strike *need not*. |
| Three | Where nothing leaks, nothing is owed. Where nothing is owed, the deduction has no object. | The deduction is a tithe for the Hearth and is not a loss. |
| Four | The Return is not mercy but the consequence of a leak. A vessel that holds has no dead to send back. | The Return is mercy. Strike *consequence*. |
| Five | The Want is a rate of loss and not a hunger. Stop the loss and the Want ceases of itself. | The Want is the portion kept back. Strike *of itself*. |
| Six | A vessel that holds everything feels nothing. This is not a punishment but the end of accounting. | The Kept are not to be pitied. Enter as: the account is secure. |
| Seven | The Final Banking is not a drawing-in but a sealing. The world made retentive is the world at rest. | The Final Banking is in the hands of the Keeping. Strike *at rest*. |

The sixth is the one the master turns face down before he reads the seventh. I have seen him do it four times and have never seen him hurry. A man who keeps a paper he will not use will also take his time over it; there is no hurry in a room where nothing is going to be done.

The paper asks for no new way and opens no way in the arm. It asks for the stopping of every way there is. That is why an assessor can take the verbs out of it and still find something left to fear, which is the nouns: *leak*, *owe*, *Want*, *rest*. A verb can be struck; a noun has to be answered.

# The nine who read it

*The tin's copy gives seats and not names. I have not invented the names, and I have not asked the Vorr, who would say that the numbers are correct and turn the slate face down.*

| Seat | What the tin sets beside it |
|---|---|
| The keeper who wrote it | gone in 704; no completed return |
| The Steward of the Hearth | the seat stands |
| The College of Assessors | three readers, not named |
| The bank at the Hearthseat | two readers, not named |
| The roll-room | one reader, not named |
| The keeper of the Hearth-fire | |

That is nine by seat, with the keeper counted at the head of them. Eight seats have a thing set beside them and the ninth has a line left blank. The blank stands where the keeper of the fire should have an answer; the fire is still burning, which is a fact and not a reader. A blank is not an absence in this room; it is a place where a clerk has put down his pen and gone to stand at the door. I have copied the blank as I found it.

# The burning-song

*Taken down at the shaft mouth from a carrier come up with a lead cart, who would not give his number and said the numbers were correct. Sung going up, never going down. He said he had learned it from a man who carried in 710 and would not say which of the nine the man had carried.*

> Nine were written and nine were read,
> and one was read with a hand that shook;
> the paper said a vessel might
> be made to hold and never take.
>
> Eight went on the grate in the survey room,
> the ninth went down in a tin of stone;
> the stone keeps warm what the fire would not,
> and the tin keeps shut what the stone keeps warm.
>
> No leak, no owing, no coming back,
> no cold to count and no debt to keep;
> the keeper said it would end the Want,
> and the Vorr said the numbers were right.
>
> There's one went first and has no grave,
> and a tin on a shelf that is warm in Ash;
> read it on the Last Day, put it back,
> and the numbers stand and the hand does not.

He would not sing the last verse until the cart was unloaded. I have set it where he put it, at the mouth and away from the table.

# What the Court's hand did

A survey-clerk of the factor's office borrowed the leaf when the survey table sat at the Keep. He writes upright and narrow, and takes the verbs out as though a verb were a thing a man might smuggle past a weigh in the fold of a cuff.

- Against *every vessel leaks*, he set *the vessel is whole by ordinance*, which is a sentence true of no cup in Keld.
- Against *the deduction has no object*, he set *the deduction is received in due order*. The order is due and the object is not named.
- Against *a vessel that holds everything feels nothing*, he set *the account is secure*, and struck the word *end*.
- Against *the world at rest*, he set *the work continues*, which is the phrase the College teaches its own to write at the foot of every closed file.
- Against the blank where the ninth reader stands he wrote nothing. The hand passed over it without a stroke, which is the readiest way of correcting a reader I have yet seen.

I have left both readings everywhere, the paper under the neat hand, as the floor keeps the first number under the second when a boy's measure changes and nobody scrapes the old one out. A clerk who takes the verbs out of a paper leaves the nouns standing, and the nouns in this paper are the only things in the First Seam that have ever frightened me.

# At the head and in the gutter

The coal at the head came off the master's sleeve when he leaned over to turn the sixth proposition face down and would not sit down to do it. The warm-stone dust in the inner margin comes off the tin every time the paper goes back in, and there is more of it on the seventh proposition than on the first, which tells a man nothing he should repeat. The curl of burnt paper in the gutter is from one of the eight; it curls inward, the way paper curls when the fire takes the edge and not the middle, and I have pasted it in with flour paste that has gone brittle. The hot lead at the fold came off a surveyor's cuff on the day a passing was measured early; it has eaten the corner of the fourth proposition and I have not written the word back in. The frost-feather came up out of the paper on the climb over the Cold Pass and was gone by the time I found the leaf to press it in. The tallow is my lamp set too near the tally. The grey along the outer fold is not mine and not the Court's. I have had this leaf from the last Opening of the Slates and have not swept it, and if the factor asks why a clerk leaves dirt on a chronicle I shall tell him it is entered as observance kept, which is a phrase he will accept without looking up.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> Eight went to the fire and one to the stone,
> the stone keeps warm what the fire undid;
> a world that holds is a world that feels,
> and the keeper who crossed has no name on the slate.
>
> Ask the tin what it keeps.

I have asked the master of the floor, a surveyor come up from the Ninth Gallery, and a Vorr who came through with a warm-stone and would not set it down, and had three answers and no hand. The Vorr said the numbers are correct. The surveyor turned the slate face down. The master cleaned a clamp and went on with the counting. I have left the line where it was written.

The tin goes back on the shelf and the key goes back on its nail. Tomorrow a boy will be brought down at thirteen and will give his number before his name, and the master will ask him the second question while he cleans the iron, and whatever the boy answers will be entered in the same neat hand as the boys who stayed warm. The paper in the tin will be a little warm in the dark. Somebody paid for that warmth and the payer is not entered in any roll I keep.
