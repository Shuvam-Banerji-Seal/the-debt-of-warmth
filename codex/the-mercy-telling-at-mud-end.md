I keep the byre at Mud End where the moot sits in winter. There is a fire until dusk and none after, because this is the Long Night and the Keeping says a man may tell the Return in the dark and be heard the better for it. I have kept this byre eleven winters and I have never once seen anybody hear better in the dark. They hear the same. They hear it with nothing to look at, which is a different thing.

We hold the mercy-telling on this night. A person tells of a death the hamlet called merciful --- one that went quietly, where nobody had to be sent for and no collector came up from Greybank for it --- and the teller must give the dead one's name. A story that keeps its name gets a peat-block set on the floor. A story that lets a figure into it gets nothing, and the teller owes a peat to the winter stack, which is the only fine this byre knows and the only one anybody pays without an argument. I do not say why a story served. A reason given is a story given away, and a person who has told one well can go home and sell it in Sill.

The moot meets here because the bog takes a man's weight in Silt and his boots in Stone, and there is no dry room between us and the river but this one. Nine houses and their peat stacks, and one byre with a floor packed hard as old bread. On the Long Night the floor is the book. Nothing is written in it that a clerk can carry off, and in the morning I tread the blocks into the ash and there is no score left, which is the point of a byre and the difference between a byre and Slate Row.

# The rules, as the byre keeps them

1. Tell once. A story told twice is a different story and is not heard; the second telling is for the teller and he may pay for it himself.
2. Give a name. A figure is not a name. A figure travels; a name stays in the room where the person said it.
3. Four blocks lie in a row and a fifth lies by the wall. The fifth is not a prize and is not the best story's block. It is for the one the year took and nobody in these nine houses could tell.
4. No fire after dusk. The last coal is banked before the first voice. A story told by firelight counts for nothing, being a story told twice --- once to the living and once to the coal.
5. Say it to the floor, the door, or the pot. Said looking at a person it becomes an argument, and there is enough argument in Mud End over the winter peat without bringing the dead into it.
6. The byre judges. I say whether a name was kept and I do not say which name I wanted.

Before the first voice we sing the short song, standing, with our hands on the byre wall because it is the only warm thing in the room by then.

> Four blocks for the four that went quietly,
> and a fifth by the wall for the one not told;
> the peat keeps warm what the peat has held,
> and the name goes round while the figure goes cold.
>
> Tell it to the floor, tell it to the door,
> tell it to the place where the fifth would stand;
> the clerk writes one and the house writes none,
> and both go down in the same dry hand.

# The four that were told

**Vey**, said the widow who cuts peat on the west bank. She said it to the pot. Her man went out after the frost with a spade and sat down in the cut and did not stand up again, and the district called it merciful because nobody had to go looking and no slate went up to Sill over it. She said his name three times, once for the byre, once for the floor, and once for the cut he sat in, and would not let me set a figure against it. "He's Vey," she said. "That's the whole of it. A number's what you write when you've lost the person." She took her peat-block and went and stood by the door until the next story, which is how a widow stands in this hamlet.

The second was put by a woman come over from Cold Acre, where the well never freezes. She told of a child returned thin in the year of the flower, come back to a house where three had gone into the measure and the house could not say which of the three had come home. The child was quiet and wanted nothing and grew no shape, and the house called it merciful because it asked for no warmth and so cost nobody. She did not give the child's name. She gave the name of the woman who kept it, said low into the pot, and said the child's name was not for a byre where a clerk had been known to lean in the door. I set a block for the keeper's name and none for the child's, and we left it so. A name kept in one house is still a name kept.

The cooper told the third. He is the man the hamlet means when it says a cooper's seventh, though he has never explained the seventh to anybody and gives a different date for it every time he is asked, which is a way of answering that costs him nothing. He told of the man they called merciful who slept before a banked kiln with the damper shut and did not wake, and he told it to the floor with his cap in his hand. "Merciful is the word the district used," he said. "His name was Ansel. You can have the one or the other, but not both, and I'll have the name." There was a silence after that, the kind that has four in it and a gap where the fifth would be, and the bell from Sill came across the frozen fields four, four and a gap, and nobody crossed himself. I set the block. Ansel's name went round the byre and came back to the cooper, and the figure never arrived.

The fourth I put myself, because the room had grown too quiet and a story put by the byre is not scored against anybody. I told of the year's fire that comes up in a clay dish before light, house to house, each hearth touching its own flame to it, and the last house keeping what is left. There was a woman in this row who took the dish in and did not pass it on, and her house was warm all that winter and she went cold in the spring, and the hamlet called her merciful because she went in her sleep and nobody had to tell her the fire was spent. I did not say where the coal came from. She did not say either. That is the part we call merciful, or the part next to it. I set no block for my own story, because a byre that scores itself will soon be a Court, and I have seen how those rooms go.

# The fifth by the wall

The fifth block lay by the wall all night and nobody moved it. A girl from the Tallow road, who had come in with wool on her sleeves and stayed silent, put her hand toward it twice and took it back, and I have not written which girl, because she would stop coming if I did. In the morning there was a small grey print of Ash where the block had lain, the colour of the ridge that gathers where a promise is not kept, and I trod it in with the rest of the floor and called it the thaw, as we call everything in this hamlet that we do not want to name. The fifth was not a story that failed. It was the year's taking that nobody in nine houses could put a name to, and a thing left in place is still a thing kept.

# What the clerk did

A factor's clerk come up from Greybank to count the winter peat borrowed the leaf on a morning when the stacks were being measured and his cuff was soft at the edge from washing. He set the four tellings down as *four merciful returns entered, nil deduction*, and put a figure of marks against each of the three names, which was more than the byre had scored, because the byre scores in whether a name comes back out of the room and he does not.

- Against *he's Vey, that's the whole of it*, he wrote *one life surrendered without claim*.
- At the thin child he wrote *third party unidentifiable; vacancy*, and put a round nought in the place where the child's name would have stood.
- Against *his name was Ansel*, he struck the name and set above it *the measure was voluntarily resigned*, and underlined it twice.
- At the year's fire he wrote *the warmth was transferred by consent*, which is a true thing in a language nobody in this byre speaks. Consent of whom he did not write, and I have not asked him.

I have put my words under his and left his standing, the way the floor keeps a peat-block under a spilled quart. He has entered four and struck a name and numbered the empty place; I have kept three names and a place that is not a number. Both hands are on the leaf. Both will travel, his further than mine, which is the whole trouble with writing.

# At the head and in the gutter

The peat thumb at the head is mine; I held the leaf down on the byre wall in the draught that comes under the door and did not know I had marked it. Peat goes into the grain and will not come out, which is why the moot uses it to sign. The press of wet wool across the first leaf came off the girl from the Tallow road when she leaned over to see what the clerk had written; the sheep's-wool fibre caught in the fold is off the same coat, and I have left it where it fell. The small ale ring is from a quart that stood from the first voice to the fifth and was drunk by nobody, which is how a quart usually goes in Mud End. The grey in the gutter came off a cuff that had no business in the corner where I keep the leaf, and I have not swept it. The frost-crisp at one edge is from the morning the leaf went out to be copied and was left under a stone too short for it; the writing there is thin and I have not gone over it, because a word written thin is a word said quietly, and most of ours are.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The block by the wall was never set. The Ash came where it stood.
> Four names went home. The fifth stayed where the year put it.

I asked the widow, the cooper and the girl from the Tallow road, and had three answerings and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The leaf goes back into the sacking and under the stone, and the blocks go into the floor with the heel of a boot, and the byre is swept for the next moot as if nothing had been told in it. If the clerk comes again with his pen I shall give him the verbs to take out and keep the names underneath, as I did this year. On the next Long Night the fire will go out at dusk and the dark will come in, and I shall set four blocks in a row and one by the wall, and let whoever has a name to keep come in out of the cold and say it.
