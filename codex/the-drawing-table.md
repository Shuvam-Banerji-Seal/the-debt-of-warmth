I keep the sleeves and the roll of hands behind the table in Slate Row. A boy comes up from Greybank with a name his house gave him and goes back down the road with a slate-name and a wool sleeve. I draw the measure. The clerk at Greybank writes the figure. There is a difference between the two. A taking and a draw are the same heat going the same way; the slate is the difference.

The table is a guild of a sort, though the word is a clay-country one and is not used in Slate Row. These articles were first gathered at Greybank in Y.H. 719, when the factor's house found it had two sorts of men to count. A rule with no grey on it is a rule nobody has had to use. These have been used.

# The articles as they stand at the table

## Of the taking

1. The table takes an apprentice, not a house. A house may bring him, or the Temple's poor may be sent, or a collector may take him off a doorstep where a draw has left nobody to answer for him. From the day he first wraps a tube the table answers for his hand. A house that will not give up the years has given nothing.
2. He is taken at fifteen, or at sixteen if the house has kept him through a hard year. A boy who cannot carry a wrapped tube to the Waiting Room and set it down without letting the iron show has no business on the road; he will spend the wool on his sleeves and call the road's.
3. His birth-name is said once at the taking and kept in the mouth afterward. A slate-name is entered in the roll in its place, in the same hand as the figures, so that no man can tell by looking which of the two was written first. A name in the mouth cannot be entered, and there is nothing in this country the Court has not tried to enter.
4. He is kept, not hired. He eats from the table's dish and sleeps in the room above the Waiting Room, and what he earns goes into the table and comes back as bread and a sleeve, neither of which is written down. An officer who tells a boy he is paid tells a true thing about a figure and a false thing about a living.
5. No boy is asked whether he wants the iron. He is asked whether he understands what is carried: a tube wrapped in wool, a knuckle laid to the wall, a hiss, a figure read twice, and a grey that comes up under the nails before he has learned to read the slate. The wanting comes after, or it does not come.

> *In the narrow upright hand of a College clerk, against rule 4:* Retained by the table; no wage arising. *Underneath, in mine:* I had written that he is fed. He is fed, and the feeding goes into no figure, which is why it can be done. I have left my word where his was set above it, as a man leaves the crack showing.

## Of the wool and the note

6. Wrap the tube before you take it up, not after. A boy can hold iron while he speaks, but he will not speak well with his fingers going the colour of the sky before snow. The wool keeps the hand one draw and lets the heat go, which is as much as any sleeve of mine is owed.
7. Set the knuckle to the wall before the iron goes to the arm. A whole vessel answers full; one cracked through answers flat, a small dead sound that stops at the surface. Write the note before the figure, and write *none* where there was no note and not a nought, because a nought is a figure and a silence is not. The kiln-masters of Sill keep the full note to themselves, which is their trade; I know it from rooms where a pot was set down and the house talked over the sound. A collector who draws blind still brings back a figure. That is the trouble with figures.
8. Look at the arm, where the ways run near the skin. Do not look at the face. A face tells you nothing you can use, and a thing you cannot use is a thing you will remember, which is worse on the road.
9. Count the draws on the fingers afterwards, never before. A figure counted before the draw is a figure already decided, and a decided figure is the same thing as a false one with the ink dry.
10. Say the four sayings in the order the road comes to them, not the order the notice prints. In the room they begin with *nothing is lost that we have kept* and end with the taking; on the road the record comes first, so it cannot comfort, and the banking comes last, because it is the true one and the one we do the most. Say them into the wool, not the air, where words go up and get entered.
11. One in twelve, as the notice says, read twice: once for the moot and once for those who came late up the road. The boy reads what is printed. What the district pays is not his hand, and the man who sets the other figure has gone up the Clay Road with it and does not carry it in the open. An apprentice who asks after that figure is told that a collector who asks after a correction will soon be making one.
12. Never say the prayer twice over one man. A second prayer argues with the first, and the first was honest, being said before he knew how much the man had. If the bell comes off the Cross four and four and a gap, stop at the gap. The gap belongs to somebody else.
13. Consent is recorded; the table writes the word as the slate has it. A boy is taught to say *lawful draw; consent recorded* and not to ask recorded by whom, because a collector who asks it in a room will ask it at a door, and a door is where a man loses his cuff. A refusal is not entered. The clip belongs to the Court's room, not to this table, and the room has its own hand for it.

> *In the leaning hand of a factor's clerk come up from Greybank, against rule 11:* Printed deduction, read twice; no second figure arises. *In mine, underneath:* Both figures arise. One is carried in the open and one under a coat. Both stand, and I have stopped asking the clerk which of the two he means by *arises*.

## The years at the table

*Kept behind the sleeves. What is written is the work that came up, in a hand that does not change between one line and the next.*

| Year | At the table | What is taken |
|---|---|---|
| Fifteen | Wrap the tubes, sweep, keep the sleeves dry, carry the slate edge-on | The birth-name, said once and kept in the mouth |
| Sixteen | Walk with an old collector; set the knuckle; say the note into the wool | The habit of counting before the draw |
| Seventeen | Set the iron under an eye that has drawn forty times; read the figure twice | The face of the person on the pallet, which is not looked at again |
| Eighteen | Take a pot alone through a draw and read the grey in his own hand | The last of a boy's account with any house |

14. Eighteen is the common end of a keeping and not a short one. He stays where the table sends him, at Sill or Greybank or a district he has not heard of. The table's figure for him is one apprentice, current, no wage arising. What stands beneath that figure is a boy who eats from the dish. The College does not choose where he goes. The roll does, and the road after it. His hand goes grey under the nails first and comes up into the creases by the second year, and after that a man may wash till the water freezes and nothing changes. Collectors say the grey comes off in a day. I have had it last six, and no clerk has asked after the sixth. A collector who says the cost is nothing has not looked at his own hand, which is the only looking he does not do.

## Of faults and their correcting

| Fault | As corrected at the table |
|---|---|
| Iron taken up before the wool | The tube wrapped in the boy's sight; the draw put off a tend |
| Knuckle not set before the tube | The note entered as *none*, which is not a nought |
| A figure counted before the draw | The figure struck; the boy set to counting sleeves for a tend |
| The face looked at | No punishment. The boy is told what he has kept and sends it nowhere |
| A prayer said twice over one man | The second struck; the first left standing |
| A birth-name written on a travelling leaf | Scraped away; no other word put in its place |

15. A fault copied twice in one tend is a fault the boy has not understood, and the old collector sits with him behind the table until he has. The tube is not a rod and the slate is not a confession.

# The wrapping-song, pasted inside the wool sleeve

*Sung low going up the Clay Road, with the tube wrapped and not in the hand. Nobody sings it in a house and nobody sings it at the Weighing. The boy whose sleeve is new says the middle verse, and nobody says his name.*

> Wool on the iron, iron on the arm,
> the note comes first and the figure after;
> four for the moot and four for the late,
> and a place where the fifth goes by.
>
> Grey in the nail, grey in the crease,
> the hand goes home ahead of the man;
> wrap the iron, turn the slate,
> wipe the hand and say no name.
>
> Wool on the iron, iron on the arm,
> the road goes up and the figure goes down;
> the note stays where the knuckle left it,
> and the gap in the bell is nobody's.

# What the borrowed hands did

A clerk of the College borrowed the leaf at Sill with ink in two fingers of a soft cuff, and took the verbs out as though a verb were a thing a man could smuggle past a weigh. Against *I set the knuckle to the wall* he wrote *the vessel was assessed*, true of every vessel in Sill and therefore of none; I have put my hand back underneath. Against *what the district pays is not his hand* he wrote *the deduction is one in twelve for every vessel of the Keeping*, which is what the notice says. I have left it standing; a clerk's true sentence is not always an honest one. Where the space stands before the last verse he put a round nought and wrote *vacancy*. A nought is a number. The place was not.

A clerk of the factor's house at Greybank wrote *no cost to officer* against the grey. I have not struck him out. A clerk's false line is what the next reader ought to see.

The hearth-maid came up from Low Kiln with a birth-slate under her shawl and waited until the pigs were weighed before she put charcoal to the leaf. Her thick letters tore the paper in two places.

- At *look at the arm, not the face*: “I have washed that arm and held it when it was warm. You will not know whose it was. I shall.”
- At *write none and not a nought*: “We put the date where the word would go, and the date is not the word.”
- At *the hand goes home ahead of the man*: “There's a stone in my house kept for whoever comes in behind. It isn't for you. Don't ask who it was for.”

I have set her words in the margin where she put them, because she would not have them on the face of the leaf where an officer might read them going past.

# At the head and in the folds

The grey at the head is mine, off the fingers I gave the figure to at Greybank, and it has gone in under the nails and will not come out. The filings in the gutter came out of the wool sleeve; there is always iron in the fold, and it has never drawn the warmth out of the paper, which is the one kindness iron has shown this leaf. The green bead came off a girl at the Cross who leaned over with the sweetness still at her mouth and touched the corner; it tastes sweet in wet weather, and I have not washed it. The frost along one edge is from the Rim Road in Stone, and I have not gone over the words it thinned; a word written thin is a word said quietly, and most of ours are. The tallow thumb is mine. The tide-line along the foot came from the Waiting Room, where the collected are kept overnight and the damp gets into everything on a bench. The bare square in the lower margin is where I scraped a word away with a knife and put no other in its place. I have not written it back, and I have not forgotten it.

On the Last Day they burn the corners and call it dealt with. There is no Last Day for a sleeve.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The wool keeps the heat out of the hand for one draw.
> Nothing else in this trade is kept.

I asked an old collector, a clerk from the College and a woman at my counter, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the two lines where they were written. The leaf goes back behind the table and the tube into its wool. Tomorrow a boy will come up from Greybank with a name he will not use on the road, and I shall set the knuckle to the wall before the iron and write the note before the College can strike it out. The grey will come off on the next leaf. I have stopped washing it.
