I tend the weigh-lamp at the mouth of the First Seam. It is a different work from keeping an account, though both begin with the hand on the lamp before the eye goes to the man standing in the light. In Ash the Rim shuts, the carts stop on the far side of the pass, and there is nothing to do of an evening but ask one another what we already know and call the asking a question. A question whose answer nobody knows is a bet. Men here will bet on the Cold Pass, which has a post and no gate and has not moved in a hundred years.

The old man had been forty years at the weigh. He came up on the last shift before the shut and had not been down since, and he would not state his number, which is the first thing a man states in this Keep, before his name or after it. “I have stated it enough,” he said. He sat with his cap on his knees and his right hand wrapped in a cloth, and told me while I turned the lamp low and the draught came up the shaft over the wick.

# The manner of it

- A question is said to the stone, never to the man beside you. Said to a man it becomes an argument, and an argument above the shaft is a debt with no date on it, which is the one debt this room will not carry through the dark.
- The wager is a shaving of warm-stone laid on the sill. We count shavings by touch and not by eye, because the eye listens to the face and the fingers do not.
- The judge is the stone. I say whether an answer serves and I do not say why. A reason given is a question given away for nothing, and the giver can take it up the Rim Road and sell it.
- No slate lies on the table. A slate turns a question into a figure, and a figure goes up the road in a clean hand and comes back as something a man owes.
- Answer in one breath. A second answer is a different question and is not heard.

There had been nine such nights before the shut and there would be no more until the thaw. This is the one the old man told me, in the words he used, which are short and have their endings struck off.

# What he told me

> Four to the stone and one on the sill,
> state what you hold and the number first;
> the shaving goes down and the answer stays up,
> and the figure goes up the Rim Road.
>
> One for the count and one for the pair,
> one for the man that the count comes back for;
> the fourth stands where the shaving stood ---
> the stone has kept it and said nothing.

He said the song is not sung at the Descent and not on the stone. It is said waiting, with the cup in the right hand and the left palm flat on the table, because a man who has held a clamp does not hold a cup in the same hand at the same time.

It was Ash, he said, eleven winters back, before the lowlands gave the year a name. The snow lay long in the pass and no cart had come down for nine days. There were six of us in the room off the weigh: an old woman who counted at the mouth, a man up from the Ninth with the sweet of hot lead still on his cuff, a survey-man come down from the stones, a clay-man from the low road who had come up before the shut and could not get back, and two of the floor who said nothing and kept their hands on the table. A warm-stone shaving went on the sill for each question, and nobody touched the cup until the fourth.

The first went to the old woman who counts at the mouth. She had her answer before the verse was out, which is why she never keeps the shaving and is always first.

> Bought and never borrowed;
> a man takes me down and brings me up again;
> I am coldest at the mouth and warmest at the end;
> nobody gives me back.
> What am I?

“A warm-stone,” she said, and said it to the stone. She did not look at any of us. A stone is sold in this country and is not lent, and a woman who has counted at the mouth for thirty years does not say a thing like that across a table where men have numbers.

The second went to the man up from the Ninth, who kept turning his cuff over as though the smell were on the wrong side of it.

> It is red and is not blood.
> It goes on the figure and not on the man.
> A man who lied wears it home on his sleeve
> and cannot wash it out before the thaw.
> What is it?

“The reeve's red,” he said, to the stone. There was a man at the table with a small red mark worked into the seam of his coat where no mark had been in the morning, and he set his hand over it and did not lift it again. Nobody asked him about it, which is the proper way to answer a man who has put his hand over something. The mine-reeve checks a man who has lied about his number in red; I have watched him do it and have never watched him look up. The shaving went on the sill and the cup went round once.

The third went to the survey-man come down from the stones, who answered slowly, as a man answers who has been a long time on a figure and not on a person.

> It grows where the warm has lately gone out of stone;
> it stands on the well lip and is gone by noon;
> a man is sent to find what it means,
> and the finding is still owed.
> Name it.

“Frost-feather,” he said to the stone. Then he said, “A feather on the well lip means warmth went out of that stone in the night, and somebody pays for the finding. There is no finding entered at this table, and no finding is the same as none.” He would not say more. A survey-man who has sat against the bare stones learns to give the figure and leave the meaning where it stood, which is a trade the Court understands better than any of us.

The fourth was put by the clay-man from the low road. He had been a fortnight above the shut and had taken to sitting with us because there was nowhere else warm, and he said his question to the room, which was not the manner of it, and one of the floor told him to say it to the stone. He said it to the stone then, and said it again here as the old man remembered it:

> A man goes down with a number on his arm
> and a stone in his fist.
> He comes up with the number on his arm
> and no stone in his fist.
> The arm tells the mother; the stone tells nobody.
> Which of the two is the man?

Nobody answered. The old woman said, “Ask the stone,” which is an answer given by people who have asked the same thing at their own mouths. The man from the Ninth said the number is the man, and the survey-man said that the numbers were correct, which is an answer to the numbers and not to the question, as any man will tell you who has asked a Vorr twice. The clay-man said, “Then you have not lost it,” and put his shaving on the sill and left it there. He went down the Rim Road in the thaw with the answer still owed, and I have never heard that he collected it.

The old man said he stayed after the others and put the fourth to the stone himself, low, with his hand on the table stone and his cap off. He told me he did not know whether the stone answered or whether he said the answer and heard it in his own mouth, which is the same thing after forty years and a difference before them. What he said was this:

“The number is the man's. The stone is the Keep's. The man is the third thing, and nobody writes it down.”

He said it to the stone and not to me. Then he got up and went out, and did not come back that night, and has not said the sentence to a man since, because a thing said to a man becomes a debt and he has no more shift to pay it with.

# What the Court hand did

A survey-clerk of the Court borrowed the leaf on the morning the survey table sat at the Keep, with ink settled in two fingers of a soft cuff. He laid his hand on the paper before his eyes went to the room, which is the manner of that table. He set the questions down as *queries entered* and the answers as *responses recorded*, valued each shaving at a half-mark of warm-stone, and wrote *gaming, not commercial* across the head of the tally, so that a night of asking came out as a night of trading with the profit left off. Against the fourth he wrote *no answer tendered; struck*. He asked who had put it. I told him a clay-man come up before the shut. He asked for the clay-man's name and I said the clay-man had given none and had gone down the Rim Road, which is a true thing and not the thing he wanted, and he wrote *party unidentified; interest in a number not entered*.

| In the woman's hand | In the Court hand |
|---|---|
| Three answers said to the stone | Three responses recorded |
| The fourth kept, not struck | No answer tendered; struck |
| Shavings counted by touch on the sill | Warm-stone, three half-marks, value to Keeping |
| A question said to a man becomes a debt | Gaming; not commercial |
| The lamp coal under the sill | Fire privately maintained; no public charge |
| The man, the number, the third thing | No account arises |

I have put his hand above mine and left mine standing under it, the way the table stone keeps the cold under the lamp and does not tell the lamp where it got the heat. He takes the verbs out because a thing always true has no need of them. I put them back because a man waiting for his turn at the weigh does not know he is hearing something always true, and wants to know which hand to hold the stone with.

# In the margin, in a clay-man's sloping hand

The leaf went down the Rim Road with the survey party and came back with a line in a sloped hand that is not mine and not the Court's. I have copied it as it stands.

> At *which of the two is the man*: I said it to the room and was told to say it to the stone. I said it to the stone and nobody answered. I have asked twice since, once at the Cross and once at the well that never freezes, and had a figure and a weather and no third thing. I am back over the pass and the answer is still owed. Do not strike it. A man who takes up his own shaving has paid for the asking and got nothing.

I have not answered him in the margin. A man writes that in a book and it follows him home.

# At the head and in the gutter

The coal smut at the head is mine; I wipe the lamp glass on my sleeve and the sleeve goes into the grain of the leaf and no wet cloth takes it out. The lead shut in the fold came off the cuff of the man up from the Ninth, who set his hand there while he was deciding whether to say the word *sweet* in a room where the mine-reeve was not, and did not say it. The filings in the gutter are off the clamps on the floor and will cut the next leaf if I do not fold it over. The frost-feather was on the sill the morning after the telling, pressed flat between the shaving and the stone; it was gone by noon and the place where it lay is cold yet. The tallow thumb is mine. The ring is not any man's; three men put a cup in it over one evening and not one will say which, which is how small ale is drunk in this room when there is a thing to say and no proper word for it. The grey along the outer fold came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying above a shaft mouth, and I have not swept it. In the clay country they sweep the corners on the Last Day and burn what they find in the morning and call it dealt with. Under the Keep the dust has nowhere to go.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> Two answers went home in men's mouths. One went down the Rim Road still owed.
> The stone was answered. The slate was not.

I have asked the survey-man, the woman who counts, and a man who has been forty years at the weigh, and had three answers and no hand, as I have had before. I have left the line where it was written. The leaf goes back into the tin under the sill and the wick goes back under its twist of oil. Tomorrow a boy will be brought down at thirteen and will give his number before his name, and the shaving will go on the sill, and the fourth will stand where it stood, with no figure and no taker, and the stone will keep it as it has kept every answer this room has ever said to it.
