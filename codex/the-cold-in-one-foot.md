I wade the shallows below the Weir and the Ferr at Stonemouth, and I take the leach out of the mud for the women at the Fishstairs. It is a poor trade and an honest one, which is more than I can say of most. A dozen dried fetch three bits and no questions, and the questions are the part that costs a man. I write this at the end of a wade with the water still in my boots and the tallow down to a knuckle, because a man forgets a cold foot once he is out of the river, and the forgetting is how he loses the count.

The grey at the head is silt and went into the fibre the first morning I set a leaf down on the bank. The brown along the gutter is iron out of the Ferr, and it stains a hand the colour of an old copper coin and does not wash off before the next wade. I have cleaned neither off. A book that has never been in the water was never used for anything.

# Where it sets

It comes in the low water, when the shallows smell of iron and mud and a man can see his own toes through the brown. In Ilmen the river is up and the leach is nowhere to be found; by Ember the water has gone down a hand's breadth and the Ferr comes off the Lead Run blue-grey, and they are there. They set against the ankle of a wader and against the legs of the eel-men at the Weir, which is why an eel-man will not stand still in one place to mend a trap. They are very near the colour of the silt, so that a man sees them only when he lifts his foot and looks at what he has brought up, and by then it has been a while.

They are thickest where the water comes through slowly and stands. I have waded the same reach of the lower Ilm three Grains running and taken nine one year and four the next and none the third, and I have not found the rule in it and do not pretend to. A man who writes a rule into a book he carries in his pocket is a man who will be asked for the use of it by somebody with a slate.

# What it is

It is a leach. I will set that down before anything else, because the blessing verse does not and because a thing a man can hold in his fingers should be called by the name he would use for it in a kitchen. It is the length of a thumbnail and the colour of wet mud, with a mouth at the head end that does not let go once it has set, and a ways of going along the bottom that I have watched and cannot describe without making it sound like something it is not. It does not swim. It reaches and holds and draws itself along, the way a wetted finger draws along a table.

I have opened four. In each I found mud and a little blood and a small knot the colour of tallow behind the mouth, and nothing with a name in it that I could set down. I have put the colour where I found it and have not written what the knot is for. A man who has taken leeches for nine winters knows when to stop opening them.

# The cold foot

It does not bite. A man feels nothing while it works, and that is the whole danger of it. It sets its mouth to a way near the skin and draws slowly, the way a collector's tube draws when the collector has all day, and a wader feels only a cold in one foot that does not go away when he leaves the water. I have kept that cold for a tend. I have known a man keep it for a month, and go to the physician about it, and be told there is nothing there to see.

It is the number that does the work. One leach on a grown man is a cold foot and an annoyance. A handful on a child is an hour, and at the end of the hour the child is cold all through and does not want anything. In Grain a woman came down to the Fishstairs with a child of two who had been set in the silt at the edge while she washed. There were six on the child's ankles. I took them off into a dish and salted the feet, which troubles a leach but does not give back what one has already taken. The child was warm when I lifted her. She was cold by the time we reached the steps, and she did not cry at the steps. I have not put her cost in the tally because there is no figure for a child and because a figure would make it look as though somebody had paid.

I have set the verse down below as it is said on the bank at the Blessing, with the line the women sing wrong left standing. They sing it wrong on purpose. A song that tells a man why a thing takes his warmth is a song he can carry home and set by his own fire and be done with.

# The verse

> Four for the shallows and one for the bank,
> it sets where the iron comes up through the silt;
> it does not bite like a dog and it does not bark,
> it keeps to the ankle and keeps to the warm.
>
> A cold in one foot and a cold that stays,
> a child in an hour and a fistful of them;
> count them going and count them back,
> and leave the fifth for the one we did not lift.
>
> Three bits a dozen and no name on the bag,
> a greyed hand for the man who lets the slate have them;
> we sing it gives to the river, and sing it wrong ---
> the gap is the foot that does not come home.

The gap is a foot. The women know it and sing it and have never once said whose foot it is. There is an eel-man at the Weir who says the fifth line twice and will not say why, and I have let him keep it.

# The tally

These are the wades I kept a true account of. There were others I did not write because my hands were full and one I did not write because I had drunk. I have set down both sorts as they stand. The last column is the clerk's, not mine; I counted what I took off, he entered what could be paid.

> *In the narrow upright hand of the factor's clerk, struck through the "Cold feet" column:* Leeches not a vessel. Cold in a foot: not entered. Warm in the shallows: no deduction arises. Account stands.

I have left his hand standing. A clerk's hand has its use: it keeps a man from arguing with what he cannot change. But the child of Grain 3 is in no column of his and in no column of mine, and the cold that stayed in my foot through the whole of Ember is written nowhere he can reach. I have put both in the margin below, where his ruler cannot go, and set my own mark beside them in charcoal.

> *Below, in my hand:* Six taken off. One child. No figure. The cold went on after the wade and I did not write it at the time because my hands were full. I write it now with the verb left in it.

# The dozen and the greyed hand

Dried leeches fetch three bits a dozen at the Fishstairs and nobody asks what they were taken from. This is a trade and I have never pretended it was a kindly one. I take them, I dry them on a strip of bark, I sell them, and the money goes into the house. What a man does with them after is not in my book.

But there is a use that is not mine to be nice about. A collector will buy them dried and set them on a wrist or a throat instead of iron and slate, and draw what he wants without entering it, and the law calls that theft and the fact calls it a greyed hand. I have seen the hand. It is the man who set the leeches who goes grey at the fingertips, not the man who sold them, which is a mercy of a kind and not one I like to think about on the walk home. I have sold four dozen in nine years and I have asked twice where they went and been told twice not to ask, and I have not asked a third time.

Salt does not stop the draw once it has begun. It only makes the foot smart, which a physician will tell you is a sign and a Fen trader will tell you is the cure. Both are paid for their answerings.

# What they say at the Blessing

They say at the Fish Blessing that the leach takes the warmth and gives it to the river, and that the river is honest with what it is given. I have put my hand in the water where a leach stood and where none stood, and the water is the same cold in both, and a river that had been given a hundred winters of warmth would not hide it so well. I have sung the line with the rest of them and I have left the sentence standing without a cause beside it. The women at the Fishstairs bless the traps in Ilmen and sell the leeches in Ember and will not hold both things in the same hand at the same time. I understand this better than I understand the leach.

> *In the pale ink of a hearth-maid, on the corner of a leaf gone soft where the river touched it:* It gives to the water, they say. I have laid a bowl of water by a child who was emptied and the water in the bowl was cold by morning, and so was the child, and I do not know which of them gave to which. Bless the trap. Say nothing at the selling.

I have put her line above my own. She wrote it at the Weir and I wrote mine in the mud below it, and neither of us has asked the leach anything.

# At the head and in the margins

The silt at the head is mine and off the bank at Stonemouth; it is grey here and brown when it dries, and it has gone into the grain the way the iron has into the gutter. The sedge across the first leaf came off the bank the morning the river came up a hand and I lay flat with the book under me and the eels gone. The nick at the fore-edge is a fish-hook, from the wade when I lost the hook and found it in the cover. The green in the lower corner was pressed there by a woman who leaned to see the tally with the sweetness of the glaze at one corner of her mouth; she did not say where it came from and I did not ask. The cuff-grey at the head is off a collector who set his slate on my leaf while he asked me a question about a wrist and took the slate up again without waiting for the answer. I have not wiped it off and I have not entered him. The singe on the third leaf is my own tallow end and the Ash print nobody will own has been there since Greybank. The clerk's ruling I have not touched. The verse is as the women sang it, with the wrong line standing and the gap the length they leave it.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The river is the road. It is not the house.

I asked an eel-man, a hearth-maid, a woman who washed at the Fishstairs, and a man with a grey hand, and had four answerings and no hand. I have left the line where it was written.

There is a reach of the Ferr above the Lead Run where the water comes down blue-grey and smells of iron even in Grain, and no eel-man sets a trap in it. Next Ember I will take a dish of salt and a coil of cord and wade it, and I will count them going and count them back, and if the cold stays in my foot for a tend after I have come out I will set that down too, in charcoal, with the cause left out and no figure at the end of the row.
