title: The Half Share, Being the Quay's Drinking Song for a Man Come Aboard
kind: A Vhaleni drinking song for the coming-aboard feast and the spring launching, taken down in Grathic letters from a mouth that has no word for guest; the verses on salt-paper squared at the edges, with three lines the damp has made void by the Council's own law, a factor's clerk's correction in Ledgerhand, a dipper's note in charcoal, and one verse in a hand nobody will own
provenance: Set down at Vhal on the Sallow coast in Ember of Y.H. 807 by Vessa of the Quay, a woman who held a boat-share from her mother, for the coming aboard of a man whose name she did not enter, because a name entered is a name held; carried up the Salt Road in Frost of Y.H. 810 in a dry tin and copied at Greybank onto the back of a spoiled harbour tally; copied forward in Stone of Y.H. 812 with the damp-void lines left void. It survives with a white salt crust along the gutter, a ring of red wine that has eaten one word clean through, a fibre of wet rope pressed into the second fold, a green bead of Glaze glass, a fish-hook nick at the head, and a thumb printed in salt where the writer signed, because a boat on this coast is signed in salt and not in ink. One correction is in the narrow upright hand of a factor's clerk who borrowed the sheet at Greybank; one note is in charcoal and is a dipper's; the verse at the foot is in a hand nobody will own.

I have set this down in the letters of the clay country, because that is the hand of the account that comes down the Salt Road and the factor at Greybank reads nothing else. The song itself is Vhaleni and loses something in the writing, the way a net loses what it was made to hold. There is no word in my language for *guest* and no word for *please*. I cannot ask you to drink. I set the cup on the bench and go away, and the cup does the asking. This is the whole of our hospitality, and it is why people upriver call us cold. They are welcome to think it. A man who has been called cold by a Grathic household has at least been noticed by one.

The song is not for a quiet room. It is sung at the coming aboard, when a man marries a boat-owning house and is given the half of a share he did not bring with him, and at the launching in Ember when the new hull goes down the ways and everybody's words are worth half of what they will be worth next year. The women sing the verses. The man come aboard answers the refrain at half voice, because his opinion on the boat counts at half and he has known it since the day he married; a man who cannot take a joke about his own share has no business aboard one. There is salt on the rim of every cup. A man who drinks the wine without the salt has drunk only wine, which is a Grathic habit and a poor one.

The tune goes like water going over water, low and without a rise, and it is sung through the nose. The last line is held until the ebb.

# The song

*The women. A cup set down. Salt on the rim.*

> The boat was laid before the daughter,
> the share went down the mother's line;
> a man may come aboard by marrying,
> but he does not steer by right of mine.

*The man come aboard answers, at half voice.*

> Half a share and a whole thirst;
> the boat goes where the women go,
> and the tide goes where it will.

*The women.*

> We count his word at half, not in spite,
> but because half is what he brought:
> a tongue, a debt, a pair of hands,
> and the boat was here before his word.
>
> Do not tell him it is kindness.
> He will spend it. He will enter it.
> Give him the half and call it half,
> and there is no argument in the room.

*The refrain. The man at half voice. Nobody crosses himself; nobody in Vhal ever has.*

> Half a share and a whole thirst;
> the boat goes where the women go,
> and the tide goes where it will.

*The women. This verse is sung to the cup, not to the man.*

> Put salt in it. The Sallow's in it.
> A man who drinks without the salt
> drinks only wine, and wine's a clay-country habit;
> we take the sea with everything.
>
> The sea is the colour of skimmed milk
> and it runs against the wind,
> and the dippers say it follows the Warm
> and not the moon. Let the moon go.
> Nobody has taxed the moon.

*Here three lines stood, and are gone. Rain got into the tin in Grain of Y.H. 810, between the coast pass and Greybank. I have left the place the length of them and written nothing in it. By the Council's own law a wet document is no document, so those lines are void, which is a virtue, says the Council, and I have not put them back. I remember two of them and have not written either. A verse remembered is not a verse kept, and I was not going to make the Council a present of one.*

*The women.*

> Write it on salt-paper. Let it rain.
> A contract that has rotted is no contract,
> which is the virtue of it, and half the fortunes
> of this port have turned on exactly that.
>
> A thing that lasts too long stops earning.
> A bench that will not rot has no business
> in a room where men are buying boats.
> Wet paper, clean hands, and a good ebb.
> That is the whole of our law and most of our religion.

*The refrain.*

> Half a share and a whole thirst;
> the boat goes where the women go,
> and the tide goes where it will.

*The women. This verse is sung at the launching, with the rope in the singer's hand.*

> At dawn the dippers trail the net
> and read the draw the Sallow makes;
> the Warm goes seaward under the hull
> and we go after it with salt.
>
> I walked the pans at fourteen barefoot
> and named a tide I had not seen;
> they gave me that name to keep
> for a thing I could not write down.
>
> The gull will steal a coal out of your fire
> and carry it upriver warm;
> the salt-thing taps the hull at midnight
> where the store of warmth lies thin.
> A boat that's warm at dusk and cold by morning
> has no slate to answer to. That's the trade.

*The women. The last verse before the refrain is said low, to the boat, while the rope goes over.*

> Our glass comes green off the Cliffs
> where the old lead sweetens in the heat;
> drink from it and you will taste the colour
> of a thing made beautiful by burning.
>
> Fill to the half. Drink it whole.
> The cup is not a guest. The cup has no name.
> If you want a passage, name a tide
> and we will say it has not happened yet.

*The refrain. The man come aboard answers at half voice; the women answer him at full, and the difference is the point of the song.*

> Half a share and a whole thirst;
> the boat goes where the women go,
> and the tide goes where it will.
>
> Come aboard. Come aboard.
> Bring nothing. It is already counted.

# How it is used

*Written small in the margin, in my own hand.*

- Sung standing. A sitting singer is a singer who has been bought.
- The man come aboard sings the refrain at half voice the first year. In the second year he may sing it at full if the women have voted it, which they never have while I have been singing.
- The salt goes on the rim before the wine goes in the cup. Done the other way it is a Grathic cup with salt on it, which is a thing nobody in this port would drink.
- At a launching the rope is held through the last verse and let go on *Come aboard*. A man who lets go early has paid for a boat that will not hold him.
- If a collector is in the room, sing the third verse and the law verse and skip the tide verse. He cannot enter the draw, and a verse he cannot enter makes him uneasy enough to drink. This is not a kindness. It is a manoeuvre.

# What the factor's clerk did

A clerk of the factor's house at Greybank borrowed the sheet on a wet morning and gave it back dry in one corner and damnable everywhere else. I have left his hand standing and mine under it, the way a boat keeps both the water's mark and the painter that holds it.

- At *the share went down the mother's line* he wrote above, *The share is entered to the female line, the male entrant taking a half interest.* Mine stands beneath. His is true in a language nobody on the Quay uses to describe a mother.
- At *we count his word at half* he set *the vote of the come-aboard is rated at one half* and underlined it twice, as though I had discovered a figure and might be proud of it.
- At *Write it on salt-paper. Let it rain* he wrote *Instrument voided by exposure to water* and added, in a smaller hand, *no value attaches.* He was quite right. He has made a living out of being quite right in a room full of wet paper.
- At *the Warm goes seaward under the hull* he wrote *The Sallow draw is a taxable current* and put a figure against it. The figure has not been paid by anybody in eleven years, which is the kind of figure the Court likes best.
- Where the three lines are gone he wrote *three lines lost, no entry* and then, underneath, *no claim can lie against a lost line, which is fortunate for all parties.* I have left it. He has taken the verbs out of a song and called the result an account, and if he had ever sung one he would know why we keep them in.

# In the dipper's hand

At the foot of the second leaf, in thick charcoal that has rubbed onto the fold, in the hand of a dipper who borrowed the sheet in the Council room when it smelled of tar and wet rope:

> At dawn we trail the net and read what the Sallow is doing. We tell the Council the figure that buys salt. The draw itself we keep. A current that is measured honestly is a current that gets taxed, and there is no virtue in being taxed honestly.

He did not sign it and did not take the sheet away. When I asked him why he had written it on a song he said a song is the only paper in Vhal that nobody thinks to read. I have left the charcoal where he put it and not washed the fold.

# At the head and in the gutter

The salt crust at the head and along the gutter came up out of the paper on the two nights the tin went damp, and has taken the ink off one word in the law verse; I have not written the word back, because a verse about rot with a word rotted out of it is in better order than I could have made it. The ring is red wine and has eaten through to the other side. The rope fibre in the second fold came off the launching rope in Ember and is wet when the weather is wet, which is a nuisance and a useful one, because it tells me when to keep the tin shut. The green bead is from a cup off the Cliffs and is not slip and not lead; it fell out of a girl's hair when she leaned over to see the writing, and she did not ask for it back. The nick at the head came off a fish-hook set down in a hurry by a man who was paying me and did not apologize. The salt thumb is mine. A boat on this coast is signed in salt, because ink will not stay on a wet hand and a wet hand is the only kind we have.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> A boat is a pot with its crack below the water.
> The sea does not knock. The sea has no knuckle.

I asked the women, the dippers, a factor come up from Greybank and a man who had been come aboard for nine years, and had four answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The sheet goes back in the tin and the tin goes in the drawer where we keep the paper we mean to let rot. A slate is a thing that gets read in a clean voice; this is a thing that gets sung with salt on the rim and goes damp at the fold, and is the better for it. If the clerk borrows it again, let him take the verbs out. The next singer will put the salt back, as I did, and the cup will do the asking.
