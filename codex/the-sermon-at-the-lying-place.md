I keep the far dry for my father, who keeps the peat-dries for the reeve, and I write because a stick holds a notch and not a sentence. Fen Cant is not written. What cannot go into Grathic I have tied in the margin in grey wool, and I have not untied any of it. Nobody asked me for this leaf. Bogga came out on the dry in the fourth morning of Reed with her skirts to the knee and lay down in the hollow between the two turf pegs, and the rest of us lay down because she did, and I lay with my ear in the peat and my cheek against the cold, hearing her through the ground better than I heard her through the air. That is how the sermon is meant to be heard. I have set down what came up through the mud.

The brown at the head is off my thumb. I held the warrant down in a wind off the black peat and did not know I had marked it. Peat goes into the grain and stays there, which is why I sign with a thumb and not with a name.

# The manner of the lying, as the dry actually keeps it

*Written small, from watching, not from any order the Court has printed.*

- The shrine is a pressing in the black peat between two turf pegs. There is no stone. Do not set one there; a stone is a thing a man from Greybank can count, and this hollow has never been counted.
- Come before the pot at the end of the stand is lit. A prayer said over a lit pot is said to the fire, and the fire has enough to do.
- Lie face down with the ear to the ground and the nose shut. Say the words through the nose, and let the last word go down into the peat instead of up into the air. A word that goes up is heard by the quay-crier and the man with the slate. A word that goes down is heard by whoever is lying beside you.
- Nobody kneels. A knee on peat is a man pretending to be a board.
- If the bog-cow comes across the dry, stop and let her stand. She keeps a count of her own and will not give it up.
- Answer twice: once into the peat, once into the stick under the loose board. The peat has no board and asks no man to lift one.
- When the clerk comes to weigh the winter turf, roll over and give him the weather. A figure given while lying down comes up standing and walks off.
- The Court has five days that belong to no month. The dry has the soak and the skin, which come without a crier and are entered nowhere.

# The sermon, as she said it

## Of the face down

A girl who had brought her first bundle back wet asked why we lay down at all, and Bogga put her flat palm into the hollow and left it there until the water came up round the creases of her hand.

“Look,” she said. “You have set your hand on a loan. It will be called in. Build on it at your peril.”

She lay back down before anyone could answer her.

“A man standing up is a man with an opinion,” she said into the peat, “and an opinion in the Fen is a thing that can be bought. Face down you have no opinion, only an ear. She does not want your eyes. She has mud of her own. She wants what you carry low, at the belly and the small of the back, where nobody has ever thought to set a slate.”

She keeps it warm and hands it back. That is the whole of it. A priest come up from Greybank told us the Hearth keeps what it is given and that nothing is lost that we have kept. I have lain on this hollow eleven winters and have never once been handed back the same warmth I put down. It comes back as a dry shelf under a sod, or a child that breathes, or a pot warm at the second hour. I have never seen it come back as a figure. That is why we use it.

## Of the thing entered

“The Court writes on slate and calls it kept,” she said. “We lie on peat and call it lent. Both of us are right about ourselves, which is the worst kind of right. Ask the slate how much warmth it has handed back and it will tell you a figure. Ask the peat and it will tell you nothing, and in the spring the shelf is dry where you put your face.

“There is a difference between a thing kept and a thing entered. A thing entered can be carried away in a cuff. A thing kept under a sod has to be lain on before it can be found. The marsh keeps what is put into it. It does not call the keeping a kindness, and it does not send a collector.”

There is grey gathered here in jars with no mark on any of them, in the charge of him who keeps the dry for the reeve. The Court will not license it, which is the reason there is no mark. A man who asks what it is gets the weather and a date, and is no wiser when he goes up the marsh road.

## Of the name

“Say it into the ground,” she said. “Say it three times, because the third is for the child who cannot yet say it herself. A name said into peat goes where no slate can follow. A number written on a slate goes where the child cannot follow, which is a different direction and a colder one.

“If the man with the tube comes before you have said it, let him have the figure. It has his hand on it already. Say the word afterward, into the same piece of ground, and tie a knot in the wool for the part that will not go into Grathic. Do not make the cross. That sign belongs to the women at the flower-shrine and not to this hollow, and I have never known whose permission would make it mine.”

Here she pressed her palm flat and did not cross two fingers, and I have written the not-doing because it took longer.

## Of them that come back

“A thin one coming back into a Fen house is a child the house can keep without buying,” she said. “That is a mercy with no payer standing under it, and I will not have a cleaner word put in its place. Ask for a whole one and somebody down the road has paid the difference, and the difference is cold, and it is paid here more often than it is paid where the figure is read.

“We do not house the returned in this country. A thing that comes back to the water comes back to the water and not to a cradle. Elsewhere they pour one into one and get a name with the handle of the cup and the price of bread. I have seen what comes back when many are poured into one. It goes toward a cup before the cup has been shown. We say the same words over it that we say over the thin one, because a prayer is not a contract and there is nobody here to pay for either.”

# The lying-song

*Said face down, the answer into the peat and not into the air. There is an answer held after the second stanza. I have left it held the length it is held, which is the length of a breath kept under water. Nobody fills it.*

> Four for the fingers and one for the thumb,
> the peat says warm and the water says come;
> press the face and let the word go down —
> what the dry keeps is not entered in town.
>
> Four for the posts and one for the loan,
> the reed says wet and the peat says our own;
> say it to the ground where the child cannot speak,
> and tie it in wool where the clerk cannot seek.
>
> *Here the answer is held. No sound. The length of a breath under water.*
>
> Four for the pressings and one left out,
> the count goes in and the count comes about;
> ask the mud what it kept — it will not say,
> but the shelf is dry where your face lay.

A child asked what the held answer was. Bogga told her to put her ear to the ground and find out, which is an answer given by people who asked the same thing at their own first cutting. The clerk set a round nought against the silence and wrote *no observance at this station*. A nought is a thing a man can carry in a cuff. The silence has to be lain on.

# The pressings, as the stick keeps them

*Set down where they stood. A figure that comes into this dry in a coat leaves nothing on the board.*

| Pressing | What was laid down | What she said over it | What was entered |
|---|---|---|---|
| First, between the turf pegs | A hand pressed flat until the water came up round it | You have set your hand on a loan | Nothing |
| Second, at the pot's second hour | A warmed stone set on the shelf and not on the peat | It comes back and is not the same | Nothing |
| Third, in the rain | A name said three times into the ground | The third is for the child who cannot say it | Nothing; a knot in the wool under the board |
| Under the loose board | The notched stick in oiled sacking | A notch cannot be asked what it meant | Nothing |

There were pressings before these that my father could set a winter to, and I have not written the winters. A row of winters is a thing a clerk can carry away, and a notch under a board is not.

# What the clerk did

A factor's clerk come out from Greybank to weigh the winter turf borrowed the leaf while the pot was at its second hour, with ink in two fingers of a cuff gone soft from washing. He gave it back with the verbs taken out, as though a verb were a thing a man could carry off in a sleeve and sell at the next crossing.

- Against *she keeps it warm and hands it back*, he wrote *the measure is banked in the peat; no entry arising*. He has taken the handing back out of it, which is the only part a man lying on the dry has any use for.
- Against *a thing entered can be carried away in a cuff*, he wrote *records are transferable*. This is true in his room and has never been true of a sentence said through the nose.
- Against the name said into the ground, he wrote *name not entered; figure supplied on application*. The damp has taken *on application* and left the rest standing, which is the sentence the Court would like to be true everywhere.
- Against the held answer, the round nought and *no observance at this station*.

He asked what a Fen learned by lying face down. I told him how many pressings there were and which way the ground gives under a boot. He entered both and went up the marsh road, and the ink in his cuff had gone brown before he reached it. I have put the verbs back and left his hand above mine, as the dry leaves the grey where the boot raised it. He takes the verbs out because a thing always true has no need of them. I put them back because a woman with her ear in the mud wants to know who is doing the handing back.

# At the head and in the folds

The peat at the head is mine and will not wash out of grain this soft. The tideline is bog-water and has taken two words clean off the second leaf; I have not written them back, because a sentence with two words gone is a sentence said slowly, and everything at this dry is said slowly. The reed across the third leaf came in on the morning the dry stood under water and dried where it lay. The grey in the gutter came off a cuff and nobody will own it; I have not swept it, and there is no Last Day at a peat-dry. The hair in the fold is off the bog-cow and my father will not name which cow, which is a way of naming her. The chip of black peat in the lower corner is warm when I open the leaf of a morning and cold by noon, and has never been entered. The knot through the hole in the first leaf is mine, tied for the word that will not go into Grathic. I will not untie it.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

Struck through once and written again underneath, very small:

> What is put down is not what comes up.
> What is written once is carried twice.
> The dry has no door and no roll, and a body can lie in it.
> The held answer is the length of a breath, and nobody has entered the breath.

I asked Bogga, my father, a mud-wife come up from Reedholt and the clerk, and had four answers and no hand. I have left the line struck through and standing, which is the only honest way to keep a sentence nobody will sign.

The leaf goes back under the loose board in oiled sacking with the stick. Next Reed the cutters will come out before the pot is lit, lie face down between the two turf pegs and say it into the peat, and the child who asks what the held answer is will be told to put her ear to the ground and find out. The clerk's figure will stand brown above my words and the knot will hold beneath them, and the black peat will give under the boot and come back warm under a grey skin, which is the only answer this shrine has ever given and has never once been entered.
