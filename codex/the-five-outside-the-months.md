I keep the slate of the Five in the drawer under this counter, between the bell-cord and the tallow end, and take it down once a year when the months leave off and the days have names instead of numbers. Five days, and they belong to no month and no tend. A man comes in during them wanting a figure, and I give him the day's name instead, which is the only thing this room has handy. The ordinance is in Ledgerhand, with nobody in any of its sentences. I put the people back underneath, because a holy day with nobody in it is a day nobody kept, and that is the kind the Keeping has always liked best.

> *At the head of the first leaf, written very small, in mine:* Copied for the counter and from the drawer. The verbs are mine and the absence of them is not. Let both stand, as the fire that comes into a house and the fire the house says it lit have stood together in every hearth in Grath.

# Title the Third, as it stands in the Court's room

*From the Ordinances of the Keeping, Title the Third: Of the Five Days Outside the Months, their Observance and what is Entered Therein. Set down in the first years of the Keeping; confirmed to the factor at Greybank by the treaty of Y.H. 418; added to after the Rising of Y.H. 668, in Y.H. 782 and in the Thin Year, Y.H. 812.*

## I. Of the days and their number

1. A year has twelve months of thirty days and five days that belong to no month. The five are told after Stone and are named Kindling, Weighing, the Long Night, the Remembering and the Last Day, in that order and in no other.
2. No day is added to the count to make the sky agree with the slate. The Court teaches that the Warm holds the seasons in their places.
3. A tend has ten days and a month has three tends. The Five break the tend. On Ilmen 1 the count begins again at Firstday.
4. No market is held in the Five and no claim is taken and no account is opened. What would have been entered is carried forward into the Opening of the Slates.

> *Margin:* A surveyor come up from Keld stood here in the Thin Year with snow on his shoulders and said that the thaw had come in Ilmen for eight hundred years by the Keld count and the Court's slate had never once had to agree with it. He said it while the factor's man was at the trough; when the man came back the surveyor turned his slate face down and asked after the road. I have entered neither the snow nor the eight hundred years. A clerk who enters a surveyor has begun to write a map, and maps get carried away.

## II. Of Kindling

5. On Kindling every hearth in Sarn is lit from the Hearth-flame. The fire comes down the road under a covered pot, from hall to hall, and from this room to the houses of the low bank last of all. No priest touches the grating. The hand that carries is not the hand that kindles, and neither is entered.
6. Collectors take the first measure of the year's roll on Kindling, before light, from the dying and from the newly born on the same day. The figure is copied into the roll in a clean hand and is not read aloud until Weighing.
7. A hearth lit from another hearth without the fire having been carried into it is entered as carried and not as lit. The house is not asked which it believes.

> *Margin:* A woman came up from the low bank with a covered pot under her apron and asked whether the fire in it was still the Hall's after three doors. I told her the ordinance asks where a fire was lit and not where it has been. She covered the pot again and went down the yard with it, and I have no article for the three doors.

> *Margin:* I carried the pot myself on Kindling in a year when the road was ice. It warms the wrist through the cloth, and then it does not, and a man goes on carrying it because the house at the end of the yard is waiting. There is no column for the carrying. I have set the wrist here instead, where no Weighing reads it.

## III. Of Weighing

8. On Weighing the year's measures are read aloud from the clean roll in the Weighing Hall, in a voice that does not hurry. The figure printed on the notice is one in twelve, as the notice says. There is no other figure in the room.
9. The reading is given twice: once for those within the Hall and once for those who came up the road too late to get into it. The second reading is the same figure and is not corrected for the lateness.
10. A whole return may be announced for a person who is not in the room. The reader does not ask where the person is. The College keeps a second file for the answers to questions of that kind, and the second file is not read aloud.

> *Margin:* I heard a figure in the yards of the low bank that will not agree with this one. I have no slate for it and no standing to weigh it, and have not entered it. A man from the College struck the word *heard* from the line above and set *a figure is current* over it, which is true of the notice and false of the yard. I have put *heard* back underneath and left his above, where a man must lean to read both.

> *Margin:* A factor come up from Greybank said a figure read aloud is a kindness and a figure paid is an account, and a man who mixes the two has begun to write a sermon. I have set his sentence down outside the article, where sermons are allowed to go. He did not know he had said it, and went away with a cup in his hand.

## IV. Of the Long Night

11. On the Long Night no fire is lit after dusk. Fires are banked and hearths covered; no light is carried between houses. What is spent in the night is not entered in the morning.
12. The stories of the Return are told in the dark. What is said in the dark is not entered, and the telling of it is not a claim upon any house.
13. No collection is made between dusk and morning and no slate is opened by firelight.

> *Margin:* In the Long Night of the Thin Year a woman sat in the low bank with a pot between her and the door and would not say what she had set there. I have no article for a pot in the dark and no column for the length of time she kept her hand on the lid. Both stayed where they were when the light came.

> *Margin:* The bell at the Cross goes four, four and a gap on an Account-day and nobody crosses himself. In the Five there is no market and the bell is not rung at all, which is the one account in this year that kept its own silence without a clerk to enter it.

## V. Of the Remembering

14. On the Remembering the names of the dead are spoken in the order of the roll. The roll is carried forward and never closed. A name spoken is not a return entered, and a return entered is not a name brought back.
15. Where a whole return is announced, the reader gives the figure and the name together in a clean voice. Where a household disputes the name, the figure stands and the name is not changed.
16. No article provides for a name that a household will not have spoken.

> *Margin:* I have carried one name forward for eleven years after the house that gave it was empty of people. Nobody came to close it and I did not close it. A name carried forward sits lighter on the slate and heavier on the counter, which is a fact about counters and not an entry.

> *Margin:* A woman stood here on the Remembering with her thumb at the middle of her palm where a ring had once been worn tight and long. She asked whether the dead came back whole. I read her the figure the notice gives, which is the only figure I can read in this room. She went away with her thumb still on the place. There is no line in Title the Third for a place on a palm.

## VI. Of the Last Day

17. On the Last Day bread is baked in every house and nothing is swept from the corners before the day is out. The grey that gathers there is left where it gathered and is not called dirt in the hearing of a child.
18. On the morning after, the grey is swept up and burnt and called dealt with. Where the factor has licensed the gathering, what is bottled and sealed is done before the burning and is entered to the licence and not to the house.
19. A pot is broken at the door of a death-house to let the heat out. The breaking is not entered and the pot is not counted among the household's goods afterward.
20. What is given at a house door in the Five is given and not entered, which is the same rule as in the months and more necessary here, where a man has no figure to defend himself with.

> *Margin, scored with the edge of a bread crust:* A woman laid a crust here while she told me where her child had been found and would not say the name of the house. I have left the grease where it fell. The brown has eaten two letters of the line beneath without taking the sense. A crust keeps the place where it was set down, which is more than this article does.

> *Margin:* A man asked whether the pot broken at a death-house was paid for out of the household's measure. I told him the breaking was not entered. He said that was an answer with nobody in it, which is the only kind this counter gives. He asked a second time and I gave him the day's name, which has nothing in it and is therefore safe.

## VII. The table of the days

*The third column is the ordinance. The last is mine and has no standing at any Weighing.*

| Day | What is done | As the ordinance has it | What is not entered |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kindling | Hearth lit from the carried fire; first measure taken | Fire carried under a pot; figure copied in a clean hand | The three doors the pot came through; the wrist that carried it |
| Weighing | Year's measures read; whole returns announced | One in twelve, as the notice says; read twice | The figure heard in the yards; where the absent person is |
| the Long Night | Fires banked; stories told in the dark | No fire after dusk; no slate by firelight | The pot at the door; the hand upon the lid |
| the Remembering | Names spoken in the order of the roll | Roll carried forward and never closed | The thumb at the palm; what the house would not have spoken |
| the Last Day | Bread baked; corners left; pot broken | Grey swept and burnt on the morning after | The crust laid here; the giving at the door |

> *Margin:* I drew the last column and a College clerk struck it out, writing *No such column* above in a hand so upright it hurts the eye. I have struck his striking out and left the column where I drew it. A place is not a debt. The College has never learned the difference, though it teaches eleven words for a deduction to its own.

## VIII. The form of the year's reading

*Set out at the end of Title the Third, with the blanks as they stand. In nine years nobody has filled the last line in the hand this room requires.*

> Entered this day, being ------- of the Five, in the year of the Hearth ------, the fire carried into this room under a covered pot and out to the houses of ---------------------- before dusk on Kindling; the year's measures read in a clean voice at the Weighing, one in twelve as the notice says, given twice; the whole returns announced for ----------------------, who is not in the room; the fires banked before dark on the Long Night; the names of the dead spoken on the Remembering in the order of the roll, which is not closed; the bread baked on the Last Day and the grey left standing in the corners until the morning after; the pot broken at the door of ---------------------- and not entered. Entered by ----------------------, who was not at the Hall when the figure was read.

# The carrying-verse

*Said low under the covered pot on Kindling, before the fire has crossed the yard. Not at the Weighing and not while the collector's tube is out of its wool. The last line is said to the pot and not to the person who carries it.*

> Five days and no month to their name,
> a fire goes out and a fire comes home;
> the figure is read and the figure is paid,
> and the corner keeps what the broom has stayed.
>
> Kindling gives and the Weighing tells,
> the Long Night keeps what the dark keeps well;
> the Remembering says what the roll forgot,
> and the Last Day leaves what the morning burnt.
>
> Four in the bell and a gap where the fifth should be ---
> that's for the market, not for these;
> a pot keeps warm what the Hall has given,
> and a house keeps warm what the pot has known.

> *In a narrow upright hand, against the last line:* One observance entered; no deduction arises. *In mine:* He has put a figure where I put a house. A house that keeps warm what a pot has known can be asked about; an observance cannot. Both stand.

# What the two borrowed hands did

The College man came down from the Hearthseat with ink in two fingers and a cuff gone soft from washing. He struck *the fire comes down the road* and set *the fire is transmitted* above, as though a pot could go from hall to hall without a wrist under it. I have put the road back underneath. On the Remembering he struck *the names of the dead are spoken* and set *the roll is recited* above, which leaves the room standing with nobody in it and nobody to say the name. Both stand, as they stood before.

The other hand is a factor's from Greybank, leaning and brown at the edge. Against the scorch he wrote *fire received in good order* and against the verse *annual observance, current; no charge to the district*. I have left both. A factor may call a fire received because a fire is a thing that can be entered and handed on; a woman who has carried a pot over ice calls it something else and has no word for it that this room will take.

# At the head and in the gutter

The tallow at the head is mine, from holding the lamp low while a woman told me which door the pot had come through and I did not want her to see me writing it down. The grey in the outer fold is Ash, off my own cuff going down to the Cross on an Account-day, where I put my hand into a corner I had no business putting into; it will not come out of the creases. The scorch on the first leaf is where a covered pot was set down while I read the Kindling article, and has not spread. The bead in the lower corner is lead glaze, not slip; a girl come up from High Kiln with the sweetness still at her mouth leaned to see the form of entry, touched the leaf and went back up the terrace without asking. The curl of grey wool in the sewing is not mine and I have not untied it. The cold-iron scale came off the bell-cord hook when I took the slate down. The tideline along the foot is the damp that comes up from the flagstones in a wet Five and takes the corners off everything left in this drawer. On the Last Day the houses burn their corners and call it dealt with. This drawer is not a house and keeps what is left in it.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The fire goes out from the Hall and comes back to every hearth.
> The figure goes out from the Hall and comes back to no household the same.

I asked a keeper of the grating, a woman who carried a pot up the yard on Kindling, and a clerk come down from Greybank, and had three answers and no hand, as I have had before. I have left the lines where they were written.

The slate goes back into the drawer under the bell-cord. Next year the months will leave off on the thirtieth of Stone and the five days will come with names and no tend-days among them, and I shall take this down and read what stands, and put the verbs back where another hand has taken them out. The fire will come in under a covered pot and go out to the low bank. The grey will stay in the corners until the morning after and somebody will call it dealt with. The roll will stay open. That is the whole of my trade here: to write down what was done, and leave a place the length of what was not.
