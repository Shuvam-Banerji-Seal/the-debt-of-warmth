I keep the rail at the edge of the bog, where the mud road comes down and the peat is cut into blocks and stacked to go south. The factor at Greybank sends me a figure and a tube and a year, and I stand between the reeds and the road and put a number on whatever passes: eels, peat, hemp, ash in jars, and people who have been made thin. The figure is cried at the rail and entered in this book, and the two are not the same thing. I have learned to touch the slate before I look at the person standing, because a person looked at first will give a different figure.

Twelve copper bits to a silver mark; twenty marks to a gold crown, a Sere coin that does not come back north. The Court books a measure in embers and calls an ember five marks, which no man has exchanged and no carrier has ever carried. A thin return is bought here for two marks and sold in the Bowl for a figure nobody at this rail is allowed to hear. A man come down from Keld told me a bought life up there is reckoned at eleven months, which is a term and not a price. I have set it in the margin where it cannot be added: add a term to a mark and you get a number with nobody in it, which is the only kind the College will sign.

One in twelve is printed on the notice at Greybank and is read twice. I have put it on this leaf once, in the place the College allows, and put the other figure in the remark, which the College struck out and which I have drawn back underneath. A column that is struck is not a column that is gone. There is a difference between taking a figure and entering one, and it fits in the width of a thumb.

> *In the narrow upright hand of a College clerk, above my second paragraph:* Figures cried; figures entered; the two not joined. *Underneath, in mine:* They are joined in the person standing, by the same mouth and in the same morning. I have set them on two lines and left them facing one another.

# The book, as I set it down

*Figures in Ledgerhand. The cried figure is what was said at the rail; the entered figure is what went into the factor's book. The remark is mine. A dash is a place where nothing stood, not a nought; a nought is a figure and can be added, and a silence cannot.*

Rows on this leaf: twelve. Rows that agree: three. Rows I left blank: two. Rows struck: two. The three that agree are the peat, the jar and the pot; I have marked them and cannot say why. They are not the whole of the leaf.

# What the rail will not carry

There is a boy who lifts the seventh basket and puts six back into the water and says nothing, and I have no row for the eel that goes back over the rail. A thing that goes back is not a thing never taken; it was counted and returned to a place where no count follows. I have set that in the remark and not the column, which is where this book keeps what matters and nothing that can be added.

The ash in the jar has a figure because a man in the south will pay one, and none at all at the post where it was dug. Grey Ash is swept up the morning after the Last Day, burnt and called dealt with; Fen ash is corked, sold and called a remedy. They are the same colour. I have put one mark against the jar and none against the colour; a man who joined the two would be drawing a conclusion, which I have no licence to draw.

A man who comes back thin has the old numbers cut out and new ones put in; the smooth place on his arm is not a blank but a thing that happened. I have kept a space the width of it in the row for the woman asking after her table and written nothing into it, because a figure put where a person stood is how this trade makes a man disappear and keeps the account tidy.

# The counting song of the Line

*Sung low by the carriers going south with the baskets on the pole, never at the rail and never while a figure is being entered. I copied it from a man who would not sing the middle verse while the factor's clerk stood on the road, which is how I know the middle verse is the one that matters.*

> Eels in the basket, six and a say-so,
> the seventh goes back where the rail don't see;
> cried at the post and entered at the boat,
> and the difference walks home ahead of me.
>
> Eleven months for a man down south,
> twelve bits to the mark and a mark to the crown;
> one in twelve on the board, says the man,
> and the other one goes up in his box, not down.
>
> A brass seam sings and a green seam lies,
> the peat keeps warm what the figure pays;
> count what you can at the edge of the bog
> and leave the rest where the reed stays.

I have put no gloss on it. A man who explains a song has begun to write a sermon, and sermons get copied.

# What the borrowed hands did

The College clerk came down from Greybank with a clean cuff and took the leaf up with two fingers, the way a man takes up a thing that might be wet. He struck the whole of my note column and wrote *No such column* above it, having first counted every row, which is how I know the column troubled him. I have drawn it back underneath and left his words standing over it.

He struck *I entered the figure and did not enter the arm* and set above it *the measure was recorded*, and above that *the entry was made*, which is further from the woman standing than the first. I have put my words back beneath both. A sentence with nobody in it is a sentence nobody can ask about, and people ask about these entries in the end, always at the rail, always after the slate is turned face down.

The other hand is the factor's. Against the woman of the Empty Cantons he struck my two marks and set one above, saying a thin return from the Cantons cannot carry two; then, underneath, he restored the two in his own small figures and wrote *current* beside it. Both stand. I have asked which is right and neither hand has told me, and I have stopped asking, because an assessor who asks after a correction is an assessor who will soon be making one.

A reeve of the Post set a mark in charcoal against the hemp row, a short stroke through the knot, which is the reeve's way of saying that the stick was read and not carried off. It is the only hand on this leaf that has put a person into the sentence, and it is not a Court hand and will not travel with the book.

# In the margins and the gutter

The black in the gutter is peat off the hand that writes, and it goes into the grain and no wet sleeve takes it out, which is why this book keeps its figures on slate and its accounts on paper nobody wants. The slime along the fore-edge came off the seventh basket; the boy who lifted it set the slate down on the wet rail while I was entering six and did not know he had marked it. The white at the head comes and goes with the weather, as salt does on this coast; it has taken one word off the fifth remark and left the figure standing, which is the rude justice of salt. The green bead in the lower corner is Cliffs glass and not Fen glass; a woman come up from Vhal leaned to see what her jar of ash had fetched, touched the leaf, said nothing, and went back to the road. I have not washed it off.

The fish-scale in the fold came off the tribute basket. The frost-feather was on the rail on Bone 26 and gone before I folded the leaf; the yard at Mud End calls that weather and the carriers a map of where the Warm has gone out of the bog. The tallow thumb at the foot is mine, from holding the lamp low while the woman with the changed number asked her question. The pale round ring is where a collector from Sill set the cold tube down and took it up; he did not ask what this rail was counting, which was a kindness of a kind. The wet corner is the rain of Bone 25; two figures ran and I entered them where they ran, because a figure that has run is still a figure and the factor's book has no row for rain. The bare square is a word I scraped away and have not written back. The grey along the outer fold is not mine, not the clerk's, not the reeve's. I have not swept it. There is a Last Day in the houses up the mud road and there is none at the edge of the bog, and even here the morning comes.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> What is counted at the rail is not what is carried from it, and what is carried is not what the south pays for. The three never stand in one room, and the room they stand in has no door.

I asked the factor's clerk, a carrier who has gone down with the eels these nine years, a reeve come up from the dry boards, and the woman with the changed number, and had four answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written.

The book goes back into the tin and the tin into the count-house at Greybank, where a man who has never stood in this bog will read the figures in a voice nobody can thank and nobody can blame. Tomorrow the baskets go south and the seventh will hold six, and I shall touch the slate before I look at whoever is standing, and write the remark down before the College can strike it out. The eels go under in the dark. The rail stands where it has always stood, at the edge of the country that empties, and the difference between the figure cried and the figure paid walks home ahead of me with no figure of its own.
