I knock a pot before I set it down. That is the first thing a potter's daughter learns and the last thing I have managed not to unlearn. In Ember of the Thin Year I took a cart of flat tile and fish-dishes up the Salt Road to Vhal, eighty leagues with a team that was not mine, and came back in Grain with a sack of Sallow salt, a coil of tarred rope, a green bead of cliff-glass given for a bit and a promise, and a contract that had gone damp on the road and was therefore no contract at all. What I saw and what I was told have not agreed, and I have not made them agree. A woman who mends pots for a living knows that two edges may be brought near and still not be the same edge.

# The road, and the dry man

Eighteen leagues of Clay Road to Greybank with the tile riding high, and then the coast pass, and then the salt country begins under the wheels before it shows in the air. At Greybank a man with a slate asked what I had brought before he asked who I was, which is a fairness of a kind, and wrote the word *tile* as though a word were a thing a man could weigh.

Below the pass the clay gives way to reeds. At the Standing Post I was put up on the boards and was not told which boards would bear me, and paid a bit for asking, which the reeve called a fine and I have entered as tuition. They say a dry man is a dangerous man. I was dry. They lodged me accordingly, on boards that moved after the rain, and gave me eel and no answer.

Beyond the Peat Line the villages stand empty in the middle of the day, with squares of bare clay in the doorways where house-rolls once hung. I asked a guide whether the people had gone north or south. He said they had gone where the account went, and would not say which direction that was. I have set the answer down and not argued with it. A man of Sill would have named a village. This one named a thing with no road under it.

# The sea that runs against the wind

The Sallow is the colour of skimmed milk. The first thing a clay person notices about it is that it does not behave like water. I stood on the Quay at the third hour with my coat held and watched the water go one way and the wind go the other, and neither of them asked leave of the other. They say the tide follows the Warm and not the moon. I have put that where the sayings go and not under a figure, because I have no instrument for the moon and do not pretend to one.

I asked a dipper why the Sallow was said to keep a Hearth of its own beneath the salt and the Court's fire to be only its chimney. He said that if I had come eighty leagues to be told where a Hearth stood I had come the wrong way, and went on trailing his net. A woman at the pans told me the same thing with a rake in her hand, and added that no man had ever taken her down to see which way the door of it opened. I have left both sayings where they were said. A saying is a thing a man carries in his mouth, and a mouth is a vessel that leaks, which is why the dippers write theirs in salt and let the sun have them.

# The four questions

Vhalen has no word for a guest and no word for a friend. It has a word for a man whose account is current, and that is what I was called before I paid, while I paid, and after I had paid. They say a stranger's coin warms before his face. My coin was cold in my purse, and nobody looked at my face overmuch.

Four questions I asked in four rooms, and had one answer in four mouths.

| What I asked | What was said to me | What I entered |
|---|---|---|
| May I lodge here tonight? | *Current.* | A bed, paid. |
| Who holds the debt on this boat? | *A twelfth goes through the mother.* | Nothing. I could not find the woman to ask her. |
| When must it be paid? | *When it rains.* | No date. A date is a thing a man can be held to. |
| What if the paper is lost? | *Then it is a virtue.* | Left standing, with the others. |

The last is the answer I have carried home. In Vhal a contract written on salt-paper that rots in the damp is no contract, and they call that a virtue. A law that lasts too long is a law that has stopped earning. I have carried paper up the Clay Road that was written to outlast a man and be read at the Weighing, and I have never once heard anybody call *that* virtuous. Both kinds of paper can let a man go. Only one of them lets him go with the witnesses gone too.

# The benches and the twelfth

At the Quay I saw the pulled benches of the salt galleys, where condemned men row seven years or until their hands give out, whichever comes first. A girl at the rope-rack said a Vhaleni hull is never long from the oars. She said it the way my mother said the kiln will have its wood, and I have written it in her words and not in mine.

Boat shares pass through mothers. A man who marries into a boat-owning family is said to be come aboard, and his opinion on the boat is counted at half, which every woman on that Quay told me as though it were a joke told against themselves and not a rule of the house. Slaves may be bought in Vhal and cannot be returned there: the Council will not pay for a thin return and will not house one. A man in a clean coat came down from Greybank asking after such a return and went away with his question still in his coat, which was the driest thing I saw in a wet country.

# What the cart carried and what came back

*Figures as they were given at the third hour, set with what I entered at home. The left column is the figure a woman carries. The right is the figure she brings back, which is never the same figure and is never the same coin.*

| Article | Entered at Sill | Given at the Quay | What I set beside it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flat tile, a score | 8 bits | 1 mark and a curse at the weight | The curse was honest. The weight came home in my shoulder. |
| Fish-dish, green glaze | 4 bits | not taken | A country with fish has dishes. |
| Porringer, coarse | 6 bits | 9 bits | I knocked it and it answered full. He paid as though a pot had promised him something. |
| Salt, Sallow, a sack | 3 marks at the Quay | — | It rained on the road. The salt did not mind; the paper did. |
| Rope, tarred, a coil | 2 marks | — | The tar came home on my hands and is in the seam of this book still. |
| Green bead, cliff-glass | given for a bit and a promise | — | The promise is not current. |
| A contract on salt-paper | entered at 1 mark at the tide-tally house | void | The clerk wrote *instrument void by exposure; no claim lies*. I have kept the strip. |

Twelve bits to the silver mark and twenty silver marks to the crown. The Court books an ember at five marks, and no man I met had exchanged one, north or south. I left the embers out of the table. A row with nothing in it is a thing no clerk can take off a woman, which is more than I can say for the other rows.

# The net-song at first light

*Taken down at the west pan while the dippers trailed the nets and before the rakes went in. The last line is said twice, the second time to the net and not to the company. The singer would not give it while a man of the tide-tally house stood by, so we went down to the water.*

> The net goes in while the water is milk,
> and the flood comes up where the wind has gone;
> we do not ask the moon what the water wants ---
> the moon has no account in this country.
>
> A girl walks the crust and names a tide
> nobody saw; the sun takes the name.
> Salt is a teacher that asks no questions
> and writes down none of the answers.
>
> They say a Hearth lies under the salt
> and the Court's fire is the chimney of it;
> I laid my hand on the pan and the pan said nothing,
> which is the best answer I had in Vhal.
>
> A debt goes damp and the debt is gone.
> Say it to the net. Do not say it to the man.

# What the borrowed hands did

Three hands have been at these leaves besides mine.

- A clerk of the Council, in the leaning salt-coast hand, took the verbs out. Where I wrote *they charged me* he set *consideration passed*; where I wrote *a girl gave me a bead* he set *token, no value entered*; where I wrote *the paper rotted on the road* he set *instrument void by exposure*. I have put my words back underneath. A verb is a thing that happened to somebody. A clerk can take the somebody out and leave the happening standing, and the happening will travel lighter and arrive cleaner.
- The factor's clerk at Greybank, in the narrow upright hand, wrote *figures of price, not of account* against my table, and set a round nought in the margin beside the fourth question. A nought is a figure. A question is not. There was no one in that room who could have told me why I asked it, and nobody tried.
- A dipper wrote a tide-name in salt in the margin, for a thing she had seen and would not say aloud. The sun took it before noon and left a white place. I have not written the name back. What is written in salt belongs to the sun, and the Council has never found a way to charge for it, which vexes them more than a lost debt.

# At the head and in the gutter

The white at the head is salt bloom off the dockets and comes and goes with the weather; it has eaten the last letters from two words, and I have not written them back, because a word with the end gone is said slowly and this country says everything slowly. The tar at the fold is mine, off the coil I carried eighty leagues and can still smell on my thumb. The feather is a brine-gull's; he took a coal off a brazier at the pans while my back was turned and went off with it warm under his crop, and the girl who was raking said no malice in it, which may be true and is no comfort when you are the one who has lost the coal. The bead is cliff-glass, green and sweet-smelling in the heat, pressed on me by a girl at the furnaces who watched me write and said nothing. The rope fibre is worked into the third crease and will not come out. The nick is from a fish-hook at the Fishstairs, made at home, not here. The ale ring near the song is from a cup set down by a ship's master who would not drink with dippers and yet stayed to hear them. The white place in the margin was a tide-name and belongs to the sun. The scraped square is a word I took out with a knife and have not put back. I have not forgotten it either. The grey along the outer fold is not mine and not the Council's. A factor from Greybank called it Ash, which in Vhal is a thing a man must buy before he will own to seeing it. I did not buy it. I have not swept it.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> Tile went north and came back a mark;
> paper went north and came back a virtue;
> four questions asked and one answer given ---
> *current*, said four mouths, and the rain said nothing.
>
> Ask the net what it carried.
> The net has no account to enter.

I asked a dipper at the west pan, a woman who holds a twelfth of a boat through her mother, and a factor's clerk come up from Greybank, and had three answers and no hand, and a fourth if I had thought to ask the rain. I have left the line where it stands. The leaves go back into the tin that held the salt, under the wheel-shed bench, where the tar keeps the damp out and the damp keeps the writing soft. Tomorrow I knock a pot for a girl who has never seen the sea, and tell her that in Vhal a man is a friend when his account is current, and leave her to work out whether that is a kindness or only a shorter way of saying *not yet*. The kiln will have its wood. The sea may keep its word for guest.
