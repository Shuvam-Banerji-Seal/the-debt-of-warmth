I keep the roll at the post. The post stands ten leagues beyond Keld Keep where the Rim Road goes through into Grath, and there is a post and no gate, which is the first thing a man from the south asks about and the last thing anybody tells him. The roll is not a history. It is a list of measures and of the days men went through, kept so that a reeve who says the pass was shut in the seventh year may be answered out of a book and not out of a memory. There is no quarrel about the figures at this post. Everything else is quarrelled about, and the quarrel is entered under the year in which it was written, not the year in which it happened.

I write in Grathic letters, because Keldish is cut in bone and a bone does not travel down a road in a pocket the way a letter does, and I have struck the endings off as our people do. My number stands where a name would stand: nine and twenty. I have put no name to this. A post keeps measures, not men.

This chapter is the one the factor's clerk asks to see when he comes up from Greybank. I have copied it four times. On each copy the four terms stand as the slate cut them, and the one thing that will not go into a year-entry is the nought under the claim, which I have cut in chalk at the foot of the figures where a man looking for a gate will find it.

# The years as the roll has them

*Copied from the Warden's roll, which is kept face down in the survey room at the Keep and carried up to the post each Ash. The entries are in the number-first hand. I have put nothing in from the temple tells, which have their own dates and a comfort in them.*

**Y.H. 741.** The Cold Pass shut to Court traffic by Karric VII, Warden of the Rim. Entered as *the pass is shut*. Beneath it, in the same hand: *the true Hearth lies under this mountain*. Not entered again.

**Y.H. 742.** Assessors came up from the Hearthseat with slates and no soldiers. They wrote the claim down, took the verbs out of it, and went back. Entered as *the matter is under consideration*.

**Y.H. 743.** Hired men came up the Rim Road from the coast with cold iron. Entered as *precaution taken*. No dead entered.

**Y.H. 744.** A levy of clay-men out of Grath, who had no interest in the argument. They came because the levy was called and went up because it was called. Entered as *the levy was answered*.

**Y.H. 745.** The road downriver stopped short of Greybank. Carts went by the river and took nine days where they took five. Entered as *the road is otherwise engaged*.

**Y.H. 746.** Commissioners sat at the post with a slate and a piece of Keldish iron on one table. Entered as *a conference at the post*, with no mention of who paid for the table.

**Y.H. 747.** Terms cut and the pass opened. Entered as *peace, the road opened, the account standing*.

**Y.H. 749.** Grath, Keld, Vhalen and Sere set their marks to the neutrality of the Hearthseat. Entered as *the fire is left standing*.

> *In the narrow hand of the factor's clerk, against the entry of Y.H. 741:* An unauthorised claim is recorded. Strike *true*.

I have not struck it. The Hearth is under the Keel and the Warden's mountain is under the Rim, and both men have been told this since they were boys. The word is the Warden's, and I have not put it there. A surveyor who takes the adjective out of a claim has left the claim standing and taken away the only part of it that could be argued with, which is a great kindness to do an argument and a poor one to do a man.

# The claim, set beside what came down from Grath

*The left column is the roll's. The middle is the Court's annals, read to me at Greybank by the clerk who took the verbs out. The right came up in a sloping hand on the back of a reeve's weigh-slip, and I have set it where I found it.*

| The roll at the post | The Court's annals | What came down from the reeves |
|---|---|---|
| The true Hearth lies under this mountain | An unauthorised claim is recorded | The Hearth is under the Keel. We have seen it. This is under a mountain. |
| The pass is shut | The frontier was closed to traffic | There is a post and no gate. What was there to shut? |
| The levy was answered | Precautions were taken | The clay-men came because the levy was called. They asked whose war it was and were answered with the weather. |
| Peace, the road opened | The matter is closed, the account standing | The road is open. The toll is the toll of 740 still. |

The reeve gave a date where a figure was asked, which is the custom of that country and not an evasion of it. He set down the fourth of Frost and said the ferries stopped on that day and the eels ran on every day after it, whether the pass was shut or not. I put his date in the margin where it stands. It is not an answer to the question the roll asks, and it is the only true thing that came down from Grath in those seven years.

| 743 | 88 | 88 | 12 | Precaution taken |
| 744 | 60 | 58 | 9 | The levy was answered |
| 745 | 47 | 44 | 6 | The road is otherwise engaged |

| 747 | 1,188 | 1,188 | 91 | Peace, the road opened |

The figures are neat. The sixty of 744 were clay-men from Grath who had been levied to fight a Warden over a Hearth they had never seen, and the roll enters them as *the levy was answered*, a phrase with no men in it and no cost. I asked the old tally-man what the sixty had been. He said they had been cold, and went on with his counting. I have put *cold* in the margin and not in the table, because a table is a thing a clerk reads and a margin is the only place in a roll where a silence is allowed to stand.

Under the 744 row I have cut a nought in chalk. It was there when I came to the roll, and it is not a figure for men. In the old roll a gate was drawn in the margin where the post stands, and somebody cut the gate away and put a nought in its place. A nought is a number. A post is a place. The two have stood side by side in this book for seventy years and nobody has asked me to make them agree.

# The four terms, cut on the slate in Slate Row

*The treaty slate stands in Slate Row in Sill, which is a Court street and not a Grathish one, and is read at the Cross on market morning. The thane of Clay was not asked. The Grathish say this is how all important treaties are made; the Court says the slate was cut in the presence of four powers and the thane's absence is not an entry. I have copied the four terms as they are cut.*

| Clause | As it is cut on the slate | What the roll set beside it |
|---|---|---|
| One | Keld keeps the pass and the survey shaft. | *The post stands.* A surveyor goes down and comes up, or does not, and the Warden's men do not promise the third thing. |
| Two | Grath keeps the road. | *The road is open.* Open to whom is not cut, and the toll at the post is the toll of 740 still. |
| Three | The Hearthseat is neutral ground. | *The fire is left standing.* None of ours has been allowed to stand near it, and none of ours has asked. |
| Four | The border is fixed at the survey post. | *A post and no gate.* The reeve's nought was cut at the slate's foot and then scraped away, and the scrape is still there. |

In Y.H. 749 the four powers set their marks to the neutrality the slate had only promised. I have copied the marks as four strokes and not as a word, because a stroke is the only part of a treaty a man at this post can check with his own eyes.

# The road-song

*Taken down at the post from a carter going down with lead, who would not give his number and sang it instead. Sung going down the Rim Road, never going up.*

> Seven winters the pass stood shut,
> the Warden's fire under the stone;
> the assessor came with a slate and a word,
> and the word went down like rain.
>
> One for the Warden's mountain,
> one for the Keel beneath;
> four for the powers that set their marks,
> and none for the man between.
>
> There's a post and there's no gate,
> and a nought where the gate should be;
> the number goes on the arm, not the road,
> and the mountain keeps its own.
>
> The clay-men came and asked whose war,
> and were told to ask the levy;
> the road stands open to every cart
> and the toll stands where it stood.

He said he had learned it from his mother and she from a man who went up in 744 and came back in 747, and that the man never said which of the seven years he had spent in the pass. I have left that where he put it.

# What the Court hand did, at Greybank

A clerk of the factor's office borrowed the leaf on a market morning. He writes upright and narrow, and takes the verbs out as though a verb were a thing a man could smuggle past a weigh. He laid his hand on the top sheet before he looked at either of us, which is the manner of that table.

- Against *the true Hearth lies under this mountain*: *an unauthorised claim is recorded*.
- Against *the pass is shut*: *the frontier was closed to traffic*.
- Against *the levy was answered*: *precautions were taken*.
- Against *peace, the road opened*: *the matter is closed, the account standing*.
- Against *there is a post and no gate*: *the frontier is unguarded*. There is no gate to guard and never was, but a sentence with a guard in it can be entered.
- Against the nought I cut in chalk he wrote nothing at all. The hand passed over it without a stroke, which is the readiest way of correcting a figure I have yet seen.

I have left both readings everywhere, the thin one under the neat one, as a repaired wall keeps the old break under the new keying and does not pretend the first hand never touched the stone. A clerk who takes the men out of a sentence leaves the road standing by itself, and a road standing by itself has carried nobody and cost nobody, and there is no figure in it to argue with.

# At the head and in the gutter

The smut at the head is mine; I hold the leaves down with my whole hand because the draught at the post comes along the floor and takes paper before it takes a man. The iron-red at the second corner came off the mine-reeve's point when he checked a man who had given a figure that would not hold; he did not look at me and I did not look at the man. The chalk at the foot of the figures is the nought, and it comes away on the cuff and is put back each Ash, which is more than can be said of the gate it replaced. The frost-feather was on the survey stone after a warm night and was gone by the time I found the leaf to press it in. The lead bead rolled out of the Ninth Gallery; a surveyor laid it on the leaf and went away without it. The tallow is my lamp set too near the roll. The grey along the outer fold is not mine and not the Court's. I have not swept it. There is no Last Day at this post.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> Four powers set their marks to the fire,
> and one man cut a nought in the stone;
> the road went open, the pass went shut,
> and the account came back the same.
>
> Ask the post what it kept.

I asked the tally-man, a surveyor come up from the shaft, and a carter who had been down in 747, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The roll goes back to its nail beside the post. Tomorrow a man will state what he holds before he says who he is, the number will be entered, and the gate will still not be there.
