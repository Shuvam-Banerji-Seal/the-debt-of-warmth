I am called Bowl. It is the name the house gave me when it bought me, and the name I had before that is written on the inside of a bowl on the lower shelf of the court cupboard, under the lead glaze, where I am not permitted to look. I wash the bowls of the Ten Bowls. That is the whole of my office: to take what the court has emptied, to scour it, and to set it back with the rim turned outward so that nobody sitting at the table can see what is kept under the glaze. There are nine bowls in that rack and I am the tenth, which is a jest the kitchen made once and has never made again.

A woman came south from Greybank with a consignment of glass and asked us at the basin what we sang when the court had gone. She wrote down the first line and went away believing she had learned Sere. I have set down the whole of it, with the ends put back where the court lets them fall, the clerk's gold left standing above, and my charcoal put back underneath where it belongs. A repaired bowl keeps the old break under the glaze; a corrected leaf keeps the woman under the verb.

The ring at the head is wine. It was warm when I set the tally down and is the colour of the glaze now. I have not washed it out. A bowl that has stood a whole feast and been set down twice is entitled to one stain it did not ask for.

# The manner of it

- Sung at the basin after the court has gone and before the bowls are stacked. Never in the court: a song with no debt in it is a song somebody will try to enter.
- The bowls are washed in the reverse of the order in which they were emptied, and the song follows the bowls. The first bowl washed is the last that was handed. There is no reason for this and nobody has asked for one.
- A sip is taken from what the bowl still holds before it goes into the water. This is not a perquisite and is not entered. The woman who drinks is not the woman who washed the bowl, so that no woman drinks her own leavings.
- Said to the bowl, with the face turned toward the basin. Said looking at another woman it becomes a question, and there is always a woman who will answer it, and then the kitchen has two griefs instead of one.
- Do not turn a bowl to the light. The word was cut under the glaze before the firing and the fire shut it in, and the glaze has kept it better than this house has kept anything else. A name that is read is a name the court can enter.
- If a bowl comes up from the court still warm, hold it a moment before the water. That is the only warmth in this house that nobody has bought for us and nobody will enter against us.
- When the tally-clerk crosses the yard, stop in the middle of a verse and begin again at the same place when he has gone. A song with a figure in it travels; this one does not.
- The last place is kept the length of a name. Do not put a nought in it. A nought is a number. A name is a vessel.

# The song

*Said low, from bowl to bowl, with the ends of the lines let fall into the basin. The refrain is said to the rim after every part.*

> The bowl went down the length of the court,
> from the high seat to the last stone bench;
> each man named the debt he meant to carry,
> and the fire applauded when he said it.
> The wine was warm at the first of them
> and had gone the colour of the rim at the last.
>
> *Pass the bowl. The bowl goes round.*
> *What it held is poured out. What it keeps is under the glaze.*

> It came to us with a finger of wine in it
> and the smell of a fire we were not asked to smell;
> we drink what the court let fall
> and the basin keeps what we let fall.
> The basin has no column and asks for none.
>
> *Pass the bowl. The bowl goes round.*
> *What it held is poured out. What it keeps is under the glaze.*

> There is a word on the inside of the third bowl,
> cut under the lead before it went to the fire;
> the fire shut it in and the glaze has kept it
> better than this house has kept a promise.
> I know the shape of it under my thumb
> and have never once been told the sound of it.
>
> *Pass the bowl. The bowl goes round.*
> *What it held is poured out. What it keeps is under the glaze.*

> A child of this house is given seven names
> and keeps whichever the household likes;
> a bought woman is given one name
> and the one she brought is forbidden.
> The bowls are the only vessels in this court
> permitted to have kept two.
>
> *Pass the bowl. The bowl goes round.*
> *What it held is poured out. What it keeps is under the glaze.*

> They burned the slate by lot when the eating was done
> and made a new one out of the same fire;
> the debt went up in the smoke and came down in a clean hand,
> and the house stood where it had stood before.
> Nobody clapped for the bowl that held the wine.
>
> *Pass the bowl. The bowl goes round.*
> *What it held is poured out. What it keeps is under the glaze.*

> The glaze is sweet at the rim and the gum goes pale
> and the hand forgets the cup at the end of it;
> nobody asks who paid for the wine,
> nobody asks who paid for the fire;
> the bowl stands full for the next of the house,
> and the next of the house is not named.
>
> *Pass the bowl. The bowl goes round.*
> *The word on the inside is not read aloud.*
>
> *The bowl keeps it. The bowl gives none back.*

There is a space between the second line of the last refrain and the third. I have left it on the tally. It is the length of a name and of nothing else, and the women leave it when they sing, though none of them has ever said what stands in it.

# What the glaze keeps

A bowl for the court is glazed inside and out. Before the glaze goes on, a word is cut into the clay with a point fine as a needle, and the glaze runs over it and shuts it in, so that the word is there for the life of the bowl and can be come at only by the thumb or by breaking the bowl. This is done to some of the people the house buys and not to others, and I do not know why it was done to me. The kitchen says it is done to those who were once somebody and might be tempted to remember it. I asked whether the house was afraid of a word and was told the house is not afraid of anything, which is how a house answers a question it does not want entered.

The rule is that a bought woman is never to look into the bowl that carries her former name. It is not written anywhere. It is said at the basin the way a pot is said to be hot, which is information and not an order. The bowls go into the rack with the rims outward, and there is a woman at the end of the bench whose whole work is the turning of them, and who has been at it longer than I have been alive. She has never once turned one the other way. When I scour the inside I keep my eyes on the water and my thumb goes where it goes, and that is the whole of the arrangement.

A girl who washed here before me turned a bowl to the lamp and read the word on the inside. She was not beaten and nothing was entered against her. She was given a new clay-name in the morning, and the old word was scraped out of the bowl with a knife and the place filled again with glaze, so that there is a smooth green patch on the inside where the letters had been. She is at the grain mills now. I have not asked which name she answers to. That bowl is fourth from the left on the middle shelf, and I have washed it every tend since and have never turned it to the light.

I have tried to say the word once, to see whether the sound of it would still be mine. I could not get it out. This is not the same as forgetting. A thing under glaze is not lost to the woman who cannot see it; it is lost to the house that thinks it has covered it over.

# What the tally-clerk set above

The tally-clerk of the Ten Bowls came into the scullery with his gold pen and set his hand on my leaf while I was still finding the end of a line. He writes very small, in thin gold, and took the verbs out of four lines, leaving the women standing outside them.

- At *we drink what the court let fall*, he wrote *the residue of the public bowl is not a discharge of any obligation*. It is true of the wine and does not mention the throat it went down. I have left it.
- At *the basin has no column and asks for none*, he set *all premises appear in the house inventory*. The basin is not in the inventory. I have put my line back underneath and have not crossed his out.
- At *I know the shape of it under my thumb*, he wrote *unauthorised handling of house ware is a fault*, and underlined it twice. I was set to wash the bowl. A woman cannot scour the inside of a bowl with her nails.
- At *the debt went up in the smoke and came down in a clean hand*, he wrote *measure for measure*, which is what a man says in this country when he has already decided what the two measures are.
- Where I left the space in the last refrain, he put a round nought and wrote *one bowl, current* beside it. A nought is a figure and a bowl is a vessel. The two have never touched, and now they stand on the same line.

I have put my words back beneath his everywhere, as the word under the glaze lies under the glaze. He strikes the doer out because a thing always true can be carried into a counting room. I put the doer back because a woman at the basin wants to know whose hand let the wine fall, and the answer is in the sentence if there is somebody in it.

# In the margin, in charcoal, a northern woman's hand

The charcoal belongs to a woman bought down from the low country, who came here with a skill at the press and has never learned to say the south properly. She writes on the backs of spoiled tallies and presses hard at the ends of words.

> In the low country they break a pot at the door of a death-house so that the heat may get out. Here they wash the bowl and the heat goes down the drain with the wine. Somebody paid in both countries. Nobody asks in either.

And below, in the same hand:

> I have washed eleven years here and can still count in marks. They gave me a clay-name the first week and I have not said my own since, but I know the number of it, which is a thing no clerk has thought to take.

I have set both where she wrote them. She would not sign them.

# At the head and in the gutter

The wine-ring at the head is mine and I have not washed it. The oil at the second leaf is press-oil off the sleeve of the woman who wrote in the margin; it has gone into the grain and will come out with nothing. The green bead in the lower corner is lead glaze, broken off a bowl that went to the rack with a star in it; do not touch the edge, the sweetness is in it and the sweetness is not a kindness. The fleck of gold came off the tally-clerk's pen when he leaned over me. The grey bead in the fold is ash off the burnt slate of the feast; they burn the debt and then buy the ash back, which is a way of keeping a thing and calling it gone. The pale print is from a girl of the glaze room with the pale line at her gum, who leaned over to read and touched the tally and went back to her work. The thread is grey wool off a kitchen sleeve. There is no Last Day in a scullery and nothing gets swept.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The court empties the bowl. The basin keeps the dregs.
> The slate burns. The name stays under the glaze.
> Ask the tenth bowl what debt it intends to carry,
> and listen to the answer before you applaud.

I asked the woman who turns the rims, the northern woman at the press, and a tally-clerk, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written. The leaf goes back into the oiled silk and the silk into the fold of the cloth. Tomorrow the court will empty the bowls in the same order and we shall wash them in the reverse of it, and the third bowl will go back on the lower shelf with its rim turned outward, and the new slate is already in the counting room with a debt in it that nobody at the basin will be asked to carry.
