I count at the mouth. That is my office: to take the numbers as a pair goes down, to put the warm-stone into a boy's hand after the knife, and to keep the cup on its nail for the men who come up. Twenty-two winters I have done it and never gone below; my mother did the same before me. It is not cowardice. The women of Keld count above ground and hold the mine-reeve's keys, and a woman's measure is said to lie too near the surface and to draw the sweat out of the stone, and the stone is not ours to trouble.

A survey-clerk come up from Greybank stood at the weigh with a cuff soft from washing and asked what was sung when a man was walled. I told him, and he wrote it while I was still saying it, with the doing taken out and a figure standing where a place should stand. So I have set down what we said before he came and what we said again after he was gone, and left his hand above mine. A man is set facing the deep seam and the stone is built up in front of him; the surveyor adds a number to a slate nobody reads aloud, and we say the mountain keeps its own, which is a true thing and not a comfort. The smut at the head of this leaf is coal off my sleeve. I did not know I had marked it.

# The manner of it

- Sung sitting, at the mouth, when the walling is done and the slate has gone up toward the Warden's room. Not inside the seam and not during a checking: a song said while a number is being checked is entered as a disturbance, and the mine-reeve does not ask twice.
- The cup goes by number and not by age and not by hand. The man who holds the most lifts it first and the man who holds the least lifts it last; a man corrected in red at the weigh lifts nothing at all. Passed by age it would become a kindness, and there is no kindness in this cup.
- The last place on the bench stands. It is not drunk and not turned face down; in the morning it is poured on the stone at the mouth and not on the ground inside, which belongs to the mountain. The cup at the end of the Duelling Floor is another cup and belongs to the Order.
- The last word of every part is said to the iron and not to a man. Said to a man it becomes a question, and there is nobody here who can answer it.
- The number is said before the name. In this song the name is not said at all, and where it would stand I have cut a notch in the edge of the leaf, which keeps better than a word and cannot be carried up the road. The warm-stone beside the cup is sold, never lent; the stone that goes into the wall is the one he was given at thirteen, and no man here has called it a gift.

# The song

*Sitting. The cup goes from the highest number to the lowest. The last word of each part goes to the iron.*

The refrain, as the mouth keeps it after every part:

> Pass by the number. The last place stands.
> What goes into the wall keeps what it had.
> The mountain is retentive. We are not.
> Set the cup down. Do not drink the place.

## I. Of the count

> He gave the figure first, the way a man does here,
> and the name came after it and went back home;
> the figure went down with him and the name stayed up,
> which is the order of a man and the order of a road.
>
> The tally-man called it and I wrote it down.
> A number said twice is a number asked to change,
> and the mountain has never answered a second asking.
> Hold the rail. The rail has never answered one.

> *Pass by the number. The last place stands.*
> *What goes into the wall keeps what it had.*
> *The mountain is retentive. We are not.*
> *Set the cup down. Do not drink the place.*

## II. Of the stone

> The stone they gave him at thirteen is in his hand
> and goes into the wall with the hand that held it;
> it is the only stone in Keld that is not sold,
> and nobody here has had the heart to call it given.
>
> A stone keeps heat longer than a man keeps warmth.
> It held one night on the floor and was cold by morning.
> We paid for the night when we bought it at the mouth;
> the morning came without a price and nobody entered it.

> *Pass by the number. The last place stands.*
> *What goes into the wall keeps what it had.*
> *The mountain is retentive. We are not.*
> *Set the cup down. Do not drink the place.*

## III. Of the iron

> Nine clamps on the board and one upon the nail;
> the tenth goes to the stone and is not brought back.
> One bite holds a vessel and one takes off a hand;
> the iron has never asked which bite it was.
>
> He came up off the first way with a rope-coloured hand
> and lifted with the other and said his number.
> The number stood. The hand did not.
> We set the cup down and did not lift it for him.

> *Pass by the number. The last place stands.*
> *What goes into the wall keeps what it had.*
> *The mountain is retentive. We are not.*
> *Set the cup down. Do not drink the place.*

## IV. Of the seam

> The rock sweats in the Ninth and the lead smells hot;
> that is the warning and that is the whole of it.
> Frost-feather grows where the warm has gone out of stone
> and is gone by noon, and the finding is still owed.
>
> He faced the deep seam and we built the face before him.
> The surveyor set a number on a slate nobody reads aloud.
> The tube took what the blood was carrying off;
> a figure travels well and arrives with nobody in it.
> The Vorr say we were never owed. We say the mountain keeps its own.
> The notch in this leaf is the width of my thumb.

> *Pass by the number. The last place stands.*
> *What goes into the wall keeps what it had.*
> *The mountain is retentive. We are not.*
> *Set the cup down. Do not drink the place.*

## V. Of the last place

> The cup at the end of the bench is set down and stands;
> no man lifts it and no man turns its face to the boards.
> In the morning we pour it on the stone at the mouth,
> not on the ground inside, which is not ours to spoil.
>
> Say the number once. Do not say the name.
> The boy going down at thirteen will have need of both;
> the man in the wall has kept the one and let the other go,
> and the bench keeps the place where a man would sit.

> *Pass by the number. The last place stands.*
> *What goes into the wall keeps what it had.*
> *The mountain is retentive. We are not.*
> *Set the cup down. Do not drink the place.*

There is a space before the last refrain, the length of the notch in the edge. I have left it on the leaf. It is the right length for a name and for nothing else, and the woman beside me at the weigh put her thumb into it and would not say why.

# The part we do not say while a survey-clerk is at the weigh

I have this from the man who keeps the tally, with the space before it standing, which is the space the cup leaves at the end of the bench. He would not say it to the clerk and said it to the iron on the nail with his back turned to the road.

> Six bits to the stone and the stone to the wall,
> the man to the seam and the number to the slate;
> eleven months in the house that bought him,
> and the twelfth is the one the household keeps.
>
> Ask the mountain what it took. It will say the number.
> Ask it what it kept. It will say the stone.
> Ask it what it owes, and there is no word in Keld
> that a man will say twice in the same room.

I asked him what the twelfth was. He said a man who had never been bought would not know where the foot comes up in the dark, and went to hang the cup on its nail. I have set it down as he gave it, without mending the sense.

# What the survey-clerk did

He writes upright and narrow and takes the verbs out as though a verb were a thing a man could carry past a weigh in the seam of a cuff.

- At *the figure went down with him and the name stayed up* he wrote *the measure was deposited; designation retained*, and underlined the figure twice. Both are true in a counting room and neither has a person in it.
- At *the mountain keeps its own* he set *no return due; remains retained on premises*. It is the cruellest thing on the leaf and the truest, and I have not crossed it out.
- At *the cup at the end of the bench* he put a round nought and wrote *vacancy* beneath it. A nought is a figure. The place is a place.
- At *do not drink the place* he wrote *no residue arises*. He has taken the cup away and left the bench standing, which is what his house has always done with a man.

I have put the men back underneath and left his above. He takes the verbs out because a thing always true has no need of them; I put them back because a man at this mouth with his cup on the nail wants to know which hand poured it.

# What the leech put in the gutter

The leaning hand is a leech come up from the Ninth Gallery, who writes on the backs of survey sheets and presses the words in with a thumb gone grey from the leads. I have copied it where he set it.

At *it held one night on the floor and was cold by morning*: **A stone in a dead hand keeps its night and gives it up in the morning. I have sat with three such hands and have never entered the night to anybody. Do not call the cold a cure.**

At *the rock sweats and the lead smells hot*: **A man who has smelt it does not need the saying, and a man who has not will not believe it. I have written this twice and struck it twice. It stands.**

At *the number stood. The hand did not*: **A hand that has failed once is not argued back into a body by a second clamp. I said this to the floor and was told the mountain keeps its own, which the mountain did not say.**

# At the head and in the gutter

The smut at the head is coal off my sleeve and has gone into the grain; no wet sleeve takes it out, which is why I sign in charcoal. The iron-red at the second corner came off the mine-reeve's point while he checked a man who had given a figure that would not hold. The lead bead rolled out of the Ninth Gallery and a shift-man laid it down and went away without it. The warm-stone shaving held its heat one night on the stone floor and is cold now, which is the order of things. The frost-feather was on the mouth stone after a warm night and was gone before I came to put it in. The sour ring at the foot is where the last cup stood. The grey in the gutter is blighted-seam dust, not the grey the lowlands leave in their corners; there is no Last Day at a mine mouth and no corner here where dust is gathered and called dealt with.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The wall keeps the face. The slate keeps the number.
> The cup keeps the place. The road keeps the name nobody said.
> The mountain keeps its own and is not asked to give it back.

I asked a man off the Duelling Floor, a surveyor who carries the slate up, and a woman who has counted here longer than I, and had three answers and no hand, as I have had before. I have left the lines where they were written. The cup goes back on its nail and the last place stays on the bench. Tomorrow a pair will go down and I shall take the numbers as they are called and put the warm-stone into whichever hand the knife leaves free; the cup will go by number from the highest to the lowest and stop where the bench ends, and I shall pour the last of it on the stone as I have poured it these twenty-two winters. The mountain sweats in the deep and the smell of hot lead gives what warning it gives, and the figures of the last tend show through the figures of this one, which is how a man at a mouth reads what he has been told.
