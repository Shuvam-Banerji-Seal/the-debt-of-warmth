I count at the mouth. That is my office: to stand at the weigh when a pair goes down and take the numbers as they are called, to put the warm-stone into a boy's hand after the knife, and to keep the cup on its nail for the men who come up. I have done it twenty-two winters and never gone below. My mother counted here before me and neither did she, which is not cowardice in a mountain where the women do the counting above ground and hold the mine-reeve's keys; a woman's measure is said to lie nearer the surface and to draw the sweat out of the stone, and the stone is not ours to trouble.

I set this down because a survey-clerk come up from Greybank stood at the weigh with a cuff soft from washing and asked what was sung when a man was walled. I told him. He wrote it while I was still saying it, and what he wrote had the doing taken out of it and a figure standing where a place should stand, and he went away over the pass with his boots clean. So I have set down what we said before he came and what we said again after he had gone, and left his hand above mine where he put it.

A man is set facing the deep seam and the stone is built up in front of him. The surveyor adds a number to a slate nobody reads aloud. We say the mountain keeps its own, which is a true thing and not a comfort, and I have never once heard it said at a fire, because there is no fire at a mouth that has not been paid for. The smut at the head of this leaf is coal off my sleeve. I did not know I had marked it.

# The manner of it

- Sung sitting, at the mouth, when the walling is done and the slate has gone up toward the Warden's room. Not inside the seam, and not at the weigh while a number is being checked: a song said during a checking is entered as a disturbance, and the mine-reeve does not ask twice.
- The cup goes by number and not by hand and not by age. The man who holds the most lifts it first and the man who holds the least lifts it last; a man who gave a false figure at the weigh is corrected in red in the morning and lifts nothing at all. Passed by age it would become a kindness, and there is no kindness in this cup.
- The last place on the bench stands. It is not turned down and it is not drunk. In the morning it is poured on the stone at the mouth and not on the ground inside, because the ground inside belongs to the mountain and we do not put our leavings into another house's floor. The cup at the end of the Duelling Floor is a different cup and belongs to the Order; do not set this one beside it.
- The last word of every part is said to the iron and not to a man. Said to a man it becomes a question, and there is no man here who can answer it.
- A warm-stone is set beside the cup and it is sold, never lent. A man who warms his hands on it pays the man who brought it, and the figure is spoken before the hand is held out. On the day of a walling the stone that goes in with the man is the one he was given at thirteen; it is the only warm-stone in Keld that is neither sold nor lent, and no man here has called it a gift.
- The number is said before the name. In this song the name is not said at all. Where the name would stand I have cut a notch in the edge of the leaf, which keeps better than a word and cannot be carried up the road.
- Not sung at the Descent and not sung on the Duelling Floor. The boys have their own going-down words and the Order has its iron-song pasted inside the board cover. This is a mouth song and belongs to the men who came up.

# The song

*Sitting. The cup goes from the highest number to the lowest. The last word of each part goes to the iron. The notch in the margin keeps the place where the name would stand.*

The refrain, as the mouth keeps it after every part:

> Pass by the number. The last place stands.
> What goes into the wall keeps what it had.
> The mountain is retentive. We are not.
> Set the cup down. Do not drink the place.

## I. Of the count

> He gave the figure first, the way a man does here,
> and the name came after it and went back home;
> the figure went down with him and the name stayed up,
> which is the order of a man and the order of a road.
>
> The tally-man called it and I wrote it down.
> A number said twice is a number asked to change,
> and the mountain has never answered a second asking.
> Hold the rail. The rail has never answered one.

> *Pass by the number. The last place stands.*
> *What goes into the wall keeps what it had.*
> *The mountain is retentive. We are not.*
> *Set the cup down. Do not drink the place.*

## II. Of the stone

> The stone they gave him at thirteen is in his hand
> and goes into the wall with the hand that held it;
> it is the only stone in Keld that is not sold,
> and nobody here has had the heart to call it given.
>
> A stone keeps heat longer than a man keeps warmth.
> It held one night on the floor and was cold by morning.
> We paid for the night when we bought it at the mouth;
> the morning came without a price and nobody entered it.

> *Pass by the number. The last place stands.*
> *What goes into the wall keeps what it had.*
> *The mountain is retentive. We are not.*
> *Set the cup down. Do not drink the place.*

## III. Of the iron

> Nine clamps on the board and one upon the nail;
> the tenth goes to the stone and is not brought back.
> One bite holds a vessel and one takes off a hand;
> the iron has never asked which bite it was.
>
> He came up off the first way with a rope-coloured hand
> and lifted the cup with the other and said his number.
> The number stood. The hand did not.
> We set the cup down for him and did not lift it for him.

> *Pass by the number. The last place stands.*
> *What goes into the wall keeps what it had.*
> *The mountain is retentive. We are not.*
> *Set the cup down. Do not drink the place.*

## IV. Of the seam

> The rock sweats in the Ninth and the lead smells hot;
> that is the warning and that is the whole of it.
> Frost-feather grows where the warm has gone out of stone
> and is gone by noon, and the finding is still owed.
>
> He faced the deep seam and we built the face before him.
> The surveyor set a number on a slate nobody reads aloud.
> The mountain keeps its own. It does not keep it warm.

> *Pass by the number. The last place stands.*
> *What goes into the wall keeps what it had.*
> *The mountain is retentive. We are not.*
> *Set the cup down. Do not drink the place.*

## V. Of the slate

> The tube took what the blood was carrying off,
> and the figure went up in a hand that will not shake;
> a figure travels well and arrives with nobody in it,
> and the man who carried it does not travel with it.
>
> The Vorr say we were never owed. We say the mountain keeps its own.
> Two refusals and neither has a place where a name would stand;
> the notch in this leaf is the width of my thumb,
> and I cut it once and will not cut it twice.

> *Pass by the number. The last place stands.*
> *What goes into the wall keeps what it had.*
> *The mountain is retentive. We are not.*
> *Set the cup down. Do not drink the place.*

## VI. Of the last place

> The cup at the end of the bench is set down and stands;
> no man lifts it and no man turns its face to the boards.
> In the morning we pour it on the stone at the mouth,
> not on the ground inside, which is not ours to spoil.
>
> Say the number once. Do not say the name.
> The boy going down at thirteen will have need of both;
> the man in the wall has kept the one and let the other go,
> and the bench keeps the place where a man would sit.

> *Pass by the number. The last place stands.*
> *What goes into the wall keeps what it had.*
> *The mountain is retentive. We are not.*
> *Set the cup down. Do not drink the place.*
>
> *Set the cup down. Do not drink the place.*

There is a space before the last refrain in the singing, the length of the notch in the edge. I have left it on the leaf. It is the right length for a name and for nothing else, and the woman beside me at the weigh put her thumb into it and would not say why.

# The part we do not say while a survey-clerk is at the weigh

I have this from the man who keeps the tally, with the space before it left standing, which is the space the cup leaves at the end of the bench. He would not say it to the clerk and would not say it where the clerk could hear. He said it to the iron on the nail with his back turned to the road.

> Six bits to the stone and the stone to the wall,
> the man to the seam and the number to the slate;
> eleven months in the house that bought him,
> and the twelfth is the one the household keeps.
>
> Ask the mountain what it took. It will say the number.
> Ask it what it kept. It will say the stone.
> Ask it what it owes, and there is no word in Keld
> that a man will say twice in the same room.

I asked him what the twelfth was. He said a man who had never been bought would not know where the foot comes up in the dark, and went to hang the cup on its nail. This is the answer the mouth gives to everything it does not want entered, and I have set it down as he gave it, without mending the sense.

# What the survey-clerk did

A survey-clerk of the Court borrowed the leaf at Greybank on the morning the lead went down. He writes upright and narrow and takes the verbs out as though a verb were a thing a man could carry past a weigh in the seam of a cuff.

- At *the figure went down with him and the name stayed up* he wrote *the measure was deposited; designation retained*, and underlined the figure twice. Both are true in a counting room and neither has a person in it. I have left them.
- At *the mountain keeps its own* he set *no return due; remains retained on premises*. It is the cruellest thing on the leaf and the truest, and I have not crossed it out.
- At *the cup at the end of the bench* he put a round nought and wrote *vacancy* beneath it. A nought is a figure. The place is a place. He has spent his whole trade putting one where the other stood and has never once been thanked for it.
- At *the notch in this leaf* he wrote *one mark, unentered*, which is the nearest he has come to saying what I cut it for.
- At *do not drink the place* he wrote *no residue arises*. He has taken the cup away and left the bench standing, which is what his house has always done with a man.

I have put the men back underneath and left his above. He takes the verbs out because a thing always true has no need of them. I put them back because a man at this mouth with his cup still on the nail wants to know which hand poured it, and a true thing with nobody in it has never yet held a cup.

# What the leech put in the gutter

The leaning hand is a leech come up from the Ninth Gallery, who writes on the backs of the survey sheets and presses the words in with a thumb gone grey from the leads. I have copied it where he set it.

At *it held one night on the floor and was cold by morning*: **A stone in a dead hand keeps its night and gives it up in the morning. I have sat with three such hands and have never entered the night to anybody. Do not call the cold a cure.**

At *the rock sweats and the lead smells hot*: **A man who has smelt it does not need the saying, and a man who has not will not believe it. I have written this twice and struck it twice. It stands.**

At *the number stood. The hand did not*: **A hand that has failed once is not argued back into a body by a second clamp. I said this to the floor and was told the mountain keeps its own, which is an answer the mountain did not give.**

# At the head and in the gutter

The smut at the head is coal off my sleeve and has gone into the grain; no wet sleeve takes it out, which is why I sign in charcoal and not in ink. The iron-red at the second corner came off the mine-reeve's point while he checked a man who had given a figure that would not hold; he did not look at me and I did not look at the man. The lead bead in the fold rolled out of the Ninth Gallery on the day the passing was measured early, and a shift-man laid it down and went away without it. The warm-stone shaving held its heat one night on the stone floor and is cold now, which is the order of things. The frost-feather was on the stone at the mouth after a warm night and was gone before I came to put it in the leaf. The sour ring at the foot is where the last cup stood from the first part to the last; I have not washed it and would not if I knew how. The grey in the gutter is blighted-seam dust off a counting-stone, not the grey the lowlands leave in their corners, and there is no Last Day at a mine mouth and no corner here where dust is gathered and called dealt with.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The wall keeps the face. The slate keeps the number.
> The cup keeps the place. The road keeps the name nobody said.
> The mountain keeps its own and is not asked to give it back.

I asked a man off the Duelling Floor, a surveyor who carries the slate up, and a woman who has counted at this mouth longer than I, and had three answers and no hand, as I have had before. I have left the lines where they were written. The cup goes back on its nail and the last place stays on the bench. Tomorrow a pair will go down and I shall take the numbers as they are called and put the warm-stone into whichever hand the knife leaves free, and the men will come up or not come up, and the cup will go by number from the highest to the lowest and stop where the bench ends, and I shall pour the last of it on the stone as I have poured it these twenty-two winters. The mountain sweats in the deep and the smell of hot lead gives what warning it gives, and the figures of the last tend show through the figures of this one, which is how a man at a mouth reads what he has been told.
