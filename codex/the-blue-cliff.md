The road-book is not a history. It is a list of distances and of what was paid upon them, kept in the thane's counting room so that a man who says the Clay Road took six days in Silt may be answered out of a book and not out of a memory. Distances are the only thing in Sill that nobody argues about. Everything else is argued about, and the argument is entered under the year in which it was made, not the year in which it happened.

This chapter is the one the factor at Greybank asks to see when he comes up. I have copied it four times. On each copy the three hundred have stood as a round sum, because the clerk who first entered them counted households the way a potter counts a row of wet pots, by the places and not by the heads in them. I have left his round sum standing and put his rows underneath it, where a clerk of the Keeping will not think to look, which is the gutter.

# The years as the moot-chest has them

*Copied in the clay-hand out of the book with the split board cover. I have put nothing in from the temple tells, which have their own dates and a comfort in them.*

**Y.H. 300.** Lead workings in the lower Ferr pass out of Keldish hands into those of contractors. Lead falls at Stonemouth, clay rises at Sill. Entered as *a readjustment of the workings*.

**Y.H. 318.** Vhaleni salt-masters buy the mines from the Keldish contractors. A clay cart out of Sill is turned back at the Ferr and its load tipped into the water. Entered as *an obstruction of the road*.

**Y.H. 326.** Two kilns at Stonemouth are burned by men of Sill. There is no battle and no dead man entered. Entered as *a firing in the matter of the road*, which is the clay-country phrase for a burning and for the firing of a kiln both, and the clerk who wrote it knew it.

**Y.H. 339.** The wells by the Ferr carry a blue-grey dust. The thane's physician says the water is not good. The entry says *the water was complained of*. I have kept *complained*.

**Y.H. 401.** A hard winter. Three hundred households of High Kiln drink from the Ferr through the season. Their measure goes thin. Entered as *a visitation of the water*. The word is the physician's, and the households did not choose it.

**Y.H. 411.** The Clay Road is stopped past Greybank. Carts go down by the river and take nine days where they took five. Entered as *delay, and the road otherwise engaged*.

**Y.H. 417.** Commissioners sit at the Blue Cliff. Salt-paper, iron clamps and a piece of Sill clay are laid on one table. Entered as *a conference at the cliff*, with no mention of who paid for the table.

**Y.H. 418.** Terms set and the road opened. Entered as *peace, the road opened, the account standing*.

> *In the tallies of the Vhaleni factor, against the entry of Y.H. 339:* The water was not complained of. It was found unequal to use. Strike *complained*.

I have not struck it. A man who complains is a man who expects an answer. A man who finds a thing unequal to use has carried it out of his house and barred the door and gone on. Both are true, and the difference between them is whether anybody was still standing in the room to be told. The factor's strip is pasted to the leaf and has begun to rot, which the Vhaleni hold to be a virtue in paper. It has not improved the entry.

# The terms, as the slate has them

*The treaty slate lies in the moot-chest under the road-book. The factor at Greybank keeps a copy on salt-paper, which has perished in the middle and been pieced with a straw-coloured thread. I have set the slate's words in the left column and what the road-book entered beside them in the right.*

| Clause | As it stands on the slate | What the road-book set beside it |
|---|---|---|
| One | Grath keeps the clay towns and the right to fire its kilns. | *Fire without let.* The kilns of High Kiln were fired that Ember as they had been fired the Ember before. |
| Two | Vhalen keeps the coast and the salt. | *The salt to the sea.* Salt came up the Clay Road that winter at the old price. Nobody thanked anybody. |
| Three | Keld keeps the lead and the right to sell it to whom it will. | *The lead to the mountain.* Lead came downriver in fine ware, which the slate does not name. |
| Four | The eastern bound is set at Greybank. | *The bank is the bound.* An assessor may now stop a cart on the Clay Road without asking the thane. |
| Five | The Clay Road is declared open to all three powers. | *The road is open.* A road open to everybody is a road nobody can shut when the collector comes. Entered as *open* and left at that. |
| Six | The wells of the Ferr are to be sweetened at the charge of the workings. | *Sweetening to be done.* No charge was entered, and the factor pasted his salt-paper over the clause. |

The paste has gone soft and lifted at one corner, so that six words of the clause show through and the rest do not. A man can read what was hidden without taking the strip off, if he is willing to lean over the leaf and put his thumb on it. There are three thumb-marks on that corner, one of them grey, and I have not cleaned them off.

# The three hundred

The figure is entered in the book as *300 households, High Kiln and Stonemouth, visitation of the water, no dead entered*. It is entered in the neat hand and it balances. I have set the rows underneath as I found them, and the fourth row as I found it too.

| Row | The thane's clerk | The other hand, small and hard-pressed |
|---|---|---|
| First | High Kiln, west row — 96 households | and the pot with the pale line at the lip |
| Second | High Kiln, east row — 112 households | and the cup that would not hold a pouring |
| Third | Stonemouth, by the Ferr — 71 households | and the well that was never sweetened |
| Fourth | — | and twenty-one that went downriver and were not entered again |

Ninety-six and a hundred and twelve and seventy-one make two hundred and seventy-nine. The writer of the fourth hand knew his figures, which is a thing a man in a clay town does not often keep to himself. He wrote *twenty-one* small and close to the gutter, as a man writes who expects his slate to be scraped in front of him. I have copied it as it stands. The factor's clerk later set *visitation: 300 households; no deaths entered* against the whole and underlined it twice. The three hundred are the round sum. The twenty-one are the odd ones, and a round sum has no room for them.

# The well-song

*Taken down at Stonemouth from a woman at the washing, who had not been born in the year of the water and had learned it from women who had. It is sung at the tubs and not at the kiln. She would not sing the second verse until the tubs were put away.*

> The Ferr came down with a blue-grey skin,
> and the lead went down to the sea;
> we drank all winter and called it thin,
> and the cup answered flat to me.
>
> A pale line sits where the lip should be
> where the glaze is tasted at the rim;
> the slate writes *three hundred*, a house writes *rows*,
> and the odd ones go downriver with him.
>
> The cliff is blue and the salt-paper white,
> the road stands open to every cart;
> the well was sweetened one winter's night
> and the water kept the larger part.

She said she did not know who made it, and then said that songs about water are made by the water, which is not an answer and is the custom of that shore. I have put it where I found it, at the washing and away from the fire.

# What the second clerk did

The College hand came up from the Hearthseat on the factor's business and corrected the leaf while I was out of the room. It is a careful upright hand and it has never in this chapter changed a figure.

- Against *the water was complained of* it set *the water was found unequal to use*, which I have already set down above, so that the two readings stand together.
- Against *three hundred households made thin* it wrote *a reduction in the measure was recorded*.
- Against *the Clay Road was stopped past Greybank* it wrote *the road was otherwise engaged*.
- Against the fourth row it wrote nothing at all. The hand passed over the twenty-one without a stroke, which is the readiest way of correcting an entry I have yet seen.

I have left both readings everywhere, the thin one under the neat one, as a potter leaves the old break showing under a new seam and does not pretend the first hand never touched the clay. A clerk who takes the drinker out of the sentence leaves the water standing by itself, and water standing by itself has done nobody a harm, and no measure has gone thin in a house where nobody ever drank.

# What I have set side by side

In this year the glaze of High Kiln is still tasted at the rim. The sweet sickness still shows first as a pale line at the gum. The Clay Road is still a Court road, and lead still comes down from Keld to be sold to whom it will. I have entered these four things under this year and not joined them. A chronicler who joins them has begun a sermon, and a sermon gets copied oftener than an account.

The Long Cold is in the book as a line of figures and then as the word *walking thin*, which the district uses of people who keep moving because stopping is the same as cooling. I have entered the figure and the word without asking which came first. This is the whole craft of the road-book: to keep the place where a thing stood, so that the next hand has something to copy besides the clean version.

# At the head and in the gutter

The clay in the gutter came off the floor of the counting room and has been there since the first copy; a man who keeps a book of distances walks clay into it every day and does not sweep it out. The green at the lower margin is not ink. A glaze-mixer come up from High Kiln leaned over to see the well-song and touched the leaf with her thumb, and I have kept the mark where she put it. The ring at the head is small ale spilled by the man who brought me the moot-chest key, who said the treaty was older than any man's grandfather and set his cup down on the leaf to prove it. The grey on the sixth clause came off a collector who leaned over the sweetening and did not wipe his hand; he is not the writer of the fourth row, and I have never asked him. The strip of salt-paper has lifted at one corner and shows six words of what it was meant to cover. The struck word in the year-entries is mine: I wrote *the water was poisoned*, and have put *the water was complained of* above it, because the book holds what was entered, and what was entered was the complaint. What was in the water is in the margin, where the College hand does not reach.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> Five clauses on the slate and none at the well;
> the road was opened and the water was not.
> They entered the households and left the rows,
> and the twenty-one went downriver with the debt.
>
> Ask the pot at the lip what it held.
