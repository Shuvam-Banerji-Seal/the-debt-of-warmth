kind: A pedlar's pocket ledger, kept in a waxed oilcloth sack under the small goods; figures set down on whatever paper came to hand, each road's due written against the goods that paid it, with an entire salt-paper leaf lost to weather and its debt entered from the other end; corrected in three borrowed hands and never copied fair
provenance: Kept by Fenna Tallow, a pedlar of Sill who walks the Clay, river, marsh and Salt roads with tallow, needles, coarse pots and small news; begun in Grain of Y.H. 812, the Thin Year of the Long Cold, written up behind carts, at the Cross, at the Greybank beam and at the tide-tally house, and carried forward at the Opening of the Slates, Ilmen 1, Y.H. 813; the last figures set down at the Cross on Ash 10 of that year. I carry my figures in a sack, under the tallow and the needles. A slate is a thing that lies on a table, and a table is a thing a pedlar does not own. I write on the back of whatever is given me: a Court notice soft at the fold, a weigh-slip the beam clerk has finished with, birch bark out of the Fen, a leaf of salt-paper I keep dry in spite of the country. Four sorts of paper, and no two of them hold a figure for the same length of time.

Twelve copper bits to a silver mark; twenty marks to a Sere crown, which comes north because it must and goes south again because it can. The Court calls five marks an ember. I asked an officer's clerk once what an ember looked like, and he said it was a figure that had been entered. I set that down, because a woman who walks should know what she is not carrying.

> *In the narrow upright hand of a clerk out of Slate Row:* Coin and reckoning entered. Ember: five marks. *Underneath, in mine:* He has copied the Court's own sentence onto my page and taken the verbs out of it, as though a verb were another article I might sell from the sack. I have put *calls* back. It is the Court that calls it five marks. My sack does not agree.

At each place I write the price and the due on the same line, because they come out of the same purse. A clerk at a board writes one figure for the goods and another at a gate for the road, and a man who stays in one town may believe the two are different things. I have walked the road. They are not.

# Sill, at the Cross

*Account-day, the tenth, twentieth and thirtieth. Read twice: once for the moot, and once for those that came late. The bell goes four, four and a gap and nobody crosses himself.*

| Article | Asked | Given |
|---|---|---|
| Brown-edged barley loaf | 1 bit | 1 bit, eaten on the step |
| Quart of small ale, watered | 1 bit | 1 bit, and watered twice |
| Sour milk, a quart | 1 bit | 1 bit, if the pail is still out |
| Salt herring | 2 bits | 2 bits |
| Smoked eel, Fishstairs, before the shade goes off the board | 3 bits | 3; salted, 2 |
| Eel skin, dried | 1 mark | 1 mark; it has been down the water to be sold back up it |
| Coarse pots, the score off the racks | 4 bits | 4 bits, and a fifth pot if one has a hairline |
| Tile, the hundred | 9 bits | 9 bits, delivered, which is to say dropped twice |
| Barley gruel at a house door | not sold | given, and not entered |
| A crust laid in a basket in the shade | not sold | not lifted, not named |

*The dues I paid going out:*

| | Due |
|---|---|
| South bridge, loaded cart | 1 bit |
| North bridge | nothing; the Fishstairs argued it and won |
| River road, beast on the bank way | 1 bit |
| Plank walk at the Weir | 1 bit; the boards are counted, which is not the same as saying which will bear a woman |
| Pig driven north to Cold Acre | 2 bits, and weighed at the block besides |
| Barrow of grey out through the north gate | not weighed, not entered, nothing asked |
| Man going up for the well that never freezes | 1 bit; he brings back a common stone and does not call it a warm-stone |

> *In the Slate Row hand:* Nought against the grey. No such due against the north bridge. *Underneath, in mine:* A nought is a figure and the grey is not one. It goes out every market afternoon heaped a little above the sides where the load has shifted, and nobody looks at it twice. I have left his nought standing beside my space, the way a pot stands beside its own crack.

# Nine Pots, Reedholt and Mud End

Eight leagues west to the kiln village in old feud with Sill; four east to the reed huts; three downriver to the peat hamlet. I carry needles to all three and come back with something heavier.

| | Due paid |
|---|---|
| Nine Pots road | no toll, and none ever collected; it is ours and theirs both, which is why neither mends it |
| Cart using it in Silt | a pot from the shared pit, or a knuckle; the two villages have never settled which |
| Reedholt, firm boards pointed out to a stranger | half a bit, and half again after rain, because the firm boards move |
| Barrow of peat out of Mud End in Stone | 5 bits; no figure in the Long Cold, when there was none to sell |
| Ash-mite paste, a thimble, at Mud End | 4 bits, no questions |

> *In mine:* I paid a pot going west and a knuckle coming back, and I cannot say which cost me more, though the knuckle healed badly and the pot was a good one. The road is free. The being let across it is not.

# Down the Clay Road to Greybank

Eighteen leagues, packed clay over corduroy; five days by cart in Grain, six in Silt. Two bits at this end, two bits more at the quay, and an assessor may stop the cart on it without asking the thane, and does.

| | Due paid |
|---|---|
| Cart with a load, Sill end | 2 bits |
| The same at the factor's beam | 2 bits more |
| Beast on the hoof | 1 bit |
| Foot passenger | 1 bit; nothing on Account-day, when a man comes to hear the figure |
| A barrow of grey going up | not weighed; the factor's man has never once asked |

Here a sack of salt from the pans stands at **3 marks** on the factor's board. Down at the pans they asked me a mark at the flood and two at the ebb. A carter will tell you all three figures are true, because each is said where the salt is standing, and he is right in a way that keeps him walking.

> *In the factor's clerk's hand:* Road charges, current. *Underneath, in mine:* He has written *current* over what I paid, as though a figure were a man who might be owed money by a road. I have left my row where it was. A road does not owe a carter anything, and the carter knows it.

# The Salt Road and the Quay

Eighty leagues by the coast pass. Salt, glass, rope and dried fish go out; coin and contracts on salt-paper come back, and the damp gets into both.

| Article | At the flood | At the ebb |
|---|---|---|
| Salt, a sack off the pans | 1 mark | 2 marks, and the carter does not complain |
| Glass, a green sheet off the Cliffs | 1 mark | 2 marks, after the Glaze boat has come in |
| Rope, a tarred coil | 1 mark | 1 mark, and tar on my hands before I pay |
| Tar, a pail | 4 bits | 1 mark when the galleys are refitting |
| Dried fish, a string | 1 bit | 1 bit, or two if the gulls have followed the boat in |
| Brine-gull feathers, a bundle | 1 bit | 1 bit; the bird is not consulted |
| Red wine, a jar out of Sere | 2 marks | 2 marks; salt on the rim and no charge for the salt |
| A bed on the Quay, the month | 4 marks | 4 marks, and the floor is a bit |

*The dues:*

| | Due |
|---|---|
| Sack of salt at the coast pass | 1 mark to the Council of Tides |
| Coil of rope, or a sheet of glass | 1 bit |
| Cart with a load, this end, and again at Greybank | 2 bits, then 2 bits more |
| Contract written on salt-paper | no fee; it will not last the journey, which the Council calls a virtue |
| Man going up with the glass at the turn of the month | 3 bits, returned if he comes back with the answer |
| A keel at the Quay, the month | 2 marks, or a fish off the catch |
| A boat passing upriver with a share aboard | by the lot, not the league; no figure |
| A net trailed at dawn under the tally house | no fee, and the draw is not entered |
| A man carried down for the salted boat | 1 bit for the carrying; he is entered before he goes |
| A man who dies at sea | no slate; the sea keeps its own account |

> *In the Council clerk's leaning hand:* *toll* struck out and *contribution freely entered* written over it. *In mine:* He has left every figure standing. A Council that cannot charge a toll has found a finer name for one, and I paid it out of the same purse.

# The page the rain took

I keep my salt-paper dry, contrary to the practice of the country, and the country had its way with me between the coast pass and the quay. One leaf went. On it I had written three things: what I gave for salt at the turn of the ebb, a due paid to a man who would not give his name, and a figure a woman asked me to carry upriver in my fist and not speak. All three are gone. The paper did not tear; it let go, the way a wet thing lets go of a hand.

On the leaf facing it, in the narrow upright hand at Greybank, the factor's clerk has entered a debt for a sum that may or may not be the one I carried. He writes *the figure stands*. I have not crossed it out and have not joined it to my empty place. One figure is gone and one remains, and the remaining one has a hand behind it, which is more than I can say for mine.

> *In the Council clerk's hand, against the space:* Instrument void by exposure; no claim can lie. *Underneath, in mine:* True. It is the kind of truth a man has who has never stood in the rain with a sack on his back.

# The verse I carry

*Said while the bags are being counted, never while a slate lies open and never facing the direction of Slate Row.*

> A bit at the bridge and a bit at the shade,
> a mark at the pass and a pot at the pit;
> the beam asks twice and the water asks none,
> and south to the fire there's a place with no gate.
>
> Four for the roads and a fifth for the ford,
> and one for the road that has never a post;
> they pay at the post and they pay at the beam,
> and the road to the fire pays wherever you are.
>
> The rain took a figure, the slate kept one;
> I ask at the load and I pay at the load;
> the hand knows the weight and the mouth knows the price,
> and the weight goes down where the figure should be.

A Slate Row clerk set *four roads entered* against the first line of the second verse. There are five and I have put nothing in the place. A nought is a number and the road to the fire is a direction.

# What the leaves show

The brown at the head is tallow, mine, from holding the quire open with one hand while I counted the other. The barley grease in the first gutter came from a crust laid down on the Sill leaf while a woman told a child to eat and the child would not. The pig bristle went in at the weighing board with a driver holding one ear and the weigher the other. The eel slime is from a basket set down at the Fishstairs edge where no Court slate has reached. The fish-hook nick came off a hook put down in a hurry by a man paying for six herrings and charged for five, or the other way; he would not settle it and I have not entered it. The marsh mud is Reedholt on the Nine Pots corner. The brown drop is wine from a jar out of Sere, set beside a figure I had no column for. The grey along the margin is not mine and not any clerk's, and I have not swept it. The reed was pressed between two leaves by a girl at the Weir who wanted to know if it would keep, and it has kept better than some of the figures. The scorch is where the book lay too near a brazier on the Quay; it took the corner off one row and not the sense of it.

There is no Last Day in a sack. Nothing in it gets burnt in the morning.

Tomorrow is the twentieth of Ash and the market comes back to the Cross. I shall set the basket in the post's finger's width of shade, ask the figure before I lift the load, and write it on the back of whatever comes to hand. The book goes under the tallow and the sack goes over my shoulder, and nothing in either has been closed.
