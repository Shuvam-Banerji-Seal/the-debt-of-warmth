I cut reed at the east marsh and I have kept this book three winters. It is not a book of natural philosophy. I have no slate-name and no learning, and I set down only what I have put my hand to. A man who writes what he has heard from a hearth is writing a hearth, not a beast. I have known both kinds of book and this is the second sort, being the sort a man can use.

The brown thumb at the tally is peat, not Ash. There is Ash enough on the beast without bringing it into the book. I have not washed any of it off. A book that has never been wiped on a sleeve was never taken into the marsh.

# The voice of it

It does not howl. This is the first thing a new man gets wrong and the thing that gets him bitten. A wolf that howls is a wolf you can walk away from; you know where it stands and it knows you know. The mouth in the reed makes a low clicking with the tongue, under the water's noise, so that you hear it in your boots before you hear it in the air. It carries over water as a man's talk carries over a floor. Four clicks, then a gap, then four again, and the gap is where it is listening.

I have set the call down below as we use it, answered across the water to draw them to a stand. It is not a song for a fire. It is a thing said under the breath with the tongue behind the teeth, and the words are only there to keep the count for the men who cannot click true.

> Four for the mud and one for the slack,
> it comes to the warm and it comes to the crack;
> it comes not for singing, it comes for the place
> where the way runs shallow and under the face.
>
> Click once in the reed, then hold, then click:
> a man who runs is a man they pick.
> Stand to the elbow. Show them the bare.
> It wants the one place and it finds it there.
>
> Five in the open and nine in the wet,
> and one goes still and the rest go yet;
> count them going and count them back,
> and leave the fourth for the man who lacked.

A reed-cutter goes in pairs. This is not a kindness. If a man goes alone into the east marsh in Bone he will come back with one arm or he will not come back, and the pairs are because a beast that has opened a way in one man must be driven off the other before it finishes. The last line of the song is for the one who does not come back. We leave the fourth click for him and we do not say his name at it. There is a man at Reedholt with two fingers gone who clicks four for himself and a fifth he will not explain, and I have let him keep it.

# How it kills

It goes for the throat and the inside of the elbow. That is the whole of it; there is no wrestling and no throwing down. I have seen a Held man, a man who carried more than his natural measure and was twice the weight of me, go into the mud under one of these and be under before he could shape a shout. The mud did as much as the beast. A pack can pull down a Held man in mud, which is why we cut reed in pairs and why I have no fine opinion of my own standing among them.

It does not worry a victim. This is what the hearths get wrong when they say it worries. It opens one place and waits at it. I have watched from a stand with my spear across my knee and seen a beast open a man's elbow and then lie with its jaw against the place and wait, as steady as a collector with a tube against a wrist. The man stopped struggling. The beast did not shake him or worry him or drag him. It kept the one place, and the warm went out of it, and when I drove it off the man was already a thing I would not have sent for a physician to save.

I will set down what I have seen and not what it means. It killed quickly, at one place, and there was no anger in it that I could see. A collector's draw is the same work done with iron and a slate. I have stood near enough to a draw to know the smell of the cold iron and I have stood near enough to one of these to smell the wet wool and the mud, and the two smells are not the same, but the work is. I have thought about that more than is good for a reed-cutter and I have not written a cause.

# The tally

These are the hunts I kept a true account of. Some nights I did not write because my hands were full and some I did not write because I had drunk. I have set down both sorts as they stand. The figure in the fifth column is the Stonemouth figure, not mine. I counted teeth; the clerk counted marks.

| Night | Place | Heard | Taken | Teeth | Marks | Lost |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bone 4 | East bank below Reedholt | five | two | six | 12 | a dog, bitten in the elbow, shot at dusk |
| Bone 9 | The marsh road cut | seven | three | nine | 18 | none |
| Bone 14 | Standing water north of Mud End | nine | two | five | 10 | one of ours, hand taken, lived |
| Bone 20 | The lower Ilm, silt side | five | one | three | 6 | none; the wind turned and we were heard |
| Bone 27 | Old run east of the reedbeds | seven | four | eleven | 22 | spear lost in the mud, not recovered |

> *The narrow hand wrote against the "Heard" column:* five, seven, nine are not a number. Enter as 7. Average 7 to a pack, 6 teeth to a hunt. This is the figure the factor will ask for.

> *And along the foot of the "Marks" column, in the same hand:* 12+18+10+6+22 = 68. Received. Account stands.

I had the book out at Stonemouth on Bone 30 and the weigh-clerk laid his cuff on the leaf before he looked at me, the way they do, and set the figures down. He is a clerk and I am a reed-cutter and the account is his, so I have left his hand standing. But a pack is five or seven or nine according to the night and there is no factor in the marsh to give it a number. He has put seven where I put three figures, and under it he has written *average*, which is a word that means nothing to a man standing in water up to his calf with nine of them clicking in the dark.

# The teeth and the pelt

The teeth are set into clamp-handles, and a good tooth is worth two marks at Stonemouth. This is not a pretty trade and I have never pretended it is one. A tooth remembers the shape of a way; that is what the clamp-men say when they buy, and they pay for the saying. I have seen a handle set with one of these teeth bite true where an iron one would slip, and I have no explanation for it and no wish to make one. I took eleven teeth on Bone 27 and I would take the same again if it bought the winter.

The pelt is worth nothing. The hair carries Ash, and a coat of it makes a man go thin by degrees without his knowing how. I have seen a Fen trader try to sell one in Mud End with the Ash worked out of it, and he could not, because you cannot work Ash out of a thing, you can only move it somewhere else. We burn the pelts. We do not sell them and we do not keep them and we say so plainly when a man asks, because a man who buys a reed-wolf pelt is a man who will come back for another.

# What the hearths say

The hearths say it is a drowned man's grudge that has Learnt to click. There are songs about it in the Fenmarch with a man's name in them and a wrong done at a weir. I have heard them and I have no quarrel with a song. But I have taken six-and-thirty of these in three winters and opened most of them, and what I found was a wolf. It is a wolf that took to the water and stayed there, and the cold of the water has gone into it the way the wet goes into a coat. It kills the way water kills, at one place and without wanting anything from the man it kills. I have written *no anger* in the margin and the clerk has written over it:

> *In the clerk's hand:* Malice: not entered. Sentiment is not a figure and has no place in a tally.

I did not write that it had no malice because I forgave it. I wrote it because I watched. Those are different things and a clerk's hand cannot tell them apart, which is the whole reason I keep this book in a bark wrap under a board instead of on a slate in Stonemouth.

Two winters ago a man of Reedholt went in alone and came back walking in the direction he had been facing, and his mother lived in the next hut and did not know him. We did not send for a collector because there was nothing in him to draw, and because the collectors do not come into the east marsh in Bone. He walked to the water and stood, and in the morning he was not there. I have set this down because it happened and not because it explains anything. The hearths will make a song of it and put a name in it and the name will be wrong.

# At the foot

The tooth-grey along the fore-edge is from Bone 27, when I wiped my hand on the leaf before I could find a rag. The round mark is a two-mark piece the buyer set down and forgot; I have left it where it fell. The wet reed across the first leaf is from the morning the wind turned on us and we lay flat in the cut until the clicking went away. The peat thumb is mine. The clerk's figures I have not touched, and the hunter's hand that wrote *no anger* under them is my own and will not be struck.

There is a new run east of the reeds that I have not walked. The water stands in it when it should have drained and the clicks come from it in fours with a longer gap than I have heard before. I do not know what the longer gap means. I will go in at the next Bone with a pair of cord and a fresh spear, and I will count them going and count them back, and if there are nine where there should be five I will set that down too, in my own hand, with the verb left in it.
