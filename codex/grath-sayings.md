title: The Sayings of Grath, Being the Maxims of the Clay Country, Gathered in the Clay Yard at Low Kiln and Glossed for a Child Come Back from the Bank
kind: A slip-treader's gathering of the proverbs, pot-bench maxims, firing-counts and household threats of the Grathic, set down in the common tongue; the sayings spoken low and said to the ground, the pot or the fire, the glosses in the treader's own charcoal hand, with a kiln-master's correction in broad charcoal and a Court clerk's figures in a narrow upright hand, and one line at the back in a hand nobody will own

She set it down because she had begun to forget one of the sayings and because a child returned thin to the yard would want to know what the house used to say; bark is used because a saying that travels must go where the speaker does not, and slate is for figures that stay put. I have trodden slip in this yard for one and thirty years, and this winter I found I could not bring up one of our sayings when I wanted it. It was gone the way a name goes from a person banked too often: I knew the shape of it and not the word. So I have put them here, in the tongue we speak, on bark, because bark will go where a body is not going.

A child came back to us this year who had been paid for out of the bank and knew nothing but a liking for the smell of wet clay and a hand that went toward a cup before he had seen it. He is ours and he is not ours, and what the house can give him is the old sentences. A saying said at the fire stays at the fire. A saying written on bark goes on with him.

These are not sung. Say them low, to the pot, the clay, the floor, the fire, in that order, and to a person last. Said looking at a person they become an argument, and there is enough arguing at the Cross without carrying it home. A Grathic sentence is short and has a hard edge to it; if you say it soft and long you have made a Vhaleni sentence, which is a thing with no ground under it.

# Of the pot and the note it gives

1. “Knock it. A whole pot tells you what it is; a cracked one tells you what it was.”

*Gloss — The first thing taught in a yard and the last thing a potter does before he lets a jar go. A sound vessel answers full under the knuckle. A vessel gone through gives a small flat knock and no amount of looking at it will put the note back. We do not ask the pot why. We set it by the door or we break it, according to whether it held anything worth keeping.*

2. “Never praise a pot before the firing.”

*Gloss — Green clay will stand up straight all day and slump in the night. There is a firing between the thing you made and the thing you have, and until it has come through the fire it is only an intention with a wet edge.*

3. “The pot remembers the hand. The clay forgets it.”

*Gloss — Thumb marks go into the slip and stay there; that is the clay doing what it is told. What the pot keeps is the shape the hand meant, which is a different thing. A kid asks which one is the real pot. Feed it and stop asking.*

4. “A patch honestly set is a patch. A patch set to hide a crack is a crack with a lie over it.”

*Gloss — There is a cupworm that bores from the inside, so a pot looks sound and goes at the first pouring. A false repair is the same trick done by a person. Both answer flat, but one of them wanted to be found out and the other did not. Only one of those is a sin in this yard, and it is not the one with the visible seam.*

5. “Set a wet thing down and it will tell you in the morning what it would not tell you tonight.”

*Gloss — Said of pots, and of wages, and of a promise made with a hand still wet from the bucket. You will pay for it in the thaw, whatever it was.*

# Of the fire and the iron

6. “Don't set that near the fire. You'll pay for it in the thaw.”

*Gloss — The whole of Grathic household wisdom in eight words, and it is said to the thing before it is said to the person who set it there. The pot, the coat, the bread, the child: all of them learn it from the same sentence.*

7. “One iron holds the heat in and takes the heat out. Say which mercy you mean.”

*Gloss — The damper. We have no word in Grathic that holds both bites of it, so we make the speaker choose. A man with his finger in it will hold his tongue instead, which is the answer most of us give.*

8. “A banked fire is a fire with its mouth shut. Ask it in the morning what it kept.”

*Gloss — The carried coal comes up the yard under ash at the turn of the year, and each hearth touches its own flame from it and passes on what is left. Nobody says where the first flame was lit or who paid for it. A child who asks is told to ask the fire, which is an answer given by people who asked the same thing at their own first firing.*

9. “The kiln's breath is a merciful death to a man who has not had to carry the body out.”

*Gloss — It kills in sleep and leaves no mark worth speaking of. The people who call it merciful are the people who have not had to go in after breakfast. Do not say this at a death-house; say it at the pot.*

# Of the slate and the figure

10. “One in twelve, as the notice says.”

*Gloss — Printed on the Weighing notices and read twice over the basket on Account-day. The notice is not a lie. It is not the whole truth either. In this district the figure that goes into the slate and the figure that comes out of the house are two figures, and the difference is not called a tax, because a tax has a name and this has none.*

> *In the narrow upright hand, written against this saying:* Deduction one in twelve. Printed. Current.
>
> *Underneath, in mine:* He has put a figure where I put a sentence. A figure travels well and arrives empty. A sentence stays where it was said and knows who said it.

11. “A number goes into a slate, not into a well.”

*Gloss — Said to a man who brings a figure up out of his fist and wants it spent on something he can see. If the house wants it counted, there will be a slate at the watch. Until then it is a man's own trouble and he may carry it back down the road with him.*

12. “A debt left lying is not a debt forgiven.”

*Gloss — This was said over Garl's tile debt through seven days of high water and it was true then. The debt lay where it lay. It did not rot. Nothing in Grath rots as slowly as a debt nobody has picked up.*

13. “The collector comes with a slate, not a threat. The threat is in the slate.”

*Gloss — He does not raise his voice. He writes. A house can shout at a man and be done with it; a house cannot shout at a figure that has already been entered. A cold iron tube and a clean hand are the whole of the law as it arrives at a door.*

14. “Rent is eight marks and the frost is free.”

*Gloss — This is the dry joke of the district and it is not funny in Stone. Fuel is four marks and food is twelve, and a man who misses three days of work has begun to owe. The frost asks nothing and gives less.*

15. “A thin return comes back liking a country it cannot name.”

*Gloss — Many measures pooled and divided make a stranger with a taste for a house that was never his. We feed them. We do not ask them to remember us, and we do not say what a whole return costs, because a person who buys one has paid somebody, and here we know who.*

# Of the grey in the corner

16. “The grey in the corner is not dirt.”

*Gloss — It gathers where a promise was not kept and shows plain by tallow-light. In the daytime it is not there. On the Last Day it is left where it lies and burned the next morning, and the clean line where it stood is not the same as gone. This is not a saying for a feast.*

# Of children and the yard

17. “Knuckle the pot. If it sings, the house eats. If it lies flat, the yard takes another year.”

*Gloss — The First Firing at twelve. A clear note and there is a feast; a flat note and the child serves a year in the yard and tries again. It is the same test every pot must pass, and it excuses the child from nothing.*

18. “A foundling is named by whoever writes first.”

*Gloss — The hearth-maid or the collector, whichever reaches the bench before anyone looks at the face. The name is what the slate can hold, not what the child is. It has no house-name until a hearth claims it, which is the way of keeping a person from inheriting a debt he did not make.*

19. “Good axe, two marks. Cow, twenty-four. A boy sweeping the yard, six bits a day.”

*Gloss — Not a proverb. A list. It is kept here because a returning child will ask what he was worth and a grandmother ought to be able to tell him the price of an axe without pretending it was love that set it.*

# Of the river

20. “Ask the river what the water cost. The river will tell you the weather.”

*Gloss — An answer given with the weather in it and no figure anywhere. It comes down from the Fishstairs and up the Weir and is the same sentence in every mouth along the Ilm. A person who wants to know what the flood took had better have a slate, because the river has none.*

# Of the south and the Court

21. “A man without a kiln-mark is a man without a debt, and we do not trust him.”

*Gloss — Travellers are lodged at the Cross and watched. Sere traders are disliked because they buy poor measures and pay in crowns that will not change at the Cross, and are sold to anyway. A Wasteling is not disliked so much as pitied in a way that closes the door.*

22. “Measure for measure means what the man who said it has already decided.”

*Gloss — A southern phrase, brought back by men who went down the Peat Line with wool and came back with a figure in their heads. At home we say one in twelve, as the notice says, and the notice and the man between them keep a whole district warm for a season. The south empties a bowl and calls it the same thing as a number.*

23. “The Court owns no slaves and calls that civility. Its Kept belong to their accounts and calls that kindness.”

*Gloss — Fewer than twenty in the world. They sit in rooms with good fires they cannot feel. Say this at the pot and not at the Court table, where there is a clerk who will write it down as an opinion and send it up with the figures.*

# The tread-song

*Sung going round the slip pit in the first warm days of Seed, feet bare, one hand on the rail and the other at the small of the back. It is not a work-song exactly; the work gets done whether it is sung or not.*

> Tread it round, tread it slow,
> the clay knows more than the hands will know;
> fire it stiff, knock it light ---
> a whole pot sings and a cracked one lies flat.
>
> One for the pit and one for the rack,
> one for the firing that will not come back;
> the last goes round without a name ---
> the yard keeps it. The slate does not.

*Gloss — Children ask what the last one is. They are told to ask the clay, which is what their mothers were told, and their mothers before them. It is not a board and it is not a pot. It is the count that goes round the yard and never stands still long enough to be entered.*

> *In the broad charcoal of the kiln-master who borrowed the bark at the firing:* A pot that sings before its time is green and will slump. There is no note to argue with.
>
> *Underneath, in mine:* I have left his correction standing. He is right about the pot and wrong about the song, which was never about the pot alone. He has read every line of this bark with one eye on the kiln.

# At the head and in the gutter

The chalk at the head is my thumb; I held the bark down on the bench in a draught from the yard door and did not know I had marked it. The scorch along the lower edge is where I laid the fold too near the kiln mouth one evening, and it took the corner off three sayings; I have not written them in again, because a saying with its corner gone is a saying the yard has half forgotten, which is how most of them reach us. The sherd between the third and fourth leaf is a piece of a jar that answered flat and was set here to remind me what flat sounds like. The grey in the gutter came off a cuff that lay where no cuff had business lying, and I have not swept it. The green at the corner came from a woman who stood at the pot board with glaze in the crease beside her mouth and put her hand down here while she was deciding whether to buy. She did not buy. The strip of grey wool is mine and holds the fold; there is a knot in it for the one saying I could not bring up, and I have not untied it to see whether the word has come back.

# At the back, in a hand nobody will own

> The pot remembers the hand. The hand forgets the pot. The slate remembers neither.

I have asked the kiln-master, the hearth-maid and a man come up from Greybank, and had three answers and no hand. The bark goes back under the loose board beside the wheel. If the child who came back from the bank asks what the house used to say, let him be told: tread it round, knock it light, and do not put a wet thing near the fire. That is the whole of it, and the rest he can learn by staying.
