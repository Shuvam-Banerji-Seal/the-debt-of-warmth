I draw the year's first measure on a day before the year has turned, which is the Court's way and not mine. Stone the twelfth, with the Ilm still under its skin of ice and the district saying three days to the Five while counting three Account-days and not three mornings. A collector may draw from the dying and from the newly born on the same day. The ordinance says *may*; the district says *must*; both are entered in the same neat hand by men who have stood in different rooms.

I set the slate down before I look at anybody. Hand to slate, then eyes to the bed, then the mouth to say what the slate has already said. If a woman tells me her mother's name while I am writing the figure, I have the figure and not the name, which is what she wanted me to have, though she would deny it at her own door.

Twelve copper bits to a silver mark; twenty marks to a gold crown, a Sere coin that comes north with the factor and will not stay in Grath. The Court books an ember at five marks and no man in this yard has exchanged one for the other. A newborn vessel holds twelve to fifteen, a labourer in good case forty to sixty, one of the Kept three hundred, which is a figure no household can spend. One in twelve is printed on the notice and read twice at the Cross, once for the moot and once for those who came late. I have set no second figure beside it. I have heard a figure spoken in the yards and not entered it, not being a figure I can weigh.

Draws on this leaf: five. Lines with no draw in them: three. Figures struck: one. Columns struck: one. Figures read twice: one, and both readings the same.

# The order at a death-house

*Copied from what I was told at the table and have used for eleven years. The second column is mine and is not read at any Weighing.*

| Step | As the notice has it | As I keep it |
|---|---|---|
| The asking | Put once while breath remains; the answer entered before the breathing is counted | I have asked twice in eleven years and entered both as fresh. Neither answer was worth the ink. |
| The answer | Where the vessel cannot speak, the house answers; entered to the house | The house is not asked whether it understood. A woman held her mother's hand and said a sentence that was not an answer; I set it at the edge of the leaf, the length of it. |
| The tube | Unwrapped after the entry; of cold iron, wrapped in wool | The wool is for the hand that holds it. The ordinance has no place for such a hand. I hold without pressing. |
| The figure | One in twelve, as the notice says; not changed for the bed or the hour | Read twice at the Cross before I came down the yard; entered once at the bed. The same figure. I have not made one out of the two readings. |
| The pot | Broken at the door after the tube is wrapped, before the slate is carried away | Never entered. A pot is a piece of clay, and what was in it is not on any slate. |

# The note column, and why a man is not a cup

Before the tube I set a knuckle on the rim or the bed-rail. A whole vessel answers full; one cracked through answers flat, a small dead sound that stops at the surface; one with air under it answers nearly full, which the yards have no word for. A vessel turned toward in expectation may give nothing at all. I have entered that as *none* and not as a nought, because a nought is a figure and a silence is not.

At the death-house the room gave none. I did not tap the man. A man is not a cup, and I have learned not to set my knuckle to one. There was a cup on the bench by the bed and it had stood there long enough to take the room into itself; it answered nothing, and I wrote nothing beside it. The hearth-maid later turned a written note down under a milk cup, and the circle of that cup is pressed into the second fold of this leaf; it is her cup and her note and I have entered neither.

The College man came down from Slate Row with ink in two fingers of a cuff gone soft from washing and struck the column through from the first row to the last, writing *No such column* above it in his narrow upright hand. He drew his thumb down every row before he could write that there was nothing in any of them worth entering, and I watched him do it. I have drawn the column back underneath and left his words standing over it. A man does not count what he has not looked at.

# The child on the third day

The returned child in that room was three days back and worse before dusk. The women worked with the certain kindness of a thing they have known all their lives, warming from the outside and slowly, and saying almost nothing over the pallet. The physician come up from Low Kiln calls it the second chill; the hearth-maid calls it a graft settling. I have set both phrases in the remark and neither in a column. A child who comes back and then grows cold again is a thing the yards know and the slate has no place for. I entered the measure at thirteen, the clerk set twelve above it, and both stand, because a vessel three days returned cannot hold thirteen after the draw and yet the slate beside the bed said thirteen when I read it.

# What the two borrowed hands did

The College clerk struck *I drew* against the man at the bed and set *the measure was drawn* above it, and underneath that *the warmth was entered*, which is further from the bed again. I have put *I drew* back beneath both. A sentence with nobody in it is a sentence no woman can ask about, and women ask about these draws in the end, always at the door, always after the tube is put away.

A factor's man from Greybank wrote in a leaning hand with a little gold in the ink. Against *the house answered* he set *the answer was entered*. Against *the room called it merciful* he set *the draw was lawful* and underlined it twice. Both stand. Lawful is true and does not mention a man on a board-bed; merciful is what the room said after I had wrapped the tube, and there is no column for what a room says. He struck the word *none* in the note column and wrote *vacancy* above it. A nought is a figure and can be carried out in a cuff. The silence at the bed stays where it was.

# The verse the collectors say going down the yard

*Said low going down the Clay Yard after the tube is wrapped, never while the slate is open, and never twice in one house. The last line is said to the pot, and the man who said it to me would not say it while the factor's man stood at the gate.*

> Wool on the iron, slate on the knee,
> the tube goes in where the breath would be;
> one in twelve as the notice says,
> read twice at the Cross and once at the bed.
>
> Four go down and four come up,
> and a place where the fifth should stand;
> the pot goes out with the house's heat
> and the grey goes home ahead of the hand.
>
> The name goes down before the face,
> the child comes back and wants no cup;
> I write the note and not the room
> and the room keeps what I never took down.

I have copied it as it came and put no gloss on it. A collector who explains a song has begun to write a sermon, and sermons get copied.

# In the head and in the gutter

The grey under the nails at the head came off my own fingers giving the figure to the clerk at Greybank last tend; it goes in under the nails first and comes up into the creases by the second year, and after that a man may wash until the water freezes and nothing changes. The filings in the gutter came out of the wool sleeve and have never once drawn the warmth out of the paper, which is the only mercy iron has shown this leaf. The tallow thumb is mine, from holding the lamp low while a woman in the death-house was telling me something I wanted the end of; she did not tell me the end, and went back to sitting. The bone-pale streak came off the thrower's forearms when she set her slate beside mine to wait for a figure. The green bead is lead glaze and not slip; she leaned over with the sweetness still at her mouth, touched the paper, said nothing, and went back up the terrace. I have not washed it off.

The pale round ring was made by setting a cold tube down on the leaf while I reached for the slate, and the paper is thinner inside it. The tube was not used and the ring is not a wound, but it is the only mark on this page that looks like one. The circle in the second fold is the milk cup. The tide-line along the foot came up from the house where the pot was broken and comes back every wet month. The small bare square is where I scraped a word away with the edge of the tube and put no other in its place. I have not written the word back, and I have not forgotten its length.

On the Last Day the houses sweep their corners and burn what they find in the morning and call it dealt with. There is no Last Day in a coat.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The slate keeps the figure. The room keeps the answer. The pot keeps the heat that went out with the shard, and none of the three is entered to the same house.

I asked a hearth-maid, a factor's man, and a collector's boy come up from the yard, and had three answers and no hand, as I have had before. I have left the line where it was written.

The leaf goes back into the oiled paper and the tube into its wool sleeve. Tomorrow the district will go on saying three days to the Five and counting them its own way, and a figure will be read twice at the Cross, and I shall set the slate down before I look at the bed, and write the note before the College can strike it out. The grey will come off on the next leaf. I have stopped washing it.
