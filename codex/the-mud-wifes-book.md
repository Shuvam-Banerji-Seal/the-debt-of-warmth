I am a mud-wife of Reedholt and I have caught children on these boards for twenty-one years. Nobody asked me for this book. A girl came in the lean week with a foot gone dark inside three turns of wool and the fever going through her in turns, and asked me what I did for it, and I heard myself answer in my mother's voice. A remedy you have to say in your mother's voice is a remedy nobody has looked at in a long while. I have looked at some of mine here. The rest I have left standing, because a woman at three in the morning wants a hand more than a correction.

Fen Cant is not written. It is said through the nose, slow, with the last word dropped into the mud, and what must be kept is cut in a stick or tied in a knot. I learned my letters off an eel-trader out of Sill who could tally but could not read what he wrote. I write in the south's letters so a reeve's daughter can read this. The words that will not go into Grathic I have tied in the margin, and I have not written what the knots are for. A thing tied is not a thing entered.

Nothing in this book is free. Here a thing that costs nothing has usually cost somebody who cannot write.

The brown at the head is peat. I know peat from Ash the way a woman knows her own debt from another's: both stick to the hand, and one stays in the house after you have gone. There is Ash enough in this marsh without bringing it in on my thumb. I have not washed it off.

# The order of a birth-room on boards

*Written small on the first strip, with one word gone over in bog-water and a knot tied through the hole beside it.*

- Say the reed-name three times. Once into the child's ear, once to the pot, once into the ground under the boards. The third saying is for the child who cannot yet say it.
- Set the child on dry sacking, never on reed. Reed holds water, and a wet child cools faster than a dry one, which is a thing every mother here knows and no Court man has ever written down.
- Do not let a man with a slate sit down in the room. If he comes before the name is given, give him a place by the door and nothing to rest his hand on.
- Put the stone from the peat fire in wool and try it on your own wrist before it goes to a belly. The stone takes from a peat stack, and somebody cut that peat.
- When the third night comes, let the woman do the counting. A number counted for her belongs to whoever counted it, and there is no figure in this house to give away.
- Untie the knot when the child breathes. Do not untie it early to see whether the child will breathe.

# The remedies, as they are used

| Trouble | What is set to it | The manner of it | Who pays |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mire-fever (the burning after a drainage cut) | Face down in peat, fever-mud on the wrists; a fire if the house has peat | We say the Mother takes back what the ditch let in. The physician from Sill says draining lets something up into the water and that there is no physic but keeping warm, which the poor cannot do. I laid six face down and kept two by the fire; all eight burned and went colder, and the two had fewer turns. Both stand. | The ditch, and the household that dug it. |
| The ankle-cold (a foot out of a cut) | Three turns of wool, a stone at the belly, and work | Cold comes up through the boards and takes the ankle first. A moving woman does not notice how far gone she is. The physician says a cold vessel cannot clot and that a frozen foot set to the fire is a road to losing it. I wound the wool and set them to work and saw two toes go on the fourth day, and went on winding. | The foot, and the woman who cannot stop working. |
| A silt-leach on the ankle | Take them off, count them, salt the foot | The leach sets its mouth to a way and draws as a collector's tube draws, without the slate. Salt troubles the foot but does not stop the draw. Dried leeches fetch three bits a dozen at the Fishstairs and are used to draw without a slate, which is theft and answers to a greyed hand. I have never sold any. I have taken them off children and counted them. | Whoever put them there. |
| A reed-wolf's bite (throat or inside elbow) | Tallow, a clean fold of hemp, a grey-wool knot above the bite | The wolf opens one way and waits. We tie the cord to keep what is left in. The physician says a cord above a wound is a clamp, and a clamp takes as well as holds. I tied four; two hands below them went the colour of old rope. The fifth I left with tallow only. | The hand, if the cord stays. |
| The birth-print (the red branch) | A wet reed pressed to it; the reed-name said three times into the ground | The Mother sets it and the Mother takes it, we say. The physician says it is a mark in the blood and a reed does nothing. A woman of Mud End washed one and her child went flat by evening. I have set that beside the six I did not wash and not joined them. | No account has named it. The child's skin, if a body that cannot speak can pay. |
| A bought breath in labour | Warmth, a hand, and no questions | The term is in the woman's mouth; there is a hook in it that keeps her from the word she would need to complain. I deliver her and say the child's name to the ground before the buyer's man sets a figure. The physician says to send for the factor's clerk. I have sent for neither. Nothing here alters a term already sold. | The woman, in a term; the child, in a name unentered. |
| The third-night chill | Watch to the fourth day, a living body under the fold, the woman's own counting | It comes after the warming, not before. A hearth-maid from Mud End calls it the second chill and says a warmed body gives heat away faster. I watched nine and held three; all twelve saw the fourth morning, and one child went thin. | The household, one night's sleep and the peat. |
| Kiln's breath in a hut | Hang a kiln-hound's tongue in the room; if it discolours, get the sleeper up | The dog's tongue discolours, the only useful thing about the dog. A man goes to bed and does not wake and his lips are too red. There is no physic for bad air but air, and a draught spoils the peat, so nobody lifts the flap. | The sleeper, whom the hamlet calls merciful. |
| A cut from a reed | Wash in running water, bind with hemp; never the grey bottled at the Post | Fenfolk gather the Ash and sell it, where Grath leaves it in the corner and burns it after the Last Day. I have seen bottled grey put on a cut and sit under the skin afterward, and have not got it out of a hand. | The hand, in a white line it keeps. |

# The full note, and why a child is not a pot

Tap a sound pot with the knuckle and it answers full. Tap a cracked one and it answers flat, a small dead sound that stops at the surface. The women of the drying racks know it in their cups and I have borrowed it from them, as every trade borrows from the trade beside it.

I tapped a child once, gone thin after a thin return, and put my ear to the little flat I thought I heard. The flat was not in the child. A body is not a cup. The pot on the bench knows more about whether the room held its warm through the night than the child knows about himself, and I set the stone by the pot's note.

# What the peat keeps

The Marsh Mother keeps it warm and hands it back, we say, and lie face down in the cut until the cold becomes ordinary. The physician from Sill struck that out and wrote that the warmth is held in the peat, a true thing in a language nobody in this hut speaks. He put the verbs where I had put the Mother, and I have put mine back underneath.

I have put the back of my hand on peat where a fen-leech stood and on peat a spear-cast away, and the two are not the same. The hunter at the Post says warm water comes up through the bog and the beast has found where it comes and stands still in it; he says it standing on the boardwalk, which is dry ground and a place to say anything. I laid children face down where a beast stood and where none stood, and have not written which went warmer. A place written down is a place a man from Greybank can come to with a list.

# The song for the third night

*Sung low through the nose, to the woman and not to the child. Said to the floor, the pot, or the boards; said looking at a person it becomes an argument. The last word falls into the mud and is not finished.*

> Four for the boards and a fifth for the mud,
> the reed says wet and the child says cold;
> lay it on sacking, never on reed,
> and say the name to the ground three times.
>
> The Mother keeps what the ditch let in,
> the leach takes out and the collector writes;
> a cord on the arm is a bite with two ends,
> and one of them opens the wrong way.
>
> Watch till the fourth and let her count;
> what comes back is not what went.
> Say it to the floor, say it to the pot ---
> the last word falls where the water is.

I have changed one line in twenty-one years. I sang the Mother's line as I was taught and now sing it with the ditch in it, and the women singing with me have not noticed, which is how I know the line wanted changing. A line said over a hundred births belongs to the room, not to the woman who carries the book.

# What the two borrowed hands did

The physician came down the marsh road from Sill with a book that had the word *water* in it and a hand that wrote in the margin without asking. Against the peat he set *no efficacy; keep the patient warm* and underlined it twice; against the knot he wrote *no efficacy* again, and against the birth-print *a mark in the blood*. He was right about the blood and right about the water and had nothing to put in their place that a household here could pay for. I have left his three sentences above mine, where a body will find them if it wants them.

A factor's clerk borrowed the leaves at Greybank when the winter peat was being counted; the ink sat in two fingers of his cuff and the cuff was soft at the edge from washing. He took the verbs out of *the Mother keeps it warm and hands it back* and wrote *the warmth is held in the peat* above it, as though a thing always true had no need of anybody doing it. Where I had kept the bought-breath woman's name out he wrote *no party of record*, true in the language he writes and false in the hut where she gave birth. He put a round nought in the song's fifth place and wrote *nil* beneath it. A nought is a figure and can be carried away. The fifth is a place and stays where it is.

I have put my verbs back under his and left his above. A sentence with its verbs taken out arrives empty, which is what the Court wants of a sentence. A knot arrives as it was tied.

# At the head and in the gutter

The peat at the head is mine, from setting the leaf down on the hut bench in a wind off the bog. The tideline came up the second leaf on the morning the Post stood under and has taken two words clean off it; I have not written them back, because a remedy with two words gone is a remedy said slowly, and everything here is said slowly. The press of wet sedge is from the morning we lay flat in the cut until a beast's pushing had gone past. The scratch at the corner is a reed-wolf's tooth, from a man who paid me with his hand still on the knife. The dried smear is fever-mud off a wrist that has gone back to work. The knot through the hole in the first strip is mine and I will not untie it; it holds a word that does not go into Grathic. The small pale place is where a collector's cuff was laid while I told a girl to eat and she would not. I do not know which collector and have not asked. There is no Last Day in a tin under the boards and nothing here is swept up and burnt and called dealt with.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The mud keeps what it is given and asks no figure.
> The collector takes what he can carry and writes it down.

I asked the reeve, the physician out of Sill, and a woman whose term is not out, and had three answerings and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The leaves go back into the tin under the boards. Tomorrow a girl will come over the walk with a foot gone dark or a child that will not breathe, and I shall say the name into the ground, put the verbs back, and untie the knot when the child breathes.
