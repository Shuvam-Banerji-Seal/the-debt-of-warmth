I keep the Bent Nail, which is the last house on the Rim Road before the climb, and in Stone there is no passing it. The snow comes down the pass in a white tongue and lies in the road until the thaw takes it back, and the men who have to go up to the Keep or down to Greybank stop here because the next six leagues are a place a man does not sleep in if he can help it. I keep a fire between dusk and first grey and a lamp after that, and I have one rule in this house: no slate lies on the table. A man who wants a thing entered can carry it six leagues up to the mine-reeve or a hundred down to the factor, and he will not get a chair for it here.

That is why I have written this one. It was told on the ninth night of Stone, with the road shut behind the door and four men in front of the fire, and it was told twice. The first telling was for the room and the second was to the fire, which is the way a man tells a thing he does not want carried away. I have set down the first as it was said and put what he said to the fire underneath, where a clerk's hand cannot easily reach.

The men were a Keldish survey-clerk going down to Greybank with a roll of slips, two of the Warden's men who said nothing and kept their boots to the blaze, and old Nils, who carts iron off the Keep for a living and has done it since before the lowlands gave this year a name. Nils's hands are the colour of the sacks he carries and the little finger of the left is bent and will not straighten. He took his ale standing, with his back to the room, the way a man does when he means to say something and has not decided to whom.

# What he told the room

> You can count a team in the yard and you can count it on the road and get two counts, and the difference is not a missing horse. I have hauled iron off this Keep for nine and thirty years and I have never once carted what I was charged with, and I have never once been believed about it.
>
> That year the snow came early and lay long. I took twelve bars off the weigh at the Keep with the reeve's man counting them into the cart and me counting after him, which is a thing a carter does and a reeve does not like. Twelve. There was a frost on the bars that bit the hand. I wrapped them in a rug and a chain and went down with four in the team and a fifth place in the trace where a spare shaft lay if a horse went lame. That is why there is always a fifth place in a trace and never a fifth horse, and a man who says otherwise has not carted in a winter.
>
> On the second night the snow shut the road proper. I camped under the survey post, where there is a post and no gate, and made a fire of a broken shaft and three coals I had kept from the last house. A man came to the fire out of the white. He had a coat the colour of the pass and no grey on it, and he held his hands out to the blaze the way a man does who has been a long time without feeling them. I saw his forearm when he turned it, and there was no number cut into it. That is a thing I have seen once in nine and thirty years, on this road and not off it.
>
> I asked him his measure, because a man on this road gives his number before he gives his name and this one had given neither. He said eleven months. I said that was a term and not a measure, and he said yes, it was a term, and held out his hands again.
>
> He asked for a warm-stone. I had one the factor had lent me against the cold in the small of my back, and a man does not give away a stone lent to him by a factor, but I gave it to him, and I have never been able to say why, and I have never once been sorry. He put it inside his coat and thanked me without looking at me, the way you thank a dog or a fire. Then he said, "Tell them the fifth is coming back," and went off up toward the pass, where the post stands and nobody has ever put a gate to it.
>
> In the morning there were five traces in the snow where my cart had stood. Mine and the team's, four and another, and the fifth went up and did not come down. At the end of it the snow had a round place melted in it the size of a stone, and the stone was not there. I counted the bars before I loaded and I counted them at this house by lamp, and there were eleven, and the chain was fast and the rug had not been opened. Eleven. I have never made that twelve again in all the years since, and I have never once put a figure to it for a man with a slate.

He stopped there and took his ale, which had gone flat standing, and set the cup down without drinking. The survey-clerk said something about a man who would steal a warm-stone from a factor would steal a bar of iron from a factor, and Nils looked at him for a moment and said nothing at all, which in a carter is a whole answer.

# What he sang to the fire

*After the cup went down he sang this low, with his face to the blaze. He would not sing it looking at a person. The third line is the one the Warden's men sang with him, and they had no business knowing it, which he did not remark on and I have not asked about.*

> Four in the trace and one in the snow,
> the road takes the fifth and the cart doesn't know;
> a stone for the hand and a bar for the toll,
> and eleven months to the end of the roll.
>
> Don't count him coming, don't count him back,
> the post at the pass has a mark and no gate;
> he gave his term and he kept his name
> and went where the term goes.
>
> One for the fire and one for the road
> and one for the place where the stone sat down;
> we sing him flat and we leave him there ---
> the fifth that never comes to the inn.

# What he said to the fire

*Set down from where he stood with his back to the room, after the song. He said these to the blaze and not to any man, and the room went quiet for them, which is how a room receives a thing it will not repeat.*

He said that the man had not asked for the stone for warmth. He said the man had asked for it because it was lent, and that a thing lent carries further than a thing owned, which is a saying and not a fact, and he would not have it written as a fact. He said the eleventh bar went because the cart was carrying twelve and there is always one too many in a count that a man makes by lamplight. He said the term was nearly out. Then he put the flat cup on the mantel and said to the fire that he had been paid for the stone out of his own pocket and paid for the bar out of his own count, and had charged neither to the factor, and that was the whole of what he would give a clerk.

# The tally, in charcoal on the way-bill

I counted the bars into the cart myself when he set it under my door that night, because he asked me to and because I wanted the thing in two hands. I have set down both counts. The last column is not mine.

| Where counted | Bars | Who counted | Entered |
|---|---|---|---|
| Keld Keep, shaft-mouth weigh | twelve | the reeve's man | twelve |
| The Bent Nail, by lamplight | eleven | Nils, the carter | eleven |
| The Bent Nail, second count | eleven | I counted after him | eleven |

> *In the narrow upright hand of the survey-clerk of the Court, struck through the "Entered" column:* Bars entered at source, twelve. Cartage loss: one in twelve. Deduction printed; current. Fifth trace: no entry arising. Measure not stated; party unidentified. Account stands.
>
> *Underneath, in mine:* He has put the figure that ought to be there where Nils put the figure that was there. A bar that goes without a man taking it is a loss in my house and a deduction in his. Both are the same bar and they are not the same thing. He has left the fifth out because a fifth has no number and a number is the only thing his hand can carry. I have put the fifth back where Nils left it, at the post where there is a mark and no gate.

# At the head and in the margins

The coal smut at the head is mine, off the sleeve I wipe the lamp glass with; it goes into the grain and no wet cloth takes it out, which is why I sign in coal. The frost-feather between the second and third leaves came up out of the paper in the cold of the room and was gone before I pressed it, and a feather that has been gone by noon is still a feather that was here at midnight. The press of wet wool across the first leaf came off the Warden's man who held the door while the clerk came in, and I have not written down which man, because he would stop holding doors if I did. The bent nail in the fold is out of this house's own door; it has been bent since before I came here and I have never straightened it, because a straight nail in a bent hole is a thing that will not hold. The ale ring near the song is Nils's, and he denied it, and then set his cup in it again. The grey along the gutter came off a cuff that had no business lying in the corner where I keep the tin, and I have not swept it. There is no Last Day at a wayhouse; nothing here is burnt in the morning and called dealt with.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The road keeps what the cart cannot carry.

I asked the survey-clerk, the two of the Warden's men and a man come down from Greybank with a way-bill, and had four answerings and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The leaf goes back in the tin beside the wick, and the nail goes back in the fold from which it came, and the road will open at the thaw and the carts will come down and I shall count iron into them that was counted out at the Keep, and the two counts will agree on paper as they always do. On the ninth night of Stone Nils will come in again and take his ale standing, and if he asks for a warm-stone I shall give him one, and I shall not ask him what he wants with it, and I shall count the bars by lamplight as before. The post at the pass has a mark and no gate, and there is a place in every trace where a spare shaft lies. I have stopped asking what goes in it.
