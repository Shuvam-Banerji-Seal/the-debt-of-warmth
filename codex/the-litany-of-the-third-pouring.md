I keep the cloth on the shelf above the pots at Nine Pots. It was cut from the birth-sheet of the woman who is now coming back for the third time, and it has three knots in one corner and a fourth place the length of a thumb left empty. I tied the first knot myself. I tied the second with my sister's hand over mine, and I tied the third alone, after the figure had gone up the Clay Road and nobody in the house would say what it had been read over.

The Court has an order for the drawing and an order for the pressing and a form for the joining of vessels. It has no words for a house that has had the same woman poured three times and must set a cup down for her all over again. So we say this. I sign with the three knots because a name on a prayer is an entry, and an entry can be read out at a Weighing by a man who never held the cloth.

The brown at the head is clay and has gone into the grain. The streak in the gutter is slip off a marriage-pot set down while I sought a word and left standing until it went pale. The print at the third fold is a wet pot's foot; it was not made on purpose and I have not rubbed it out. The grey came off a cuff that had been in the corner where the grey gathers, and there is no Last Day in this house that has ever reached that corner.

# The manner of it, written small at the head

- Lay the cloth on the table before the child is brought in, and count the knots with your thumb. Said to the child these sentences become questions with answers owed, and not one of them has an answer owed in it.
- The answer belongs to the house, including the girl at the wheel and the man come up from Greybank with the slate, who says it with his hands behind him. Do not correct him, and do not correct the child, who says it worst.
- The bell at the Cross is eight leagues off and does not come up the road. We keep four and four and the gap without hearing it, which is a poorer thing than hearing and is the only thing available.
- Set a cup down at every station and do not drink from it. At the third station the cup is set on the cloth and is not lifted until the end. Nobody pours for herself in this house on this evening.
- Do not untie the knots to see which name is which. The names went soft after the second pouring and we stopped reading them. A knot keeps what a mouth lets go.
- Leave the fourth place the length of a thumb. Do not tie it and do not put a figure in it. It is not waiting for anybody; it is the room the house has and cannot fill.

# The litany

*I read from the cloth. The house answers to the knots. The answer is the same at every station, which is why it is a litany and not an argument with a bank.*

## I. Of the cloth

> The cloth was cut from a birth-sheet and hemmed with a potter's thread.
> Three knots are tied in it and no fourth is tied.
> A name given at the pouring is a name given to be spent.
> A name kept in the cloth is kept to be looked at and not read.
> We do not untie the first to find the third.

*Answer:* Keep the name. Leave the place. Come back as you can.

## II. Of the figures

> The first came back whole, at twelve hundred marks,
> with forty unspent measures nobody has explained.
> The second came back thinner. The third came back thinner again.
> The deduction stood at one in twelve through all three,
> as the notice says, read twice and not argued with.
> The figure fell twice. The deduction never fell.
> That is the whole of the arithmetic this house knows.

*Answer:* Keep the name. Leave the place. Come back as you can.

## III. Of the pouring

> One measure into one vessel: they say she comes back with her name,
> with the hand that held the cup and the price of bread in a year gone by.
> Many into many: they say she comes back as she can,
> with a hankering for a table nobody in this house set.
> Both are poured into a clean hand. The clean hand is not the hand that held the pot.
> We have set the two sayings down side by side and have not made them one.

*Answer:* Keep the name. Leave the place. Come back as you can.

## IV. Of the cup and the hand

> The hand goes to the cup before the eyes go to the face.
> She came back and went toward a cup before the cup was shown.
> The cup was shown. She took it and did not drink.
> Her grandmother drank from it and would not say why.
> Set the cup down anyway. It is the oldest courtesy here.

*Here the cup is set on the cloth. The ring it leaves is not a figure and has never been counted. My daughter says the ring is the only thing in this house that nobody has tried to explain.*

*Answer:* Keep the name. Leave the place. Come back as you can.

## V. Of the door

> She says a name this house never gave her
> and reaches for a door this wall has not got.
> Stand by the cloth and not by the door.
> A door stood by is a door somebody will come through.
> Do not ask which of the three she has come back as.
> Ask which of the three she is not, and do not ask it aloud.

*Answer:* Keep the name. Leave the place. Come back as you can.

## VI. Of the iron

> The tube came up the Clay Road in wool and went away with a figure.
> Cold iron takes from the drawer as well as from the drawn.
> Somebody's fingers went grey at the third pouring.
> In this house we have not asked whose, which is not the same as not knowing.
> The collector does not come into this room and does not hear the answer.
> What he takes is entered. What it cost to take is said here and nowhere else.

*Answer:* Keep the name. Leave the place. Come back as you can.

## VII. Of the fourth place

> The fourth place is the length of a thumb and is not tied.
> It is not a name forgotten and it is not a nought.
> It is where the cloth has room for what the house cannot hold.
> Do not tie it. A knot tied too soon is a debt paid too soon.
> Keep her. Keep the cup. Leave the place as it is.
> Come back as you can. Come back as it can.

*Here the house leaves a space the width of a thumb. It is not a line forgotten. It is the place where the third name would stand if we had kept one, and where the second woman sat when the first figure was read, and where I sat when the third was read. Nobody ties it.*

*Answer:* Keep the name. Leave the place. Come back as you can.

# The pourings, as the cloth keeps them

*Figures in the clerk's hand, copied from the slate. The last column is mine and has no standing at any bank. A dash is a place where no figure was made, not a nought.*

| Pouring | Year | As the slate named her | Figure entered | Figure that came back | What the house did |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| First | Y.H. 806 | A thrower of the low yard, come up to this house | whole, 1200 marks | forty unspent measures | Cut the cloth. Tied the first knot. |
| Second | Y.H. 809 | The same, entered as a new vessel | 210, struck to 190 | — | Tied the second knot. Stopped reading the names. |
| Third | Y.H. 812 | A child three days old in this house | 22 | not weighed | Tied the third knot. Left the fourth place the length of a thumb. |

Draws on this leaf: three. Knots tied: three. Figures struck: one. The cup-ring and the pot's foot are not figures and have not been added.

# What the clerk did

A clerk of the factor came up from Greybank with the evening tally, with ink settled in two fingers of a cuff gone soft from washing. He did not take the verbs out of this leaf, which surprised me. He took the woman out instead, which is the stranger thing to do to a cloth.

- At *keep the name*, he wrote *a birth-name was entered on the slate; no further entry required*. Both stand. The slate has one name and the cloth has three, and they are not the same name.
- At *the figure fell twice*, he set the printed figure above each fallen one, larger. He went down the three rows with his thumb before he wrote it, which is how I know he had read what he was correcting.
- At *she reaches for a door this wall has not got*, he wrote *disorientation following the pouring; not unusual*. True in his language and of no use to a woman standing by a wall.
- At the forty unspent measures he wrote *not in this jurisdiction* and underlined it twice. They have been in every jurisdiction for seven years and nobody has explained them, which is not the same as their being nowhere.
- At the fourth place he put a round nought and wrote *vacancy* beside it. A nought is a number. The place was the length of a thumb.

I have left every line of his standing above mine and mine under it, as the new glaze lies over the old break and does not take the break away.

# What the bowl-leech pressed in the margin

He came up from the south through Greybank with myrrh in his coat-fold and a hand that writes cold.

- Against the door: *a disorder of the memory, not a visitor*.
- Against the third pouring: *settling of the graft; the ordinary worsening*.
- Against *unsafe to marry*, which the old women say when no officer listens: he wrote nothing, and drew a small line under the word *three*.

He is right in a cold language. I have kept his sentences above ours so the next reader can tell which hand was nearer the woman and which was nearer the book.

# What my daughter put in the charcoal

The thick letters are hers, made with a stick from the kiln and not a pen. She has sat at two of the three pourings and says she will not sit at a fourth.

At the cup: “She did not drink. I put the cup in her hand and she held it the way you hold a thing you have been given before and cannot place. Then she let go. Her grandmother drank it and looked at the door. I have asked her twice and she will not say why.”

At the third figure: “I was in the room when they read it over her. They read the number twice and did not once say the word child. I have kept that and put nothing under it.”

At the fourth place, beneath the clerk's nought: “Not a vacancy. The place where my mother's mother stood when the first figure was read. I stood there when the third was read. It is the same place and it is not a number.”

At the answer: “I have said it wrong at three pourings. I have not let anybody correct it.”

# At the head and in the cloth

The tallow is off the lamp I keep low, so that a woman at the table will not change her words when she hears the charcoal. The crust went into the grain at the fifth fold and I have not brushed it; a woman from Low Kiln held it for a child not in this room and would not take a mark for it. The thread of grey wool comes out of the cloth and goes on coming out, though I have shaken the leaf three times. The green bead is lead glaze, pressed in by a girl with the sweetness still at her mouth who leaned over to see what was being written. The iron has never once drawn the warm out of the paper, which is the only kindness this shelf affords.

We sweep the floor of this house on the Last Day and burn what we find in the morning and call it dealt with. The corner above the pots is not swept and has never been called dealt with. I have kept the print where it fell.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The bank keeps the figure. The cloth keeps the knots.
> The woman keeps neither, and comes back anyway.

Struck once and written again underneath, very small:

> She is not the sum of the three. She is what is left after the third.

I asked the clerk, the girl at the wheel and a woman who has sat at a pouring in a house two streets over, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the struck line where it was struck.

The leaf goes back into the fold of the cloth and the cloth goes back on the shelf above the pots. Next Account-day a clerk will come up from Greybank with a soft cuff and put another nought in the fourth place, and I shall read seven stations to the knots and leave the gap where the bell is not heard, and the girl at the wheel will say the answer wrong and I shall not correct her. There is still room in the cloth for a fourth knot, and I have not tied it, and I go on not tying it.
