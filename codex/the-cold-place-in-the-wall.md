I keep the deep watch at the shaft mouth and set down what the wall does when no man stands over it. I have never gone down the Descent. My number is cut in my arm and I state it before my name because that is the order here and the dark does not care what a man calls himself. What follows I have put my hand to or stood near. What I have only heard I have set down as heard, with the word standing where I heard it, because a saying said to the stone is one thing and a saying said looking at a man becomes a bargain.

The smut at the head is coal off my own sleeve. A book of the deep that has never been wiped on a cuff was never taken below. I have not knocked it off.

# The cold place

You know it before you see it. Standing at the wall with the lamp held low, you come to a patch of stone that takes the heat out of the back of your hand, and the patch is not where it was at the last watch. It moves along the crack the way a thumb moves along the seam of a cup, slow and with no haste, and the surveyors enter the moving as a change in the numbers. I have laid my palm on a place that gave back nothing and on a place a hand's breadth off that gave back the lamp's warmth, and the two are not the same stone. The Keldish call the first a cold place and say no more than that over it. To say more gives a thing a place in a man's mouth, and a thing in a man's mouth will be asked about by somebody with a slate.

It keeps to the wall and not the floor. I have never found it crossing the open stones of the Duelling Floor, and men who work the face below the Ninth say the same of that gallery. It goes where the crack goes. A man who follows it with a lamp finds himself at a wall and then at another wall, and the cold place is behind him, and he has carried up nothing he can set on a slate. One of the surveyors told me he had entered its passing three times in a tend and could not have said, on any of the three, which way it had gone. He said this standing at the mouth, which is a dry place and a place to say anything.

It does not hurry and it does not turn aside for iron. I have held a clamp against the stone where a cold place lay and left it there the length of a watch, and the clamp went cold and the cold place was where it had been. Cold iron takes, as the saying goes. Here it takes from the iron and not from the thing, which is a reversal of the order a man learns above ground and one I have not made a lesson of.

# The touch

A miner touched by the seam comes up thin. That is the whole of what I have seen. He does not fall and he does not cry out. In the afternoon his fingers are the colour of milk that has stood two days, and by the end of the shift the warm has gone out of him the way water goes out of a jug left in the cold, and he asks for the stone and not for the physician. I watched a Held man, one who carried three times my number and could swing a clamp the length of a shift, go up the rope after a touch at the face and be thin by the time his boots came to the head. The rope did as much as the thing in the wall. He said nothing, and there is no word in Keldish for what had been done to him and none in Grathic that a man will use above ground.

They say a man touched twice will not come up a third time. I have seen one man with the grey in him twice; he came up both times and went down on the third and did not. I have set the saying and the two together and drawn no line between them. Three men are not a rule and the mountain keeps no second figure. There is a cup at the end of the Floor that nobody takes, and a man may stand beside it all the length of a watch and no one will tell him whose it was. I have not written a name beside that cup and I have never asked.

# The song

*Sung going down at the first watch, not coming up. The number is left as it is not spoken, and every man sings it that way on purpose, because a song that gives the cold a figure is a song a man can carry up and enter. I have set it down as it is sung, with the number where it was not said.*

> One for the lamp and one for the rail,
> one for the arm that the dark will read;
> it goes in the wall where the crack has been,
> and the place it was is the place it leaves.
>
> Count to the wall and do not say the sum ---
> the number stands where the figure stood;
> count them going and count them back,
> and one for the hand that did not close.
>
> It takes through the stone and gives no sign,
> it leaves a man with the colour of milk;
> the Vorr look up and the numbers are right,
> and nobody asks what the numbers tell.

A man going down wants to know where the cold will be. The song tells him he will not know, which is a kindness of the same order as the post at the pass: it stands and says nothing, and a man who walks past it has been told. The last line of the third part is the old line. We sing *tell* where the old word was something else, and I have not set the old word down. A song that gives the cold an appetite is a song a man cannot carry home and set by his fire.

These are the watches I kept a true account of. There were others I did not write because the lamp was low, and one I did not write because I had been touched and had forgotten which watch it was. I have set down both sorts as they stand. The last column is the surveyor's, not mine. I counted what went thin; he counted what the wall gave up.

> *In the narrow upright hand of a Court survey-clerk, borrowed against the leaf at the mouth:* Serpent not a vessel. No deduction arises. Thinness of miners: not entered. Rim-seam figure entered as survey correction. Shed skin: no value entered, being unpriced. Account stands.

I have left his hand standing. He has set *not entered* over the man who did not come up and *no value entered* over the skin, which is the neatest piece of work I have seen out of a Court hand. A survey figure can be carried up and entered and a man who went thin at the face cannot, so there is a kind of justice in it, if the figure is held by the right cuff. I have written the man and the skin underneath in charcoal, where his column cannot reach, and put the figure back where it was not said.

> *In the broad slow hand of a Vorr come up from the Stones, written in the lower margin and not above:* The numbers are correct.

He was asked what the change in the figure meant. He said it twice and turned the slate face down. In Keld that is an answer a man can carry away and learn nothing from, and the sort that keeps. I have not joined his line to the clerk's, and there is a finger's width between them where neither can reach the other.

# The skin

Once in the Ninth a skin is found in the gutter of the face, thin as the skin inside a cup and longer than a man's arm. They say it is the oldest repair material in the seam and that nobody will name it. I have held a shred of one in the lamp-room and put my knuckle through it and found it whole on the other side, which is a thing I cannot explain and have not tried to. I have shut the shred into the fourth fold and set no figure against it.

That is not a kindness on my part. A thing nobody names is a thing no clerk can tax, which is a mercy of a kind and costs the Court nothing, being a thing it has never seen. The Vorr will look at the skin and say the numbers are correct and ask for nothing, and a Vorr who asks for nothing is not a poor man, being a man who has no use for what he would be given. I do not know which of those two facts is the stranger and I have set them down side by side with a finger between.

# What the hearths say

They say it is the First Breaking still moving in the crack, the repair coming undone under the world and going on undoing itself while men cut stone above it. There are songs in Keld with a name in them and a wrong done at the making. I have heard them and I have no quarrel with a song. But I have put my hand on the wall where a cold place lay and on the wall a spear-cast off, and I have watched a place move off a man and settle on another man, and I have followed it to a wall and lost it there. If it is the Breaking still moving, then it moves in particular places and the particular place is the whole of what I can see. What it wants there I have left where I found it.

I think it drinks what the wall loses. I have written that sentence once and have not crossed it out and have not written it a second time, because it is the kind of sentence that gets a man called a natural philosopher, and natural philosophers are not set over the deep watch with a lamp and a length of cord. What it gives in exchange I have not seen, and I have asked no man to look for it.

# At the head and in the margins

The coal at the head is mine and came off the sleeve I wipe the lamp with; it goes into the grain and no wet cuff takes it out. The iron-red at the second corner came off the mine-reeve's point while he checked a man who had given a figure that would not hold; he did not look at me and I did not look at the man. The hot lead at the fold came off a sample wrapped in the same paper on Bone 19 and has burnt the word *seam* clean through; I have not written the word back in, because a hole in that word is the honest state of it. The feather between the second and third leaves was on the wall below the survey room after a warm night and was gone by the time I found the leaf to put it in. The shaving in the fourth fold kept its heat through one watch on the stone floor and is cold now, which is the order of things here. The ring is off a cup that stood from the first watch to the last and was drunk by neither of us, which is how small ale is drunk in Keld when there is a thing to say and no proper word for it. The grey along the outer fold is not mine and not the Court's, and I have not swept it. There is no Last Day above the shaft mouth, and below there is less.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> It does not take the man. It takes the place the man was keeping.

I asked a miner who had been touched, a surveyor come up from the stones, a Vorr, and a clerk of the factor at Greybank, and had four answerings and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The leaves go back into the board cover and the cover under the lamp, and my number is called at the mouth whether the arm that carried it came up or not.

There is a cold place beyond the Ninth that I have not walked to, where the paper sweats and the figure has not been changed by any hand. Next Stone I will go in with the lamp shut and a coil of cord and stand there the length of a watch, and if the wall gives back warmth where no man has stood and the numbers move without a hand to move them, I will set that down too, in charcoal, with the verb standing as it happened and no figure at the end of the row.
