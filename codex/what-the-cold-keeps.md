title: What the Cold Keeps, Being an Account of the Country Beyond the Cold Pass
kind: A quarryman's itinerary and travel account, set down on the back of a spoiled Keldish survey sheet and a strip of birch bark; part road-log, part inventory of what came back, part complaint, with the corrections of a Court survey-clerk in Ledgerhand, a Keldish surveyor's note in the margin, and one line at the foot in a hand nobody will own
provenance: Written in Bone and Ash of Y.H. 811 at Keld Keep, on the Rim Road and beyond the Cold Pass, by Orrel of Stonemouth, a quarryman who went west as a hired guide to a survey party and came back down the Rim Road in Seed of Y.H. 812 with a stone in his pocket, one year gone by the slate and three by his face, and no memory of the debt he went to recover. Copied at Greybank in Ilmen of Y.H. 813 onto the back of a discarded tally-roll, sewn with wire into a fold of oiled sailcloth and carried in a tin. I went west to recover a debt and came back with a stone. The debt had a name on it when I left and no name when I returned, and I have left the place where the name stood rather than put a figure in it. A figure would stand. I have seen what stands and what does not out there, and I am not the man to say which of the two is true.

I am a quarryman of Stonemouth. I cut lime and coarse sandstone out of the bank where the Ferr comes down, and I know the weight of a stone by the hand before I know it by the eye. In Seed of Y.H. 811 a surveyor come through the village hired me to carry instruments and guide a party up the Rim Road, and there was a debt between us that I thought a man could collect if he walked far enough behind it. This is the account I kept. I have set down what happened and what did not, and where the two disagree I have left both standing, which is a thing a clerk cannot do and a quarryman can, because stone does not care which of your accounts it lies under.

# The road as far as the last pass

From Sill to Keld Keep is a hundred and twenty leagues of improving road and worsening weather. Only fools and factors travel it without a warm-stone. I went with one in a fold of wool against my belly and another in the cart, and I do not say now who paid for the second, because I have asked and the answer comes back in a voice I do not recognise.

At Keld Keep a man states his measure before he states his name. I stated mine and was told it was a lowland figure and would not carry me ten leagues beyond the pass, which was true: it did not. The survey-clerk who hired me wrote this down in the margin as a kindness, since it cost him nothing. In that city a man's number is cut into his forearm at thirteen and cut out again when he comes back thin, and I saw three men with smooth places on their arms where a number had been, and I saw a fourth who had had his changed twice and could not say which of the two he had been born to. None of them asked me my name. All of them asked my measure. This is a difference a Grath man notices and does not enjoy noticing.

Ten leagues beyond the Keep the road goes through the Cold Pass, which Karric VII shut in Y.H. 741 and the agreement of Y.H. 747 fixed as the border. There is a survey post there and no gate. The Warden's surveyors do not promise to bring a man back, and this also was written down, in the same hand. I asked the clerk whether a promise not made was a promise kept, and he said that was a question for a lowland moot, and went on writing.

Past the last pass no map is honest. I carried that sentence out of Keld and set it here because it was the only true thing anybody told me in that country. The survey party turned back at the post. I went on, because the debt was on that side and because a man who has come a hundred and thirty leagues to collect a thing is a fool in exactly the way that keeps him walking.

# The country itself

There is no weather in the Waste. Weather is a change, and the cold there does not change. It is not a great cold, the way Stone is cold in Sill; it is an even, patient cold that has nowhere to go and all the time in the world to get there. A man does not freeze in it so much as come gradually to agree with it.

The Keeping teaches that the Waste is the sink where everything unheld goes. I have no quarrel with that; a man who has worked a quarry knows about sinks. What the Keeping does not teach is that a sink is a country, and a country has people in it, and those people have no use for a slate. There is nothing to count out there. No ember changes hands, no mark is made, no collector comes with his iron tube and his clean figure. The Court has never opened an account beyond the pass, which is the only mercy in this world that costs the Court nothing.

They say the cold is not empty. They say it is full of what nobody kept. I have no reason to disbelieve them and several to believe them, and one of those reasons is in my pocket.

# The people, and what they give

The bands beyond the Rim are called Wastelings. They have no banks, no Returns and no word for a debt. They are not the Unbanked the Court speaks of, who have a design and a name and people to argue with; they are simply the spent. A man there owns what he is still carrying and nothing else, which makes for a short inventory and a long silence.

They leave their dead sitting upright facing the way they came. They do not cover them. I asked why, and a woman who had stood beside one for a morning said that a man who had come that far had a right to be seen off in the direction he had travelled, and that covering him would be a sort of argument with him, and they did not argue with the dead. I set that down as she said it. A Grathic hearth-maid would have covered the body with a fold and crossed two fingers and given me a date. Both acts are kindnesses. I have never seen two kindnesses so unlike and so complete.

A Wasteling will give a stone, or a name, or a day's warmth, and expect nothing back. This stopped me longer than anything else in that country, because in Stonemouth a gift that expects nothing is the one thing a man cannot enter, and a thing a man cannot enter is a thing he cannot afford. I took the stone. I have not entered it. The woman who gave it to me wanted nothing, and I still do not know what to do with that.

Ask a Wasteling what he fears and he will ask whether you are warm. At first I took this for evasion. On the ninth day I understood it was the whole of his information, and the only question worth asking a man who has come that far.

# The ninth day

I went out with a porter of Keld who had opened a way in his forearm and had the numbers cut out and put in again twice. He was a man of few sentences and those all about the road. On the ninth morning out he stood close to my little fire, closer than a man stands to warm himself, and I thought he was warming himself. He was not warming himself.

The Keldish call such a shape a cold-eater. It stands close to a vessel and draws its Warm away; it takes and does not give. There was no struggle and no sound. In the afternoon his fingers were the colour of skimmed milk gone off, and by the third watch he had forgotten his numbers, and by morning he had forgotten that he had ever had a name. We did not say the thing's name aloud, because a name gives a thing a place in an account, and there is no account out there.

The cost is the part the books leave out. I carried one warm-stone out and brought two back, and one of them is his. I have not given it to anybody because there is nobody to give it to, and because if I set it down I should have to say what it cost. It sits in the gutter of this account where a figure should be, and the Clerk has been at it, as you will see.

# A song they sing at the sitting-down

*Set down in Grathic letters from the mouth of the woman who gave the stone. The last line is said three times, and the third time is for the one who cannot say it.*

> Beyond the last pass there is no weather,
> no mark, no debt, no name to spend;
> the dead sit facing the way they came
> and the cold keeps what nobody kept.
>
> A stone for a stone and a name for a name,
> and a day's warmth for whoever can carry it;
> ask me what I fear and I will ask you this:
> are you warm? are you warm? are you warm?

I asked her what the third asking was for. She said it was for the child who had not yet learned to be cold, and that a child could not answer and the question was asked anyway, which is the way the world treats a child. I have left that in, though a clerk would take it out.

# What went out and what came back

| Went out with | Came back with |
|---|---|
| One warm-stone in a fold of wool | Two stones; the second is not mine and I have not entered it |
| A clay cup out of the meal mill at Sill | A cup, cold, that answers flat when set down, and nobody to tap it |
| A slate with a name and a debt on it | The same slate, one year older, with the name gone and a figure in another hand |
| A porter of Keld with numbers on his forearm | A man with a smooth arm who answered to no number and went on walking |
| A year by the slate, Seed of Y.H. 811 to Seed of Y.H. 812 | Three years by my face, and a morning I cannot account for |
| A debt to collect | A stone to keep |

There is a place in this table where a line should stand and does not. I have set the stone in it. If you hold the leaf to the lamp you can feel the stone through the paper, which is more than I can say for the figure.

# What the Clerk did

A survey-clerk of the Court borrowed the leaves at Greybank. He struck out *the cold keeps what nobody kept* and set above it, *The measure is retained in the Waste.* Against the second warm-stone he wrote *no value entered.* Where I had left the place where the porter's name stood, he put a round nought and wrote *vacancy* under it.

Against the debt he set a figure and wrote *satisfied* beside it. It is the only satisfaction I have ever been given that I did not receive. I have left his hand standing and mine under it, the way a quarry leaves the spoil above the stone.

# From the man who hired me

At the foot of the second leaf, in the broad slow hand of the Keldish surveyor, who borrowed the leaves before the Clerk did and returned them with the fold changed:

> The numbers are correct.

He was asked, beyond the post, to translate a marking cut in a stone that was not cut with iron. He gave that answer and turned the slate face down. I asked him twice and got the same words both times. In Keld that is an answer a man can carry away and learn nothing from, and it is the sort of answer that keeps. I have set it where he wrote it and added nothing.

# At the head and in the gutter

The coal dust at the head came off the surveyor's cuff in the lamp-room at Keld Keep; he leaned over my sheet and would not sit down. The hot lead at the fold came from a sample wrapped in the same paper. It has burnt a small hole clean through the word *pass*, and I have not written the word in again, because a hole in that word is the honest state of it.

The frost-crust in the gutter came up out of the paper on the ninth morning, when I carried the leaves inside my coat and the coat froze to my chest. The white hair is not mine and is not the porter's; it lay pressed between the leaves when I found them in the snow, and I have kept it there. The small grey stone is the one I came back with; I have folded it into the gutter so that the account and the thing it accounts for travel together. The grey print along the outer fold is not mine. There is no Last Day out there and nothing gets swept.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> A man is a vessel set down facing the way he came.
> Nobody covers him, and the cold does not lose him.

I asked the woman who gave the stone, the survey-clerk and a factor come up from Greybank, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The leaves go back in the tin, and the tin goes under the bench at Stonemouth where I keep the things I cannot enter. If I am asked what I went for I shall say a debt, because that is the word I was taught. If I am asked what I brought back I shall say a stone, and put my hand in my pocket to feel it, as I have done every morning since Seed of Y.H. 812.
