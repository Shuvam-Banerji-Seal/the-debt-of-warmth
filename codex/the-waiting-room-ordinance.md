I keep the waiting-book beside the collector's table. A vessel is drawn at a door, brought in at dusk and banked at first grey; between the two there is a night, and the ordinance has no word for a night. I have copied the articles as they stand in the College book and put the people back underneath, because an ordinance read with nobody in it is one no person in this room has to answer for. Where a hand has corrected me I have left both readings, the way the damp leaves its line and the line stays when the wall is dry.

They were gathered at the Hearthseat in Y.H. 669, the year after Pell Varr put down the Ash Rising, when it was found that a vessel could be kept from dusk to morning without anybody having to say what had been done to it in the dark. A rule with no night on it is a rule nobody has had to keep. These have been kept.

# The ordinance as it stands beside the table

## I. Of the receiving

1. A vessel is received, not a person. It is received at dusk and is not banked in the hour in which it was drawn. Nothing is entered against the hours between.

> *Margin:* A person is brought in and set on a pallet. They walk in, and most look at the door on the way to see where it is. I have put the looking underneath, because the ordinance has a line for the vessel and no place to put the face.

2. The room holds what the tube took until the banker comes. The heat stands in the vessel overnight, which is the reason given in the College book and is not a kindness, being entered nowhere.

> *Margin:* It is a kindness. I have not written the word in the article, because a kindness written in an ordinance becomes a due, and a due wants a payer. I have put it here at the edge, where it asks nobody for anything.

3. No birth-name is taken in this room. Where a slate-name stands, that name is used; where none stands, a place is left the length of a name and is not filled by the officer. A name given after dusk is not entered, being a thing said where no writing is done.

> *Margin:* A woman told me her name at the second changing of the lamp and asked me to put it on the slate so that somebody would know. I told her the slate went up the road and the name did not go on it. *Then it goes with me,* she said, and put her hand to the middle of her palm where a ring had been worn tight and long. I have left the place unfilled.

4. A hearth-maid who comes with a stone is not turned away and is not entered. The stone is set at the middle and not at the ends. What the room does with a warmed belly between dusk and morning has no column and is not asked about at the bank.

## II. Of the night

5. The lamp is kept low and the tube is wrapped and hung on its nail; the tube is not left on the bench. A pale round place left by a tube set down is not a wound, is not entered, and is not rubbed before the officer has gone.

6. The officer visits once in the night and does not wake a vessel that is sleeping. Where a vessel speaks, no matter arises and nothing is entered. What is said to the wall is not a bargain and does not bind the Keeping to anything.

> *Margin:* Three sentences were said here in the night I wrote this leaf: one to the wall, one to the dish of water, and one said to me while I held the lamp up. I entered none. I set them at the edge, the length of each, the only place where a sentence can stand without a person being made to answer for it.

7. Nothing is owed to a vessel that is waiting. What it gives, refuses or promises between dusk and morning is not a payment and is not received. The account is not opened in this room; it is opened at the bank.

> *Margin:* A man offered me a figure in the night for a debt not his to pay. I told him the room took nothing. He shut his fist on it until the banker came, then put it back into his sleeve and went up the road. A room that takes nothing keeps a figure where it cannot be entered, which is more than the slate can do.

## III. Of the morning and the banking

8. At first grey the vessels are counted and handed to the banker. One part in twelve is the figure read, as the notice says. No other figure is read in this room.

> *Margin:* The figure the households of this town pay is not the figure on the notice. I have heard it under the moot-post and have never seen it written. I have no column for the other figure and have not made one, so it stays at the edge of a leaf that goes up the road in a tin.

9. A figure read with feeling is read again in a clean voice. The reader is set back and the person whose figure it was is not named.

> *Margin:* I read a figure once with my voice going toward the pallet instead of toward the book, and the banker's clerk set me back one reading. A figure read at a person is a figure about a person, and by supper it wants a payer under it.

10. Where consent is withheld, the wrist is held until consent is entered. No figure is entered for the holding and no deduction arises upon it. The holding is not a punishment, being provided for in the ordinance.

> *Margin:* There is a pale round place on the left wrist of a man who came through this room, the shape of the clip and not the tube. I have seen it twice and do not know which visit left it. Both rings stay at the edge, where a clerk can see them and choose not to read them.

11. A vessel that has gone worse before morning has had a settling of the measure. It is not a worsening and is not an entry. The watch is kept to first grey and the banker is told the hour and nothing beside it.

> *Margin:* She went colder between the second changing and the lamp going out, and made a small sound into the dish of water. I entered the hour. The sound stays at the edge, shorter than most figures on this leaf and harder to carry up the road.

# The table of nights

*The figure is the banker's and is set first. What stands beneath is mine and is not a figure.*

| Condition | As entered at the bank | What stands beneath |
|---|---|---|
| Vessel received at dusk | one vessel, pending; no deduction arising | A person set down on a pallet who looked at the door |
| Night spoken | no matter arising | Three sentences, the length of each |
| Tube set down on the bench | no entry | A pale ring, not a wound; the thinnest paper |
| Consent withheld | consent entered in due course | A wrist held past the turning of the watch |
| Vessel worse at morning | a settling; no entry arising | The small sound made into the dish of water |

13. The banker enters a woman who has spent the night on a pallet as one vessel, pending, no deduction arising. It is a true figure and a true figure about the wrong thing.

> *Margin:* In the narrow upright hand of a College clerk, against the first row: *One vessel, pending; no deduction arising.* In mine, underneath: *A person set down on a pallet who looked at the door.* Both stand. I did not learn the looking from a page, which is the only reason I can still say it.

# Of faults and their correcting

| Fault | As entered at the bank | What the under-clerk sets beneath |
|---|---|---|
| A verb in the ordinance | The page copied again, in the same hand | The verb restored where it stood |
| A birth-name spoken in the room | Nothing entered | The name, at the edge, the length of it |
| A figure read with feeling | The reader set back one reading | The person whose figure it was |
| The tube left on the bench | Taken up and wrapped; the ring not rubbed | The ring, at the edge, and the hand that set it down |
| A stone set at the ends | Unwrapped, set at the middle | The wrist that knew before the eye did |

14. A fault copied twice in one night is a fault the under-clerk has not understood, and an older clerk sits with him beside the tin until he has. The waiting-book is not a rod; it is a book with a place where a person stood.

# The form of a waiting-entry

*Copied with the blanks as they stand. In nine nights no officer has filled the last line with his own name.*

> Received this day, being ----day of -------, in the year of the Hearth ----, one vessel, slate-name ----------------------, drawn at the ------- hour, set on the pallet before the ------- hour, the night passed with no matter arising, consent entered at first grey, banked to ----------------------, one in twelve as the notice says; written by ----------------------, whose hand went to the slate before his eyes went to the pallet and whose mouth came last.

# The pallet-song, pasted inside the tin

*Said low at the changing of the lamp, with the dish between the pallet and the door. Never while the banker's clerk is at the book, and never twice in one night. The last line of the second verse belongs to the women on the pallets; the officers say it wrong on purpose and have never been told why.*

> Four on the board and one for the nail,
> a pallet low and a dish of water;
> the tube on its nail and the wool on the tube,
> and a night nobody has to answer for.
>
> One in twelve as the notice says,
> read twice for those that came late up the road;
> the figure goes up and the ring stays here,
> and the room keeps what the room was told.
>
> Hand to the slate and then to the face,
> and the mouth comes last, which is the rule;
> do not ask the wall what it heard in the dark,
> the wall has no column and does not reply.

The scorch across the third verse came from holding the leaf too near the lamp while a woman said something I wanted the end of. I have flattened it; the words hold. She did not tell me the end.

# What the borrowed hands did

A clerk of the College came down from the Hearthseat with ink in two fingers of a cuff gone soft from washing. Against *a person is brought in and set on a pallet* he set *the vessel is received*, true of the figure and false of the looking at the door; I have put mine back underneath. Against the three sentences at the edge he drew a line and wrote *No such place*; he had to read every one before he could write that none was worth keeping, and I watched him do it. Against *consent entered at first grey* he set *consent recorded*, a hand further from the wrist.

The other hand is tall and leaning, from the Steward's room. Against *one in twelve* it wrote *the lawful deduction*, and against the pale round place on the bench, *an article of value, not entered to a person*. I have struck neither out. A hand that writes articles of value for persons not standing in the room has made a trade of not saying which.

# At the head and in the gutter

The brown at the head is iron-gall and mine, from holding the quire open through a reading. The filings in the gutter came out of the wool sleeve the tube rides in; there is always iron in the fold, and it has never once drawn the warmth out of the paper, which is the one mercy this room has shown a leaf. The damp along the foot came up out of the wall, which is colder than the Court room next door; it takes the corners off figures and leaves the figures. The grey in the fold is Ash off a cuff that had been in a corner where no cuff has business lying; I have not swept it. The pale ring is where the tube was set down while the officer went for a cup; not a wound, but the thinnest paper on the leaf. The green bead is lead glaze, pressed in by a girl come up from High Kiln with the sweetness still at her mouth; she touched the paper and went back up the terrace. The bare square is where I scraped a word away and put no other in its place. There is no Last Day in a Waiting Room. The damp stays where it fell and goes into the next night under the book, and nobody calls it dealt with.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The room keeps the night. The slate keeps the figure.
> Between the two a person lies down and gets up and is carried into a number with nobody in it.

I asked the banker's clerk, a woman who brought the dish of water, and an officer from the collector's table, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written.

The leaves go back into the tin. Tomorrow a vessel will be brought in at dusk, and I shall write the figure before I look at the face and put the verbs back where a borrowed hand took them out. At first grey one in twelve will be read in a clean voice, and what was said in the night will go up the road with the tin, which carries a great deal and reads none of it.
