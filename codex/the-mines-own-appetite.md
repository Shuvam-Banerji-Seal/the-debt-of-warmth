I keep the Ninth Gallery for the Warden, or I keep the men who work in it, which is a different duty and a colder one. I have been down nine years and I have stopped a shift twice. The first time was for a man. The second was for a beetle. I have set the two in one tally and drawn no line between them, because the mine-reeve's figures do not allow a line and neither do mine.

This is the backs of seam-tallies the slate-damp spoiled, and the figures went up to Greybank where figures go, which was a mercy of a kind: a figure the damp has eaten cannot be entered against a man. I write in charcoal now, on folds of oiled survey-cloth sewn with wire, because coal goes into the grain and no cuff lifts it off. The dust at the head is mine. There is no Last Day beneath this Keep; the dust has nowhere to go and nobody comes down to sweep it. A book that has never been carried in a pocket was never taken on a shift.

# What it is

It is a grub. I set that down before anything else, because the cutter's song does not, and because a man who calls it the mine's own appetite has half explained it already and will want a figure for the other half.

It is the colour of a lamp-flame seen through smoke, curled like a fingernail, and soft in a way nothing else down here is soft. I can find no eyes on it. It has a mouth that works at the stone without stopping, and the stone goes to powder behind it the way a crust goes to crumbs in a wet hand. I have held one in the flat of my palm and felt it take the warmth out of my skin the way a wet cloth takes it --- slowly, and without haste, and with no sign that it knew I was there. I have opened eleven. In every one I found stone meal and a small clear place near the head that was warm when I put the back of a finger to it. I have set the clear place down and have not written what it is for. A man who writes what a grub wants in a book he carries under his coat is a man who will be asked next what the Keep wants, and there is no end to that sort of work.

# The note in the block

It eats from the inside. This is the first thing a new man gets wrong and the thing that costs him a shift. A block that looks sound will go to dust in a man's hands if the grub has been at it, and a block that goes to dust in the hands has taken a man's wage and given him nothing to show for it. You tap before you cut. A sound block answers full under the knuckle; one with a grub in it gives back a small dry sound, like a pot with a hair in it. I have heard the two notes off the same block a tend apart, full and then dry, and the grub was between them.

The second sign is the sweating. A gallery with grubs in it sweats where no seam was cut, and the air turns sweetish and hot, and the smell of hot lead comes up before anything shows. That smell is the whole warning a man gets down here. I have known men who knew it and went on cutting because the block was nearly out and a block nearly out is a wage not yet paid. I have written what they did and have not written what I would have done in their place, which is the same thing said by a man who has never been that hungry.

# The stop

The mine-reeve stops a shift for one grub. This is not kindness. He counts every life that goes down and every ember of iron that comes up, and those two figures have never agreed and were never meant to. A stopped shift is a figure that does not balance, and a figure that does not balance is a thing he has to explain to a man with a slate in Greybank. He stops it anyway. I have seen him stand at the shaft mouth with a beetle on a shaving of stone in his palm and send nine men up the rope, and say nothing to any of them about what he had in his hand.

I will set one sweating down, because it happened and because it stands behind every shift I have stopped since. It came three years before the Thin Year. There was no cry and no bell, only the sweetish lead smell coming up the gallery ahead of the heat, and the men at the face went down where they stood. The stone afterwards was rich and was cut into warm-stone that held its heat a generation and went south for ten marks a block on the Keel. I came up. I have not written how many came up with me, because the reeve's figure and the figure I carry are not the same figure and I will not set one over the other.

In this country a man states his measure before his name. I have stated mine to the mine-reeve nine times and had it entered nine times. The grub has never been asked for either.

# The verse

*Sung going down the shaft, not coming up. The third line is the old line and every man sings it wrong on purpose, because a song that gives the grub a reason is a song he can carry up and set by his own fire, and a man with a reason by his own fire goes down the next morning with his hands steady. I have set it down as it is sung, with the wrong line standing and the gap where the men leave it.*

> One for the lamp and one for the lead,
> one for the block that answers full;
> it comes to the warm and it comes to the cut,
> and the mountain takes what is owed.
>
> Stop for one and the shift stands;
> stop for two and the shift goes up;
> the reeve counts iron and I count men,
> and the two will not lie in one row.
>
> Fried in the pan for the ones below,
> the only warmth they are given to keep;
> we sing the old line wrong and leave the gap ---
> the place where the block stood before.
>
> Count them going and count them up,
> count the dust and count the full;
> six go down and the sixth comes back ---
> the old man's line, and we sing it wrong.

They sing it wrong on purpose. It does not come to the cut. I have set a block cut this morning beside a block that no tool has touched, and had the grub in the untouched one by the second watch. The men know this as well as I do. They sing it the other way because a grub that wants nothing from a man is a creature a man cannot forgive for taking the block, and a man who cannot forgive has to carry something up the shaft. There is a cutter of the Duelling Floor who sings the last line twice and will not say why, and I have let him keep it.

# The tally

These are the shifts I kept a true account of. There were others I did not write because my hands were full and one I did not write because the lamp went out and I sat in the dark until the next bell. I have set down both sorts as they stand. > *In the narrow upright Ledgerhand of the mine-reeve's clerk, struck through the "Stopped" column:* Grubs not a vessel. Delay to working: not entered. Warm in stone is standing account, realised on cutting. Account stands.

> *And below the "Entered" column, in the same hand:* 1+0+1+2+0+4 = 8. Received.

I have left his hand standing. He has put *realised on cutting* against stone that was warm before any of us came down, which is a neat phrase and a true entry and a lie besides. I have written the smell in the margin below, where his ruler cannot reach, and put my own mark beside it in charcoal.

> *Below, in my hand:* Bone 19. Hot lead at the face before first lamp. Stopped at one grub. The iron and the men did not agree, as they never do. No figure, and none asked for.

# The pan

The under-vessed eat them fried. There is a brazier at the change of shift and a woman who fries what the grubs are swept into, and the ones below eat it because it is warm and because nothing else they are given is. An old woman of the working said to me that it is the only warmth they are given that they get to keep. I have not put that sentence in the tally. A thing of that kind, once written, gets carried up the shaft and comes back with a figure on it.

> *In broad charcoal on the corner of the second leaf, the letters gone soft where a hand with meal in it touched them:* Do not write our names on the pan. We eat what comes out of the stone because it is put in front of us. If the grub is the mountain's anger, then the mountain is kinder to us than the men who count us. --- A.

I did not write her line and she did not write mine. I have set hers above my own, because hers went soft first and is entitled to stand.

# What the hearths say

They say the mountain sends the grub to punish greedy digging, and there are songs in the First Seam with a man's name in them and a wrong done at a face in the year before the Descent was cut. I have heard them and I have no quarrel with a song. I have opened eleven and found a beetle, and found the stone it was eating warm though no fire has burned in that gallery in the years I have known it. I have found the same grub in the walling-in below the survey room, deeper than any shift I have worked, and I have no way to date a beetle. I have written what it does and not what it wants. I have written the sentence about the stone being warm before the Keep was cut out once and crossed it out twice, because it is the kind of sentence that gets a man called a natural philosopher, and natural philosophers are not set over the Ninth Gallery.

# At the head and in the margins

The coal at the head is mine and came off a block I carried two shifts in this pocket before I thought to wrap it; it has worked into the grain and comes up in damp weather, so that the leaf tastes of the gallery in any room. The sweet in the second leaf is hot lead and not a thing I put there; it came up out of the paper after Bone 19 and has gone into the fibre. The warm-stone shaving in the fold kept its heat one night on the stone floor and is cold now, which is the common fate of things kept warm for somebody. The scorch on the third leaf is where the lamp guttered while I held the fold to read, and the hand that pulled it back was mine and is not entered. The charcoal is thin where the wrap lay against my thigh. The clerk's ruling I have not touched. The woman's line I have set above my own. The song is as the men sang it, with the old line wrong and the gap standing the length they leave it. There is no Last Day down here and nothing of this is swept in the morning and called dealt with.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> It eats what somebody paid to keep warm. The pan is the only account that pays back.

I asked a clerk, a cutter who sings the last line twice, an old woman with meal in her hands, and a man who came up from a sweating, and had four answerings and no hand. I have left the line where it was written, which is the only honest way to keep a sentence you cannot sign.

There is a place at the end of the Ninth Gallery where the stone sweats though no seam has been cut in it and the air comes sweet before the lamp is lit. I have not cut into it. Next Bone I will take a lamp and a short clamp and go down ahead of the shift, and I will count the grubs and the blocks and the men, and if the stone comes up warm where no fire has been I will set that down too, in charcoal, with the verb left in it and no figure at the end of the row.
