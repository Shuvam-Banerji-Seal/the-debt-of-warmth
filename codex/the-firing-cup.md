I keep the cup at the firing. It is not my cup. It stands on the shelf above the racks with a thumb-wide flaw in the lip and comes down only when the kiln is drawn, and I set it on the bench and go away, and the cup does the calling. There is no word in our yard for a man who pours for himself, which is a mercy, because there is a word for almost everything else.

I set this down because a man come up from Slate Row stood at the yard well and asked what the women sang when the last pot was out. He wrote the answer in a book while I was still saying it, and what he wrote had no verbs in it and no hands, and he went away thanking me. What follows is what we said before he came and what we said again after he had gone back over the north bridge. I have put his hand above mine where he set it, and the kiln-master's above his, and the hearth-maid's in the margin where she wrote it, and my words under all three, as the floor keeps the ash and the foot that raised it.

The charcoal at the head is mine. I held the tally down on the kiln shelf in the draught off the yard door and did not know I had marked it.

# The order of the cup

*Written small on the back of the tally, before the verses, in the hand I use for the rack count.*

- Sung at the draw, after the last pot is out and before the embers are raked. A song that runs into the raking is a song with a figure in it, and no figure has ever come out of this yard singing.
- Standing, with faces to the kiln mouth. Said looking at a person it becomes an argument, and there is enough argument in Low Kiln over the winter firing without carrying it home.
- One cup goes round. A woman sets it down; the next woman lifts it. Nobody pours for herself, and nobody wipes the rim, which is how a flaw in the lip comes to be known by everybody without being named.
- The first pour goes to the fire, the second to the pot that answered full, the third to the pot that answered flat. The fourth stands where it stands and is drunk by nobody, and is carried back to the shelf still full.
- Stop while the bell goes at the Cross. It is counting a different thing. Begin again when the stroke after the gap has gone.
- The last verse is said twice, once to the fire and once into the bench. The second saying is for the ones who carried in and did not carry out. They are not named in it and their places are not filled.

# The song

*The cup goes left. The kiln breathes behind the wall. Refrain after every verse, said low into the bench.*

> Round with the cup and round with the fire,
> a whole pot sings and a cracked one lies;
> one in twelve goes up the road
> and the rest goes round the yard.

## I. Of the cup

> This cup came down off the highest shelf
> with a thumb-wide flaw in the lip;
> we keep it for the firing and for nothing else,
> and a woman who asks why may carry her own.

> *Round with the cup and round with the fire...*

## II. Of the first pour

> The first pour goes to the fire, not to a face;
> the damper's shut and the fire keeps its mouth.
> It takes what we set to it and gives back heat
> and never once says what it kept.

> *Round with the cup and round with the fire...*

## III. Of the whole pot

> The second to the pot that answered full,
> knocked on the bench with a clean whole note;
> it'll hold water till the day it won't,
> and that day's another firing's business.

> *Round with the cup and round with the fire...*

## IV. Of the flat one

> The third to the pot that gave back flat,
> set by the door with the crack showing;
> don't mend it pretty and don't put it back ---
> an honest fault's a thing the neighbours know.

> *Round with the cup and round with the fire...*

## V. Of the backs and the hands

> Here's to the backs that the wet pots bent,
> here's to the hands that went in and stayed;
> the kiln doesn't ask whose back it was
> and the cup doesn't know who's drinking.

> *Round with the cup and round with the fire...*

## VI. Of the bell

> The bell at the Cross goes four and four
> and stops where the fifth should stand;
> we drink to the gap and say nothing over it,
> the way the yard says nothing at the grey.

> *Round with the cup and round with the fire...*

## VII. Of the figure

> One in twelve goes up the road in a clean hand,
> printed and current and read twice aloud;
> a figure you hear isn't a figure you paid ---
> pass the cup. It's the only figure here.

> *Round with the cup and round with the fire...*

## VIII. Of the going of the light

> There's grey in the corner where the promise went;
> the broom goes round it, the bucket goes round it,
> and the house goes round it at the going of the light;
> we leave it on the Last Day and burn it in the morning.

> *Round with the cup and round with the fire...*

## IX. The last verse

> So pass it left. Don't pour for yourself.
> The pot keeps the note and the cup keeps the crack,
> the yard keeps the place where the flat one stood,
> and the name goes round when the cup goes round.

There is a space before the last refrain in the singing. I have left it on the tally. It is the length of a name and of nothing else, and the cup goes around it the way the broom goes around the grey. The refrain is then said twice, the second time into the bench, and nobody lifts the bench to see what was said to it.

> *Round with the cup and round with the fire,
> a whole pot sings and a cracked one lies;
> one in twelve goes up the road
> and the rest goes round the yard.*
>
> *The rest goes round the yard. The rest goes round.*

# What the kiln-master set above it

The broad charcoal belongs to the man who fires the Vail kiln. He marks hands with it and writes as though he were cutting a notch. He borrowed the leaf on a morning when the kiln was going down and returned it with the fold changed.

- At *a whole pot sings and a cracked one lies*: “A pot that sings before the firing is green and will slump. There's no note to argue with till it's been through.”
- At *an honest fault's a thing the neighbours know*: “Set it by the door. Don't leave it where a buyer can knock it.”
- At *don't mend it pretty*: “Mend it how you like, but let the seam show. There's a cupworm in this yard that bores from inside and leaves a pot looking sound.”
- At *the kiln doesn't ask whose back it was*: “It doesn't. That's why the count's kept at the door and not at the mouth.”

I have left his corrections standing above my words. He is right about the pot and wrong about the song, which was never only about pots, and he has read every line of it with one eye on the kiln. He did not sit down while he wrote, and said a song that had never been in a kiln-house was not worth the paper.

# What the clerk did

A clerk of the factor's house at Greybank borrowed the leaf on an Account-day, being a man who cannot let paper lie. He writes in the narrow upright hand, with the verbs taken out as though a verb were a thing a man could hide in a cuff and carry past a weigh.

- At *the fire keeps its mouth* he wrote *fuel consumed; no entry*, which leaves the fire standing and takes the mouth away.
- At *it takes what we set to it and gives back heat* he set *heat transferred to chamber, current*, and underlined it twice.
- At *one in twelve goes up the road* he wrote *deduction one in twelfths, read twice, current*, as though the reading and the paying were the same act performed in two places.
- At *a figure you hear isn't a figure you paid* he wrote *figures of account, not of price*. I have never argued less with a sentence of his.
- At *the cup's got a lip and the lip's got a flaw* he set *vessel fractured; retained in service*, which is the truest and coldest thing on the leaf.
- Where the space stands before the last refrain he put a round nought and wrote *vacancy* beside it. He has put a figure where a name should stand, which is the whole art of that hand.

I have put my words back underneath and left his standing above. He takes the verbs out because a thing always true has no need of them. I put them back because a woman at the end of a firing does not know she is hearing something always true, and wants to know which hand held the cup before hers.

# What the hearth-maid put in the margin

The small careful hand is Alda's, who keeps the birth-slates for the district and carries soap and warm milk in the same order every morning. She wrote in the margin because she would not have her words on the face of the leaf where a collector might read them going past. I have copied her as she set them down.

- At *the first pour goes to the fire, not to a face*: “Same order at a birth. The hand goes to the slate before it goes to the child. Don't make more of it than that.”
- At *here's to the hands that went in and stayed*: “A round forgets who it was for by the third cup. Names are better. Ask me why I say that and I'll give you a date.”
- At *we drink to the gap*: “The fifth isn't a drink. It's a place. Leave it the length it is.”
- At *an honest fault's a thing the neighbours know*: “A child came back to us this year who liked the smell of wet clay before he had seen a pot. I have written that nowhere else. Keep it where a collector's thumb won't go.”
- At the space before the last refrain: “The third saying is not for the pot and not for the fire. I have been asked whose it was and answered with a date. Both stand.”

# At the head and in the gutter

The grey curl at the head is off the kiln shelf and went into the grain when I held the leaf down. The tallow is mine, off the lamp I keep turned low behind the racks. The crust-grease on the second leaf is from a browned edge laid down while I was telling a girl to eat and she would not. The ring is small ale, set down at the first verse and taken up at the last, by which time it had gone the colour of the pot it stood on. The green bead came off a woman from High Kiln who stood with the sweetness at one corner of her mouth, leaned over to see the writing, touched the leaf and went back up the terrace without buying. The scorch took one word off the sixth verse and I have not written it back, because a verse with a word gone is a verse said slowly, and ours are all said slowly. The grey along the gutter came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying; nobody will own it, and there is no Last Day behind a rack. The twist of grey wool through the hole is mine, tied for the line I could not put down. I will not untie it.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The fire keeps what it's given. The pot keeps the note.
> The cup goes round and the name goes round,
> and the slate goes up the road with neither.

I asked a woman at the racks, the kiln-master and a man come up from Greybank, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the verse where it was written.

The crock goes back behind the lowest rack and the cup goes back on its shelf with the thumb-wide flaw uppermost. Tomorrow the kiln will be drawn again and the cup will come down and go round, and the fourth pour will stand where it stood and be carried away full, and if the clerk borrows the leaf let him take the verbs out. The next hand will put them back, and wet the rim before it sings.
