I keep the birth-slates of the district and I sit with the collected overnight in the Waiting Room off Slate Row. That is my office as the Court has it: to write the name within three days of the birth, to hold the slate ready for the evening figure, and to stay in the room with those who are to go up the road in the morning. There are two benches, a cradle that has had a dozen bottoms in it, a cup that stands where somebody set it, and a slate which I turn face down before I sit. The room smells of wet wool and cold iron and a little of tallow, and underneath those the sour mineral smell everybody in this town knows and nobody says.

A man come up from Greybank stood in the door last Ash with his cuff washed soft and a slate under his arm, and asked what we said to the children in the night before the drawing. He wrote my answer down while I was still saying it, with the doing taken out and a figure put where the cradle stood. So I have set down what we said before he came and what we said again after he had gone, and put the verbs back where he took them out, as the room keeps the child under the figure.

The tallow at the head is mine. I held the sheet down in the draught that comes under the door and did not know I had marked it.

# How it is said

- At the second watch, after the evening figure has been read in the Court's room across the way and the boots have gone back up the road. Not at the first watch; a child at the first watch is still hearing the house and will not settle to a song.
- Warm the stone on the inside of your own wrist before it goes near a belly. A stone that is cold in your hand is a stone and no more, and every maid in this room has learned that on a child.
- Turn the slate face down before you begin. A name said in the same room as an open slate travels, and a name that travels arrives in a house the mother will never be told of.
- Say it to the cradle, not to the child, and not looking at the door. Said looking at a person it becomes a question, and a child who is to go up the road in the morning cannot answer a question.
- Say the last line twice. The first is for the child still hearing it; the second is for the child who will hear it in a house up the road and will not know where it came from.
- Stop while boots cross the passage. Begin again when the passage is quiet. A song that runs into a reading is a song with a figure in it, and there is figure enough in this room already.
- Do not sing it at a death-house door or over a pot broken for the dead. That is a different song and belongs to the sherd.

# The song

*Said low to the cradle, sitting. The stone goes to the belly before the first line. The hand that holds the child does not hold the slate. The room keeps the refrain; the maid says the verses.*

The refrain, as the room keeps it after every verse:

> The stone is warm and the slate is turned,
> the cup stands full where the cup was set;
> what the room keeps overnight it keeps,
> and the morning asks, and the morning gets.

## I. Of the room

> The bench is cold and the cradle is not;
> the cradle holds what the bench cannot.
> I sit where the maids have sat before,
> and I keep the door and the draught keeps the floor.
>
> The fire's in the Court's room across the way,
> and the warm comes round and goes away;
> a house is a cup with its rim in the cold,
> and this room is the cup the house lets go.
>
> > The stone is warm and the slate is turned,
> > the cup stands full where the cup was set;
> > what the room keeps overnight it keeps,
> > and the morning asks, and the morning gets.

## II. Of the stone

> I tried the stone on the inside of my wrist
> before I let it come near your belly;
> a stone that is cold in a woman's hand
> is a stone, and that is all it can do.
>
> The warm in it came out of somebody's fire,
> and somebody kept that fire through the night;
> I did not light it and I will not say whose it was,
> but I have held it here till it held you.
>
> > The stone is warm and the slate is turned,
> > the cup stands full where the cup was set;
> > what the room keeps overnight it keeps,
> > and the morning asks, and the morning gets.

## III. Of the name

> I wrote your name in the book before you came,
> the ink set right and the figure plain;
> a name in a book goes where you do not,
> so I say it here to the floor and nowhere else.
>
> They will call you a number up the road,
> and the number will answer when they call;
> the name I said to the floor stays in this room,
> which is the only house that has ever held it.
>
> > The stone is warm and the slate is turned,
> > the cup stands full where the cup was set;
> > what the room keeps overnight it keeps,
> > and the morning asks, and the morning gets.

## IV. Of the figure

> They read one in twelve as the notice says,
> twice to the room and once to the door;
> the figure goes up and the child goes down,
> and neither of them knows what the other cost.
>
> The cup on the bench is not for the road;
> it stands for the hand that set it there.
> I do not drink it and I do not throw it out;
> it goes cold in its place the way the room goes cold.
>
> > The stone is warm and the slate is turned,
> > the cup stands full where the cup was set;
> > what the room keeps overnight it keeps,
> > and the morning asks, and the morning gets.

## V. Of the morning

> At first grey the boots come down the passage,
> clean at the heel and cold at the cuff;
> he takes what the room kept and writes it down,
> and the cradle stands where the cradle stood.
>
> The grey in the corner has not been swept;
> we leave it where the year left it and say nothing.
> The cup goes with the child and comes back empty,
> and the slate goes face up and takes a name.
>
> > The stone is warm and the slate is turned,
> > the cup stands full where the cup was set;
> > what the room keeps overnight it keeps,
> > and the morning asks, and the morning gets.
> >
> > The stone is warm and the slate is turned ---
> > the morning asks, and the morning gets.

There is a space before the last refrain in the singing, the length of the time between the turning of the slate and the coming of the boots. I have left it on the sheet. I do not fill it and I do not measure it, because a space held between two sounds is the only part of this room that has never been entered.

# What the collector's clerk wrote above

He borrowed the leaf on a morning when the evening figures were being carried across the yard. He writes in the narrow upright hand, with the ink set in two fingers of it that will not come out, and he takes the verbs out as though a verb were a thing a man could hide in a sleeve and carry past a grating.

- Against *what the room keeps overnight it keeps* he wrote *vessel retained overnight; no deduction arises*, and underlined the figure twice.
- Against *the figure goes up and the child goes down* he set *measure transferred; subject re-entered at the bank*, which is the same journey with the child taken out of it.
- Against *I say it here to the floor and nowhere else* he wrote *name unentered; no entry lies*, which is true in his language and false in this room, where the name has been said three times and the floor has it.
- At the space before the last refrain he put a round nought and wrote *interval, nil* beside it.

I have put my words back underneath and left his standing above. He takes the doing out because a thing always true has no need of it. I put it back because a maid who has sat nine winters does not know she is hearing something always true, and wants to know which hand held the child and which hand wrote the figure down. He has put a number where a watch should stand. A number travels; a watch stays in the room.

# What the woman at the well put in the margin

The charcoal belongs to a woman who came to this room in the Thin Year and carried her child home again in the morning with the figure still on the slate beside her name. She writes on the backs of spoiled tallies in a hand that presses hard at the ends of words. I have copied it as it stands.

- At *I wrote your name in the book before you came*: *They wrote mine before I saw her too. I have carried that slate nine years and have never said whose figure stood under it. A woman who says that name is a woman who wants it entered.*
- At *and the morning asks, and the morning gets*: *Mine came back and would not say whose house she had been in. I asked the floor, as you do, and the floor kept it. Both my answers stand, and neither of them is a figure.*
- At *the cup goes cold in its place*: *I set a cup there at the first watch and took it up at the last. It had gone the colour of the bench. That is the whole of what the room told me, and I have asked it nothing since.*

# At the head and in the gutter

The tallow at the head is mine and has gone into the grain and will not wash out, which is why the maids sign with it and not with ink. The crust-grease on the second leaf is off a brown crust a woman set down for her child and could not get him to eat; the edge was burnt, which is the kindness in this district. The ring is the cup of barley water that stood from the first verse to the last and went cold without being drunk, the way every cup in this room goes. The pale round came off a tube-wrap laid on the leaf while the clerk counted the evening's measures, and no hand has come forward for it. The grey along the outer fold is not mine and not the clerk's; it came off a cuff going through the door and nobody will own it, and I have not swept it, because there is no Last Day in a Waiting Room and nothing here is gathered up and burnt and called dealt with. The green bead came off a girl with the sweetness of the glaze still at her mouth who leaned over to see the writing and touched the leaf and went back across the yard; I have not washed it. The grey wool in the sewing is from the birth-cloth and was cut from the hem the year I wrote a name I was told to scrape out, and I have kept the thread and not the word. The damp took one word off the back sheet and I have not written it in again, because a song with a word gone is a song said slowly, and all of ours are said slowly.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The slate is turned. The cradle is not.
> What keeps overnight keeps until morning.
> The morning does not say whose it was.

I asked the collector's clerk, a woman who has waited in the room, and a carter who brings the children up from Low Kiln, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written.

The sheet goes back into the birth-cloth and into the fold of my apron. Tomorrow at first grey the boots will come down the passage, clean at the heel and cold at the cuff, and the child will go up the road with a number, and the cradle will stand where the cradle stood. I shall turn the slate face down and warm the stone on my own wrist and say the last line twice, and when the clerk has gone I shall put the verbs back. The room keeps what the room keeps and the road takes what the road takes, and nobody in this town has ever asked the cradle what it cost.
