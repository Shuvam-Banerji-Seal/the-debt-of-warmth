I write the names in this house. A woman comes across from the low bank with a child in a fold of grey wool and asks me to write the child's name, and I write the name the slate gives me, which is seldom the one she hoped to hear. She wants a house for the child. The ordinance provides a vessel, a finder, a claimant and a hand. It has no column for the woman who carried him up the yard.

I put the verbs back where the College took them out. A woman standing at my counter wants to know who did what, and the ordinance will not tell her, because in the Keeping a thing entered is a thing that has always been entered. I have had both my putting-back and the College's striking copied forward. Let the two hands stand together, as they do on every slate in this district that anybody believes.

> *At the head of the first leaf, written very small, in mine:* Copied for the house, not for the College. If this is read in the Hearthseat, let it be seen that the verbs were mine and the absence of them was not. Let both stand.

# The extract, as it stands in the book

*From the Ordinances of the Keeping, Title the Second: Of the Finding, the Naming and the Claiming of Vessels. Sent down to the factor at Greybank in Y.H. 418, entered in this house when it was built in Y.H. 776, amended in Y.H. 782 and again in the Thin Year.*

## I. Of the finding

1. A vessel found without a house entered within three days of the finding, as a birth is entered. Entry in Ledgerhand. Hand that writes not hand that carries. Finder not claimant.
2. Where entry made after the third day, the reason entered also. No reason entered for the third day itself.

> *Margin, in mine:* A child left at the meal mill in a fold of grey wool, with a stone warmed at another hearth under his belly, was entered on the second day. The finder brought the stone in with her and asked whether the stone was a name. I said no. She wrapped it back and carried it away, and I wrote a name that will do him until a hearth claims him, which in this district means until a house with a chimney and a roll of its own takes him in.

> *Margin:* Hand that writes not hand that carries. This is the only article I have ever had cause to be grateful for. A woman who has carried a child three doors up the yard in the cold should not have to hold the slate as well as the child.

## II. Of the name

3. Foundling named by the hand appointed to the slate. Birth-name not entered. House-name entered in the margin where a hearth claims him; the first name not struck out.
4. Where no hearth claims by the thirtieth day after finding, entered as unhoused, the slate-name standing alone.

| What is entered | Where it stands | What is not entered |
|---|---|---|
| Slate-name | the line | Birth-name |
| Day of finding | the head | Hour of finding |
| House-name, if claimed | the margin | The woman who carried him |
| Figure | the account | Who paid for the finding |

> *Margin:* I drew the last column and left it empty. A man from the College borrowed the leaf and struck the column out, writing *No such column* above it in a hand so upright that it hurts the eye. I have struck his striking out and left the column as I drew it. A place is not a debt. The College has never learned the difference, though it teaches the eleven words for a deduction to its own.

> *Margin:* I have heard tell of a branching red flower written across a child's ribs and have not entered it. There is no column. The hearth-maids cross two fingers over such a thing and cover the sign with a date, which is a kind of writing and not the kind that reaches this house.

## III. Of the claim

5. Claim entered before the tenth day after finding; after that only by leave of the assessor. Where two houses claim, the house of longer standing entered first and the other entered also and not struck.
6. Claim made for warmth already paid entered as a kindness and not as a debt. Kindness entered.

> *Margin:* Longer standing means the house with the most pots. I write *standing* because that is the word the ordinance uses, and a woman from the low bank who has no pots has no standing, which is the whole of it. In the Thin Year a house came for a child and wrote its own name above his and set a thumb to it. I put the house-name in the margin where the ordinance requires and kept the child's name in the line. The woman who had carried him stood with her hands in her apron and asked whether the hearth was a good one. I told her I had no slate for the warmth of a hearth. This was true, and was not what she asked.

> *Margin, in a second and narrower hand, the letters leaning, in charcoal:* A crossed sign, which the maids make at a birth-flower. It is not a word and I have not translated it. It was laid against article six, where the kindness is entered.

## IV. Of the house roll

7. Claimed vessel entered upon the house roll in a careful hand. Roll kept in the house. No duplicate required.
8. Every vessel entered upon one line of the slate. Two vessels not entered upon one line.

> *Margin:* No duplicate required. I have asked to see a roll and been offered a cup instead. A house that keeps its own account does not need to explain the keeping, only to keep it. This is the article the houses of Low Kiln live by, and the Court has never thought to enforce it, which I take to be the Court's one kindness that it does not enter.

> *Margin:* There is a boy in a kiln-house who says: *He's a foundling. I'm a debt. Don't put us in the same sentence, Sella.* I have written them on two lines of one slate, which is the only sentence a slate knows. A household sorts its own members and calls the sorting kindness, and the ordinance has no word to dispute it with.

## V. Of the drawing and the space after it

9. Where no claim entered, the measure drawn at the next collection and the account opened in the name written at the finding. Consent entered by the hand of the officer where no household offers it. Refusal is an unclosed account; the vessel clip-held by the wrist until consent entered.
10. No article provides for a measure left undrawn where no officer was called.

> *Margin:* On Ilmen 9 of this year I was set at the Fishstairs with a slate to take a measure that nobody had called for. I sat from the turning of the tide-light until the light went, and wrote the hour at which I was set and the hour at which I was not dismissed, and left the space between them empty. There is a line in the account for a figure and no place in it for a figure that never came. A woman went into the clay-heavy water at the end of nineteen steps and came out on a board, and the yard lifted her with their hands, and I had no article to read to anybody. The clerk at the Cross says a nought is a number. I put nothing. I sent the slate back as it stood and it was entered as a kindness.

> *Margin:* The clip leaves a pale ring that shows through the winter and is spoken of as nothing. I have seen a woman hold up her wrist and ask whether the ring would go, and I said it would in the thaw, which is what my mother said about everything, and is not in the ordinance.

## VI. Schedule of the days of a foundling

| Day | Entry | Where it stands |
|---|---|---|
| First day | Finding | Head of the slate |
| Third day | Entry, or reason | The line |
| Tenth day | Claim, or leave sought | The margin |
| Thirtieth day | House-name, or unhoused | Margin, or the line |
| The Five | No market, no claim | Nothing entered |

> *Margin:* No claim taken during the Five, and no market either. The clerk at the Cross counts the bell four, four and a place where the fifth should be; I count the empty column. We are each of us keeping a place open, and neither of us has a word for it.

# What the College hand did, and what I did after it

The College hand struck three of my verbs, struck the empty column, and wrote *No market, no claim, no entry* against the last row of the schedule, which is true in the way a closed fist is true. I struck out *no entry* and set *nothing entered* above it. There is a difference between the entry of nothing and a place where a woman might have stood, and the difference is the whole of what I have to do in this house. I have left the College's fist standing above my words, as I have left the striking of the verbs above the verbs themselves. A slate with two hands on it is the only kind the household believes.

The brown spot on the second leaf is from a crust laid there while a woman was telling me where the child had been found and would not say the name of the house. The sour milk is the child's; there was no cup in the house that morning and I gave him mine. The grey came off the outer fold when I carried the leaf in my coat pocket down to the Fishstairs on Ilmen 9, pressed against the wrist where the clip had been, or against a wrist that had never known the clip; I do not now remember which arm I used, and have not tried to find out. The tallow thumb at the head is mine. I held the leaf down with my whole hand because the door stood open and the yard wind came through.

One line at the foot was struck through and written again in my hand, and I have left both readings standing:

> No article provides for the measure left undrawn. In the low houses they keep these also.

The leaf goes back into its board cover and hangs on its nail behind the counter. Tomorrow a woman will come up from the low bank with a child in a fold of wool and ask me for a name, and I shall write the name the slate gives me, and put nothing in the last column, and she will go away knowing that the nothing is where she stood. That is the whole of my trade: to write names and leave places, and not to say which of the two will keep him warmer.
