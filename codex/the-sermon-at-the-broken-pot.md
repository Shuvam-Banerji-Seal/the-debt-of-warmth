I keep the niche of broken pots at the Bone Orchard. It is my work to set the year's pots in a row and lift the flat stone on the Remembering and put the sherds back. What follows is written on the back of a grave-tile that came away whole from the row and would not break, so I kept it, the way this ground keeps everything that fails at the work it was made for.

The old woman stood with her back to the north gate and spoke first to the pots, as potters do, and to the clay under them, and to the fire that had made them, and only at the end to the people standing in the row. She had broken pots here for one and thirty years and had never once broken one at the foot. She said the same things every year. I have written them as she said them, with the verbs where she put them, because a sermon said over a pot is a thing a household has to use.

The grey in the gutter came off my cuff when I measured the ridge two doors down in Low Kiln and came back to write, and I have not swept it. The salt at the head is from a woman's hand laid flat on the tile to hold it still in the wind off the ridge. The tallow is mine. The green bead is not slip; a girl with sweetness still at her mouth leaned over to see what I was writing and touched the rim with her thumb. The tile has a chip at the upper right where somebody tapped it with a knuckle and did not say what answer it gave.

# The manner of the breaking, as the niche keeps it

- Set the pots in a row on the flat stone with their lips to the north. Feet toward water is for the body; lips toward the Hearth is for the pot. Do not mix the two.
- Tap each pot with the knuckle before you lift it. Listen for what it answers. Do not say what you heard where a man with a slate is standing.
- Break at the lip with one blow. A blow at the foot sends the heat into the clay and the clay will keep it, which is a kind of burying twice.
- Break it whole. A pot broken into dust has let the heat out through a hundred little doors and cannot say which way it went.
- If a pot answers flat, break it gently. It has given already and asks no help from you.
- When the bell comes up the north road from the Cross, stop. It has counted something that is not your pot. Begin again after. Four, four and a gap, and the gap is not ours to fill.
- Break the nameless pot last. Do not let anybody bring it. It comes out of the niche by itself.

# The sermon, as she said it

## Of the lip and the foot

She put her thumb in the chip at the rim of a milk pot and turned it so the row could see where the star was.

“You bring me a pot and you say, break this, it is my mother's. I ask you nothing. I break it at the lip, because the lip is the mouth and the heat goes out of a mouth the way breath goes out of a person. Break it at the foot and you have put your mother's warmth into the ground twice: once in the grave and once under the grave, where the clay keeps it and will not let it go.”

She struck the pot with the heel of her hand and it opened clean across, two pieces and a third small one that fell among the sherds.

“A whole pot keeps. A broken pot gives. That is the whole of my preaching and I have had one and thirty years to find another sentence and could not.”

## Of the note

“Before I break, I tap. You have all heard a pot tapped at the wheel. A sound one answers full and a cracked one answers flat, a little dead sound that stops at the surface and will not go through. You bring me a pot that answered flat three days before the person went and you say nothing about it, and I say nothing either. We are not fools in this district. We know what a pot was doing while we were looking at the person.”

She tapped the cup with the cupworm in it. It gave nothing that I could hear from where I sat, and she put it down on its side.

“This one I break gently. It has been giving since it came off the wheel and there is no heat in it to let out. Do not break a pot that has given already as though it had held. That is a cruelty to the pot and a lie about the household.”

One of the women in the row said that a pot was only a pot. The old woman turned her head a degree toward her and said: “A body is only a body, too, and we wash it and put its feet to the water and send for a man with a tube. Do not tell me what a pot is. I have made them.”

## Of the one nobody brought

There is one pot in the niche that no household brings. It is a plain clay pot, smaller than a milk pot, with no mark on it and a lip worn smooth by hands that have lifted it every Remembering since I can remember. The old woman took it out from under the flat stone and held it a moment against her apron before she set it on the row.

“This is for them that have no house to break for them,” she said. “The little graves lie over there beyond the broken stake, shallow, because the clay was frozen when they were put in. You can walk past them and not see them, and people do, and I do not blame them, because a person can only look at so much in a winter.”

She struck it at the lip. It opened with a dry sound, smaller than the milk pot's, and the three pieces lay where they fell.

“The Court reads a whole return at the Weighing in a clean voice for a person not in the room. I have heard it done. I have stood outside the door and heard it done, and come up here and broken this. Somebody has to break for the ones the clean voice does not name. That is not an argument with the Court. It is a pot with a name left out of it.”

She would not say how many graves lay beyond the broken stake, and when the clerk asked her she said the clay was frozen and she had not counted that year. A woman beside me wrote something in the margin of her tally and covered it with her thumb. I have not read it and it is not on this tile.

## Of the keeping

A factor's clerk had come out from Greybank with a cuff gone soft from washing and ink in two fingers, and stood at the end of the row with a slate held to his forearm. He asked the old woman what became of the heat once the pot was broken.

“It goes up,” she said. “That is what I know of it. It goes up out of the mouth and into the air and the air is bigger than the house. I have stood at more breakings than I could set a figure to, and never once seen it come back to the pot.”

“It is banked,” the clerk said.

“It is gone from the pot,” she said. “I have not said where from. Those are two sentences and I will not have one put over the other.”

“Nothing is lost that we have kept,” he said, in the voice that takes the speaker out of his sentence.

“I have never kept one,” she said. “I have let go of a great many. If the Hearth wants what went up, let the Hearth come out of its iron and fetch it. It has not come yet, and I have been waiting one and thirty years.”

After that she gave the mark on the niche the same answer she gives every year. The clerk asked what it was, the crossed thing cut in the stone above the pots, which the surveyor draws in the chart and does not explain. She said it was cut in Y.H. 668. He asked her again in a different way and she gave him the same year.

> *In the margin, in my hand:* A date is safe. The factor's clerk says so himself, and he is right, because a date is the only answer that cannot be argued with. I have set down her year and left the question standing.

# The breaking-song

*Sung low, standing, while the nameless pot is lifted out of the niche. The row says the last line with her. There is a gap before the last line, the one the bell leaves, and I have left it here as it is sung.*

> Break at the lip and not at the foot,
> the heat goes up and not into the root;
> a whole pot holds and a cracked pot gives,
> and the clay keeps only what the clay receives.
>
> Four for the corners, four for the wall,
> and one for the place where a name should fall;
> the bell comes four and four and a gap,
> and the pot gives back what the pot had.
>
> Do not ask the fire where the fire has gone;
> do not ask the ground what it stands upon;
> say the name, or leave the place it had,
>
> and come back warm, as the Keeping said.

A girl from High Kiln asked what the space before the last line was for. Nobody answered her. The clerk put a round nought against it later, which I have left where he set it.

# The row, as the niche counts it

| Pot | Brought by | Broken | What was said over it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Milk pot, lip starred | a woman of High Kiln | at the lip, one blow | her mother's name, said three times |
| Cup with a cupworm in it | a man of the Clay Yard | at the foot, by mistake | nothing; the heat went into the clay |
| The nameless pot | the niche | at the lip | no name; a place left the length of one |
| A grave-tile, came away whole | nobody | not broken | kept, and written on |

There were two and twenty pots in the row besides these, and I have not set them down, because a tally of pots is a thing a clerk can carry away and a name is not. The two and twenty went into the barrow with the grey the next market-day and nobody in the square looked at it twice.

# What the clerk did

He borrowed the tile on the morning after and gave it back with the verbs taken out.

- Where she said *a whole pot keeps, a broken pot gives*, he wrote *retention and leakage are lawful conditions of a vessel* above and left hers below.
- Where she said *the heat goes up*, he wrote *the measure is released to the keeping* and underlined it twice.
- At the nameless pot he put a round nought and wrote *vacancy* beside it, and when I asked him what a pot was worth that nobody had brought, he said nothing a clerk could enter, which was the first true thing he said all morning.
- At the line about the Hearth coming to fetch its heat he wrote *claim not established*, and drew a small stop between two clusters of his neat dark hand.

I have put the verbs back underneath where she put them and left his hand standing above, as the flat stone leaves the sherds and the sherd leaves the clay. He takes the verbs out because a thing always true has no need of them. I put them back because a woman standing in the cold wants to know who did the letting go, and whether it was her own hand.

# At the foot

Struck through once and written again underneath, very small, in a hand that is not mine, not the old woman's and not the clerk's:

> A whole pot is a lie told by a sound vessel.

> A whole pot is a vessel that has not yet been asked.

I asked the old woman, the woman of High Kiln, the man who brought the cupworm cup, the girl who asked about the gap, and the clerk, and had five answerings and no hand. I have left the line struck through and standing, which is the only honest way to keep a sentence you cannot sign.

The tile goes back under the flat stone with the flat side down, so the writing is kept from the wet. Next Remembering I shall lift the stone and set the row out, and the nameless pot will come out of the niche and be broken at the lip as it is every year. If the clerk comes again I shall let him take the verbs out. I shall put them back.
