I carried a pack. That is the whole of what I am. I went down the marsh road in the reed month with clay pipes in the top of it and needles in a little tin and salt herring wrapped in oiled reed at the bottom, and I came back with rushes, a hank of eel-sinew, a knot of grey wool I cannot untie without losing what it stands for, and a name I have written down and will not say aloud. I have set down what I saw. Where the south and the mud disagree I have left both standing, which is the only honest way I know to carry a pack.

# The road, and what it took

Mud End stands three leagues downriver of Sill on a dry shelf above the marsh, with nine houses and their peat stacks. The byre there has a floor packed hard as old bread, and the moot sits in it when the peat is too wet to stand on. I slept in the byre on my first night with my pack under the bench and my boots toward the door, which is how a woman from the clay country learns to sleep in a wet one. In the morning a man asked me what I carried and I said herring and pipes. He said the marsh would take the herring and leave me the pipes, which was the first true thing told me on that road and the one I have entered first.

From Mud End the marsh road goes to Reedholt, where the reed-cutters live in huts at the edge of the beds and the wolves come in after dark. A man there sold me a night's lodging and a drink of small ale and told me the wolves will not take a woman with a pack, because a pack smells of many things and none of them is woman. I asked him what they would take. He said a child cutting its first bundle in the dark, if the child goes alone and brings the bundle back dry, which is the Reed Cut and not a thing a visitor asks about twice. I have put his sentence where he said it and have not asked him again.

Beyond Reedholt the road goes into the Quiet Acre, which the Court calls the Empty Cantons on its own maps. There are wells there and doorways and no people. A doorway is built for coming back through, and a square of bare clay above a lintel is where a house-roll once hung. I saw eleven doorways and did not count the twelfth, because a thing with no figure in it is what that country keeps. I asked a peat-carter who goes that road whether anybody had come back through the doorways. He said the doorways had not been shut, which is not the answer I asked for and is the only one the road has.

The Standing Post is ninety leagues south-west of Sill and is the only place in the fen a stranger is shown. It is a walk of four planks laid across a cut no man has sounded, and a fifth place where no plank is laid. The man who keeps the boards told me the crossing costs nothing and the ground costs a bit. When I set my foot where he did not point I went in to the calf and had to be pulled out by a woman with a hook on a pole. She pulled me out and asked nothing, and I gave her a clay pipe and she would not take it. I have left the pipe standing in the account below and the pulling-out where it happened.

# What was asked, and what was given

*The left column is the figure I was told at the Cross in Sill or by the beam at Greybank. The right is what left my pack or came into it here, where no clerk stands with a slate. I have set what I think of it on the back.*

| Article | As told below | As given in the mud | What I set on the back |
|---|---|---|---|
| Salt herring, a string | 2 bits at the Fishstairs | 2 bits, or a notched stick under an apron | The stick is honest. The eel-women set their own figure and do not say it. |
| Clay pipes, a dozen | 3 bits in Sill | not taken; a woman pulled me from the cut and would not take one | A thing given for a thing not given has no price and stays in the pack. |
| Needles, a paper of six | 1 bit at the Cross | 2 bits, in kind, for a hank of eel-sinew | The sinew sewed these leaves and the seller would not take the bit back. |
| Rushes, a bundle | not cried on any board | ask the arm that cut it; the arm knows weight and not cost | The arm is not a scale. I have bought rushes all my life and never once weighed them. |
| Peat, a barrow | 2 bits in Grain, 5 in Stone | a barrow cut by the buyer, or a day of bought breath | Never by the stack. A stack is a house's winter. |
| Grey, a thimble, bottled | 60 marks in a Court jar, licensed | 4 bits here, in an unmarked jar | The same grey in two vessels. One has a Court name and one has none. |
| Thin return, a household | 30 marks to the Court | a third, in eels and peat, and no questions | What comes back is a stranger the house keeps. I asked whose warmth and was asked why I wanted it. |
| Bought breath, a season | not spoken of below | a term paid in iron or peat, entered in months | The hook is in the word the seller cannot say. |
| A child named before the collector comes | no figure | a name said three times into the ground under the boards | No stick can hold it. I have tied a knot instead. |

Twelve bits to the silver mark and twenty marks to a Sere crown, and the crowns that come north with the southern buyers will not change at Greybank, so they go south again and leave nothing behind but the figure. The Court books in embers and calls five marks an ember; in sixteen journeys I have never seen one paid and have twice seen a man try. I have left the ember column off. A column with nothing in it is the only one in this book no clerk has corrected.

# The standing, and how they chose a reeve

They chose a reeve while I was at the Post. Reeves here are chosen by standing in a bog until the others have had enough, and it is not strength: a strong man goes in hot and comes out early, and a reeve stands until the mud has taken his measure and found it enough. About half of them are women and the saying does not change for it.

The one they chose that morning was a woman with peat to the knees and her hands folded across her chest. She stood while the light went off the reedbeds and came back on them, and when a man went in to take her arm she came out under her own power and said nothing. Her first fine was a barrow of peat to the winter stack, laid on a man who had let a drain run into a neighbour's cut. He paid it. The fine went on a stick. When I asked what became of a fine that was not paid, the board-man said a fine unpaid becomes a term and a term unpaid becomes a measure, and that there is no Last Day in a tin under a board where a man might sweep either one away.

I asked the woman reeve, through the board-man, whether standing so long made her right. She said the mud called her right and that she had only stood where she was put, which is a large answer in a small voice and the only one I got. I have set it above the price table, because it is the only thing on this leaf nobody has tried to write over.

# The mother, and the number before the name

A child was born in a hut off the Post while I was there. The mother named it before the collector came up the boardwalk, which the fenfolk do, and which a man from Greybank told me was the closest thing they had to a declaration of war. She named it three times: once to the child, once into the ground beside the hut, and once to the pot, and would not write it. When the collector read his figure she gave him the number and kept the word, which is their saying and not mine.

I asked a woman at the washing why a mother would spend a name on the ground. She said a written name travels where the person does not, and a name said at home is a door, and a number is a hole in the wall. Then she said I should not put that question to a mother, because a mother who answers it has told me what she paid for the child, and no visitor has a right to that. I have left the question in and the answer out, which is how the fen keeps its doors.

What the collector drew went at a third of what the Court charges, and nobody asked where it came from. A man who asks where warmth came from has decided not to hear it, the board-man said, and he said it to the water and not to me. I saw a pale round place on an arm that shows through the winter. I did not see the tube. I have written what I saw and have not written what I was told happened after, because I was told it by three people in three words and none of them would say the fourth.

# The song said face down

*Taken down at the Standing Post from the woman who pulled me out of the cut. She would not sing it standing up, or looking at me, or while a slate lay open on the board, so we lay face down in the peat at the edge of the reeds and said it to the ground. The last word of each verse falls into the mud and is not finished. Where a word will not go into the south's letters I have tied a knot in the margin.*

> Four for the boards and a smooth for the fifth,
> the reed says wait and the reeve says stand;
> the mud takes the name and the water takes the mark,
> and what was lent goes back to the [knot].
>
> A bit for the Line and a bit for the plank,
> a stick for the eels and a term for the stack;
> the Court writes one and the river writes none,
> and the man who comes over is dry till he is not.
>
> She keeps it warm and she hands it back,
> the Marsh Mother, and no priest in a coat;
> the cold iron takes and the [knot] takes,
> and one of them writes it down.
>
> Say it to the board, say it down to the water,
> the last word falls where the board has no foot;
> a name said at home is a door in the mud,
> and a knot in the wool is the whole of the account.

She said songs from that country are made by the country, which is an answer the clay countries do not give and the coast does, and would not give the name of anybody who had made it. I have put the song where I found it, face down, and away from the slate.

# What the borrowed hands did

The factor's clerk at Greybank borrowed the leaves on a market morning. He writes upright and narrow and takes the verbs out as though a verb were a thing a man could carry off in a cuff. He had never been south of Reedholt and had strong views about peat.

- Against *the mud called her right* he wrote *the reeve was duly chosen*, and underlined it twice. I have put the mud back underneath. A reeve duly chosen is a reeve with nobody in the bog and no water on his boots.
- Against *a name said three times into the ground* he wrote *naming not entered; vacancy*, and put a round nought in the fifth place. A nought is a figure. The fifth place is where no plank is laid. He has made the two agree and I have left them as they stood.
- Against *they gave and asked nothing for it* he wrote *no consideration recorded; gift void*. Perhaps it is void in the language of his table. The woman pulled me out and would not take the pipe, and I have left the pipe in the pack where she left it.

The second hand is a College clerk come down from the Hearthseat on the factor's business, who took the leaf while I was at the well and set *a reduction in the measure was recorded* above the thin return, and *seasonal labour* above bought breath, which is a thing a house can empty and refill. I have set my words under both. A verb is the difference between a thing that happened to somebody and a thing that happened in a counting room, and a leaf with three hands on it is the only kind a man at these boards will believe. A leaf with one hand is a Court notice, and a Court notice has never told a woman which plank will bear her.

# At the head and in the gutter

The peat at the head is mine. I held the leaf down on the board in a wind off the bog and did not know I had marked it, and peat goes into the grain and will not come out, which is why the fen signs with it. The tideline in the gutter is bog-water from the morning the Post stood under and has taken two figures clean off the second leaf; I have not written them back, because a figure with two figures gone is a figure cut slowly, and everything there is cut slowly. The wet reed across the second leaf came off the bundle I bought and was pressed flat under my pack. The pipe-stem in the fold snapped the day I offered it to the woman who pulled me out and she would not take it, and I have kept both pieces where they fell. The scale at the foot is a salt herring's, off the string I carried down and did not bring back. The nick at the second corner is a reed-wolf's tooth, from a man at Reedholt who paid with his hand still on the knife. The grey along the outer fold is not mine and not the factor's and I have not swept it; there is no Last Day under a board on which a woman might sweep it. The knot of grey wool through the hole in the first leaf is mine, tied for a word that will not go into the south's letters, and I will not untie it. One word on the third leaf I scraped away with a knife-point and put nothing in its place. I have not written it back.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> Four planks and a smooth where the fifth should lie;
> a woman stood in the bog and the mud called her right.
> I carried names in a tin and brought home a knot;
> the figure went up and the child stayed in the mud.
>
> Ask the doorway in the Acre who came back through it.

I asked the board-man, the woman who pulled me out, and a factor come up from Greybank to count the winter peat, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The leaves go back into the sacking and under the loose board at the landing, and tomorrow I take my pack up the clay road to Sill and sell rushes by the armful and never once say where I got them, and by evening it will be a thing that was true. The clerk at the Cross will read my figure twice and call it a kindness. I shall keep the knot in my pocket, where no clerk has thought to look for an account.
