title: The Price Board at Greybank, Being the Rates for Goods, Carriage and the Tolls of the Four Roads, Copied from the Beam Beside the Quay
kind: A weigh-clerk's fair copy of the public board, with the dues of the Clay, Salt, Rim and Hearth roads set after it, corrected in two borrowed hands and left standing in a third
provenance: Written and kept at Greybank, eighteen leagues downriver of Sill, by the clerk who tends the weighing beam on the quay; begun in Y.H. 812, the Thin Year, after the Long Cold, and copied forward at the Opening of the Slates in Y.H. 813. The board itself hung on the quay wall beside the grain doors and was scraped and rewritten each tend; this leaf was pinned inside the Vhaleni counting house for the factor's use, and survives soft at the nail-hole, with a white salt bloom along the gutter, a tar thumb at the head, a dark streak of eel slime at the foot, a green bead of cliff-glass in the lower corner, a spot of barley grease, a grey Ash print along the margin, two corrections in hands that are not the writer's, and one line at the foot in a hand nobody will own.

I keep the beam on the quay. A man brings a barrow of peat or a basket of eels and wants a figure, and I give him the figure on the board, which is the figure of that morning, and by afternoon a barge has tied up and it is not the figure any more. The factor wants a number that will stand still. I have told him that a number which stands still is a lie told by a man with a roof over his goods, and he has told me to copy the board again in a better hand. I have copied it in the same hand. What follows is not what things are worth. It is what they fetched at the beam between the last market of Stone and the Opening of the Slates, set down so that a carter may know what he is being charged before he has unloaded it.

Twelve copper bits to a silver mark. Twenty silver marks to a gold crown, which is a Sere coin and does not stay in Grath. The Court books in embers and will tell a man an ember is worth five marks; no man has ever exchanged one for the other, and I have not left a space for them on the board.

> *In the narrow upright hand of the Court's factor's clerk:* Coin and reckoning entered. *Underneath, in mine:* An ember is a word the Court uses for warmth it has already counted. He takes the verbs out because a thing always true has no need of them; I put them back because a carter standing in the rain does not know he is hearing something always true.

# The board, as it stood on the quay wall

## Bread and the pot

*Copied from the Cross at Sill, where the same figures are cried on Account-day. These do not move with the barges.*

| Article | Price |
|---|---|
| Coarse barley loaf, brown-edged | 1 bit |
| Quart of small ale, watered | 1 bit |
| Sour milk, a quart | 1 bit |
| Salt herring | 2 bits |
| Smoked eel | 3 bits |
| Turnips, two | 1 bit |
| Barley gruel, a bowl at a house door | not sold; given, and not entered |

> *Margin, in my hand:* The factor's clerk took the last line off the board, saying a thing not sold has no price. I have put it back. In Low Kiln a bowl given at a door is the greater part of what the district eats and the whole of what it does not discuss.

## Goods that go downriver and goods that come back

*Weighed at the beam on the north bank. Eels and coarse pots go down to Greybank; salt and iron come up. The figure is for the load at the beam, before the road takes its share.*

| Article | Price |
|---|---|
| Coarse pots, the score | 4 bits |
| Tile, the hundred | 9 bits |
| Peat, a barrow | 2 bits in Grain; 5 bits in Stone |
| Eels, a basket at the Fishstairs | 3 bits the eel smoked; 2 bits salted |
| Eel skin, dried, at this quay | 1 mark |
| Salt, a sack from the pans | 3 marks |
| Iron, a bar, Keldish | 2 marks in the lowlands; half a mark in Keld |
| Glass from the Glaze Cliffs, a sheet | 2 marks |
| Rope, a coil | 1 mark |
| Tallow, a stone | 2 marks |
| Lead, a pig, from the western workings | 6 marks; the seller does not say which workings |

> *Margin:* The peat row stood blank through the Long Cold of Y.H. 811. Fuel doubled in the first tend of Stone and then stopped being quoted, because there was none to sell. I have set two figures above it for the Thin Year, and struck neither out. A man who buys peat in Grain and pays the Stone figure does not complain, which is how I know the board is not a list of what things are worth.

> *In a tall upright hand, the letters leaning, the ink gone brown at the edges:* A sailor's share is two marks a month and keep; a salt-worker gets one mark and a binding promise. The Council has instructed me to enter these beside the wages of the clay towns, so that a man may see which country feeds him. *Underneath, in mine:* I asked the gentleman whether a binding promise could be spent at this beam. He said it could not, and went on writing.

## Articles a man asks for after he has paid

*Not on the board. I keep the list behind the beam because people come back for them when the stall is shut.*

| Article | Price |

## The Court's figures, copied from the notice and not traded here

| Entry | Figure |
|---|---|
| Deduction, as printed on the Weighing notice | one in twelve |
| Thin return, paid by the district | 30 marks, in labour |
| Whole return, booked by the Court | 1,200 marks and forty unspent measures |
| Ember, as booked | 5 marks |

> *Margin:* The factor keeps a damp leaf of these in his drawer and desires that the board agree. One in twelve is printed and unchallenged and I have put no other figure beside it. I have heard other figures spoken upriver and have not entered them, not being figures I can weigh.

# The tolls of the roads

Four roads leave Greybank, and a fifth, which is the river. The dues below are what the beam collects or what the factors at the far end collect, and the difference between the two is the whole art of travelling.

## The Clay Road

From the Cross at Sill to this quay, eighteen leagues, packed clay over corduroy, a Court road since the treaty of the Blue Cliff in Y.H. 418. Five days by cart in Grain, six in Silt; rutted and then clay again. Because the treaty made it a Court road, an assessor may stop a cart on it without asking the thane, and does.

| | Due |
|---|---|
| Cart with a load, at the Sill end | 2 bits |
| The same at this end, by the factor's man | 2 bits |
| Beast on the hoof | 1 bit |
| Drove of pigs | 4 bits, and the pigs weighed at the Cross besides |
| Foot passenger | nothing on Account-day; 1 bit any other day |
| Barrow of grey going out through a north gate | not weighed, and not entered |

> *Margin:* The cartmen say they pay twice to travel one road, once to the moot and once to the Court, and both of them say the road is in a better state before the payment than after. I have put the last row on my own copy and not on the board. What goes out of Sill in a barrow is the afternoon's work, and no figure reaches it.

## The Salt Road

From this quay north-east to Vhal, sixty-two leagues beyond the eighteen, eighty in all, by the coast pass. Salt, glass, rope and dried fish go out; coin and contracts on salt-paper come back, and the damp gets into both.

| | Due |
|---|---|
| Sack of salt, at the coast pass | a mark to the Council of Tides |
| Coil of rope, or a sheet of glass | 1 bit |
| Boat-share passing inland | paid in kind, by the lot and not the league |
| Contract written on salt-paper | no fee; it will not survive the journey, which the Council calls a virtue |
| Man going up with the glass at the turn of the month | 3 bits, returned if he comes back with the answer |

> *In the Council clerk's leaning hand:* The word *toll* above is struck and *contribution freely entered* written over it. *In mine:* He has left the figures standing. A Council that cannot charge a toll has found a finer name for one, and a man pays it all the same.

## The Rim Road

West-north-west from Sill to Keld Keep, a hundred and twenty leagues of improving road and worsening weather; the Cold Pass ten leagues beyond, at a hundred and thirty. Karric VII shut it in Y.H. 741 and the agreement of Y.H. 747 fixed it as the border. Keldish surveyors keep the record and state a man's measure before his name.

| | Due |
|---|---|
| Cart, at the pass | 1 mark, or iron to the value |
| Beast on the hoof | half a mark |
| A man without a warm-stone | the stone off the next man who has one |
| Surveyor's mark upon a load | 2 bits; the load is not weighed again in Grath |
| Traveller going farther into the Rim | no due, and no guarantee |

> *Margin:* Only fools and factors travel it without a warm-stone, and the factors charge the stone to the account. I have kept the last row because a man who reads the board should know that the Warden's surveyors do not promise to bring him back.

## The Hearth Road

South-east from this quay to the Hearthseat, a hundred and forty leagues, the merchants' road to the Court, spoken of in Grath as if it were a fever. There is no barrier on it and no figure at any gate.

| | Due |
|---|---|
| Toll at the barrier | none; there is no barrier |
| Grain going south, weighed here | by the deduction, entered at this end |
| The same again at the Hearthseat | booked in embers |
| A man who asks what he has paid | the figure is read to him twice, once for the moot and once for those who came late |

> *Margin:* A carter from High Kiln asked me what the Hearth Road cost and I showed him the empty column. He said that was the most honest figure on the board and the dearest. The Court takes nothing at the gate because it has already taken it at home; I have entered that sentence here and not on the board, where it would stop nobody from loading.

## Bridges, ford and river road

| | Due |
|---|---|
| South bridge at Sill, loaded cart | 1 bit |
| North bridge at Sill | nothing; the Fishstairs folk argued it and won |
| River road to the Weir and Stonemouth | 1 bit a beast; passable on foot in Silt when the low way is under water |
| Winter ford at this quay | 1 bit, when there is water to cross |
| The ford when the Ilm bears ice | nothing, because no collector can cross |
| Plank walk at the Weir | 1 bit; the boards are counted and nobody says which will bear a man |

> *Margin:* In the Long Cold the Ilm bore ice for ninety-four days and no collector crossed from this quay. I have left the figure standing against the frozen ford because the factor wishes the board to be complete. It is the only figure on it that has never been paid.

# The verse the carters tie to the board

Four roads out of Greybank and one road down,
Clay to the bank and Salt to the sea;
Rim to the stone and Hearth to the fire,
and the four pay twice and the fifth pays free.

Four and four and a place where the fifth should be,
the beam keeps the weight and the river keeps the rest;
a man goes up with a mark in his coat
and comes back with a figure he cannot digest.

> *In the narrow Court hand, against the fifth line of the first verse:* Four roads entered. *Underneath, in mine:* There are five. I have put nothing in the place. A carter sang this to me on the quay and would not sing it while the factor's clerk was within hearing, which is how I know the fifth line is the one that matters.

# What the two borrowed hands did

The factor's clerk took the verbs out of my first paragraph and struck out *brown-edged* before *loaf*, saying a price board has no use for a description. I have put both back underneath. A loaf with no edge is a thing no household in Low Kiln has ever seen, and a price without a verb is a thing a man cannot be told to his face.

The Council clerk struck *toll* to *contribution freely entered* and numbered the roads as three, having left the river and the bridges out. I have written four above his three and left his three standing. Where he put a round nought against the fifth line of the carter's verse I have set nothing at all; a nought is a number, and that line is a place.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The road to the Hearth is paid at home. The road to the river is paid by the river.

I have asked the porters and the carters and the factor's clerk, and had four answers and no hand. I have left it where it was written. The leaf goes back inside the counting house and the board goes back on the quay wall, and tomorrow the barge comes in with salt from Vhal and the peat row will want copying again. A man will bring his barrow and ask for the morning's figure, and I shall give it to him, and by afternoon it will be a thing that was true.
