I keep the Covered Fire in the lane under Slate Row, and I cover the hearth at dusk on the Long Night, which is a different work from banking a fire to save it. In this town a fire is covered for one night in the year because the ordinance says no fire after dusk and no light carried between houses, and people sit in the dark and tell the stories of the Return. The fire in this room was lit from the Hall on Kindling, two days before, under a dish of ash carried up the lane by a woman who burned her wrist under the lip. I have kept this house eleven years and have never stood before the fire in the Hall. I have heard it under the grating from the passage and have not looked at it.

There are juniors from the College in the lane on that night and clerks come up from Greybank who cannot get back down the Clay Road before the Five are out. They ask one another questions whose answers they already know, which is a riddle in this trade. A question whose answer nobody knows is a wager, and there is wagering enough in the Hearthseat without a riddle in it.

I have set this down because a man came the next morning and asked whose voice had answered the fifth, and I could not tell him. Nothing is entered of what is spent in the night, which is an article of the Five. I wrote it at first grey with the hearth still covered, from what I remembered of the voices, and put the clerk's figures where he set them afterwards, above mine, the way the ash of the Last Day is burnt in the morning and called dealt with.

# The manner of the night

1. A riddle is put in the dark. Ask for a lamp to answer it and you owe a quart, because the light was for your own pleasure and not for the night.
2. Answer in one breath from where you sit. A second answer is a different riddle and is not heard, because in the dark nobody can tell whose mouth it came from, which is the reason here and the only one.
3. Four are scored from a seat. A fifth may be put from the floor and is not scored; the floor has no place in the dark and cannot be read in the morning.
4. The judge is the dark. I say whether an answer serves and I do not say why. A reason given is a riddle given away for nothing, and there is nobody here to buy it until the fire is uncovered.
5. No slate lies on the bench after dusk. A slate in the dark is a figure with the speaker left out, and the College can write those without help from this room.
6. The stake is a hand laid flat on the bench. At first grey I read the warmth of the hands and not the names, because a name spoken in the dark belongs to whoever says it in the morning.

# The four that were scored from a seat

The first went to a junior of the College, with ink settled in the crease of one cuff and the clean voice they teach in Slate Row.

> I stand between the flame and the hand;
> the hand that tends me has never felt me;
> a man may count my bars and never count my heat.
> What am I?

“The grating,” he said, before I had finished him; then, remembering the order of his house, “before the Hearth-fire, behind the iron.” He said it with the done-to left in, as juniors do when they are not being paid. I scored him and set a warm place down in my mind where his hand had been. There was no light to see whether he sat again.

The second went to a physician come down from the rooms of the Kept, who was on his way back up and would not drink in the dark.

> He is warm and does not know it;
> he is reckoned at three hundred and pitied under his own roof;
> a hand is brought to him and taken away again,
> and he never asks whose warmth it was.
> Who sits?

“One of the Kept,” said the physician, low, in the voice of that passage. “He has no want and no memory of a want, which is why we are paid to hold his hand.” Nobody passed the pot. A man who has just held the hand of a Kept vessel does not drink to it, and no rule tells him why.

The third went to a clerk of the Steward's room, who had come down for the notices and stayed because the lane was dark.

> I have no last line and I am never closed;
> a kinsman comes to end me and goes away with his name unentered;
> I am read once a year in a clean voice,
> and the mouths that said me are not on me.
> What am I?

“The roll,” he said. Then, in the hand's own phrase: “The account carried forward.” He said there was no other kind of account in his room, which is true there and not in the clay country, where a debt left lying is one the slate has never heard of. I scored him. He asked me who had put the question and I told him the dark had put it, which was true and not what he wanted.

The fourth came from the floor, which is not a seat and is not scored, and I set it down because it was said and I had no rule against hearing it.

> I am covered at dusk and uncovered at first grey;
> I was lit from the Hall and I am not the Hall's;
> what is spent in me is not written down.
> What am I?

“The Long Night,” said a woman who washes the College linen, into the bench and not to the room. Then she said: “And the fire under the ash is the same fire that was carried on Kindling, which nobody says out loud at the Weighing.” The junior asked whose voice it was and I said the dark's. There are five warm places on the bench and I have not set a name under any of them.

# The fifth, which was put from the door and answered from the floor

A woman stood at the door with a dish in her hands and did not come in, which is a thing a woman does in this town when she has carried something and wants to set it down where nobody will enter it. She had come up from the rooms of the Kept with coal under one thumbnail she had not washed off. She put the fifth without sitting:

> What is the figure for a thing spent in the night?

Nobody answered at first, because there is no figure for it and the College has an article about the morning. Then a voice from the floor said: “There is no figure. What is spent in the night is not entered in the morning.” It was a low voice from the side where the dish had been set down, but in the dark a voice has no place, and I did not see a mouth.

The junior asked who had said it. I said I did not know. The physician asked whether the woman at the door had said it, and she said she had carried a dish and not a sentence, and went back up the lane with the dish held against her coat. I have not asked her what was in it.

# What I read by touch at first grey

*The fire was still covered. I went along the bench with the backs of my fingers before I lifted the ash, and set down a place where the wood was warm and left the rest cold.*

| Question | Put by | What came back | Warm places |
|---|---|---|---|
| The grating | a College junior | the grating; the fire behind iron | one, at the College end |
| The Kept | a physician | one of the Kept; a hand held and paid for | one, by the door to the passage |
| The roll | the Steward's clerk | the roll; the account carried forward | one, at the notices |
| The Long Night | the floor | the Long Night; the carried fire | not scored; the linen woman's place |
| The figure for the night | a woman with a dish | no figure | not scored; one place, no name |

There were four scored places and two that were not, and six places of warmth on a bench the College would have entered as nil. I have left the sixth where it was. When the fire was uncovered the places went off the wood in the time it takes to say one in twelve, as the notice says, a figure read here every Weighing and never entered.

# What the College clerk did

He came down on the morning of the Remembering with ink settled in two fingers of a cuff gone soft from washing, and borrowed the leaf while I was setting the cups out. He writes narrow and upright and takes the doer out of every sentence.

- At *the hand that tends me has never felt me* he wrote *access to the Hearth-fire restricted; no approach*. True of the grating, and silent about the woman who carried the dish and burnt her wrist under the lip two days before.
- At *we are paid to hold his hand* he set *attendance recorded; no charge arising*. The hand is gone out of it and the payment with it, which is the way the College makes a room warm without anybody in it.
- At *the mouths that said me are not on me* he wrote *roll carried forward; names current*. He has set the mouth where the name should be, and left the name where the mouth was. Both stand.
- At *what is spent in the night is not written down* he wrote *observance; no transaction*. That is the article as it is printed. He did not ask why a room full of men would spend a night asking one another for nothing, and I did not tell him.
- At the fifth he wrote *no response tendered; struck*, and asked who had answered. I told him a voice from the floor. He wrote *unattributed voice; no figure arises*, and underlined it once, which is as near as a College clerk comes to being troubled.

I have put my words under his and left his standing above, the way the ash is left on the Last Day and burnt on the morning after. A clean line where the grey stood is not the same as gone, and there is no article in the Five about the line.

# In the margin, in broad charcoal

The woman who carried the dish up from the Hall on Kindling borrowed the leaf while I was turning it. She writes with a piece of the coal she carried, and the letters are wide and hard to keep in a line.

> The fire you covered was lit from the Hall and I carried it under a dish. The lip burnt me here and here and there is no entry for it in any book. You write of the dark as if nobody had paid for the light you put out at dusk. I have kept the burn two years and it has not gone.

I have copied it as it stands and not answered her, because the answer is an article of the Five and she has a better one under her wrist.

# At the head and in the gutter

The tallow at the head is mine, off the lamp I turned the leaf by at first grey before I covered it again. The lamp-black is in the grain of every word and no damp takes it out, which is why the Court's notice shows through it at one corner and cannot be washed off either: *one in twelve, as the notice says*, with the corner gone soft where the damp took it. The ring is a cup of water that stood from the first question to the fifth and went cold without being drunk, which is how water is drunk in this room when there is a thing to say and no proper word for it. The grey in the gutter came off the skirting board of a Kept room, the only Ash here that entered without a hand. The scorch is a spark off the grating caught when I lifted the ash; I put it out under my palm and did not enter the burn. The press of wet wool came off the physician, who held the door while the clerk came in and did not let go when he was through. The charcoal has rubbed thin where a sleeve went over the bench in the dark. The damp along the foot came out of the sealed-file room, which is colder than the rest of the Hearthseat. I have not swept the grey. There is a Last Day for it, and it is not this one.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The Hall keeps the flame. The grate keeps the hand.
> The night keeps the answer and the morning keeps none.
> The roll keeps the name, and not the mouth that said it.

I asked the junior, the physician, the clerk of the Steward's room and the woman who carried the dish, and had four answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were. The leaf goes back under the dish beside the banked hearth, and tomorrow the names of the dead will be spoken on the Remembering and the returns announced in a clean voice, and the Last Day after that, when the grey is left where it lies and burnt in the morning and the clean line is not called a loss; and on Kindling a year's fire will go out under a dish and be carried to every hearth in Sarn. This room will light from it. I shall cover it at dusk next Long Night and put the questions to the dark again, and if a man asks me what this house is named for I shall tell him: the fire that was covered, and not the one nobody may touch.
