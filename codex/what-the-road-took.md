I carry a pack on the Ilm circuit and have done these nineteen years, which is longer than most men last at the Court's work. Every market morning I hear a figure cried that I have already paid for in shoe leather, and every evening I set down what I gave, and the two never agree. This book is the second figure. The board on the quay tells what a thing fetched under the beam; it cannot tell what it cost to bring there, because no man behind a beam has walked the road.

There is a saying among the reed-cutters that a bundle is counted in the arm that carries it, and that the arm knows weight and not cost. My back knows the weight. This book knows the cost. I have never got the two to sit at one table.

> *In the narrow upright hand of a man from the College, written above my first line:* Figures entered in the ordinary way. *Underneath, in mine:* There is nothing ordinary about a figure that has walked eighty leagues. I have put the verbs back.

Twelve copper bits to a silver mark. Twenty silver marks to a gold crown, which is a Sere coin and does not stay in Grath. The Court books in embers and will tell a man an ember is five marks; no man has ever exchanged one for the other, and I have left no column for them. A bit, in this book, is a bit I parted with. A mark is a mark I did not want to part with.

> *In the College hand, struck through and written above:* An ember is valued at five marks. *In mine, restored underneath:* The Court values it. I do not. A verb with nobody in it is a verb that cannot be argued with, which is why they take them out.

# The pack, and what it fetched

*Figures cried at the Cross on Account-day set against what I actually put down between the last market of Stone and the Reed Account-days. The left column is what a man with a roof over his goods calls the price. The right is what I carried away less than I brought.*

| Article | As it is cried | As I paid |
|---|---|---|
| Coarse barley loaf, brown-edged | 1 bit | 1 bit, and a crust over at a door where nobody entered it |
| Quart of small ale, watered | 1 bit | 1 bit at Sill; 2 at the Weir, where they know it is watered |
| Salt herring | 2 bits | 2 bits, or 3 when the boat has been in and nobody says why |
| Eels, by the basket, at the boards | 3 bits smoked, 2 salted | a figure named under an apron, never the same twice |
| Coarse pots, the score | 4 bits | 4 bits at Sill; a pot broken at the feud post costs the seller |
| Tile, the hundred | 9 bits | 9 bits, and two hollow ones put in that I set out again |
| Peat, a barrow | 2 bits in Grain, 5 in Stone | the Stone figure, in Seed, without complaint |
| Salt, a sack off the pans | 3 marks | 3 marks, and a mark again at the coast pass |
| Iron, a bar, Keldish | 2 marks in the lowlands | 2 marks here; half a mark in Keld, where the road costs more than the bar |
| Glass, a sheet from the cliffs | 2 marks | 2 marks, and it broke before Stonemouth |
| Rope, a coil | 1 mark | 1 mark, and it left tar on this leaf |
| Sheepskin coat | 3 marks | 3 marks, or two and a promise in a hard year |
| Good axe | 2 marks | 2 marks; half a mark in Keld, which is the same axe |
| Ash-mite paste, a thimble, at Mud End | not on any board | 4 bits, no questions; the same grey in a Court jar is sixty marks and sealed |
| Reed bundle, an armful, at the Weir | not on any board | ask the arm that carried it; the arm does not know cost |
| Barley, a peck, at Cold Acre | not cried | 2 bits, though the well there gives water for nothing |

> *Margin:* I have set the eel row without a figure of my own, which is a place and not a nought. A buyer names a number under an apron; the seller puts her thumb to a cord and keeps it there longer than the number wants. There is no figure in that. A chapman who explains a stall does not last a tend at these boards.

> *Margin:* The paste and the sealed jar are the same grey in two vessels, one with a Fen name and one with a Court name. I carry neither. A man I drank with keeps a hand in a glove. I have not joined the jars, and have scraped out the word I wrote beside them; the bare square is the length of it.

# The markets of the circuit

The Cross at Sill holds its market on the tenth, twentieth and thirtieth, when the bell goes four, four and a gap and nobody crosses himself, and the slate is read twice, once for the moot and once for those who came late. I have stood under that bell in nine years of weather and never heard a figure that agreed with the one I paid at the stall. At Greybank the market is every day and the beam is always warm. At the Weir the eels are sold at dawn and the traps blessed on Ilmen fifteenth, and the eels run whether anybody blesses them or not. Stonemouth sells lime, sandstone and a reed-wolf tooth for a clamp-handle. Nine Pots sells pots and has never sold Sill a road.

There is a figure spoken in the yards of Low Kiln which is not the printed one. I have heard it and have not set it here. I have no beam to weigh it on, and a man who writes a figure he cannot weigh will be asked to read it aloud.

# The tolls of the roads

*The gate figure is what is demanded. The last column is what left my pocket. Four great roads and a fifth which is the river, and a handful of little roads that no board has ever heard of.*

|---|---|---|
| Clay Road, Sill end, loaded cart | 2 bits | 2 bits, and 2 more at the factor's man at Greybank |
| Clay Road, foot passenger | 1 bit; nothing on Account-day | 1 bit; on Account-day I walk with the crowd and pay nothing |
| River road to the Weir and Stonemouth, a beast | 1 bit | 1 bit; passable on foot in Silt when the low way is under |
| Plank walk at the Weir | 1 bit | 1 bit, and I took the board that was trodden flat |
| South bridge at Sill, loaded cart | 1 bit | 1 bit |
| North bridge at Sill | nothing | nothing; the Fishstairs folk argued it and won |
| The Nine Pots road, west from Sill | no gate | a pot set down at the feud post, which Sill calls an insult and the potters call a toll |
| Cold Acre road | no gate | a bit left under the well-cover, which nobody calls a toll because the well gives for nothing |
| Fen boardwalks to the Standing Post | 1 bit for a dry foot | a marked stick for a wet one; outsiders are not told which boards bear |
| Road to the Bone Orchard | nothing | pots broken at the grave-mounds cost the household, not the road |

| Rim Road, a cart at the pass | 1 mark, or iron to the value | 1 mark in iron, the iron being heavier than the mark |

> *In the Council clerk's leaning hand, against the salt row:* toll is struck and contribution freely entered written over it. *In mine:* The figures stand. A Council that cannot charge a toll has found a finer name for one, and a man pays it all the same.

> *Margin:* A carter from High Kiln asked what the Hearth Road cost and I showed him the empty column. He said it was the most honest figure on the page and the dearest. The Court takes nothing at the gate because it has taken it at home. I did not put that in the demanded column; it would stop nobody from loading.

# What no gate charges

These are not dues. No slate takes them and no factor enters them, and they are the reason a book like this has to be kept.

The Ferr runs lead dust off the western workings, and a man who drinks it through a season comes back thin; there is no figure at Stonemouth for that. The sweet sickness from bad glaze shows first as a pale line at the gum and ends with a man who cannot hold a cup, and the glaze-mixers of High Kiln taste it every morning and call it their work. A brine-gull went off below the north bridge with a coal not yet cold, and no slate in Sill stood one mark the poorer. Beyond the Cold Pass the cold takes a year and gives back a thinner man who has forgotten what he went for. A woman at the lower boards gave me a date where I asked for a figure and said I could shout at either one.

I have set all this under the roads and not among them. A due is a figure somebody will stand behind. These have nobody behind them, which is why they are the ones that get paid.

# The verse the chapmen carry

*Sung under the breath on the Clay Road, never at the Cross while a slate is out.*

> Twelve to the mark and twenty to the crown,
> and five to the ember the Court books down;
> the board keeps the figure the beam can hold,
> and the road keeps the rest in the soles of a man.
>
> A pot at Nine Pots and a bit at the Weir,
> a bit for the bridge and a mark at the mere;
> the Clay pays twice and the Salt pays twice,
> and the Hearth pays at home where the fire lies.
>
> Ask the arm, ask the cord, ask the running river,
> the Court has a figure for all of the three;
> I have a book with the figures I gave
> and a column kept empty for what the road took.

> *In the College hand, against the last line:* Three figures entered. *Underneath, in mine:* There are four, and I have set nothing in the place of the fourth. A nought is a figure. That line is a place.

# What the two borrowed hands did

The College man strikes the verbs out of what I write. Where I set *I paid*, he sets *was paid*, as though a mark could pay itself and nobody carry it up the road; where I set *the road took*, he sets *was taken*, as though a cold had no hand in it. I put both back underneath and leave his words standing above. A verb is the difference between a price and a debt, and a man who loses the verb has lost the man who owed.

The other hand is a counting house hand out of Sere, with a little gold in the ink. Against *2 bits in Grain, 5 in Stone* it wrote *seasonal rate*, and against the eel row, *market rate*, and put figures in the margin where I had put the price. I left both standing. A house of Sere can call a figure a rate, because a rate is a thing that can be emptied and refilled. I have held peat in a barrow in Silt and called it what it was.

# In the margins

The eel slime on the fore-edge is mine, from holding the board still at the Fishstairs with the hand that held the book. The silt in the gutter came off the second step below the north bridge and I have never got it out. The white at the head is salt bloom blown up from the Sallow; it comes and goes with the weather and is never entered. The tar at the fold came off a Vhaleni coil. The green bead is lead glaze from a woman who leaned to see the figures with the sweetness still at her mouth, and touched the paper, and said nothing. The docken leaf I pressed flat on the Cold Acre road for a blister; the bit gone verdigris in the bottom margin has been there since the Long Cold and is the only coin in the book never spent. The Ash at the head is off my own thumb turning the leaf toward the light; the iron-gall is in two fingers and will not come out. The bare square is the word I scraped away. There is no Last Day for a chapman's pocket, and nothing here is swept and burnt and called dealt with.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The board keeps the figure the beam held. The book keeps the figure the man carried. The difference has no column and no name.

I asked a carter, a stall-keeper at the boards, and a clerk come down from Greybank, and had three answers and no hand, as I have had before. I have left the line where it was written.

The book goes back into the inner pocket, against the place the strap has worn. Tomorrow I take the Clay Road up to Sill with salt and rope and a sheet of glass not yet broken, and a figure will be cried at the Cross that I shall pay in coin and in leather and in a day I shall not get back. By evening it will be a thing that was true. I shall set it under the figure they cried and leave the space between them as it is.
