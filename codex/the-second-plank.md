I keep the second plank at the Weir and the landing basket on it. Men come down the water with eels and reeds and go up with salt and iron, and they ask two things before the weather: what the thing fetched, and what the road took. I can answer the first by what I have seen go over this plank, the second by asking where a man came from, which gets a true answer when his boots are wet and a clean one when they are dry.

Every book of rates I have been shown puts the river fifth, in a place left empty or written as nothing. I have put it first, because I stand on it. The four roads feed it; a carter here has come down one, and his wheel paid before his load came to water. What follows is what I have taken and what the Cross has cried, on one leaf, because a man's purse does not keep them in two pockets.

Twelve copper bits to the silver mark; twenty marks to a gold crown, a Sere coin that goes back south and is no use at this plank. The Court books in embers and calls five marks an ember; no ember has ever been laid on this board, and I have left no column for one. A bit here is a bit I have held. A mark is a mark I have given change for.

> *In the narrow upright hand of the moot clerk, written above my first line:* Figures entered in the ordinary way. Rate of the plank. *Underneath, in mine:* There is nothing ordinary about a bit earned standing in water to the calf. I have put the hand back into the figure.

# What came down to the water

*The left column is the figure cried under the moot-post at Sill on Account-day, when the bell goes four, four and a gap. The right is what I have taken over the second plank, or what the woman at the boards told me under her apron. I do not say the two are the same. The moot does.*

| Article | Cried at the Cross | At this plank |
|---|---|---|
| Brown-edged barley loaf | 1 bit | 1 bit, carried in a basket and never weighed |
| Quart of small ale, watered | 1 bit | 1 bit here; 2 at the Weir, where they know it is watered |
| Salt herring | 2 bits | 2 bits, or 3 when the boat has been in and nobody says why |
| Smoked eel, at the boards | 3 bits | a number named under an apron, never twice alike |
| Salted eel | 2 bits | 2 bits and a thumb held on a cord longer than the number wanted |
| Eel skin, dried | 1 mark at the Greybank quay | nothing here; the skin goes down, not up |
| Reed bundle, an armful | not on any board | ask the arm that carried it; the arm knows weight and not cost |
| Coarse pots, the score | 4 bits | 4 bits, and a broken one at Nine Pots is the seller's |
| Tile, the hundred | 9 bits | 9 bits, with two hollow ones put in that a man sets out again |
| Peat, a barrow | 2 bits in Grain; 5 in Stone | the Stone figure in Seed, paid without complaint |
| Ice-mouse skin | 1 bit in Sill | 1 mark in Keld, which is the same skin with a road under it |
| Ash-mite paste, a thimble at Mud End | not on any board | 4 bits here; the same grey in a Court jar is sixty marks and sealed |

> *Margin:* I have left the eel row without a figure of my own. That is a place and not a nought. A woman names the number into her apron and puts her thumb to a cord. The grey in the Court jar and the grey in the Fen jar came out of the same corner, but I have not joined the jars and have scraped out the word I set beside them. The bare square is the length of it.

# What went up from the water

*Weighed at Greybank before the road takes its share, or not weighed at all. What a man pays at the far end is not what he paid here.*

| Article | Figure | What the road added |
|---|---|---|
| Salt, a sack off the pans | 3 marks | a mark at the coast pass, written as a contribution |
| Iron, a Keldish bar | 2 marks in the lowlands | half a mark in Keld, and a warm-stone off the next man |
| Glass, a sheet from the Glaze Cliffs | 2 marks | 1 bit for the coil it rides in; the sheet broke before Stonemouth |
| Rope, a coil | 1 mark | a bit if it goes on a cart, and tar on this leaf |
| Lead, a pig from the western workings | 6 marks | the seller does not say which workings, and the dust is not entered |
| A kiln hand, the day | 1 mark | 6 bits for a child sweeping the yard |

| Pig, over the weighing board | 6 marks | 4 bits for the drove and a bit to have it cried twice |

> *In a Council clerk's leaning hand:* *Seasonal rate* against the peat, *market rate* against the eels. *In mine:* A house of Sere can call a figure a rate, because a rate is a thing that can be emptied and refilled. I have held peat in a barrow in Silt and called it what it was.

# The tolls of the roads that come down here

*The figure demanded is written above the figure I saw go out of a pocket. Four roads come to this water and one road is the water itself. I have set the water first, which is where the clerk's hand will want to correct me.*

| Road and place | As demanded | As given |
|---|---|---|
| The river, going down or coming up | no toll and no guarantee | nothing at the plank; the river takes what it takes and enters none of it |
| Plank walk at the Weir | 1 bit | 1 bit on the board that bears; I do not say which board it is |
| North bridge at Sill | nothing | nothing; the Fishstairs folk argued it and won |
| Winter ford, when there is water to cross | 1 bit | 1 bit; in the Long Cold the ice bore for ninety-four days and no collector crossed |
| Clay Road, loaded cart at the Sill end | 2 bits | 2 bits here and 2 more at the factor's man at Greybank |
| Clay Road, foot passenger | 1 bit; nothing on Account-day | 1 bit; on Account-day I walk with the crowd and pay nothing |
| Salt Road, a sack at the coast pass | 1 mark to the Council of Tides | 1 mark, freely entered, which is what they call it |
| Rim Road, a cart at the pass | 1 mark, or iron to the value | 1 mark in iron, the iron heavier than the mark |
| Rim Road, a beast on the hoof | half a mark | half a mark and a warm-stone off the next man who has one |
| Hearth Road, at the barrier | none; there is no barrier | none; the figure was read to me twice at home |
| Nine Pots road, west from Sill | no gate | a pot set down at the feud post, an insult in Sill and a toll in the yards |
| Fen boardwalk to the Standing Post | 1 bit for a dry foot | a marked stick for a wet one; outsiders are not told which boards bear |

> *In the moot clerk's narrow hand, against the first four rows:* Roads entered, three. *Underneath, in mine:* There are five, and the first is under my feet. I have written my four above his three and left both standing. A river is not a gap in a list; it is the thing the list was made to reach.

> *In the Council clerk's leaning hand, against the salt row:* *toll* struck and *contribution freely entered* written over it. *In mine:* The figures stand. A Council that cannot charge a toll has found a finer name for one, and a man pays it out of the same purse.

> *Margin:* A carter from High Kiln asked what the Hearth Road cost and I showed him the empty row. He said it was the truest figure on the page and the dearest. The Court takes nothing at the gate because it has taken it at home. I did not put that in the demanded column; it would stop nobody from loading.

# What no gate takes

These are not dues. No slate takes them and no factor enters them, and they are why a book like this has to be kept.

The Ferr runs lead dust off the western workings, and a man who drinks it through a season comes back thin; there is no figure at Stonemouth for that. The sweet sickness from bad glaze shows first as a pale line at the gum and ends with a woman who cannot hold a cup; the mixers of High Kiln taste it each morning and call it their work. At the Weir the traps go under ice and a man who lifts them comes up with his hands the colour of old rope; the water keeps no count. Beyond the Cold Pass the cold takes a year and gives back a thinner man who has forgotten what he went for.

A due is a figure somebody will stand behind. These have nobody behind them, which is why they get paid.

# The song of the second plank

*Sung at the landing when the lamp is lit and nobody is crossing. Not at the Cross, and not while a man from Slate Row is on the boards.*

> One for the board and none for the stream,
> the stream pays the boat and it never pays the moot;
> four roads come down with a bit in the shoe,
> and one comes down with the whole of the crew.
>
> Twelve to the mark and twenty to the crown,
> and five to the ember the Court books down;
> the beam keeps the weight and the bag keeps the sum,
> and the river keeps going and takes none of it home.
>
> A pot at Nine Pots and a bit at the pass,
> a mark at the coast and nothing at the bridge;
> ask the arm, ask the cord, ask the running water,
> the Court has a figure for all of the four.
>
> I have a book with the figures I gave
> and a row kept empty for what the river took.

> *In the moot clerk's hand, against the last line:* Three figures entered. *Underneath, in mine:* There are four, and I have set nothing in the place of the fourth. A nought is a figure. That line is a place.

# What the borrowed hands did

The moot clerk struck the river out of the count and numbered the roads as three, leaving the water and the bridges out. I wrote four above his three and put the water above the four, so a man sees where his load came from before he sees what it cost. The Council clerk struck *toll* and wrote *contribution freely entered*, and left every figure standing. The factor's clerk called the peat a seasonal rate and the eels a market rate. I have left their words above mine and mine under theirs, as a mended pot keeps the old break showing.

My granddaughter did the sums in the corner and got the crown wrong, putting twenty marks to a bit and carrying one. I have not rubbed it out. She will learn which figures a man can argue with, which is the harder lesson and not taught at any College.

# In the head and in the fold

The varnish at the head is eel slime, off the hand that held the basket while I wrote; no washing takes it out. The silt in the gutter came in on the boots of every carter since the thaw, and is the same silt that lies under the sill in Sill, which the moot does not count and the river does. The salt bloom has eaten the last letter of the date, so I cannot read whether it was Stone 23 or Stone 24 the wind came on, which is the only loss I mind. The green bead is lead glaze off a cup whose rim had gone thin; a girl come down from High Kiln set it on the leaf, with the sweetness still at one corner of her mouth, and went back without asking what it was. The reed I pressed flat on the walk below the plank, where the boards move after rain and nobody will say which will bear. The grey along the outer fold is not mine and I have not swept it; there is Ash enough here without borrowing a stranger's.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The board keeps the figure the beam held. The plank keeps the figure the man carried. The river keeps neither, and takes the road in winter.

I asked a carter, a woman at the eel boards and a clerk come down from Greybank, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written.

The quire goes back under the second board and the reed goes back around it. Tomorrow the ice may bear or it may not, and a man will come down with a sack and ask what his salt fetched and what the road took, and I shall give him the figure I gave today. By evening it will be a thing that was true.
