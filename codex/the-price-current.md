I keep the draws at the tide-tally house on the Quay. A captain comes in off the ebb with salt in his hold and a paper in his cuff, and asks what his salt will fetch and what the road will take out of him on the way up. The first I can tell him. The second I know only at the far end, and the far end writes to me on paper that has already begun to go soft in a coast pocket. A carter from Greybank sends his figures on good paper and complains when mine will not last the week. We make our contracts on salt-paper here, and we account it a virtue that the damp gets into them. A debt left out in the rain is a debt made nothing, and half the fortunes of this port have turned on that.

This leaf I keep dry on purpose. It is the only dishonest thing I do.

Twelve copper bits to the silver mark; twenty marks to a Sere crown, which comes north with the buyers and goes south again with them, a fine thing to hold and a poor thing to spend on this Quay. The Court books in embers and will tell a man an ember is worth five marks. Nobody has ever brought one through this door, and I keep no column for it. A stranger's coin warms before his face, we say; that is not the same as being welcome, and it is not a figure.

> *In the narrow upright hand of a factor's clerk come up from Greybank:* Coin and reckoning entered. Ember: five marks. *Underneath, in mine:* He writes *is worth* where I should write *the Court calls it worth*. A thing called five marks and a thing that changed hands at five marks are two things, and only one comes through this house.

# What the Quay gives

*The first column is the figure at the pans or the furnaces, before a load is moved. The second is what is cried at the Quay on an Account-day, which is the figure a captain carries in his head. The wheel of the tide turns between them and takes its share without writing.*

| Article | At the pans or the furnace | Cried at the Quay |
|---|---|---|
| Salt, a sack, cure | 2 marks | 2 marks, and a mark at the pass called a contribution |
| Salt, a pound, for the north cure | — | 4 bits, in a dry wrap; the wrap is not returned |
| Glass from the Cliffs, a sheet | 2 marks | 2 marks; sold as far as Sere |
| Rope, a coil, hemp or warp | 1 mark | 1 mark; wet rope weighs more and no buyer has ever noticed why |
| Dried fish, salt herring, the dozen | — | 2 bits |
| Smoked eel | — | 3 bits; the eel-women at the Fishstairs set their own figure |
| Tallow, a stone | — | 2 marks; most of it goes south and comes back as a Sere lamp |
| Tar, a small cask | 2 bits | 3 bits; the extra bit is the cooper's, and he will not say what for |
| Eel-sinew, a hank | — | 1 bit; it sewed this leaf's wrapper, and the seller would not take the bit back |
| A sailor's share, the month | — | 2 marks and keep |
| A salt-worker, the month | — | 1 mark and a binding promise; the promise is kept wet |
| A boat built, hull and oars | — | 30 marks; the shipwright curses every hour of the work and takes the marks |
| A man come aboard | — | an opinion at half; no price, and never entered as a share |

> *In the leaning hand of a Council clerk, who will not write upright:* Against *a mark at the pass called a contribution*, strike *toll*. Write *contribution freely entered*. *Underneath, in mine:* He has left the figure standing. A Council that cannot charge a toll has found a finer name for one, and a sack pays it all the same.

> *In a Greybank factor's narrow hand, against the salt row:* Salt, three marks, as at the beam. *In mine:* Two at the pans, one at the pass, three at the beam. I have left both figures, because the middle mark travels under a name the Council chose itself.

# Articles a man asks for after he has paid

*Not cried on the Quay. I keep the list behind the tally bench, in the drawer that will not close in damp.*

| Article | Price |
|---|---|
| A phial of quiet ash, by the Court's licence | 60 marks |
| Grey in an unmarked jar | 4 bits, from a dealer who came up from the Fen; the same grey in two vessels, one with a Court name and one with none |
| A passage south for a thin return | not taken here; the Council will not pay for one and will not house one |
| A salted boat, pushed out on the ebb | watched until it is out of sight, which is held to be enough; no figure |

> *Margin:* A man came in with a licence for quiet ash and asked what the licence cost besides the sixty marks. I told him the licence was the sixty marks and the keeping was his own. He went away and wrote it down on salt-paper, which is the proper way to preserve nothing.

# The dues of the roads

Four ways leave this Quay and one leaves the world. What is below is what is asked of a man before he loads, and what is written on his paper when he comes back. The two do not agree, and the difference is the whole art of travelling.

## The Salt Road, up to Greybank and Sill

Eighty leagues by the coast pass, sixty-two beyond the eighteen of the Clay Road. Twenty days on foot at four leagues a day, more in a thaw, and a cart makes it in six days in Silt if the driver is cruel and the horse is fresh. Salt, glass, rope and dried fish go up; coin and contracts come back, and the damp gets into both.

| | Due |
|---|---|
| Sack of salt, at the coast pass | 1 mark to the Council of Tides, entered as a contribution freely entered |
| Coil of rope, or a sheet of glass | 1 bit |
| Man going up with the glass at the turn of the month | 3 bits, returned if he comes back with the answer |
| Boat-share passing inland | paid in kind, by the lot and not the league |
| Contract written on salt-paper | no fee; it will not survive the journey, which the Council calls a virtue |
| Cart with a load, at the Greybank beam | 2 bits, and the same again when it turns down the Clay Road to Sill |
| A barrow of grey going north through a gate | not weighed, not entered, and no figure reaches it |

> *In a boat-master's blunt charcoal, added at the foot of the column:* Paid at the pass and paid at the beam and paid again for having carried it, and my paper gone soft before I could read what I had paid. *In mine:* All three stand. A road has two ends and both have a hand; the sea has one hand and does not write.

## The shore road to the Glaze Cliffs

Five leagues along the coast from the glass furnaces. The cliffs are stained blue-green by old lead working and smell faintly sweet in the heat. Children are warned not to lick them, which is a warning the sea does not give to cargo.

| | Due |
|---|---|
| Cart of glass-sand going up | nothing at any gate; there is no gate |
| Sheet of glass coming back | 1 bit at the pass, entered to the Council |
| A man who licks the cliff | his teeth, which are not entered anywhere |
| Lead out of the old working | 6 marks the pig; the seller does not say which working, and we do not ask |

## The pans road

| | Due |
|---|---|
| A cart to the pans | nothing; the pans are the works of the port and not a road out of it |
| A dipper blessing the nets at dawn | the net is trailed in the sea and nothing is charged for the sea |
| Salt taken before the crust has set | paid by weight, and the weight changes after you pay |

## The passages of the Sallow

The Sallow lies milk-coloured against the north-east, and its tides run against the wind. The dippers say it follows the Warm rather than the moon, which a tally-house cannot enter because it has no figure that will stand still.

| | Due |
|---|---|
| The ebb going out | no toll; the tide takes the boat and is not asked for a receipt |
| The turn | a count is taken elsewhere and two counts in one morning make a debt |
| The flood coming in | 1 bit on the boards, which the Fishstairs folk argued and lost |
| A salted boat with a dead man aboard | watched until it is out of sight; no due, and no return entered |
| A galley on the pulled benches | seven years, or until the hands give out, whichever comes first |

## The roads that are not roads of this port

The Rim Road takes a mark or iron at the pass and promises no man back. The Hearth Road takes nothing at any gate, having taken it at home; the Clay Road takes twice, once at each end. I have kept these lines from the tallies of other men and set no figure of my own against them. A tally-keeper who writes what he has not weighed is writing a Court notice.

# What is not entered

| | What is taken | What I write |
|---|---|---|
| A contract left in the damp | the debt, by the Council's own account | nothing; the paper has done it |
| A man whose account is current | the price of welcome | nothing; we have no word for guest |
| An opinion at half | the man come aboard | nothing; it is not his to sell |
| Warmth off a cold tide | carried seaward, by report | nothing; no collector follows it |
| The grey along the gutter | left where a cuff was laid | nothing swept and burnt and called dealt with |
| A name said at home | kept under a roof, not on any roll | a door, which I do not draw |

# The song the tallymen say

*Said low at the head of the flood, to the pot and not to a man, and never while a clerk from Slate Row stands in the door. The last word of each verse falls into the water and is not finished.*

> Two at the pans and one at the pass,
> and three at the beam where the factor stands;
> a bit for the rope and a bit for the glass,
> and the paper pays nothing in Vhaleni lands.
>
> The ebb takes a boat and the flood takes a bit,
> the turn takes the count and gives none back;
> a stranger's coin warms before his face
> and the Council writes what the rain will unwrap.

> *In the Council clerk's leaning hand, against the first line:* Three figures entered. *Underneath, in mine:* There are four roads and a fifth place where the passage stops. He has put a nought against what I left bare. A nought is a figure a man can carry away in his cuff.

# The hands that are not mine

The Greybank clerk takes the doer out of every sentence, as though a sack could pay itself and a rope could coil itself at the turn of the month. The Council clerk calls a toll a contribution, which is the same figure with the doer invited in to thank it. The boat-master writes in charcoal because ink will not sit on a wet table. I have set my words under all three. A leaf with four hands on it is the only kind a man on this Quay will believe. A leaf with one hand is a Court notice, and a Court notice has never told a captain what the pass would charge him.

A child of the Quay did her sums on the back in salt-paper charcoal, two bits going into a mark where four should have stood, and the rain took one of them clean away. I have not rubbed out the other. It is the only figure here nobody will be asked to pay, and the only one that will still be here when the rest of us have gone soft.

# At the head and in the gutter

The white at the head is salt bloom off this house's own paper; I keep the leaf dry and the bloom comes to find it anyway. The tar at the fold is off the coil a boat-master set down while he argued the second mark. The wet rope pressed into the lower leaf looks like a figure and is not one. The green bead came from a girl with the sweetness of the cliff at one mouth-corner, who touched the leaf and said nothing. The brine-gull peck went through a word of the song and I have not written it back, because a verse with a word gone is a verse said slowly. The ring is not ale, and I did not see the cup go down. The grey in the gutter came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying, and I have not swept it. There is no Last Day at a tide-tally.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The paper keeps the debt the rain unmakes.
> The road keeps the mark the pass renames.
> The Sallow keeps the boat and does not enter it.

I asked the keeper of the draws, a carter off the coast pass, and a woman who holds a share through her mother, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The leaf goes back into its tin, and in the morning the Council clerk will come for the tallies and find this one dry, which in Vhal is the most suspicious thing on the Quay. A captain will ask the figure I gave him yesterday and be given it again; by the time his paper reaches Greybank it will be a thing that was true.
