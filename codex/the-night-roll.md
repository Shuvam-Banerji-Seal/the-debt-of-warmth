I keep the room between the drawing and the banking. A collector brings a vessel in at the going of the light with the tube still warm in its wool and a slate-name already written, and I set it on a pallet until the banker comes before first grey. I do not draw and I do not bank. I hold. There are three of us in this trade in Sill — the reader at the Cross, the man who carries the slate up the road, and I who keep the room — and our figures never meet, which is the arrangement the Court has always liked.

I have no house and no kin, so the room is my house and the roll is my kin. I write in the upright hand and leave the doer out, as I was taught, because a sentence with nobody in it can be read in the Weighing Hall without anybody being asked to stand behind it. What I cannot leave out I have set on the back of the leaf, where a correction written on the face cannot follow.

Twelve copper bits to a silver mark; twenty marks to a Sere crown, a coin that comes down the Clay Road and goes back up it. An ember is booked at five marks and no man in this room has ever exchanged one. A newborn vessel holds twelve to fifteen embers, a labourer in good case forty to sixty. One in twelve is printed on the notice and read twice over the basket at the Cross. The other figure travels up the road in a locked box and does not come into this room. I enter the printed figure and leave the other where I heard it, on the back.

> *In the narrow upright hand of a clerk of the College, struck through the column headed "What was said":* No such column. *On the back of the leaf, in mine:* He struck it on the face and did not turn the leaf. A man who has corrected a thing has still had to read it before he can write above it. I have kept what was said where he could not put his hand.

Draws held overnight on this leaf: eight. Lines with no draw in them: two. Figures struck: two. Columns struck: one. Stones gone cold on the pallets: nine, including the one that was not a vessel.

# The order of the room

- Receive the slate before the face. The collector hands the slate over and I set my eye on the writing first; the person on the pallet is looked at afterwards, when the door is shut. This is the order of the Keeping and I have kept it nine years. A face looked at before the slate is entered is a face a clerk will remember, and a clerk who remembers is a clerk who can be asked.
- Set a warmed stone to the belly of a child and not to the palm of a grown person, who has had more warmth than the child and will not be thanked for it. Take the stone away at first grey whether the child has warmed or not. I have never been told why, and have stopped asking the physician.
- Do not tap a person to hear them. A body is not a banked cup. I did it once to a woman gone thin and got nothing back for the knuckle, and have not done it again.
- Write what is said after the slate is shut on the back of the leaf, and never under a heading. A heading makes it an account, and an account is a thing a person may be called to answer for at the Weighing.
- The pot is broken at the door of a death-house before the vessel is brought here, not after. I have found shards on the step in the morning and have not swept them into the gutter with the straw. A shard left on a step is somebody's instruction carried out, which is more than I can say for the figure.

A woman who came in with the green at her mouth said her girl had a name before she had a number, and asked which of the two the banker would press into the infant. I told her the figure was banked and the account stood, because those are the words I am paid to use, and put a stone in her hand so that she would have something to do with it. She set it on the pallet and it was cold when the banker came. I have entered the figure and not the stone.

# What was said with the slate shut

These were set on the back of the leaf and struck on the face. I have left the holes where the College hand went through, which is the only honest mark that hand has ever made.

- Against the widow: *Three loaves to the levy and one to the pot. Ask the pot what it cost.*
- Against the clay-bound hand: *He said the wrist was his and then said nothing else all night.*
- Against the child three days returned: *A name said into the ground before the collector came. Not repeated.*
- Against the thrower: *The pot was his. There is no column for a pot.*
- Against the glaze-room girl: *She asked after her girl. The girl has a number. The mother wants a name.*
- Against the Weir man: *Four, four and a gap. He crossed himself at the second four and said he had not.*
- Against the woman come back a second time: *She gave the old number and would not give the new. Both are hers.*

A nought is a figure and can be carried out of a room in a cuff. A name said into the ground is a place and stays where it was said. The College has eleven words for a deduction and no word for any of these, which is the whole of the difficulty between us.

# The pallet-song

*Said low in the room, to the stone and not to the person holding it. Never while the collector's tube is out of its wool, and never at the banking. The last answer is left out by those who know it.*

> Four on the near board, four on the next,
> and a place where the fifth should be;
> the hand goes to the slate before the face,
> and the face goes out with the figure.
>
> One in twelve, says the notice, read twice at the Cross;
> the road carries another and will not show it.
> A stone for the belly, a cup by the wall,
> and a name said low to the stone.
>
> The measure is banked, the account stands,
> nothing is lost that we have kept;
> ask the pot, ask the weather, ask the pallet straw ---
> the answer is not entered here.

A woman brought up from Low Kiln sang the first two verses while she waited for her child to be banked and would not sing the third while the banker was in the room, which is how I know which verse is the one that matters. I have copied what was said and put no gloss on it. A clerk who explains a song has begun to write a sermon, and sermons get copied.

# The two borrowed hands

The College clerk struck the night-column on the face of the leaf and wrote *No such column* above it. He had to read every row before he could write that there was nothing in any of them worth entering, and I watched him turn the leaf and set it down again without turning it back. I have kept the sayings on the back. The face is his; the back is mine, and paper has two sides even in the Keeping.

The other hand is tall and leaning and comes out of the factor's house at Greybank. Against the child three days returned it struck thirteen, set twelve above, and wrote *a vessel returned cannot hold thirteen after the draw*, which may be true and does not mention the child on the pallet. Against the foundling it set a round nought in the column where no slate-name stood, as if a child without a house were a child without a figure. Both stand. A nought travels; a foundling cannot, until a hearth claims him. I have put my words underneath and left the correction above, as the pallet keeps the flat of the straw under the smooth of the blanket.

# What the room leaves on the leaf

The straw in the gutter comes off the pallets and I have never got it out; it works in deeper than the Ash. The tide-line along the foot is from the washing of the floor on Ash 10, when a pot was broken on the step and somebody used more water than the step wanted. The cold dark spot at the head is where a warmed stone stood on the leaf while I wrote the row for the child and went cold before I had finished the remark, which is the order of things and not an omen. The grey rubbed into the grain came off the corner of the room where the Ash gathers by tallow-light and is invisible in daylight, as everybody knows and nobody names. The pale ring is a cup set down by the woman with the green at her mouth and not lifted; it has gone the colour of old lead. The press of grey wool is where a collector's sleeve lay while he waited for me to take the slate. The green bead is not slip but lead glaze, pressed into the corner by a girl who leaned to see the writing with the sweetness still at one mouth-corner. The hair in the fold came off a head that lay on the pallet through the middle dark and was gone before first grey. The small bare square is where I scraped a word away with a knife and put no other in its place; I had written *ninth*, meaning the ninth stone that went cold, and then could not say which of the eight had been a vessel and which had not, so I took the word off. There is no Last Day in this room, and nothing here is gathered up and burnt in the morning and called dealt with.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The slate keeps the figure. The pallet keeps the shape of the person who lay on it. The bank keeps neither, and presses both into a child.

I asked the College clerk, the man who carries the slate up the road, and a woman come up from Low Kiln with a child on her hip, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written.

The book goes back on the shelf beside the wool sleeve and the tin of tallow ends. The banker will come before first grey and take what the room has held, and I shall receive the next slate before I look at the next face, and write the figure on the face of the leaf and the rest on the back. The stone will go cold on the pallet at about the second four of the bell. That is my trade: to hold what has been drawn until it is called for, and to keep, where no correction can follow, the length of the time it was held.
