I keep the chronicle of the Keeping, which is not the same thing as the chronicle of Grath. Grath writes what happened, when it can get a man to stand still long enough to ask. We write what was entered. Where the two disagree we set the entry down and leave the gap where it fell, because a chronicler who closes a gap has begun to write a sermon, and a sermon gets copied oftener than an account and lasts longer.

This chapter is the hardest in the book to copy, and not because of the figures. The figures are neat. It is the things that stand beside them that will not go into a year-entry, and so I have put them in the margin. A margin is the only place in a chronicle where a silence is allowed to stand.

# The years as the roll-room has them

*Copied from the Hearthseat original, kept face down. The entries are in Ledgerhand. I have put the verbs back where I found them taken out and left the College's hand standing above mine.*

**Y.H. 668.** In Low Kiln a levy was taken of ninety children's warmth, for one whole return. No household in Sill could say whose return it was. There followed eleven weeks of disorder in the district, in the course of which the peace of the Keeping was disturbed and certain collectors were prevented from the discharge of their office. Entered as *a disturbance of the peace*.

**Y.H. 669.** Assessor-Marshal Pell Varr came up from the Hearthseat with twelve collectors and a list. Forty-one persons dead, all of them spent in the keeping of the peace. The rising ended in the ninth week of his sitting. Entered as *peace restored, account standing*.

**Y.H. 675.** Certain survivors of the rising first named in a Court file as being of the sealed opinion.

**Y.H. 683.** Quiet ash sold openly in the Hearthseat for the first time, by licence of the factor.

> *In the narrow hand of the College, against the first entry:* The levy was for a whole return. The return is entered at Greybank. Strike *no household could say whose*.

I have not struck it. The return is entered at Greybank, which is true, and no household in Sill could say whose it was, which is also true, and the two truths have different shapes and must not be laid over one another. A man who writes the annals learns early that the word *disturbance* costs less than the word *children*. The word is chosen for the same reason a potter chooses a thin glaze, because it covers and because it is cheap.

# The list

Varr came with twelve collectors and a list. The list was the heavier thing he carried, and the only one he ever set down. It was made in the Hearthseat before he left, so that the names stood in a clean hand while the men who bore them were still going about their work in Low Kiln. This is not a cruelty. It is a method, and the method has a name in the College, which is *economy of office*. I entered the words as they were spoken to me by a clerk who was present at the reading and has since been returned whole.

The list, as it stands in the roll-room, has four headings.

| Heading | Number entered | What the heading covers |
|---|---|---|
| Persons taken for the whole return | 90 | Children of Low Kiln, entered under their houses |
| Collectors detailed to the district | 12 | Named, with their districts after them |
| Persons spent in the keeping of the peace | 41 | Named in the body of the report, not on the list |
| Weeks from the levy to the restoring of the peace | 11 | Entered as nine weeks sitting, plus two weeks travelling |

Forty-one dead, all of them spent in the keeping of the peace. The phrase is exact, and it is the only phrase in these annals I have ever had to read twice to believe. A man spent in the keeping of the peace is a man who died while other men were being made to keep it. The College has weighed the phrase and finds it sound. I set it down as it came and have not tried to make it kinder.

There is one line in the report that I cannot place. It stands between the twelve names and the forty-one, in Varr's own hand, and it reads *and the corners also*. I asked three men of the College what the corners were, and got the same answer three times, which was that a corner is where two walls meet. This is true and is not an answer.

# The corners that were not swept

The hearth-maids of Low Kiln say nothing. I have tried four times to get it onto the page. Silence is not an entry, and the College takes verbs out of anything that is not one; in the end I wrote the only thing I could write, which is what the district does with the grey.

On the Last Day the ash in a Grath corner is left where it lies and burnt in the morning. That is the ordinance and the observance both. In the years after Y.H. 669, word came up from Sill that in the houses of Low Kiln the grey was not burnt in the morning either, but left in its ridge along the skirting board into Seed and past it. I could not get a man to say this before a witness. I asked a woman who had carried birth-slates in that district for thirty years and she gave me a date for answer, which is what they do when the thing asked for is not a date.

| In the chronicle of the Keeping | What came up from Low Kiln |
|---|---|
| Forty-one spent in the keeping of the peace | Ninety taken, and no household paid them back |
| Eleven weeks of disorder | The kilns stood cold eleven weeks |
| Peace restored, account standing | The corners were not swept |
| The matter is closed | The grey is still there |

> *In the narrow hand of the College, against the third row of the right-hand column:* Corners are swept in due order on the morning after the Last Day. Enter as observance kept.

He has entered it as observance kept. Both rows stand. The ridge of Ash is the only entry in this chapter that cannot be corrected, because it is not a figure and not a word and has no verbs for him to take out. It is a grey line about two thumbs wide which turns the corner of a room without a break, and which somebody has to have put there, because Ash does not lay itself along a skirting board in the shape of a kept thing.

I set the hearth-maids' answer in the margin rather than under the heading, because it is not an answer to the question the chronicle asks. The chronicle asks how many and when. They answer with a corner. It is the custom of that district to answer a number with an object, and an officer who does not know the custom goes away thinking he has been refused, when in fact he has been given the only true thing that was in the room.

# The song out of Low Kiln

*Taken down from a woman of the drying racks who had not been in the district in Y.H. 668 and had learned the song from those who were. Sung low, at the washing, not at the fire.*

> Ninety went up in a grain-cart's belly,
> eleven weeks and the kilns stood cold;
> twelve came up with a list in a clean hand,
> and the list had the names before they were told.
>
> Forty-one spent in the keeping of the peace,
> the slate says, in a hand that never shook;
> four for the corners and four for the wall,
> and one for the place where the ninety stood.
>
> We do not sweep. We do not burn.
> We lay the grey where the promise fell.
> The Court has a word for the thing we keep;
> we have a corner, and that serves as well.
>
> Come back, come back, little pot of the clay,
> the seam keeps the word that the mouth denied;
> the grey is not dirt and the corner is not dumb,
> it holds the count that the slate would not hold.

The woman would not sing the last verse until the fire was banked, and would not say who made the song. I have left both things as I found them. A song that is sung at the washing and not at the fire is a song about work, and work is a thing a household can remember without being told to remember it, which is more than can be said of an entry.

# The sealed opinion

From the survivors of those eleven weeks came the people the Court calls Unbanked. They call themselves nothing at all, which is a hard thing for an office to enter, and the College has entered it as *refusal of designation*. Their argument is old and I have set it down once, plainly, because it is the only part of this chapter that the chronicle has had to make room for: if the world must be kept, let it be kept perfectly, so that nothing leaks, nothing is owed, and no one can give anything away. A world of unbreakable vessels would be a world of the walking dead. That is their saying, not mine. The Court's public answer is that they are mad, and the answer is entered in the Weighing notices, where the figure one in twelve is also printed.

The Court's private answer is in a sealed file in this building, two rooms from where I write. I have not opened it and am not likely to be asked to. There are doors in the Hearthseat that a chronicler passes every day and is never required to go through, and a man who keeps the annals learns to measure a room by the doors it has as well as by the walls.

In Y.H. 683 quiet ash was sold openly here for the first time. I am old enough to have seen the first phial go over the counter and to have known the woman it was for. I have not put her name in the entry. She paid in marks, which is a thing anyone can write down, and with something she said at the counter, which is not.

# What the College hand did

Where I wrote of the ninety children that they were taken and not returned, a narrower hand set *the account stands* above and left mine under it, in the manner that takes a verb for a thing a man might smuggle in a cuff. Where I wrote *the corners were not swept*, it wrote *observance kept*. Where I wrote that Pell Varr came with a list already made, it wrote *precautions taken*. It is a careful hand and a clean one, and it has never once in this chapter changed a figure.

There is a line at the foot in a hand I do not know, struck through twice and written again underneath, and it says: *They took the warmth and left the account, and the account is the only thing that came back whole.* I have copied it as it stands. The hand that wrote it wanted to be found and did not want to be known, which is the state of most hands that write in the margin of this book.

# At the foot

The iron-gall at the head is mine; a chronicler who keeps a quire open through a tend marks himself before he marks the page. The soot came off the grate on a night when the Hearth was low and I held a leaf too near to read it. The hard drop of tallow is from the lamp in the roll-room and has been there since the first copy. The slate dust in the gutter came off a surveyor come up from Keld who leaned over my table with the Rim still on his cuffs and would not sit down. The grey along the outer fold is not mine and is not the College's. I have had this leaf four years and have never swept it, and if the factor asks me why a chronicler leaves dirt on a chronicle I shall tell him it is entered as observance kept, which is a phrase he will accept without looking up.

The leaves go back into the drawer. The annals go on, as annals do, and next tend I copy the quay tallies from Greybank and after that the Weighing figures. The ninety have no row in the figures and the corners have no heading, but the grey turns the corner without a break and the levy is still read at one in twelve in every Court room in Sarn. I have put both down where they stand. A chronicler does not settle a thing by writing it. He keeps the place where it stood, so that the next hand has something to copy besides the clean version.
