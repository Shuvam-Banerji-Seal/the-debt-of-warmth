I have delivered children in this town for two and twenty years, and kept the book of their births for nine. The book lies on the bench under my apron when I am not using it, and a girl come up from the clay yard asked me once what I wrote in it. I told her figures. She asked what else. I told her the sayings my mother said while she held the basin, which are not in the book and never will be, because a magistrate cannot enter them and the Court does not pay for them.

So I have put them here, on the backs of slates that went wrong. A slate with a figure in the wrong column can be washed with barley-water and written over, which is a mercy the Court allows to stone and to nobody else. Say these low. Say them to the stone, the slate, the fire and the bench, and to a person last. Said looking at a person, a saying becomes a question with an answer owed, and there is no answer owed in any of them.

The Court has paid us since seven hundred eighty-two to write a birth within three days. Before that we wrote when we had a mind to, which was usually the day after. We objected when they came with the order, and they paid us, and the objection stopped. I have set that down because a girl should know that the hand which writes the slate is the same hand that holds her, and that it is paid by two houses at once.

# Of the stone and the first warmth

1. “Warm the stone on your own wrist before you put it to the child.”

*Gloss — The first thing taught and the last thing checked. A stone taken from the warm place behind the hearth and laid straight on a newborn takes more than it gives, and a child has little enough to be taken from. Hold it inside the cuff against the inside of the wrist until your own flesh says it is ready. This is also said of a cradle, a bed, and a word you mean to say to a woman who has just lost one.*

2. “A child comes cold and wanting. That is the whole of its case.”

*Gloss — Not a prayer and not a complaint. It is the account a midwife can make without a slate. They come with a mouth and no way to keep a breath warm, and the whole trade of the house is to answer those two facts until the child can answer them itself. I have said it to mothers who wanted a reason and fathers who wanted a price, and neither has ever thanked me for it.*

3. “The third day is the one to sit by.”

*Gloss — A child that has been rekindled grows worse on the third day before it grows better. This is the second chill, and it is ordinary, and the physicians have a longer name for it which they use when no hearth-maid is in the room. We keep the stone to the child. We keep it anyway. A woman who has had four will sit up through the third night without being asked; a woman who has had one will ask whether it is always so, and will be told yes.*

4. “A stone kept warm is a stone paid for.”

*Gloss — Somebody sat near a fire for it. Ask who, and you will be told to ask the fire, which is an answer given by people who asked the same thing at their own first birth. I have never known a household to enter the stone on a slate. That is the one thing about warmth this town has never thought to charge for, which is why I have put the saying first.*

# Of the name and the slate

5. “Write within three days or the Court writes for you.”

*Gloss — Ever since the order of seven hundred eighty-two. If the hearth-maid reaches the bench first the child has the name the house wants; if the collector reaches it first the child has the name the slate can hold, and the house finds out at the next market. We are paid to arrive first, a strange arrangement.*

6. “Give the child a name you can say in the dark.”

*Gloss — Grathic names are tools and materials and small domestic things, not poems. Clay, stone, reed, a hand, a bird. A name you need a slate to remember is a Court name, and a Court name goes wherever the slate goes, which is sometimes up the Clay Road and not back. I have written this because a girl asked me what to call a child born in the dark before the lamp was found, and I could not give her a better answer.*

7. “No house-name, no hearth. No hearth, no name that lasts.”

*Gloss — A foundling is given a name by whoever writes first and has no house-name until a hearth claims it. That is how a person is kept from inheriting a debt he did not make, which is a kindness done with a pen. We say this to the door after the mother has stopped asking, never to the child. The child will find out soon enough which column it is written in.*

8. “Break a birth-slate and you have bought a year in the yard.”

*Gloss — The penalty. Some break them anyway, usually in a kiln, where a slate takes four minutes and leaves nothing to enter. The household's name then goes into a second column which the hearth-maids do not read aloud and the Court does not print. I have kept the saying because I was asked once whether a slate could be unmade, and the true answer cost a woman a year of her life to find out.*

# Of the collector

9. “The tube goes to the arm, not to the face. Do not watch the face.”

*Gloss — The draw is small. There is iron, a small hiss, and grey at the collector's fingertips afterwards, and the mother watches the arm because the face has stopped being a place where answers live. I hold the child and I watch the mother, which is the whole of my office at that hour. This is not a saying for a father to hear. He will remember the face anyway.*

10. “What is drawn at the birth is not kept at the birth.”

*Gloss — On the Hearth Drawing a collector may take from the dying and the newly born on the same day. We say this to the slate, which has no answer in it. A woman once asked where her child's first warmth went, and I told her the figure was booked and the account stood, because those were the words I was paid to use, and then put a stone in her hand so that she would have something to do with it.*

11. “The maid holds the child. The slate holds the figure. Neither looks at the other.”

*Gloss — An arrangement, not a bargain, and no better than one. The house trusts a hearth-maid to come into a room where it will not let an officer come, and pays the officer to write what the maid has seen. I have kept both hands in this sentence because I have had to use both.*

# Of the fire and the bread

12. “Every fire in the valley is lit from one fire nobody lit.”

*Gloss — At Kindling each hearth touches its flame from the carried coal, and nobody says where the first coal was kept through the Five or who sat up with it. Ask the fire. A fire tells nothing, which is why the answer has lasted so long.*

13. “Bake on the Last Day. Do not sweep.”

*Gloss — Bread is baked and the grey in the corners is left where it lies and burnt the next morning. The floor shows where it stood by daylight after, which is the only honesty the floor has. In the Fenmarch they bottle the grey and sell it, and in the Hearthseat they bottle it for the rich, and here we burn it and say it is dealt with. I have written this at the end of the section because it is the one saying in this leaf I would rather not have had cause to know.*

14. “Give the child the browned crust. The burnt outside is the part that keeps.”

*Gloss — What the kitchens of Low Kiln give to children, and considered kindness. The soft inside is for the working hands. A girl from the clay yard told me this at her own first firing, and I have set it down in her words because mine would have made it a lesson.*

# What the women are for

15. “We come before the officer and after the priest.”

*Gloss — The midwife at the birth, the keeper of the book at the bench, the woman with the stone on the third night. The collector comes after, and the priest at the end. We are paid by the house and by the Court, which is two payments and only one of them a wage. Put this at a bench and not at a table.*

16. “At the birth-flower a cross is made and nothing is said.”

*Gloss — Every maid I know does it. My mother did it before me, and I have never once said what I saw, and neither did she. A girl who wants to know what the cross is for should ask her own mother, if her mother is still living, which is the answer I was given.*

# The bench-song

*Said low, going from house to house at dusk with the book under the apron, not at the birth and not at the third night. The fifth line of the second part is held, and the space after it is the length of a held breath. Nobody fills it.*

> One for the wrist and one for the fold,
> one for the name that the father will hold;
> three days to write and a cross at the flower,
> and the stone goes cold in the cold hour.
>
> Warm it, warm it, the old ones say,
> the third day takes what the first gave away;
> one for the pot and one for the door,
>
> *
>
> The slate goes up and the child stays here,
> the figure goes up and the face stays here;
> hold the stone and hold your tongue,
> and the book comes home with the name in it.

*Gloss — The held line is not a fifth day and not a fifth name. Girls ask and are told to ask the stone, which is what their mothers were told. The clerk from Slate Row put a round nought in the space and wrote *not entered* beneath it. A nought is a figure. The space was a breath, which is a thing no office has ever counted.*

# What the clerk did

A collector's clerk borrowed the leaf at Slate Row on an Account-day, with ink settled in two fingers of a soft cuff. He writes narrow and upright and takes the doer out of every sentence, as though a thing with nobody in it were truer than a thing a woman had done with her hands.

- At *warm the stone on your own wrist*, he wrote *the vessel is warmed by an external source*, and underlined it. Mine stands beneath. His sentence is true in a room with no child in it.
- At *the third day is the one to sit by*, he wrote *the afterdrop is observed*. He has taken away the sitting, which is the part that costs somebody a night.
- At *we come before the officer and after the priest*, he wrote *attendance is recorded*, which is a thing I have never seen done for a woman holding a basin.
- At the held line he put the round nought. I have left it where he set it and put the breath back underneath.

I have put the sayings back under his hand. He takes the verbs out because a thing always true has no need of anybody doing it. I put them back because a girl at a first birth wants to know which hand to hold the stone with.

# In the margin, in an old woman's broad charcoal

> I have had six. The stone never warmed one that was cold from the beginning. It warms the hand that holds it, which is something, and I have held six hands.

I have not asked which six, and she has not said. There is room in the margin for what she did not write, and I have left the room.

# At the head and in the gutter

The charcoal at the head is mine; I held the slate down on the bench with the hand I write with. The tallow thumb is from the lamp set too near the book one winter when the wick would not trim. The barley-water ring went dark in the three days it took me to find the word for what a thin child's hand does at an empty cup; I have not washed it. The crust between the leaves was given by a woman of Low Kiln who would not take a mark for it, and the brown edge has gone into the grain. The hair is a newborn's, swept off the bench and shut in the gutter. The iron scratch came off a tube-case set down hard beside the book by a man who did not apologise. The grey along the outer fold came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying in a room where children are born. I have not swept it, and there is a Last Day for it, though it is not this one.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> We hold what we cannot keep.
> We write what we cannot say.
> The slate goes where the child does not.

I asked a woman who has delivered forty, a clerk come down from Slate Row, and a man who carries the tube, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written. The book goes back under the loose board beside the bench. Tomorrow there will be a birth below the sill, or there will not, and I shall warm a stone on my own wrist before I go, and write within three days whatever the house and the Court between them decide the child is to be called.
