I am a ridge-wife of Cold Acre and I have set the bones and caught the children of six houses for nine-and-twenty years. Nobody asked me for this book. A woman came up the ridge road in the lean week with a hand gone white from the well pail and asked what I did for the cold that stays, and I heard myself answer in my mother's short way of speaking, and did not know whether I believed it. A remedy you have to say in your mother's voice is a remedy nobody has looked at in a long while. I have looked at some of mine. The rest I have left standing, for a woman at three in the morning wants a hand more than a correction.

I write on the back of a barley tally the meal mill spoiled when a wet thumb went over the figures. The figures went into the meal and out of the house, and what is left is paper enough for a book nobody asked to see. The well-clay at the head comes up under the nails in this hamlet and no washing takes it out of the grain. The grey in the gutter came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying. There is a well here that has not frozen in any winter anyone can name, and nobody asks why; I have kept that beside the grey and made no lesson of it.

# The order of the sick-room

*Written small on the first leaf, with one word gone over in well-water.*

- Set the pail outside the door before you go to the child. A pail of well-water standing in a room takes the warm out of a side faster than the north wind, and the north wind has the decency to be cold all over.
- Turn the slate face down. The slate-woman up the ridge writes the names and I do not; a hand that writes before it looks has already chosen the child.
- Try the stone on your own wrist before it goes to a belly. It came off somebody's hearth and somebody in these six houses paid for the coals.
- Do not wash the red branch. Cross two fingers over it and say the word the mother gives you.
- When the second chill comes, count with her. Do not count for her.
- If a man comes up the ridge road with a cold tube and a slate, give him the mother and keep the child behind the pail. This is not refusal. It is the order of the room.

I asked the slate-woman what she saw under the two fingers and she crossed them again and gave me a date. A date does not spoil in the mouth. I have put the date in the margin and left the question standing where it was when I asked it.

# The remedies, as they are used

| Trouble | What is set to it | The manner of it | Who pays |
|---|---|---|---|
| The well-cold (a hand gone white at the pail) | A stone at the belly, and work | The well has not frozen in any winter anyone can name, and still it draws from a hand faster than the Ilm draws from a stone. We say it has somewhere to put what it takes. A physician come down from Greybank says water draws faster than air and the well is only water. I held one hand in the pail and one in the north wind for one count; the pail hand was colder. Both stand. | The well, which keeps no account. |
| The threshing cough | Steam of barley-water under a cloth; honey if the house has it | The dust of the meal mill gets into a man and he coughs up a colour he will not discuss. A physician says grain dust in the lung and no physic but departure. I sent two down to Sill and both came back worse, for the roads have dust of their own. | The mill, which cannot stop and does not ask. |
| A turnip-knife cut | Tallow, then a clean fold; never the grey | The women put grey from the corner in a cut to dry it, believing the grey is clean. I dressed three such and found grey under the skin in all three, and could not get it out. | The cut, in a white line it keeps. |
| The sweet line | Sour barley-water; wood-ash at the gum | A girl come down from the Glaze Room with the pale line at her gum says a sweet answers a sweet. A physician says lead. I have seen the line go off one who left that room and come back on one who stayed. I have not seen the lead. | The glaze room, which cannot stop and does not ask. |
| The second chill | Warmth, barley-water, a watch to the fourth morning; we hold them now | It comes after the warming, not before. A physician says a warmed body spends faster and holding still spends less than walking. I walked nineteen and held five; of the walked three went thin and one did not rise, of the held one went thin. I have kept the counts on birch bark and not added them, for a count added is a count a clerk can carry down the ridge. | The household, one night's sleep; sometimes the child. |

| The member gone dark | Wool, a stone at the middle, and work | The extremity goes first and the middle holds out, and there is an hour on the far side of the warming when a body spends what it was given faster than it takes in. I have wound the wool and set them to work, and seen a foot go dark through a lean week, and gone on winding. | The woman. This is the row where I have been least honest. |

Nothing in this book is free. It is carried from one ridge house to another and called a cure. That is the only sentence I will stand behind.

# The note under the knuckle

Tap a sound cup with the knuckle and it answers full. Tap a cracked one and it answers flat, a small dead sound that stops at the lip and will not go through. The women at the meal mill know it in the cups they drink from and I have borrowed it from them, as every trade borrows from the trade beside it.

I tapped a woman once, gone thin after a thin return, and the flat I thought I heard was not in her. A body is not a cup, though we set cups on the bench in the shape of bellies. But a house is: I can tell by the note in the pot whether the room held its warm through the night, and set the stone by that, for there is no clock in Cold Acre and no Court figure that will tell you what a pot knows.

The well gives back a low note when the bucket goes down. The slate-woman says it is the river running under the ridge; Gedde who keeps the curb says a well that gives a note will not freeze. I have left both sayings where I heard them.

# The well that does not freeze

The water comes up in Stone as in Ilmen, and a hand goes in either month and comes out the same colour and the same cold. A well that will not freeze is a kindness or a theft, and I have never decided which. We say it has somewhere to put what it takes, which is what the collectors say in a better room with cleaner cuffs, only they write it down. A physician says there is no mystery in it, only water, and is right about the water; he has never stood at this curb in Stone with the wind off the north gate and one hand in the pail. I have no remedy for the well-cold but a stone and a hand to hold, and have left the place where a better one would stand the length a remedy of that kind wants.

# The song at the well

*Sung low at the curb while the pail goes down, to the water and not to the person. The twelfth is held in the singing, the length of a hand going into the pail and coming out empty. Nobody fills it.*

> Four for the houses and one for the well,
> one for the pail that will not freeze;
> a hand goes in and a hand comes out
> and the cold keeps what the water leaves.
>
> Two for the mill and two for the knife,
> two for the girl with the sweet at her mouth;
> the line comes west and the line goes east
> and the glaze room never asks what it cost.
>
> Warm the middle and wrap the end,
> count with her, do not count for her;
> the name goes down and the face comes up
> and the stone keeps warm in the old tin's cup.
>
> Eleven I counted and one held out,
> and the twelfth goes down where the well keeps.

I changed one line in nine-and-twenty years: *what the water takes* is now *what the water leaves*, because a woman at this curb wants to know what is left in the pail when the hand comes out. The women singing with me have not noticed, which is how I know the line wanted changing. A factor's clerk put a round nought in the held place and wrote *vacancy* beneath it; a nought can be carried away in a cuff. The twelfth is a place and stays.

# What the physician pressed in the margin

He came down from Greybank with a book that had the word *lead* in it and a hand that wrote without asking. Against the sweet line he set *lead, as in the south*; against the well-cold, *water conducts; no remedy*; against the birth-flower, *a mark in the blood*. He was right in all three and had nothing a ridge house could pay for. Where I wrote *we push the warmth back the way it came*, he set *no such transmission*. I have put the warmth underneath.

# What the factor's clerk did

A clerk of the factor borrowed the leaves when the winter barley was counted at Greybank; the ink sits in two fingers of a soft cuff. He struck out my column headed *Who pays* and wrote *No cost entered*, a true thing in a dishonest place. Where I wrote that the well keeps no account, he wrote *the measure is retained in the water*, taking the hands out as though a pail could draw itself. I have put my verbs back underneath his. A clerk can write without verbs because he is never at a curb in Stone with a woman's hand gone white in the pail.

# In the margin, close to the third-night row

> It was the second day, not the third, in my house also. Warm the middle and wrap the ends; do not tell the father what you call it. The tin under the bench is not a count. --- W.

She wrote it while I was at the well and set the leaf back with the fold changed. A woman who has kept the slates of this ridge for thirty-one years is allowed one sentence in a book of physic.

# At the head and in the gutter

The well-clay at the head came off my thumb in a wind off the north gate and has worked into the grain, which is why this leaf smells of the curb in any room. The frost-ring took two words clean off the second leaf when I left it outside the door; I have not written them back, because a remedy with two words gone is a remedy said slowly, and everything here is said slowly. The barley awn in the fold came off a sack by the mill door and has gone stiff as a splinter. The nick at the fore-edge is a turnip knife's, from a woman who paid me with the knife still in her hand. The pale tideline is well-water off a pail set down while I was telling a girl to drink and she would not. The tallow is off the lamp I held low so nobody at the table would know I was writing. The green bead in the lower corner was pressed in by a girl come down from High Kiln with the sweetness at one mouth-corner; she touched the leaf, went back up the terrace and said nothing, and I did not ask whose glaze it was. The grey along the outer fold came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying; I have not swept it. There is a Last Day in Sill and the corners are swept and burnt there in the morning and called dealt with. There is no Last Day on this ridge.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The well keeps what it takes and has no slate.
> The slate keeps what it cannot carry and calls it kept.
> The child between the pail and the door is warm or is not, and neither column asks.

I asked a physician, a factor's clerk, and the woman who keeps the slates up the ridge, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The book goes back into the sacking in my apron. Tomorrow a woman will come down the ridge road with a hand gone white from the pail or a child that will not breathe, and I shall turn the slate face down, try the stone on my own wrist, and leave the twelfth where it is, because a child is not an account and a woman at three in the morning wants a hand more than a remedy.
