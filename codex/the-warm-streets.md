I cart salt. It is not a trade that gets a man asked into rooms, which is why I have set this down on the backs of dockets and not on a slate, and why the figures here are the ones I was charged rather than the ones a clerk would like me to have paid. I went up the Hearth Road in Grain of the Thin Year with a load of Sallow salt out of the Court granaries at Greybank, and came down again in Ash with the same team, three silver marks the poorer, a copy of the notice they print beside the Hall, and a green bead of cliff-glass in my pocket that a girl in the lower court pressed on me for a bit and a promise. A third journey is being spoken of in the yard as I write. I have not said yes.

# The road as a cart knows it

The Hearth Road runs a hundred and forty leagues from Greybank to the Court city. The first thing a cartman learns on it is that the ground changes under the wheels before the air changes over them. You leave the clay behind at Greybank, and by the third day the ruts have a warmth in them at morning that the ruts at home never have. Put a hand down on the road in Bone and it gives back a little of the warm, the way a stone does that has lain all night near a fire and has not been near one. The drivers say the whole Keel is a pot kept on a low heat. I have no learning about pots beyond what I carry in them, but I know a warm road when I feel one through a worn sole.

They say a man should not travel that road without a warm-stone, which is Keldish talk and the sort of advice a man gives when he sells warm-stone. I took one anyway, a fist of it off a Keldish teamster at Greybank for eight bits and a promise I have not kept. It lay under the straw in the foot-well and gave out its heat for nine days and then gave out no more, and on the tenth morning I kicked it because it had stopped being useful and I was cold. A Keldish man would have put it in a tin lined with the same stone and carried it home. A carter puts his foot on it.

The smoke of the city is seen before the city. It lies along the Keel like a low brown roof, and a man coming up the last rise can tell how far off he is by the taste of it, which is lead and coal and the sweetness of a glaze room, all three together. In Grath the kilns smell of woodsmoke and wet wool. Here the smoke has a clean edge to it, almost a ministering smell, and the horses do not pull toward it the way they pull toward a stable. They know the difference between a fire that is working and a fire that is being kept.

There is no gate with a name on it. There is a yard where the carts stand and a man with a slate who asks what you have brought before he asks who you are, which is the proper order and the one I was raised to expect. He wrote *salt* against a figure and did not look at the team. A woman beside him was entering something into a second slate with a hand that went to the slate before it went to the person waiting. I have since learned that this is the order in which the city does everything.

# What a man pays in the city

I have set the figures as they were given to me. A clerk of the factor's house at Greybank borrowed the leaf on a market morning and wrote above the last two rows. Both readings stand.

| Thing | What was asked | What I paid |
|---|---|---|
| Bed in the carters' yard, a night | 3 bits | 3 bits |
| Pot of pottage, with bread | 2 bits | 2 bits |
| Quart of small ale | 1 bit | 1 bit |
| A warm-stone, fist-sized | 1 mark | 8 bits and a promise |
| Green bead of cliff-glass, in the lower court | 4 bits | 4 bits and the promise |
| Phial of quiet ash, behind Slate Row | 60 marks | not bought |
| A whole return, booked at the bank | 1,200 marks and forty unspent measures | not bought |
| One ember, as the Court books it | 5 marks | no man changes one |

> Against the last row, in the narrow upright hand: *Booking rate. Not a market rate.*
>
> Against the two returns, in the same hand: *Figures of account, not of price.*

A booking rate is a figure a man writes when he does not mean to pay it. I have carried salt eighty leagues and been paid in marks that a man can bite, and I have never yet held one of these embers that the city keeps its accounts in. A clerk will tell you an ember is worth five marks in the same voice he tells you the notice says one in twelve, and both statements are true in the way a slate is true, which is to say they stand where they are written and do not come down into the yard.

# The Hall seen from the yard

I did not go into the Weighing Hall. A cartman has no business in it and the man with the slate made that plain without raising his voice, which is a gift that city has more of than any place I have carted to. I stood in the yard on the morning of the reading and heard the Voice through an open door, and I have set down what I heard and not what I was told to say I heard.

The voice is a clean one. It takes the figures up and lets them come down without any flavour on them, the way water goes through a iron tube and comes out the same water. It read one in twelve twice, once for the people in the Hall and once, I suppose, for those who had come up the road too late to get into it, of whom I was one. Nobody in the yard crossed himself. Nobody in the yard was asked to.

Beside the door is the notice, printed, with the figure on it. I copied it with my nail-point into the docket because I wanted to be able to say, when I got home, that I had seen the figure myself and not had it told me. One in twelve, as the notice says. There is a kind of comfort in a printed notice, because a man can stand before it and know that whatever is taken will be taken according to the same words for everybody. What a poor district pays is a thing I have never been shown a notice for, and I did not ask. A cartman who asks after figures that are not printed is a cartman who finds his wagon unloaded in the morning.

A teamster who had been up the road before me told me there are nine bars across the fire and eight on the far side, and that a man who counts from the wrong end comes away with eight and thinks he has lost one. He has not. Begin at the hinge. I have put that down because it is the only thing about the inside of the Hall that a man in a yard is likely to carry away, and I would rather carry a true thing about a grate than a false one about a fire.

# The rooms beside the Hall

There is a passage that goes warm under the feet. A coal-woman with smut on two fingers held a door for me there while she took a bucket in, and she held it a moment longer than she needed, which was kindness or carelessness and I have not decided which. I looked in. There was a bench, and a cup of water standing on it with nobody drinking, and a woman sitting facing the wall with her hands in her lap. She did not turn her head.

I was told afterwards that there are fewer than twenty such rooms and that the people in them hold three hundred measures apiece. They are spoken of with pity in that city. It is the word they use, and it is the only word I heard used more than once in the Hall passage. I carried salt through a frost that took the feeling out of two of my toes for a tend, and I got it back in the thaw with a pain that made me sit down on a curb in the middle of Greybank. I would not call it a mercy to be past feeling. But I am a carter, and the Court does not ask carters what they think is merciful.

The coal-woman said one thing to me as she shut the door, and I have written it as she said it and not made anything of it. *She looks at the cup to learn that it has gone cold.* I asked the man with the slate what that meant and he said the cup was there for the room and not for the woman, and that I had spent my question.

# The wells

They lie within twenty leagues of the city, in the Keel, and a cart can be left at a stand there while a man walks in. There are nine. Four are sealed and the keys are kept in the Hearthseat, and a keeper was hanged in Y.H. 618 for drawing from one without a key, which a man in the yard told me while we were waiting for the other team and gave me the year and not the name. The unsealed ones steam in cold weather. The steam goes up off them like breath off a pot in a room with the window shut, and it smells of iron and warm stone and of nothing I could put a name to.

A woman was bathing her husband's hands in one of the open springs. She had his fingers in the water and was rubbing them with her thumb while he sat and looked at the ground. I asked her what the water was for and she said it was for what the warm had left, and then said her husband had been a collector and had better leave his own answer to it. I have not put the two sentences together. A traveller who joins two sentences that a native has kept apart has made a story, and stories are what the people of that country live on.

# The road-song

*Taken down in the carters' yard at Greybank from a man who had gone up with tallow and come back with nothing, while the teams were being watered. He would not sing it with a clerk in the yard, so we went and stood by the trough.*

> The Clay Road ends where the granaries stand,
> and the Hearth Road climbs where the ground is warm;
> I carried the salt of the Sallow in a sack
> and the Court booked it before the dawn.
>
> A hundred and forty leagues and a team gone thin,
> the smoke of the Keel in the collar of the coat;
> a man pays in marks and is paid in a look,
> and the notice reads one in twelve by the door.
>
> Nine wells in the stone and four under key,
> and the steam goes up where the cat would sit;
> the city keeps warm what it takes from the road,
> and the road keeps the ruts and the sweat and the bit.

He said there was a fourth verse and that it was not a song verse but a saying, and he would not give it. The file at the head of the leaf is where I scraped the place for it with my nail and then put nothing in.

# What the clerks made of it

Two hands have been at the dockets besides mine. The first is the narrow upright hand of the College, borrowed at Greybank, which took the verbs out of what I had written about the rooms and left the room standing with nobody in it.

- At *a woman sitting facing the wall with her hands in her lap* it wrote *one vessel, seated, orientation to wall*.
- At *she looks at the cup to learn that it has gone cold* it wrote *observation of temperature, no affect recorded*.
- At *gentlemen* I had written *the people in them*, and it set *the party is retained* above. I had not written *gentlemen*. I have struck my own word out and left his above it, because he has put a figure where I put a person and a man ought to see both.

The second hand belongs to the factor's clerk, who does not write in charcoal and cannot abide it. He has put *road figures, informal* against my table and drawn a line under the whole of the wells, which is his way of saying that a man may copy a song but may not copy a place. He has not struck a word. I would rather a man struck a word and wrote his own above it than draw a line under mine and go away.

# At the head and in the gutter

The white in the gutter is salt off the dockets and came honestly by its place. The tallow at the head is off the lamp in the yard at Greybank, where I wrote the song after the teams were watered and the clerk had gone in to his supper. The grey along the outer fold came off a surveyor's cuff and is not mine; he would not sit down and stood leaning over my table with the south-road dirt still on his boots, and I have not swept it. The filings in the second crease are off a hinge they were mending in the yard and got into the fold when I shut the leaf on them. The ring by the song is small ale, left from the first line to the last and not drunk until the singing was done. The green bead is pressed into the lower corner because a girl put it there and I had no pocket that would hold it safely. The square of bare paper is where I scraped out the fourth verse and put nothing in its place, which is the only square in this account that I made myself.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

> The road keeps the ruts and the tax keeps the figure;
> the Hall keeps the fire and gives no man a light.
> A man pays what is asked and is told what was paid.
> Ask the cup how it learned that the water was cold.

I asked the man with the slate, the coal-woman and the teamster who told me about the bars, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written.

The dockets go back into the nail-box under the factor's counter. Next tend the road opens and there is tallow going up and iron coming down, and a team that is willing, and a man who has not said yes yet but has a wife who counts marks the way the Court counts measures. That is how a third journey is decided in a house like mine. Not by a figure on a notice, but by a woman at a table with a loaf going short.
