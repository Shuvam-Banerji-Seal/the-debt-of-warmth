kind: A well-shrine sermon of the common Grathic telling preached at the niche beside the well that the road-book calls the well that was never sweetened, where the Ferr comes down to the Ilm at Stonemouth; the sayings in the flat washed speech of the washing-bench set down in Grathic, the drawing-song with the sixth line left as it is not sung, the shelf in a tally, one correction in the narrow upright Ledgerhand of a factor's clerk come up from Greybank to weigh the winter lead, and one line at the foot in a hand nobody will own

I take the quarrymen's names at the weigh-clerk's bench and give them back when the factor's man comes up, which is a way of keeping names without owning them. Nobody asked me for this. I write because the woman who keeps the dish-shelf spoke to a well and the well gave back the same water it has always given. She spoke first to the lip, then to the cup in the niche, then to the dish, and last of all to us, which is the order a person uses when somebody may be listening who wants a figure. Where she left a verb out I have put it back underneath, because a saying said over a well is a thing a woman has to use before the water is cold.

The lime at the head is off my cuff and no wet cloth takes it out of the grain. The blue in the gutter is the Ferr; it has taken two words off the second leaf and I have not written them in again. The ring on the first leaf is barley-water, set down while she spoke of the twenty-one. The grey came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying on a counted stone; I have not swept it, and there is no Last Day at this well.

# The manner of the drawing, as the well actually has it

*Written small, from watching, not from any order the Court has printed.*

- Come before the sun is on the lip. A man who comes at noon sees his own shadow in the water and calls it the well's answer.
- Do not drink. A man who drinks the Ferr and calls it thin has taken a giving for a taking, and the habit is hard to unlearn in a household that has no other water.
- Set a dish of barley-water on the shelf with a stone in it to keep the wind from dragging it off. The stone is not a payment and has never been entered.
- Tap the cup in the niche with the knuckle. It answers flat. Do not tap the well; a well answers with its surface and tells you nothing you can carry.
- If you come up from the glaze room, leave the green bead on the shelf and say whose it was to the dish and not to a person. A bead said to a person becomes a question about the colour, and the colour has a price in High Kiln.
- Do not say the count of the twenty-one aloud at this well. A number said here goes up the road in a clean hand and comes back as a figure with the drinkers taken out of it.
- When a man in a clean coat comes up from Greybank to weigh the winter lead, stop. Begin again when he has gone back down it. We have kept this habit thirty years and never once said why, and he has never once asked, which is how I know he is the right man for the road.

# The sermon, as she said it

## Of the well

She put her palm on the stone lip and left it there while the Ferr went by under the bank, carrying the blue of the west with it.

“The well gives as it gave,” she said. “It has never given anything else and it has never stopped. What it takes it does not say, and a well that will not say what it takes is called a mercy in a room three leagues east where nobody has ever drawn from it.

“There is a clause on a slate at Sill,” she said, “and the factor pasted a strip of salt-paper over it, and the strip has lifted at one corner so that six words show and the rest do not. We have leaned over it and put a thumb on it, and the six words are *the wells of the Ferr are to be sweetened at the charge of the workings*. The clause is on a slate. The water is in this hole. They have never met, and the well has kept the larger part.”

> *Margin, in my hand:* She said *never sweetened* twice, once to the well and once to a woman come up from the washing for a cup of barley-water. I have left both sayings where they stood. A date would have been safer, but there is no date on this niche.

## Of the cup

She took the cup from the niche and tapped it with her knuckle. It gave the small flat sound a pot gives when the crack has gone all the way through, and stopped at the skin of it.

“A pot tells the truth for nothing,” she said. “This one came down from High Kiln in the year of the water and would not hold a pouring. The physician wrote *a reduction in the measure was recorded* above it and the road-book wrote *a visitation of the water*, and the cup has answered flat every morning since without once asking who would write it down. A physician's sentence is true in a language nobody at this bench speaks. The cup has not learned that language and is the better for it.”

A girl asked where the pale line was, that the well-song speaks of. She turned the cup so the light went along the empty rim.

“That is on a pot still at High Kiln, not here,” she said. “I have pressed a flake of the crust off this lip into the corner of the leaf instead, because a woman can carry that and cannot carry a pot, and the crust is sweet in the same way. It is not a flower and it is not a visitation. It is the colour of the water on a lip that has tasted it through a season. The women taste the glaze to get the colour and the colour comes back and stays, and there is no clause about the rim on any slate.”

## Of the dish

She set a dish of barley-water on the shelf and put the stone in it, the way a household sets one at Sill, but with this difference: at Sill they set it for them whose measure has been banked.

“We set a dish for the twenty-one who went downriver and were not entered again,” she said. “In the morning the water is gone and the stone is where it was. Nobody says who drank, because a household here can carry a name that a slate has no room for. In Sill they call the water a keeping. Here we call it gone. It is the same dish and it is not the same saying, and I will not have one put over the other.”

She put the dish where it had stood and did not look into it. I have written the order of the placing and not what it means.

## Of the figure

A man at the bench asked what the shrine kept, since the factor's man would want a figure for it.

“Three hundred,” she said. “A round sum. Ninety-six and a hundred and twelve and seventy-one. The seventy-one are us, by this well. The twenty-one went downriver and the round sum has no room for them, so a small hard hand put them in the gutter where a clerk carrying a total will not think to look. I have kept the gutter.”

“Three hundred is a visitation,” she said. “Seventy-one is a row of houses. Twenty-one is a thing that happened to people we can still name. A round sum is a kindness to the man who has to copy it and a cruelty to the woman who has to remember which house it was. The road-book balances, and I have never once seen a house balance.”

## Of them that went downriver

She turned from the shelf to the company then, which she had not done until that hour, and spoke in the short words of the washing.

“Do not say they were returned,” she said. “They went downriver. There is a difference and it is the whole of what this shrine knows. A thin return is a thing done in a room with a slate and a tube and an infant at the end of it. These were not returned. They walked or were carried downriver and were not entered again. If the Keeping wants them, let the Keeping say where they went. It has not said.”

A man asked whether that was a mercy or a refusal. There is no Grathic word that holds the two together and she would not choose one.

“It is what we say,” she said. “Mercy is a word with a giver in it and I have never seen one at this well. I have seen a man carry a brother up the bank and put him in a cart, which is a different thing and can be said without either word.”

## Of what is not sung

There is a well-song taken down at the washing from a woman who learned it from women who had, and anybody here can sing it you. It is a true song and it is not this one. This one has a sixth line that is not sung.

“The sixth belongs to the twenty-one who went downriver,” she said. “A line sung for a person who is not in the room becomes a keeping, and we have had enough of keepings. Let the dish stand for them and let the line go unsung the length they have been unentered.”

# The drawing-song

*Sung low, standing, with the dish on the shelf and the palm on the lip. The women say the fourth line with her. The sixth is not sung and I have left the space its length.*

> The Ferr came down with a blue on its skin,
> and the cup at the lip would not hold;
> we drank all winter and called it thin,
> and the slate wrote a figure and told.
>
> Seventy-one by the well that we know,
> and twenty-one where the gutter runs;
> the clause was pasted and six words show
> and the sweetening never came.
>
> The lime keeps a print and the well keeps a name,
> the bead goes green where the lip has been;
>
> *Here the sixth line is not sung. No sound is made for the twenty-one.*
>
> A round sum balances; a row does not.
> The water goes down and the name stays here.

A girl asked what the space was for. Nobody answered her. The clerk put a round nought against it later, which I have left where he set it.

# The shelf, as the shrine keeps it

*Things set in the niche or on the shelf. What was left and what was entered. I have set them where they stood.*

| Thing in the niche | Who set it | What it was for | What was entered |
|---|---|---|---|
| The cup that would not hold a pouring | a woman of High Kiln | to hear the flat from | nothing |
| The green bead | a girl come up from the glaze room | left because her mother drank | nothing |
| The dish of barley-water | the keeper of the shelf | for the twenty-one downriver | nothing |
| A string with twenty-one knots | the keeper of the shelf | the odd ones the round sum missed | nil, written above |
| A flake of the sweet crust | nobody will say | from the lip in a dry winter | nothing |
| The strip of salt-paper | the factor at Greybank | pasted over the sixth clause | obligation outstanding |
| The grey on the lip | a cuff nobody owns | left where it fell | nothing |

There were beads and dishes before these, and the keeper can set a season to most of them, and I have not written the seasons, because a row of years is a thing a clerk can carry away and a cup is not. The string has never agreed with the road-book and has never been asked to.

# What the clerk did

He came up from Greybank on the tenth with a book that had the word *lead* in it and a hand that writes without asking, borrowed the leaf while I was at the bench, and gave it back with the verbs taken out.

- At *the well gives as it gave* he set *the source continues in yield* above and underlined it twice.
- At *the well was never sweetened* he wrote *the obligation remains outstanding*, which is the same line he set in the stone-dresser's yard-book last year and has now copied here, as though a sentence written twice were twice true.
- At *the twenty-one went downriver* he wrote *third parties not resident; no account arises*.
- At *the cup answered flat* he wrote *a vessel of defective retention*, which leaves the cup standing in a room where nobody has to hear it.
- At the gap in the song a round nought and *vacancy*, as though a line unsung were a column waiting to be filled. When I asked what a space was worth that held twenty-one people, he said nothing a clerk could enter, which is the first true thing he has said at this bench.

I have put the verbs back underneath and left his hand standing above, as the well keeps the dust under the water and the water under the lip. He takes them out because a sentence with nobody in it can be copied from one book into another and never be wrong. I put them back because a woman with her palm on the well wants to know who is doing the taking, and whether the taking was hers.

# At the head and in the gutter

The lime is mine and will not wash out of the fold; I wipe the bench with my cuff and the cuff goes into the grain. The blue is the Ferr, carried up on a wet sleeve and set down on the leaf while a man was telling me something about the upstream water; the tell dried and the blue stayed. The two words it ate I have left out, for a leaf that has lost two words to the water must be read slowly, which is no great loss at a shrine where nothing is said quickly. The ring is barley-water from the dish she set for the twenty-one. The grey came off a cuff and I have not swept it. The bead in the fold came off a girl with the sweetness still at her mouth who leaned over to see what I was writing and went back to the glaze room without asking. The flake of crust is from the lip and is sweet when the weather has been dry. The pale line in the corner came off the cup's rim when she set it down to tap it. The salt-paper has lifted at one corner, so a correction shows through that was meant to be covered, which is the only honest thing that paper has done.

# At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

Struck through once and written again underneath, very small:

> The well was sweetened on a slate and not in the water.
>
> A round sum can be carried. Seventy-one cannot.
>
> The twenty-one went downriver and the dish was set for them.
>
> Ask the cup at the lip what it held, and why it will not hold now.

I asked the keeper of the shelf, the woman at the washing, the weigh-clerk and the clerk from Greybank, and had four answerings and no hand. I have left the line struck through and standing, the only honest way to keep a sentence you cannot sign.

The leaf goes back into the niche behind the dish-shelf with the bead in its fold. Next Ash the women will set a dish for the ones that went and the clerk will come up from Greybank with his clean hand and take the verbs out, and I shall put them back and leave the nought where he set it and the sixth line unsung the length it is not sung. The Ferr will come down with the blue on it and the cup will answer flat and the well will give as it gives, which is the whole of what this shrine has kept and the reason I have written it down.
