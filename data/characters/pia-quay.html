Appearance

Fourteen when the Salt Road first brings her to Sill and later a woman of strong, compact build with salt-bleached hair and the sibilant Vhaleni way of holding a sentence: she lets the ends of words fall away, so that a listener in Grath hears half a thought and supplies the rest, which she has learned to use. Her hands are roughened by salt-paper and rope, and she has a small white scar across one knuckle from a fish-hook that went in at an angle, which she says was a gull and was not. She dresses in Vhaleni blue when the weather allows and in whatever is warm when it does not, and unlike the Grathic girls she does not cover her hair in a mill, which makes people at the Cross look twice and then look away. She smells of salt, wet rope, tar and the cold off the Sallow, which in Sill is either exotic or suspicious depending on who is doing the smelling. The thing people notice first is that she never takes off her shoes indoors, which in Vhal is ordinary and in Grath is an insult; the thing they notice last is that she counts coins by touch without looking and is almost always right, which is the same trick the eel women use with knots and which nobody in Sill has ever seen a visitor do.

Voice & Manner

Sibilant, practical, with tide-words where a Grathic person would use weather or God. She says the ebb, the draw, the turn, and has no word for please because Vhaleni has no word for a guest and therefore no word for asking. She makes contracts of everything, including kindness: a meal is entered, a night’s shelter is entered, and if she ever does something for nothing she says so at once so that the other person will know it was not a favour, because a favour in Vhalen is a debt with no date on it. She never explains a joke and never repeats herself. When she wants to say something tender she names a tide that has not happened yet, which is the closest Vhaleni gets to a promise.

  • “Salt. You pay in salt or you pay in marks. Which is it? Don’t say kindly. Kindly is the coin we don’t take.”
  • “The ebb’s at the fourth hour. Anything said before the ebb can be said again after it. I’ll let you.”
  • “A contract that rots in the rain is no contract. That’s the virtue of it. A thing that lasts too long stops earning.”
  • “You’re not a guest. I don’t have a word for guest. You’re a man whose account is current. That’s all I’ve got, and it’s better than it sounds.”
  • “I’ll name you a tide I haven’t seen. That’s what we do when we mean something we can’t write down.”

History

She was born in Vhal to a mother who held a boat-share and a father who was come aboard, which means his opinion on the boat counted at half and he knew it. She learned the salt pans at twelve and the counting house at fifteen, where she discovered that a contract written on salt-paper in wet weather is no contract at all and that half the port’s fortunes have turned on exactly that. She has the tide-name the dippers gave her at her Salt Walk, which she never uses in Grath because it makes the Grathic people uneasy in a way she enjoys. She came up the Salt Road at fourteen with a consignment of salt and glass for the Greybank factor and stayed three tendays at the Cross because a wet document had turned a debt into nothing and the factor wanted someone to blame who was not himself.

In Sill she met a foundling boy from Low Kiln who could hear a cracked cup through a wall and who had never in his life been offered a contract. She sold him a salt herring for two bits and then, because he had no more coin, sold him the same herring again for a promise to tell her what a cup sounds like when it holds. He told her. She has come back up the Salt Road every year since, sometimes with glass, sometimes with nothing, always with a question about the note, and has never once charged him for the answer. She would say the account is current, which is the only way she has of saying she has not forgotten him.

When they are both grown she will be the person who can take him out of Grath by boat and who will not do it for nothing, because a man who is given a passage in Vhalen is a man who owes a passage, and she has spent her life knowing exactly what that debt is worth. She will also be the one to tell him, in a light Vhaleni voice, that the Sallow has no Hearth of its own and that the dippers have measured the draw and told the Council only what was profitable, which is the first honest answer he will get about a foreign god.

Wounds & Fears

Her wound is the contract. She has grown up in a country with no word for friend, and she has built her whole life on accounts that are current, which means she cannot admit that she wants to see a man without putting a price on it. She fears being in a room where someone says kindly and means it, because she has no way to enter it and therefore no way to refuse it. She fears the salt-thing, which follows boats at night and taps the hull where the Warm is stored, and she fears more than that a hard winter that closes the coast pass and leaves her in a country where ash is swept up rather than sold. Her contradiction: the woman who turns damp paper into a fortune has kept one document dry for ten years — a scrap of Grathic slate-rubbing with a cup’s note on it — and calls it nothing, just a thing I carried, which is a lie in the precise Vhaleni grammar she uses for the truth.

Desires

She wants a boat-share of her own, which in Vhalen means a share held through her mother and not given by a husband, which is the nearest thing to freedom her country allows. She thinks she wants the boy from the clay country to come to Vhal and hear the Sallow draw, which she has described to him as a sound although it is not one, and which he has never stopped wanting to hear. What she needs is to understand that a person can be loved without being entered, which in Vhalen is a thought so foreign that she will have to learn a Grathic word for it before she can say it, and that the debt between them cannot be settled by a passage on a boat because it is not a debt at all. Their romance can only begin in adulthood, when she holds the share and he is no longer a boy with no coin for a second herring.

Bonds

Corin — the man whose account is current. She has sold him the same herring twice, charged him nothing for ten years, and kept a slate-rubbing of a cup’s note in a dry place, which is the most Vhaleni declaration of love ever made. The tension is that he cannot offer her a contract she can accept, because he has no house and no coin, and she cannot offer him a gift she can admit to, because she has no word for one.

Veno Brine — the Greybank factor for whom she first carried glass, a man who can turn a damp document into a fortune and has done so at her expense once, which she has never forgiven and has never refused work from. He has offered to buy her mother’s boat-share twice, which is the offer that will eventually send her back up the Salt Road for good.

Neri Sail, chief dipper — who named her tide and has never once told her what the Sallow draw really is, which she has measured for herself and entered in a private tally that the Council never sees.

Mira Silt — her supplier of eels and the only Grathic person who understands that a contract can be a kindness if both parties know it. They haggle ferociously and have never once short-changed the other, which in two different countries is the same as friendship.

Avis Wake — a Council member who votes against extending the Salt Road’s tax to the glass boats and whom Pia has begun to suspect is voting for her own share rather than anyone’s, which is Vhaleni politics in its purest form.

Solene Ember — the thrice-returned woman she met once in a counting house in Greybank, who remembered three deaths and said them in a warm Sere voice, and whom Pia has never forgotten, because a person who remembers being returned is a person who understands what a contract is worth.
