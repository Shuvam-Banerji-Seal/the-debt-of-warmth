Appearance

Fifty-one and dry as a filed quill, with the narrow, bloodless hands of a man who has written more lives than he has ever held. He wears the College’s black wool with a strip of white at the cuff to show that he teaches rather than practises, and he keeps his nails pared to the quick because ink under a nail is a thing that can be read. His slate is always ruled into columns and his chalk is always new; he has never been seen with a blunt instrument of any kind, which his students take as a warning about the kind of accuracy he expects. He has a habit of turning a slate face-down before he speaks to a person, so that the person understands they are not being entered, and another habit of turning it face-up again when he has finished, so that they understand they were. He smells of slate dust, dry paper and the cold stone of the College, which is the same smell as a room nobody has warmed in order to save money.

Voice & Manner

Ledgerhand as a teacher speaks it: the verbs removed, the person removed, the sentence left standing like a frame with the picture taken out. He never says please and never says sorry, and has taught that the two words are the same absence wearing different coats. He corrects students by their slate-names, which in the College means they do not have birth-names to be corrected by, and the correction lands somewhere colder than Ostrowski’s full name ever did. When he wants to say something kind he gives a figure, because a figure can be checked. When he wants to say something cruel he says the account stands and lets the student supply the person.

  • “Eleven words for a deduction. One of them is correct. The other ten are for people who want to feel something while they write.”
  • “You have written a sentence with a verb in it. Who is it that did this thing? In this college, nobody. Write it again.”
  • “Marchetti — I beg your pardon. Rennick. Your hand is a good hand. That is not praise. It is a measurement.”
  • “The printed figure is one in twelve. The true figure is taught in this room to people who will never print it. That is the whole of your education.”
  • “You want to know what a life was worth. That is a question with a verb in it. Sit down.”

History

He was entered on the rolls at nineteen and rose through the College by a route he describes as steady correction, which means he has never made a public mistake and has made a great many private ones. He has taught Ledgerhand for twenty-six years and has had four hundred students, of whom three hundred and ninety-one are officers of the Keeping and nine are names he will not say aloud, because they were removed from the rolls and their slates are kept in a second file that he maintains himself. He believes the College’s purpose is not to value lives but to decide who is allowed to value them, which he considers a distinction that protects the world from arithmetic done by people with a stake in the answer.

He knows the printed figure is false. He teaches the true method to his own students and prints the false one for everyone else, and has done this every year since Y.H. 790, when a Greybank slate with a line that did not balance was corrected in a second hand and filed without comment. He has never asked whose second hand it was, because an assessor who asks about a correction is an assessor who has begun to add, and adding is the first step toward a thing the College has no word for. In Y.H. 803 he was shown the slate again, with the name Marra in the first hand, and he entered nothing about it in any report, which he has entered in his private tally as the only honest thing he has ever written.

When a foundling from Low Kiln arrives in the Hearthseat with a clinical ear and an inability to read a ledger, Wessen will teach him to read it for a price: his own name on a slate, entered in Ledgerhand, which is the same as having no name at all, and one memory burned thin in the Weighing Hall to pay for the lesson. He will not tell him in advance which memory. He will say only that the account will be opened and that the boy should decide whether he can bear to be entered.

Wounds & Fears

He removed the verbs from his own speech so successfully that he can no longer say a thing happened, which means he can no longer say that anything was done to anyone. His wound is a language with no actor in it. He fears the Vorr, who recognise the numbers in the oldest slates and will not translate them, because a people who can read the Deep Hand and refuse to is a people who have already judged the Keeping and found it heavy. He fears anyone who can add, because adding a district’s deduction to a district’s warmth produces a number the College has spent eight hundred years not printing. His contradiction: the man who teaches that a life has no actor has kept a private tally of his own acts for thirty years, and calls it a record of corrections, which is the most human thing in the College and the one thing he would never enter in a report.

Desires

He wants the method to remain in the College alone and the valuation of lives to be a guild secret rather than a public argument. He thinks he wants accuracy. What he needs is to learn that a deduction with no actor in the sentence is still a deduction taken from a person, and that the passive voice is not a protection but a tool, like the clamp and the cold iron tube. When Dan reads a Sarnish ledger for the first time and understands the one-in-three, it will be Wessen’s teaching that lets him do it, and Wessen’s voice he will hear in the sentence he hates most.

Bonds

Rennick — his best student, a young assessor with a gift for addition that Wessen has tried three times to correct out of him. He has not succeeded and has not reported the failure, which is a form of protection in the College and the only kind he can manage.

Marra — an officer whose reports are the most exact in his service and whose slate once failed to balance in his own hand. He has never asked her why and has stopped correcting her work, which she has noticed and entered nowhere.

Steward Derren — the head of the Keeping, who has praised the College’s accuracy twice in twenty years. Wessen has decided that praise from that man is a warning, and has begun to keep the second file in a more secure place.

Ludo of the Old Kiln — a Grathic scholar who found a slate in the ash throat and has begun asking the College questions in the Deep Hand’s shape. Wessen has not answered him and has not had him removed, which is the precise boundary of his courage.

Corin — the lowlander with the ear who cannot add and can hear a cracked vessel through a wall. Wessen has decided to teach him because the boy’s deficiencies are interesting: a man who can read a wall but not a ledger is a man who will one day ask why the two disagree.

Alda Reed — a hearth-maid who was paid by the Court to keep the birth-slates and has kept a second set instead. Wessen knows the second set exists in the abstract, the way a man knows a drawer is locked, and has never ordered it searched.

Arc So Far

No chapters are written yet. He enters in a counting room in Slate Row, turning a slate face-down before he speaks to a boy, which the boy will not understand until he learns to read what is on it.
