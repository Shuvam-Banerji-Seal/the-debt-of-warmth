Appearance

Sixty-three and spare, with the dry, maintained look of a vessel that has been carefully tempered and has never been allowed to run cold. He dresses in the Hearthseat’s plain dark wool with a strip of lead-grey at the collar that denotes the office and nothing else; he has no rings, no house-mark and no ornament, because an officer who wears a thing gives the rolls something to read. His hands are clean to the point of aggression — the hands of a man who has not carried fuel in forty years and does not need to. His voice is low, level and slightly warm, which is the most frightening thing about him, because his listeners must lean in and therefore agree to the sentence before it is finished. He has a trick of resting his fingertips on a slate while he speaks, not to consult it but to keep it flat, the way another man might keep a cup from sliding. He smells of warm stone, clean paper and the faintest trace of the Hearth-fire, which follows him from the Weighing Hall and which he has never tried to hide.

Voice & Manner

Ledgerhand grammar made flesh: every sentence passive, every verb removed, every cruelty in the third person. He never raises his voice, never says I where he can say the Keeping, and never says you where he can say the account. He has eleven words for a deduction and will use the exact one, which is his way of being honest within the only vocabulary he considers real. When he is contradicted he becomes interested, which is worse than anger. He has one habit that his officers have learned to fear: he writes the thing down while the person is still speaking.

  • “The measure is banked. The account stands. Nothing is lost that we have kept. That is the whole of it, and it is not a threat.”
  • “You have said a thing that cannot be entered. I have entered it anyway. That is my kindness.”
  • “The world cannot be saved. The counting can be continued. Those are different propositions, and only one of them is my work.”
  • “One in twelve is printed on the notice. What a household believes is not in the notice, and is therefore not in the Keeping.”
  • “I have read the sealed file. You have not. Shall I tell you what it says, or shall we go on as we were?”

History

He was entered on the rolls at twenty-two and has never held another name. He rose not by cleverness but by an unbroken capacity to be present when a difficult slate had to be signed: the Ash Rising of Y.H. 668, the suppression of Haldre’s paper in 710, the sealing of the First Seam survey in 806, the Long Cold of 811. In each case he was the officer who wrote the final line in a hand that did not vary, and in each case the line was a kindness in the grammar of the Keeping — the district was quieted, the paper was suppressed, the survey was sealed, the levy was raised for the Hearth. He has never been accused of cruelty and has never understood an accusation when one was made, because he cannot see the person inside the account, and has spent forty years perfecting the not-seeing.

He has read the sealed file. He knows the Hearth is a wound and the Keeping is salvage from a failing repair. He knows the deduction in Low Kiln is one in three and that the printed notice is a founding invoice dressed as mercy. He knows the oldest repairs under the world are neither clay nor stone, and he knows what the Keldish translation and the Court translation of them were: both guesses, both near. He has read Haldre’s paper and has not burned his copy. He keeps it because it is the only honest document in the Hearthseat and because he has decided that honesty, left where it is, does no harm. He is not a believer. He is a continuance.

In the first weeks of Stone, Y.H. 812, he authorised a private whole return of forty unspent measures without recording the purchaser. He did this personally and with perfect composure, and has entered nothing about it anywhere except in the sealed file, where the entry reads: The instrument is in place. The account is carried.

Wounds & Fears

He has no wound he will admit to, which is itself the wound: a man who has trained himself not to feel the cold has become unable to feel anyone else’s. He fears the Waste growing inward, because it is the only thing in his ledger that does not balance and cannot be written down as a kindness. He fears a hot trace that will not cool, because it is the only measure that will not enter an account. He fears, most of all, a man who looks at a dying body and says plainly leave it, because that sentence has no passive form and therefore cannot be entered, and a thing that cannot be entered is a thing the Keeping cannot survive. His contradiction is not hidden and not a secret: he knows the world cannot be saved by the Keeping, and he continues the Keeping anyway, and calls the continuation mercy.

Desires

He wants the account to continue. He thinks he wants the world kept. What he needs is to understand the difference — which he already knows and has chosen to forget — and that a man who continues a failing repair is not keeping the world but spending it, one district at a time. He will never understand this in any way that changes him. He will understand it perfectly in the way that lets him write it down.

Bonds

Marra — his collector, the most exact hand in his service, whose fingers are going grey and whose reports have never once mentioned her own deductions. He has praised her twice and once asked her, gently, whether she believed the printed figure. She said the account stood. He has not asked again, because the answer confirmed what he needed: she is one of his own, in the sense that she has learned not to see the person.

Derren the Younger — an officer’s son with access to the sealed file and a different last name in his heart. The younger man has begun to ask about the oldest repairs in language that is almost the Deep Hand. Steward Derren has not stopped him, which is the most dangerous permission in the Hearthseat.

Pell Varr — the Assessor-Marshal who put down the Ash Rising by list. They are the two men who understand what a district costs, and they have never once discussed it, because a discussion would require the word I.

Haldre — the keeper who vanished in Y.H. 704. Steward Derren keeps Haldre’s paper in a drawer beside the sealed file and reads it on the Last Day, when the ash in the corners is not swept. He has decided that Haldre was right and that being right changes nothing.

Rusk — a name in a sealed file, a person of the sealed opinion, whom the Steward has never met and has caused to be watched for thirty years without ever ordering an arrest. He likes the man’s questions and has said so in a report, which is the closest thing to affection in the Hearthseat.

Corin — not yet a name in his account, only an irregularity in a slate from Greybank: a whole return whose deduction is zero. He has read that slate twice and has written nothing in the margin, which for him is an act of extraordinary restraint and the beginning of an interest.

Arc So Far

No chapters are written yet. He enters in a room gone cold — a single scene away from Dan, in the Weighing Hall or a counting room — where a slate is read aloud and a man is quietly entered. The reader will feel the shift in the prose before they know why.
