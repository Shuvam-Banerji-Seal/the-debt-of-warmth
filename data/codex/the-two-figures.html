I keep the chronicle of the Standing Post. It is not a history of Sarn and does not pretend to be one. The reeves of the Fenmarch are chosen for standing longest in a bog, and a man who has stood longer than any other is asked to settle the fines and the feuds, which he does in a voice thinned by the waiting. Nobody writes down the years he gave to the standing. The post writes them down. I copy the post.

This extract is the Thirty-One Years, which the Court calls the War of Deductions and the fen has always called the taking. The Court’s annals place it among the Bowl Years, between the southern purchase of measures and the fixing of the borders. We kept it in a tarred box under the third board of the walk, where the water comes up to the nails in the wet season and no paper will hold a word for a tend. The Court buys its contracts on salt-paper from Vhal, and in Vhal a contract that rots is held to be a virtue. In the fen a contract that rots is a contract nobody trusted in the first place. I have copied what the water left.

The years as the post has them

Copied out of the tarred box. The broad hand is the reeve’s and sets down what stood. The narrow upright hand beneath each year is the factor’s clerk, who came up from Greybank in Reed of Y.H. 813 and corrected the whole leaf while a boy held it against the wind. I have left both readings standing, as a mended pot keeps the old break showing.

Y.H. 420. Agents of the Bowl of Sere bought measures in the fen at prices no household could refuse, and carried them south to be returned whole, beautiful and remembering, in the open courts. Entered as the southern trade, with the prices set against the eel-count of that year.

In the narrow upright hand: A profitable commerce in measures, regulated by the Keeping. Strike carried.

Y.H. 426. A collector came up to the post with a cold iron tube and a slate. The reeve set him on the lowest board, where the water came over his boot tops, and asked him to state his office before he drew. He said his office was a taking. The reeve asked him to write that down, and he wrote a draw. Entered as a drawing of the office.

In the narrow upright hand: The office extended to the fen districts, peaceably received. Strike boot tops.

Y.H. 429. The collectors began to sit at the ferry and at the reed landings. A household of a recognised house in Grath paid one in twelve, as the notice says. A household of the fen paid one in three, and was told that the notice said one in twelve. Entered as the two figures.

In the narrow upright hand: The deduction fixed at one in twelve throughout the bowl. Strike the second figure.

Y.H. 437. Eels failed at the weirs in the lean week, and the levy was called on the standing crops of reed. A man brought the figure up out of his fist and asked which of the two the Court meant. The reeve told him the Court meant the one on the notice, and the collector drew the other. Entered as the figure said and the figure paid.

Y.H. 441. The Court’s annals begin to call the thirty years a war. There was no battle in the fen and no dead man entered. What the fen lost went out through the tube and came back as a thin child who could not say whose warmth it carried. Entered as eleven empty places in the returning line, with no names, because the collector did not give them and the reeve did not ask.

In the narrow upright hand: Disturbance of the peace, occasioned by the southern trade. Strike empty.

Y.H. 449. The collectors were withdrawn from the post for one tend and came back with a new slate. No reason was entered on either side. The reeds were cut that year by hands that had been in the strips and had not all come out again. The fen entered the number of hands. The Court entered the number of reeds.

Y.H. 451. The thirty-first year. The two figures stood as they stand now, and the notice still says one in twelve. Entered as peace, the account standing. The reeve who stood out the last of the thirty-one years stepped down and could not say, when asked, how long he had stood. I have put his standing in the margin rather than in the year, because a figure that a man cannot carry away with him is not a figure the post can keep.

In the narrow upright hand: Peace restored. The several rates of the deduction confirmed. Strike the margin.

I have not struck anything. The factor’s clerk takes the verbs out because a thing always true has no need of them, and a deduction that was always one in twelve has no need of a fen, nor of a man to stand in it. I put the verbs back. A clerk who holds a leaf down for another man in a wind is not a man who believes the leaf is always true.

The two figures

The column on the left is what is printed on the Weighing notice and read aloud at the ferry. The column on the right is what went out through the tube. Both stand in the box under the third board. Neither has ever been signed.

Who stood — Figure read aloud — Figure drawn — Where it is entered

A house of Grath in good standing, clay or coin behind it — one in twelve — one in twelve — the notice, the slate, the annals

A household of the fen districts — one in twelve — one in three — the notice only

The clay-bound of the terraces — one in twelve — one in three — the notice only

The unhoused and the foundling with no house — one in twelve — one in three — nowhere at all

The difference between the two columns is three parts in twelve, which is a figure any child at the ferry can work out with eel-bones and does. It has never been entered. I have asked a man who copies the Court’s annals why a figure might be read in one place and paid in another, and he said a figure read aloud was a kindness and a figure paid was an account, and that a man who mixes the two has begun to write a sermon. I have set his sentence down. It is the only true sentence he gave me, and he did not know he had said it.

In Y.H. 612 the Fenmarch was made a confederacy under Court protection, and the cheap thin returns began in the same year. The post entered them without comment, which was a kindness to the box. I have copied the entry here because a reader who stops at Y.H. 451 will think the thirty-one years ended. They did not end. They were entered under another name.

The song of the two figures

Taken down in Reed of Y.H. 813 at the third landing, from a woman who would not give a name and asked that the last line be left unsung. She said a song with its last line sung is a contract, and contracts in the fen are carried away by the damp. I have set a rule in its place, because a rule is the only thing a Court clerk will copy without asking who said it.

Twelve in the mouth and three in the hand,

the water takes what the slate won’t stand.

A whole comes back with its face on plain,

a thin comes back and asks for its name again.

The eel goes up and the eel goes down,

the peat goes up and the peat lies dry;

the Hearth keeps one and the Hearth keeps two,

and who keeps three will not say why.

Stand on the board till the board stands still,

count what you gave on the nails of the walk;

a figure said is a figure paid

where the water ends and the boardwalk stops.

Here the singer stopped. In the place of the last line she put her thumb into the wet boards and left it there until the print filled with water. I have set the rule down as she gave it, and I have left the print where it fell.

What the clerk wrote above the song

In the narrow upright hand, written across the third stanza: The Hearth keeps nothing that is not given. Strike who keeps three.

The factor’s clerk has a clean way with a line of verse, and no ear for the water under it. The Hearth keeps what is given, which is true, and somebody in that stanza keeps three, which is also true, and the two truths are not the same shape. He has taken the second one out because it has no name to stand behind it. I have put it back. When he came down the plank walk with his slate I asked him whether the figure on the notice was the figure the Hearth received, and he said the account stood, which is the Court’s way of closing a door without saying which side of it a man is on.

The reeve’s correction

In the broad standing hand, written above both the song and the Court hand: Nine went out of the fen in Y.H. 429 and six came back, and the three who did not come back are not entered as dead, because no collector wrote them down and no slate was opened over them. We stood them at the post. That is the whole of what we could do.

I have left the reeve’s hand standing above the Court’s, and the Court’s above the text, and the text above the peat. A leaf kept under a board in a bog does not lie flat, and nothing written on it lies flat either.

At the foot, in a hand that has never been claimed: We paid the third. We stood and let it go, which is not the same as giving it, and is the only thing in this box that I would swear to.
