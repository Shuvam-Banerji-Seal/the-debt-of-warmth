I set the pots into the throat of a kiln two doors below the Vail and have done it through seventeen firings. A man come off the north bridge with a soft cuff and a slate asked a girl at the tubs what was said while the green ware went in, and wrote her answer down before she had finished giving it. What he took away had the doing out of it and a figure standing where a place should be. So I have set the words down as we say them at the mouth and put the verbs back.

A setting-song is not a tune. A tune wants a voice with nothing in its hands, and at the throat every hand is full. We say the words down into the clay, not up into the yard. Said up they grow a melody, and a man in a clean coat can carry the melody away and leave the pots behind him, which has happened twice in my time and both times in the same week.

The wet-slip thumb at the head is mine. I held the tally down on the flagstone with the hand I set with and did not know I had marked it.

How it is set

  • Carry the green pots from the racks with the palm under the belly, never by the rim. Green rim comes away in the hand and leaves a cup with no top.
  • One wad of clay under every pot, no bigger than a thumb. The wad is the only promise in this yard that goes all the way through the fire with the thing it was made for.
  • Three to a row and a thumb’s width between. The width is the heat’s road. Too close and the heat cannot pass; too wide and it runs through and the pot comes out with its green note still in it.
  • At the back of the throat there is a place where two go and three would go. Go around it with a look and a wide step. Do not set a third there to make the row come straight, and do not say whose place it is.
  • Say the refrain into the throat. Said to the girl beside you it becomes instruction, and instruction at this kiln is instruction she will unlearn at somebody else’s.
  • Do not set on a morning when the wind comes off High Kiln with the sweet edge on it. A girl who has tasted glaze cannot smell what is in her own mouth, and leans too far over the throat.
  • The count is in the rows and the turning of the wrist. A pot counted is a pot sold, and a pot sold is entered elsewhere, which is not a thing to do while it is still wet.

The song

The last hand to come away from the throat keeps the refrain back until the wad is under the next pot. One line of the refrain to one row set.

Up from the racks with the green pots on the forearm,

palm to the belly, not the rim, or the rim goes;

wet clay is a thing that trusts the hand that holds it

and comes apart in the hand that has only just learned.

Set it. Leave the space. Let the heat come round.

Three to a row, and the back place left as found.

The fire takes both and answers to neither.

A wad of clay under every pot, no bigger than a thumb,

the pot sits on it and the wad sits on the stone;

it is the only promise in this yard that burns,

and the pot either keeps its seat or goes over.

Set it. Leave the space. Let the heat come round.

Three to a row, and the back place left as found.

The fire takes both and answers to neither.

A thumb’s width between and no more and no less;

too close and the heat can’t get by to the far side,

too wide and it goes through without stopping

and the pot comes out with its green note still in it.

Set it. Leave the space. Let the heat come round.

Three to a row, and the back place left as found.

The fire takes both and answers to neither.

At the back there’s a place where two go and three would go,

and we go around it with the look and the wide step;

don’t set a third there to make the row come straight ---

a straight row is a thing a clerk can carry away;

what we set we don’t count, and what we don’t set we don’t name.

Set it. Leave the space. Let the heat come round.

Three to a row, and the back place left as found.

The fire takes both and answers to neither.

A girl come down from the Glaze Room with the sweet at her mouth

leans over the throat to see the rows standing;

don’t let her lean. The pale line at the gum is the first of it,

and the hand that sets is the hand that will answer.

Set it. Leave the space. Let the heat come round.

Three to a row, and the back place left as found.

The fire takes both and answers to neither.

The last pot in is the one you never see again;

the clay lid goes on and the mouth is shut with a wad;

what you had in your hands at the bell you have not got now,

and the fire has it, and gives back what it chose.

Set it. Leave the space. Let the heat come round.

Three to a row, and the back place left as found.

The fire takes both and answers to neither.

There is a space before the last refrain, the length of a hand coming away from the last pot. The clerk put a nought in it, which is a thing you can hold up; a hand coming away is not.

The part they do not set while a man from Greybank stands in the yard

Said low to the throat and not across the yard. Taken up when the cuff has gone back over the bridge.

Three to a row and the back place gone around;

we do not say whose place it is and the yard does not ask.

One in twelve, says the notice, twice over the basket;

the bell goes four, four and a gap and nobody crosses.

The fire was carried here under a broken pot and answered four pulls

with the fifth missing, and nobody has named whose.

The grey goes out of the north gate heaped above the sides

and nobody in the square looks at it twice, which is the afternoon’s work;

a brine-gull took a coal not yet cold and went upriver with it,

and no slate in Sill stood one mark the poorer.

The river keeps a note under the sill and the town sets its clock by it;

the kiln keeps a count under the floor, and they were never one.

Three to a row. The back place stays where it was found.

The clerk may have his nought. It will not keep a pot warm.

What the clerk did

The factor’s clerk borrowed the tally on a morning when the weigh-slips were out drying, ink on two fingers that would not come out. He took the verbs out and set his words above mine. I have left his hand standing and put ours underneath, as the throat keeps the pot and the flagstone keeps the wad.

  • At the only promise in this yard that burns, he wrote clay seating, consumable; no residue arises. True in the figure and false at the mouth, where the wad goes all the way.
  • At three to a row, he wrote capacity, three per shelf, and at the back place, shortfall, one position. He has made the place into something owed by somebody; there is no owing in a look.
  • At the back place left as found, he wrote nil, and drew his line a finger’s breadth past the margin.
  • At the fire takes both and answers to neither, he wrote thermal transfer, unrecorded. He thinks a transfer entered is a transfer known.
  • At the count is in the arm that carried, he wrote labour, one mark the day; no deduction for injury. He has put a figure where an arm was.

What the glaze-girl put in the margin

The green belongs to a girl who comes down from the Glaze Room and has the pale line at her gum and will not say when it came. I have copied it as it stands.

At don’t let her lean: I have leaned at nine throats and been told at each of them. The sweet is not in the mouth, it is in the habit. I have never told the next girl.

At the pale line at the gum: It shows white if you put a lamp to it. We do not put lamps to one another here. We put the tongue to the glaze and go on tasting.

At what we don’t set we don’t name: There is a name for the back place on this terrace and we do not use it. Words said up there come down on the wind and go over the bridge with a figure on them.

At the kept-back part: I asked who the back place belonged to. She said it belonged to the firing, which is an answer you can carry to Greybank and still have in your mouth when you arrive.

At the head and in the gutter

The wet-slip thumb at the head is mine and has gone into the grain, which is why I sign with it. The brick dust in the fold came off the flagstone and is the only thing here put there by the trade and not by a person. The wad between the second and third leaf was pressed there by a girl to hold the paper in the draught; it has gone hard as a pot that never saw the throat, and is lighter in the hand than one that did. The scorch took two words off the first verse, which I have not written in again, because a song with two words gone is said slowly, and ours are all said slowly. The green bead came off the girl who leaned over with sweetness still at her tongue; she touched the leaf, said nothing and went back up the terrace. I have kept my tongue off it. The sherd between the third and fourth leaf broke at this throat and answers flat when I knock it on the nail, the only thing in the leaf that has been through the fire and come back to tell of it. The grey along the gutter came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying on a kiln bench; nobody will own it, and there is no Last Day behind a nail. The grey wool ties the leaf to the nail, with a knot for the one line I could not bring up.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The wad keeps the seat. The throat keeps the row.

The back place keeps itself, and the slate is read all the same.

I asked the girl at the tubs, the watcher at the damper and a carter under the sill, and had a proverb, a rest and a direction, and no hand. I have left the line where it was written.

The tally goes back on the nail. There will be firings enough, and I shall set three to a row and go around the back place with a look. If the clerk borrows the leaf he may have his figures. The pots will be where I put them, and the count under the floor will not be made to agree with the note under the stone.
