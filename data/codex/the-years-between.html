I keep the book of the years between. It stands behind the winter tallies in the lower room of the bank at Greybank, in a town that is warmer than the clay country and colder than the Hearthseat, and in all the years I have kept it nobody has asked to see it. The clean roll of the Keeping begins at the lighting and runs forward in a hand that has had the doers taken out of it. My book begins where the clean roll stops being interesting, which is the hundred and twentieth year, and ends where the Glaze Wars begin, which is the three hundredth. One in twelve had been printed on the notices since the fortieth year, before my book opens, and it stands on them still. Between those two figures the Court entered very little, and what it entered it made smooth.

I have put the verbs back where I found them taken out. A chronicle with nobody in it can be read aloud in a clean voice and gone home from believing, and I have stood at this table and listened to too many clean voices.

Two things in these years belong together and are never entered together: the glaze that came downriver out of the Keel and the pale line that came after it; the single crack the temple tells of and the layers the surveyor found underneath. I have set them side by side and left the joining to the reader, which is the only honest work a copyist is allowed.

The years as the clean roll has them

Copied out of the Hearthseat annals in the neat hand. What I put underneath is not an argument with the roll. It is the roll with the people put back.

Y.H. 128. The Return extended to villages that had buried their dead. Court hand: the burying was discontinued. Mine: villages that had buried their dead were made to bank them instead, and a woman who had carried her mother to the river was given a notice and a figure on the same morning. The notice is kept. The figure is kept. The morning is not.

Court hand: The practice of interment ceased. The mercy of the Return was extended. Strike made.

Y.H. 147. Lead-glaze of the Keel begins to be used on fine ware. Court hand: an improvement in the firing. Mine: lead came down in bowls with a blue skin, and the glaze-mixers of High Kiln tasted it at the rim and said it was sweet, which is the word a man uses when he has not yet been made ill by a thing. An improvement is a thing that happens to a pot. A sweetness is a thing that happens to a person. Both were entered; only one was entered under this year.

Y.H. 162. A pale line at the gum of a glaze-woman of High Kiln. Court hand: a complaint of the teeth. Mine: she could not hold a cup by the fifth day, and the physician at Greybank wrote the word sweetness in his report, and the sweetness stayed in the report while the glaze stayed out of it. I have copied both. The cup is in neither.

Court hand: Strike the sweetness. Enter a mild disorder of the teeth. Glaze not implicated.

Underneath, in mine: A clerk who takes the glaze out of a sentence leaves the teeth standing by themselves, and teeth standing by themselves have never hurt anybody, and no cup has gone flat in a room where nobody tasted anything.

Y.H. 178. The same pale line in the lower rooms of Greybank, among clerks who had been given the new ware to use. Court hand: a visitation in the lower rooms. Mine: they called it the winter, and the winter went, and the line did not. One of them wrote the word glaze in the margin of a tally and crossed it out before the tend was done; the crossing is still there and I have not gone over it.

Y.H. 203. A cup out of High Kiln set down wrong at a shelf answered flat under the knuckle, and a potter said a pot that answers flat has something the matter with its holding. Court hand: no entry. Mine: no entry is not the same as no cup, and I have put the cup where the entry would have been.

Y.H. 240. A surveyor out of Keld came down the Clay Road with a rubbing under his coat and would not say what he had rubbed. He turned the sheet face down when the factor came into the room. Court hand: a survey of the boundary. Mine: he turned it face down in our room as well, which is a courtesy in Keld and an insult in Greybank, and I have entered the turning and not the reason.

Y.H. 260. The first survey of the rim-seam, the oldest document in these parts that still has its original numbers. It notes a repair of unkept words, and it says the repairs lie in layers, the newest over the oldest. Court hand: no entry. The passage is in no Court history that I have read. It appears in another hand on the back of a slate out of the Old Kiln, which I have seen and cannot weigh.

From the survey sheet, in a hard-pressed hand, the words kept as they stand: as a pot is mended again and again until nothing of the first pot can be seen.

Y.H. 288. The wells by the Ferr carry a thin taste and the households of Stonemouth go down with the pale line. Court hand: the water does not bear tasting. Mine: the water was not sweet and the cups were, and I have set the two sentences on the same leaf without a verb between them, which is the nearest a copyist can come to asking a question.

Y.H. 300. Commissioners sit over the lead workings in the lower Ferr. Court hand: a dispute of the workings. Here my book ends. The Glaze Wars are written in another book, and the three hundred are entered there as a round sum, and what the round sum left out is entered in the gutter, where a clerk of the College will not think to look.

The survey sheet

The strip folded over the Y.H. 260 entry was cut from a survey rubbing spoiled by wet slate. I have set what it calls each layer beside what the factor’s copy calls the same layer, and chosen neither, because a copyist who chooses between two guesses has made a fact of one of them and will have to answer for it at the Weighing.

Layer — The surveyor’s word — The factor’s copy — Times mended

The newest but one — Stone, dressed and keyed — Stone — Twice; the second over the first

The middle — Stone and iron pins — Stone; pins not entered — Once

The old — Stone, with a repair of unkept words — Stone — Three times

The oldest that can be named — Not stone and not clay — Language — Not entered

The oldest of all is entered in the surveyor’s own speech as that which was left unsaid made solid. The Keldish translate it as Ash. The Court’s copy, which is kept in the Hearthseat and shown to no one below an assessor, translates it as language. Both are guesses. I copy both and set neither above the other.

I asked a Vorr who came through Greybank on the Hearth Road what the numbers on the oldest slates meant. He said the numbers would be found correct when the season had turned, which is the answer they give and is not a translation. I have set it down because a courteous man owes his reader the exact shape of a refusal.

The song of the glaze-women

Taken down at High Kiln in Ash of Y.H. 812, from a woman who tasted the glaze at the rim for nineteen years and had the pale line herself. She would not sing the third verse while a man from the factor’s office stood in the room, so we went and stood by the slip pits. She said the verse had been in the glaze room longer than she had, which is the custom of that terrace and not an answer.

The Keel sent down a bowl of blue,

and the clay country called it fine;

the glaze was sweet at the tasting rim,

and the pale line came in time.

Two hundred years of cups went round

and the pain went round with them;

the slate wrote teeth, the house wrote winter,

and nobody asked what was in the glaze.

A surveyor rubbed the seam at night

and found a mending under a mending;

the oldest is not stone and not clay —

ask the pot what it kept, and what it spent.

The sentence on the back of the slate

On the back of a slate that came out of the Old Kiln, in a hand smaller and harder-pressed than the figures on its face, there is one sentence, struck through twice and written again underneath: Another kept badly, and we inherited the crack. The same sentence is said to be scratched into a slate in a kiln-house in Low Kiln, where nobody knows what it is. I have copied it as I found it and put no gloss on it. A sentence a man cannot weigh is not a sentence he may turn into a fact, and the book of the years between is not the place for a fact I have made myself.

What the Court hand did

A clerk of the factor’s office borrowed the leaf on a market morning, laid his hand on the top sheet before he looked at either of us, and took the verbs out as though a verb were a thing a man could smuggle past a weigh.

  • Against villages were made to bank them, he set the Return was extended.
  • Against she could not hold a cup, he set a mild disorder was recorded.
  • Against the surveyor turned the sheet face down, he set a survey was conducted.
  • Against as a pot is mended again and again until nothing of the first pot can be seen, he wrote repairs were recorded in sequence, and passed over the words unkept and left unsaid without a stroke, which is the readiest way of correcting a sentence I have yet seen.
  • Against the sentence on the back of the slate he wrote nothing at all.

I have left both readings everywhere, the thin one under the neat one, as a repaired wall keeps the old break under the new keying and does not pretend the first hand never touched the stone. A clerk who takes the people out of a year leaves the year standing by itself, and a year standing by itself has cost nobody a cup and made nobody a figure.

At the head and in the gutter

The iron-gall at the head is mine and will not wash out of the creases; I hold the book open through a tend and have never managed to hold a light with the same hand. The green bead in the lower margin is not ink. A glaze-woman come up from High Kiln leaned over to see the Y.H. 260 line and touched the leaf with her thumb, and I have left it where she put it. The slate dust in the gutter came off the survey strip and has worked into the sewing. The tallow is my own end burnt down against the head. The ring at the foot of the song is small ale, set down by the woman who sang and not drunk until the third verse was done. The square of bare paper on the second leaf is where I scraped a word away with a knife and put no other in its place; it is the only square in this quire that I made myself. The grey along the outer fold is not mine and not the Court’s. I have not swept it.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

Two hundred years of neat accounts,

and a line the roll will not enter;

the glaze went down and the pain went down,

and neither was called the other’s debtor.

The seam keeps its layers, the slate keeps its square,

and the cup at the lip keeps the flat;

ask the surveyor what he turned face down —

ask the pot what it held, and what it had.

The leaves go back behind the winter tallies. Next tend I copy the figures of the Glaze Wars out of the other book and put the verbs back there too, and set the years between beside them where a man must move a ledger to reach them. The clean roll goes on in its smooth hand. My book goes on underneath it with the glaze and the pale line still standing side by side, which is where I found them and where I will leave them for the next hand to join or not, as he chooses.
