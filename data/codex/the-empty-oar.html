The Rope-End stands where the Quay turns toward the council stairs, and in Ash, when the salt boats are hauled up and their oars stacked under tarpaulin, the house is full from the first dark. There is no work on a laid-up galley and no one to pay for it, so the men tell one another what the sea took and put a quart on the telling. I keep the house and the quart and the bench. A man who has been rowing since his fifteenth year does not want a slate put under his story; he wants a cup set down where he can see it.

Vhalen has no word for a guest and no word for a friend. It has a word for a man whose account is current, and in Ash there are not many of those. The men who come here are current for the length of a quart. When the quart is gone they are men again, the coast road is shut behind them, and the Sallow lies milk-white at the shallows, taking the warmth out toward the Waste the way a cracked cup takes water into a floor.

I set this down because of an argument at the door after the telling. A man came in off the coast pass with a coin from a southern port and said the proverb about a stranger’s coin warming before his face, and nobody laughed, because it is true in Ash and costs a man his gloves to find out. The argument was about a seat. I have put the tale as it was told and the clerk’s figure standing above it, the way the bench keeps a charcoal score under a spilled quart.

The order of the house

  1. A story is told once. Ask for the telling again and you owe a quart, because the second asking is for your own pleasure and not for the bench.
  2. Answer in one breath. A second answer is a different answer and is not heard. A man who gives two has not understood the question.
  3. Three answers are taken and a fourth is not. A fourth would want a payer, and there is no payer on a laid-up bench.
  4. The judge is the bench. I say whether an answer serves and I do not say why. A reason given is a story given away for nothing, and the giver can carry it up the Salt Road and sell it at Greybank.
  5. No slate lies on the bench. The score goes off with the morning with the ale, and neither is entered.
  6. The cup is set down before the first word and no man lifts it until the question. If a skin forms on it while the telling runs, the night’s answering will go badly. I have known this to fail twice and would not say so before a clerk.

The man off the galley

He came in at the going of the light with his hands in his sleeves, which is a thing a salt man does not do unless there is something wrong with his hands. He had rowed the pulled benches seven years and come out at the end of a term with the fingers gone the colour of old rope and bent so that they would not close on a cup handle. He had been condemned to them, which he said without shame and without making a story of it; a man who has rowed seven years has had the story taken out of him by the oar.

He set a southern coin on the bench for his quart. “A stranger’s coin warms before his face,” he said, “and a Vhaleni’s does not, because we know what it is worth.” Nobody answered him. The dipping had been at dawn that week and the nets had come up empty, and a house with empty nets does not trouble itself with proverbs.

Then he told us about the seat.

The tale, as he told it

“I pulled stroke on the Sallow Maid, seven years and three crossings, and there was a man at the bench behind me named Drenn, who was named for a wind the old dippers still name and the young ones have forgot. In the fourth winter the tide ran against the wind and the sea went the colour of skimmed milk, and Drenn’s hands gave out at the oar. That is how it happens on the pulled benches. It is not a sickness and there is no word for it in the tally. A hand gives out, and the man sits where his hand left him.”

He stopped to wet his thumb, which would not bend enough to lift the cup, and went on.

“He died in the night crossing. By rights a dead salt man is sealed in a salted boat and pushed out on the ebb and watched until he is out of sight, which is held to be enough. We did not watch him out. The Council counts a hull by the oars that pull, and a galley short of an oar is a debt on the boat-owner, and there are men in this room who know what a debt on a boat costs. So we lashed him to the bench and I steered with him through what was left of the crossing, his hand on the oar and mine over his, and the oar went where I put it. We came into the Quay at the turn of the ebb with seven oars pulling and a full hull counted, and Drenn was still cold in his seat.”

There is a way a room goes quiet that is not the same as listening. It is the way a room goes when every man in it has done a thing and no man will say he did it.

“The share went to his mother, because a share goes through mothers and there was nobody else. She held it and never came aboard. A woman can hold a share for thirty years and never set foot on the hull, and the Council will count it as if she had pulled every oar in it. Then she sold it to a man who came aboard by marrying into the family, and his opinion on the boat was counted at half, which is the mercy the Council extends to men who come to a boat from a wife. The bargain was on salt-paper and went soft in the first wet weather, which is held a virtue in this port: a bargain that lasts too long has stopped earning. He had never pulled an oar, and he had an opinion at half on a boat Drenn died in.”

The old man laid his bent hand flat on the bench and looked at it.

“They took Drenn’s oar out when we came in and hung it in a house on the Quay, and it hangs in this one now, above the door where you came in. No man has cut a seat for it. No man will ship it. A thing that is yours has to be paid for, and that oar was never paid for, and nobody wants to be the man who says it is his.”

The question, and the answering

Then he put the question to the bench, having been told that the bench is judge and not the men sitting at it.

The tally counted it full and the sea would not take it;

it went out on the crossing and came back in the seat;

it has no name on the roll and no hand on the oar;

men have sat in it and none will own it.

What is it?

A carter said “a share,” and the old man said a share is held by a woman who never came aboard, and that was the point of it and not the answer. A glass-hand from the furnaces said “a dead man,” which is an answer given to the door and not to the bench, and was not heard. A woman who holds a share through her mother said nothing at the bench, and afterwards wrote in the margin, which I have copied below.

A dips-man at the end of the bench, with salt still white at his cuffs, said “a seat.” He said it to the bench and not to the room.

“Yes,” the old man said. “The oar is only the thing that shows it. The tally counted seven oars and a full hull and never counted the seat, because a seat is not a thing a Council can tax and a man can be owed for. Drenn died in it and I steered from it and his mother sold it without ever sitting in it, and it is the only seat on the Quay that has never been paid for and has never been empty.” He lifted his quart with both hands. “That is the trick of it.”

The score, in charcoal

What stood — What the bench heard — What the clerk set over it

The seven oars — Seven men at the pull — Seven oars, hull current

The seat — A dips-man’s “a seat,” served — Not entered

The share through the mother — No answer from the bench — Share transmitted in the female line; no deduction

The bargain on salt-paper — Went soft in the wet — Instrument lapsed; no claim

The man come aboard — An opinion at half — Holder not a party

Drenn’s name — Spoken once, to the bench — Party unidentified

The figure read at the tide-house that morning was the draw of the third hour, and the clerk read it past the charcoal without looking up, the way a man reads past a thing that is not entered.

What the tide-clerk did

A clerk of the tide-tally borrowed the leaf on the morning the draws were read, being a man who cannot leave paper alone. He writes in a tight hand that the damp has begun to run at the corners, and he takes the man out of every sentence as though a man were a thing a body could keep dry in a cuff on a wet quay. Against the Council counted a full hull he set seven oars, hull current, which is true of every hull and therefore of none. Against his mother held the share and never came aboard he wrote share transmitted in the female line; no deduction, which is the law and is not the tale. At the question he put a dash and wrote unanswered; not entered.

I have left his hand standing above mine and my charcoal under it, the way the bench keeps a score under a quart. He has entered seven oars and the share and left the seat where it was, which is what a figure does when it is done with the man it came out of. I asked him what a figure was for and he said a figure was for carrying away. He has carried away two and left the one that would not go.

In the margin, in a boat-woman’s small hand

The leaf went across the Quay with a bundle of rope and came back with a line in a hand that is not mine and not the clerk’s. I have copied it as it stands.

My mother held her share by an oar I never pulled and a man I never met, and she never came aboard in her life. I hold it now and have never pulled either. The man who married my cousin came aboard and his opinion is counted at half, and mine is counted whole because I was born to it. There is no seat for either of us in this house. Do not put a figure on the chair.

I have not answered her in the margin. A thing written on a leaf follows a woman home, and her home is a boat-share with nowhere to put a second opinion.

At the head and in the gutter

The salt at the head came up white out of the paper on the carry from the door and is the only thing in this house that entered without a hand. The tar is off the thumb I held the leaf down with in the draught that comes under the Quay door, and it has gone into the grain so that no wet sleeve takes it out, which is why the house signs in charcoal rather than ink. The ring came off a quart that stood from the first word to the last and was drunk by nobody; two men have claimed it and one woman has denied it and I have given it to none of them. The press of wet wool across the second leaf came off the man who held the door while the old man spoke, and I have not written down which man, because he would stop holding doors if I did. The grey along the gutter came off a cuff that had no business lying where I keep the tin, and I have not swept it. There is a Last Day in this town and the corners are swept on the morning after and called dealt with; there is no Last Day on the water, and nothing there is swept until the tide does it.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The tally keeps the oar. The mother keeps the share.

The house keeps the seat. The ebb keeps the name.

I have asked the man who told it, the woman who holds the share, and the clerk of the tide-tally, and had three answers and no hand, as I have had before. The oar goes back above the door and the charcoal comes off the bench with the heel of my hand. In Ilmen the galleys go down again and there will be work of an evening other than asking one another what the sea took. I will set the quart where it stood and let the next man tell into it. If he asks whose seat that is, I shall tell him it is the one the tally did not count, which is the only answer this house has.
