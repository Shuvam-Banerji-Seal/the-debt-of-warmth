I keep the walling-slate at the mouth of the First Seam and I have never been down the Descent, which is no disgrace above ground and is not mentioned below it. A man come up from Greybank in Bone with a clean cuff and asked what we said over a man who goes into the wall. I told him we said the Return. He asked me to say it. I said it, and he wrote it down and thanked me, and what he wrote had the verbs taken out and a figure standing where the man had been, and it is in the factor’s book by now, being truer than mine in the only room he knows. What follows is what we actually say, with the verbs put back and the space left the width it is.

There is a cold in this mouth that goes into slate and stays there. I have kept the leaf under the lamp while I wrote so that the charcoal would take, and the oil has run once and taken two words off the fifth station, and I have not written them back. A person who knows a thing is not therefore in possession of it; I know the two words and have left the space the length of them, as the wall leaves the length of a man.

The manner of it, written small at the head

  • Say it at the mouth, after the number is cut and before the lime is mixed. A man with wet mortar on his hands should not be asked to hold the slate; he will leave a pale print on it and the print will outlast the number.
  • The reader is whoever keeps the walling-slate. In my mother’s time it was a man of the Duelling Floor, and he said the words the way another man reads a charge-sheet, and the old women at the count answered for him because the row was too frightened to. I am not a woman of the Floor and I say them slower.
  • The answer belongs to the row at the mouth, including the boy who came up with the man and will go down in his place next year. The boy says it worst. We do not correct the saying, only the order.
  • Do not say it to the stone. Said to the stone it becomes a bargain, and the stone keeps bargains, which is more than can be said of the Hearth. Say it to the slate and let the slate hear what it cannot enter.
  • Set the dead one facing the seam. This is not part of the prayer. It is the part before, the way warming a stone is the part before in the low houses.
  • If an officer of the Court is at the mouth, give him the shift-tally and not the leaf. The leaf has no figure for a man who is not coming up, and he cannot enter a man who stays where he is put.

The litany

I read from the walling-slate. The row answers. The answer is the same at every station, which is why it is a litany and not an argument with a mountain.

I. Of the number

Say what you hold before you say who you are.

The arm gives the number; the mouth gives the name.

At this mouth the order is the other way round,

because the wall has no ear for a name

and the slate has no room for a person.

Answer: The number stands. The mountain keeps its own.

II. Of the breaking

The world was fired whole and the lid went up in one anger.

The surveyors say the repairs lie one over another,

a new seam over an old seam, the newest over all,

as a pot is mended until the first pot cannot be seen.

Do not say this in front of an officer. Say it to the slate.

Answer: The number stands. The mountain keeps its own.

III. Of the Nine

Nine hands went after what had run away.

Nine repairs were driven home and the ninth is not finished.

There is a paper in a tin lined with warm-stone,

read once a year by men who do not act on it.

I have not read it. I know the length of it.

Answer: The number stands. The mountain keeps its own.

IV. Of the iron

The tube does not keep. The clamp does not keep.

Iron opens a way and the way stays open.

One bite holds a vessel whole and one bite takes the hand off a man.

It is the same bite and it has never asked anybody’s opinion.

Answer: The number stands. The mountain keeps its own.

V. Of the figure

One in twelve is printed on the notice and is read twice.

The mine-reeve checks the figure in red and does not look at the man.

Twelve bits to the mark and twenty marks to the crown.

Count embers, not years; a year is a stone nobody weighs.

The figure that is not printed is the one this mouth pays.

Here the lamp oil ran. Two words are gone and I have left the space the length of them. A lowland clerk would write a figure into it and call the leaf whole. It is not whole. It is carried, which is a different thing and the only one known here.

Answer: The number stands. The mountain keeps its own.

VI. Of the pouring

One measure into one vessel: he comes back with his name

and the hand that held the cup and the price of bread.

Many into many: they come back as they can,

with a hankering for a table they never sat at.

Both are poured. Both go into a clean hand.

Far beyond the Rim there is a people who say

they were never owed, and no Court has entered it.

Answer: The number stands. The mountain keeps its own.

VII. Of the walling

Here is no tube and no cradle and no clean hand.

He goes in facing the seam with his number on the stone.

Lime in the joints and a flat chip at the head,

and the surveyor adds a figure nobody reads aloud.

The mountain does not fetch back what it has taken.

It does not say it has taken. That is the courtesy here.

Answer: The number stands. The mountain keeps its own.

VIII. Of the coming back

We do not bring him up and do not say we have.

Come up with the number you went down with,

or come up with the number the mountain gave.

Two go down and the count comes two.

The count is made. That was always the promise.

Answer: The number stands. The mountain keeps its own.

Here the leaf leaves a space the width of a number with no man under it. It is not a number forgotten. It is the one the slate will not take and the wall will. I have kept the space the width it is.

The walling-slate, for this season, as I keep it

Wall — Number cut in arm — Number on slate — Name said

North gallery, first — forty-one — forty-one — said twice, once to the row

Under the survey room — nine — nine, and a notch at the edge — not said; the boy asked

Third, beyond the cup — no arm to read — no figure entered — none given

The third row is not a debt. There is a cup at the end of the Duelling Floor that nobody takes, for the man whose number was changed twice. I have set the row down as it stands. An empty row is not the same as a crossed one, and there is no crossing out at this mouth. The mine-reeve entered the second man’s earlier number in red and left the later cut standing in the skin, which is how an honest clerk keeps two figures without calling either a lie.

What the clerk did

A survey-clerk of the Court borrowed the leaf at Greybank on a morning when the factor was weighing lead and his cuff was soft at the edge from washing. He writes in the narrow upright hand and takes the verbs out as another man might take the bones out of a fish.

  • At the order is the other way round he wrote the measure is declared and the person is identified, underlined twice. Both stand. He has put the man after the number, which is where the mountain puts him and not where a mother does.
  • At the repairs lie one over another he wrote the First Breaking is a single event of the faith and struck my line through. I have put it back under his. He had to read the line before he could strike it, which is a thing a man does once and cannot do again without knowing what he struck.
  • At a paper read once a year he wrote no such paper above the line, and then, underneath, in the same hand, not for entry. I have left both. The first is what he is paid to write and the second is what he believes, and a man who writes both on one leaf is not as stupid as his cuff makes him look.
  • At we do not bring him up he wrote no return recorded; account closed. It is the only honest sentence in his hand and it cost him nothing, because an account closed is a figure with a line under it and he has never stood at a mouth and watched the line drawn in lime.
  • At the answer he put a round nought against the space and wrote vacancy beside it. He has put a number where a man would stand. That is the whole art of that hand.

I have left every line of his standing above mine and mine under it, the way a new seam lies over an old one and does not make the old one go away. He takes the verbs out because a thing always true has no need of them. I put them back because a boy at thirteen with a knife at his arm wants to know which hand to hold the stone with, and a woman at a mouth wants something to say to the boy after the knife.

What the widow put in the margin

The leaning hand is Henna’s, whose man went into the wall under the survey room with a number cut into his arm that had belonged to somebody out of the Fenmarch in the year he came back thin from Sill. She has written where I set the sixth station, and I have copied her as she wrote it.

One measure into one vessel. That is what they say at Sill and what they print on the notice. My man came back from a pouring with a number in his arm that was not his, and he wanted a table he had never sat at, and the table was mine and he sat at it six years without knowing it was his. When he went into the wall the surveyor cut the number out and cut a new one in, and the old one is still under the skin. I have asked which of the two the wall will keep. Nobody has answered and I have stopped asking.

And at the space, underneath the clerk’s nought:

Not a vacancy. He is where they put him. That is not the same as lost and it is not the same as kept, and I will not have your clerk choose between them.

I have copied her as she wrote it. She has taken her shawl home and I have not gone after her with a correction.

At the head and in the gutter

The filings at the head are off the clamp I set the leaf down on while the surveyor read the second number; they have gone into the grain and no wet sleeve takes them out, which is how iron signs. The pale along the gutter is lime off the walling mortar and is not Ash; there is Ash enough in this seam without bringing a stranger’s into a leaf. The tallow thumb is mine, off the lamp I keep turned low beside the slate. The spot at the lower corner came over the Cold Pass and has been water twice and frost twice and is still a spot. The oil ran across the fifth station on the night I copied after the second wall, and two words went with it; I have not gone over them, because a word written thin is a word said quietly and most of ours are. The notch I cut at the edge with my tally-knife where a word would not come. A notch is not a letter and it keeps better in the wet. The grey along the outer fold came off a cuff that had no business lying where the slate is kept, and I have not swept it. There is a Last Day in the low houses and there is none above the shaft mouth, and even there the morning comes.

At the foot

In a hand that is not mine, not the clerk’s and not Henna’s, struck through once and written again underneath, very small:

We say the Return. The wall does the keeping.

Those are two trades. Only one of them has a slate.

I asked the surveyor who reads the numbers, a boy who came up from the second wall, and Henna at the mouth, and had three answerings and no hand. I have left the struck line where it was struck. The leaf goes back into the satchel and the walling-slate goes back on its nail. Tomorrow a boy will state what he holds before he says who he is, the mine-reeve will check the figure in red, and the number will go on being called after the arm that carried it has gone into the wall.
