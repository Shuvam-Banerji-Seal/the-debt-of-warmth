I keep tallies on the Quay at Vhal in a room that smells of tar and wet rope. In our country a contract is written on salt-paper, and if the rain gets at it the debt is made nothing, and we call that a virtue. I went south with two chests of Cliffs glass and a consignment of Sallow salt for a house of the Ten Bowls, and came back with three gold crowns, a jar of wine whose paper had perished on the road, and a green bowl that cracked the second morning at home. I have set down what I saw. Where the south and my book disagree I have left both standing, which is what a tally-man does when he wishes to be honest and does not wish to be dismissed.

The road, and how the paper fared

I took the Salt Road up to Greybank with the glass riding high and the salt riding low, because salt wets paper and glass breaks it and a man must choose which of his goods he will let suffer. The factor’s man there wrote salt against a figure and did not look at the team. Below the coast pass the clay gives way to reeds, and at the Standing Post a reeve fined me a bit for asking which of the boards would bear me, which was within his right. The Fenfolk say a dry man is a dangerous man, and lodge him accordingly, and we learned why.

Beyond the Peat Line the villages stand empty in the middle of the day, and in their doorways are squares of bare clay where house-rolls once hung. A guide of ours asked whether the people had gone south or north. The reeve said they had gone where the account went, and would not say which that was.

I carried my paper in a tarred tin inside my coat and opened it only at the third hour, when the south does its business. At Greybank a clerk told me that paper made to rot was no way to carry a debt three hundred leagues. I told him it was the only way to carry one that could be honestly lost. He wrote it down and I have left it standing.

The heat, and the dust

The south is warm the way a kiln is warm with the door standing open. I have sat out a Stone at the Sallow with the eels frozen in the baskets and never wanted water as I wanted it there. The burning winds come yellow off the east and take the skin off a traveller’s lips and leave the grain standing, which is a trick of the wind I have no learning in and several reasons to believe. My lips went first. The grain stood.

The courts have no walls. There is a fire in the middle of each and men sit in the heat of it with their coats on the ground beside them, and the dust comes down into their hair, and nobody brushes it off. I brushed mine off on the first day and was noticed. A man beside me said that a man who brushes the dust has decided he is only passing through, and a man only passing through may be charged for the hour. I let it lie after that. There is yellow of it ground into the sewing of this book, which I have not shaken out, because a clerk who shakes a leaf is a clerk who wants to pretend he has been somewhere else.

What I carried and what it fetched

Figures as the house gave them at the third hour, set above what I entered on the Quay. The left column is the figure a man carries. The right is the figure he brings home, which is never the same figure and is never the same coin.

Article — Entered at Vhal — Given in Sere — What I set on the back

Cliffs glass, a sheet, green — 2 marks at the ebb — 4 crowns — Crowns. They do not change on the Quay.

Sallow salt, a sack — 3 marks — 1 crown and a promise — The promise is not current. I entered it anyway.

Tallow-wasp resin, a nest — 4 marks — 4 crowns, to a bowl-surgeon — A surgeon, not a nest. I have set both.

Rope, tarred, a coil — 1 mark — 2 crowns — The tar came home with the figure, on my hands.

Dried fish, a string — 1 bit — not taken — A country that has fish will not pay for fish.

Keldish iron, a bar — 2 marks at Greybank — 3 crowns — The bar stood in four hands. The crowns stood in none.

A coarse bowl out of Nine Pots — 4 bits — 1 crown, for the curiosity — It cracked on the second morning at home.

Red wine, a jar, coming north — 2 marks at the Quay — — — Salt on the rim, and no charge for the salt.

In the leaning counting-hand of a clerk of the house, written in thin gold: against the glass row he set “market rate, current”; against the bowl row, “inspection fee received.” Underneath, in mine, The bowl cracked and the fee stands. There is always a hand, and a hand is what the gold leaves out.

Twelve bits to the silver mark and twenty marks to the gold crown. The Court books in embers and will tell a man an ember is worth five marks; no man I have ever met has exchanged one for the other, south or north. I left the embers off. A column with nothing in it is an honest column, and there is no column at all for the promise.

The courts, and the four bowls

At the feast of a house of the Ten Bowls I counted four bowls set on a board. I asked which of them was mine, having been told I was a customer and therefore welcome. They said the third, and that the fourth was not anybody’s and would be carried away full when the feasting was done. A sailor counts what is carried off a ship. I counted four and could not make them balance, and said so, and was answered with great courtesy in a sentence that had the shape of an answer and no figure in it.

Three went round and were poured. The fourth stood where it stood and was carried away still full, and nobody poured it and nobody said whose debt it held. I have set the bowl in the margin where a figure should be, and the gold clerk has set a figure beside it, and the two have not touched.

The burning

At the end of a feast the debt-slates of one household, chosen by lot, go into the fire. New slates are written before the wine is cold. A woman whose slate burned wept, and the company was kind to her, and then applauded the new one.

I asked a clerk which of the two accounts was the true one. He said yes. When I asked which he meant, he said measure for measure and went back to his wine. In Vhal a debt goes damp and is gone, and everyone knows it is gone. Here they burn it in front of everyone and write it again, and everyone knows it is back. I have carried both kinds of paper. The damp one is the honest one, and I have never heard either of them called a kindness by anybody who had paid it.

The people who are bowls

A thin northerner is a curiosity in Sere. I was asked to stand at a feast after the third bowl and was given three copper bits for standing, which I have not entered. They are in the tin with the crowns, where no figure is asked for. A man of that house called it an honour and a living, and said it without laughing.

Slaves there are called bowls. A slave is given a clay-name by the household and the old name is forbidden; in some houses the forbidden name is written on the inside of a bowl which the slave is never allowed to look into. A beautiful house-slave may be given a whole return and exhibited as proof of the owner’s generosity. A slave who runs is returned thin, shown at a feast, and set to the grain mills. There is a door to the room where the numbers of a house are read. A woman of that house may own the bowls and the slaves and her husband’s debt; she may not go through the door. I looked at it. It was an ordinary door, with ordinary wood in it, and I have thought about that door more than I have thought about the fires.

The bowl-surgeons buy the resin and set grafts, and a grafted person may be shown at a feast and given small coins. What a person is called in law and what a person is called at the table are two different names, and a visitor who joins them is told that there is no rudeness in Sere, only a person who has not yet been told. I was told, and have written it as it was said, with the end of the sentence thrown away, which is how those sentences are spoken.

A song from the press

Taken down at a wine-press outside the Bowl of Sere, while the tread was going. The third verse is said to the bowl and not to the company. The singer would not give it while a clerk of the house stood by, so we went and stood at the press.

We tread the press and the wine comes warm,

a jar for the north and a jar for the fire;

the slate goes on at the third hour

and the fourth goes up to the shelf.

The wind comes yellow and takes the lip;

the grain stands where the lip was bare;

a crowd may burn what a crowd has written

and clap at the writing that is not there.

Salt went south and came back a crown;

glass went south and came back a look;

ask the bowl which of them it poured

and the bowl will keep its answer.

What the borrowed hands did

Three hands have been at these leaves besides mine.

  • The clerk of the house, in thin gold, took the verbs out. Where I wrote a man carried it he wrote delivery recorded; where I wrote they burn a slate he wrote instrument discharged by lot; where I wrote a woman wept he wrote affect observed, not entered. I put my words back underneath. A verb is the difference between a thing that happened to somebody and a thing that happened in a counting room.
  • The Council clerk at Vhal, to whom I showed the perished wine contract, wrote instrument void by exposure; no claim lies. He is right in the language of the pans. The wine was drunk and the paper is gone and there is nobody to ask. I left his line above mine. It is the only line in this account that I did not wish to argue with.
  • The factor’s clerk at Greybank, in the narrow upright hand, set figures of account, not of price against my table, and put a round nought in the margin beside the fourth bowl. He has never been south and has strong views about dust. A nought is a figure; the bowl was not, and nobody has poured it.

At the head and in the gutter

The white at the head is salt bloom off the dockets and comes and goes with the weather. The tar at the fold is mine, from the tin; I have never got it out of the crease and have stopped trying. The yellow in the sewing is the burning wind, ground in at the fold on the road down; it is the colour of the dust in a court and I should rather it stayed. The wine-ring is small and belongs to the third hour. The olive thumb is mine; I turn the leaves with the hand I carry the bowl with. The myrrh came off a fold of cloth in a court where a man had been cremated the night before, and the family had bought back the ash at the price they were asked, and the house was ruined by degrees and called generous. I put my hand on the cloth because a woman there put hers there first. I have not washed the smell out and have not written what I think of it. The green bead is Cliffs glass, pressed on me by a girl at the furnace for a bit and a promise I have not kept. The crown between the fourth and fifth leaves is the only coin in this book nobody has tried to enter; it will not change on the Quay. The grain husk in the gutter came from a loaf sent north and left uneaten. The scorched corner is where the leaf lay near the court fire. The bare square is the word I scraped out with a knife and have not written back. I have not forgotten it either.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

Three are poured and the fourth stands;

the south keeps nothing and writes it down.

I carried paper the rain could undo

and came home with gold that the rain could not.

Ask the bowl what it poured. Ask the door.

I asked the clerk of the house, the woman at the press, and a factor come down from Greybank, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The leaves go back into the tin, into the pocket the strap has worn. Tomorrow I take my book to the tide-tally house and enter what I paid for salt, on paper made to rot, and the Council will call it a virtue, and by evening it will be a debt that no man can prove. I have travelled three hundred leagues and I have never seen that art done better than in the south, where they burn the proof in front of everybody and applaud.
