I keep the last-bed slate at the gate of the Bone Orchard, a league out of Sill by the north road. A household sends for the collector when the breathing has come down to a matter of watching, and a boy comes up with a pot under his coat to be broken at the door; I read what is below to whoever is left standing before the tube is unwrapped. The ordinance is in Ledgerhand, and there is nobody in any of its sentences. I put the people back underneath, because a death with nobody in it is a death nobody died, and that is the only kind this Court has ever wanted entered. What follows is Title the Tenth, copied out from the book in Slate Row so that a house may know what is being entered against it.

At the head of the first leaf, in mine, written small: Copied at the gate and for the gate. The verbs are mine. The absence of them is not. A leaf with a hand at the edge and a hand in the gutter is the only kind a household believes.

Title the Tenth, as it stands at the gate

From the Ordinances of the Keeping, Title the Tenth: Of the Last Bed, the Drawing at the Mouth, and the Breaking of the Pot at the Door. Confirmed to the factor at Greybank by the treaty of Y.H. 418; set down after the Rising of Y.H. 668; added to in Y.H. 782 and in the Thin Year, Y.H. 812.

I. Of the bed and the asking

  1. No measure is drawn from a vessel that has breathed its last. The collector asks while breath remains to answer, the answer is entered, and the tube is not unwrapped until the entry is made.
  2. Where the vessel cannot speak, the house answers. The answer is entered to the house and not to the person. The house is not asked whether it understood the question.
  3. The words of the Return are said by the house, and the question is put after them. The answer is entered before the breathing is counted.
  4. The question is put once. A second asking is a fresh entry and is entered as fresh, whether the first answer was given or not.
  5. Consent at the bed is entered as elsewhere provided in Title the Fourth. The clip is not set where a vessel is past feeling it.

Margin: Beneath article 1, in mine: the vessel answers. A woman held her mother’s hand and said a sentence that was not an answer. I have entered what the collector wrote and set the sentence at the edge, the length of it. A sentence that is not an answer has no column, and I have no column that will hold it.

II. Of the tube and the drawing

  1. The measure is drawn at the mouth, where the last of the Warm goes out with the breath. The tube is of cold iron and is wrapped in wool; the wool is not entered.
  2. The drawing is made by the collector and not by the house. The hand that holds the tube is not the hand that writes.
  3. The measure taken is entered in embers and not in marks. Marks are for the living.
  4. The figure is one in twelve, as the notice says, and is not changed for the hour, the bed, or the words spoken in the room.
  5. What is drawn is not returned. What is returned is bought.

Margin: I asked a collector’s man why the tube is wrapped in wool when the iron is the thing that draws. He said the wool is for the hand that holds it, and the ordinance has no place for such a hand.

III. Of the whole and the thin

  1. A whole return keeps the measure intact and brings back the vessel with its memory. It is booked at twelve hundred marks and forty unspent measures.
  2. The forty are not found; they are made in the counting. No collector is asked to produce them.
  3. A thin return pools the measures of many vessels and divides them. Nothing comes back that can name the house it came from. The district pays thirty marks, in labour where it has no coin.
  4. The name written at the bank is not the name spoken at the bed. No house is asked what it would call the child.
  5. An account is not closed by the drawing. It is carried forward, and the dead are kept on the roll.
  6. Quiet ash is not given at the bed. The wanting of quiet is not entered.

Margin: A factor come up from Greybank asked whether a whole return could be made to remember a face. I told him the ordinance makes no provision for faces. He said that was a pity and went back down the road. I have set both sentences here; only one can be entered.

IV. Of the pot at the door

  1. A pot is broken at the door of the death-house when the drawing is done, so that the heat that was in the house goes out with the shard and is not counted against the household. The pot is broken by the household and not by the collector.
  2. It is broken after the tube is wrapped and before the slate is carried away. A pot broken before the drawing lets the heat out of the account as well as out of the house, which is a loss the Court does not provide for.
  3. The shard is not swept until the Last Day. Where a shard is swept the same morning, the house is asked who swept it, and the answer is entered to the house.
  4. No figure is entered for the pot. A pot is a piece of clay, and the heat that was in it is not a measure.

Margin: I keep a count of the pots broken at this gate in charcoal on the inside of the cover, and it is not the count on the slate. On Ash 12 two pots went out of one house and one measure went up the road. I have not joined the two counts. A man who joins them will ask where the second heat went, and there is no column for the answer.

V. Of faults and their correcting

Fault — As entered at the gate — What the clerk sets beneath

A verb in the ordinance — The article copied again, in the same hand — The person who did the thing, restored

The question put twice — Second asking, entered fresh — The moment the house wanted, not entered

A sentence at the bed that is not an answer — Not entered — The sentence, at the edge, the length of it

A pot broken before the drawing — Loss entered to the house — The heat that went out with the shard

A shard swept before the Last Day — The house asked who swept — The hand that swept, and why it did

A factor’s figure written over the forty — The figure as the notice has it — The forty as they were made in the counting

The word that must not be written — Not entered; the place left bare — The score in the lower margin, cut with the tube

  1. A fault copied twice in one tend is a fault the clerk has not understood; the senior clerk sits with him until he has. The pot is not a rod. It is a piece of clay with a place where a house stood.

Copied from the slate at the gate. The figures are the Court’s. The last column is mine and has no standing at any Weighing.

The form of the last-bed entry

Set out at the end of Title the Tenth. I have copied it with the blanks as they stand. In nine years no household has asked to have the last line read aloud.

Entered this day, being ---day of -------, in the year of the Hearth ------, the drawing of one measure from ---------------------- of ----------------------, asked once while breath remained, answered by the house where the vessel could not answer, the tube of cold iron set to the mouth, the figure one in twelve as the notice says, the measure entered in embers and not in marks, the return ----------------------, the name written at the bank not the name spoken at the bed, and the pot broken at the door after the tube was wrapped; written by ----------------------, whose hand was not the hand that held the tube. The pot-breaking verse

Said low at the threshold by whoever is left standing, after the shard goes on the step. It is not sung. The fourth line is said to the pot.

Clay to clay and the door to the cold,

break it here where the heat would go;

one goes up in a slate and one goes out in a shard,

and the house keeps the shape of the thing it had.

Iron at the mouth and a mark at the wrist,

the figure goes up and the name goes down;

what was said at the bed is not in the book,

and the pot keeps the place where the house stood.

Break it after the tube, not before;

a pot broken early lets the account out too.

Six go up and seven go out,

and nobody asks where the second heat went.

In the narrow upright hand, against the last line: One measure entered; no discrepancy arises. In mine: There are seven pots and six measures, and he has put a figure where I put a pot. A man does not number what he has not stood at a door. Both stand.

What the two borrowed hands did

The College man came down from Slate Row with ink in two fingers of a cuff gone soft from washing. He struck the person out of article 6 and the house answers out of article 2, setting the drawing is made and answer is entered above them, both true of the slate and false of the room. I have put mine back underneath.

The other hand is a factor’s from Greybank, brown at the edge with a little gold in it. Against the forty unspent measures he wrote stock, no deduction arises, and against a whole return, article of value. I have not struck either through. A hand with gold in it can call a return an article; a hand that held the tube at a mouth calls it a person going out of a room. Both stand.

At the head and in the gutter

The grey at the head is Ash, off my own thumb turning the leaf toward the lamp, and it does not come out of the creases. The tallow beside it is off the watch candle, kept low so a household would not know I was writing. The ring at the second fold is the bottom of a pot set down while I read article 17 to a man who had not yet broken it; the clay has left a pale circle the shape of a thumb. The iron in the gutter is filings from a tube-wrap, shaken out when a collector laid it here to go for a cup. The pale streak along the edge is slip from a swaddling cloth. The green bead is lead glaze chipped from a bowl out of Sere; the thread of grey wool through the tie-hole is mine, tied there the year I wrote a word I was told to scrape out, and I have never untied it. The tide-line along the foot is water from the washing of the step after a pot was broken; it has taken the corners off two lines without taking the sense. At the Last Day the houses sweep their corners and burn what they find and call it dealt with. This gate is not a house, and the shard stays where it fell.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The slate keeps the measure. The door keeps the pot. The gutter keeps the sentence, and nobody is asked to enter it.

I asked a collector’s man, a woman with a pot under her coat, and a clerk from Slate Row, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written.

The slate goes back on its nail. Tomorrow a boy will come up with a pot under his coat and a collector behind him with the tube in its wool, and I shall read what stands here, put the verbs back where another hand took them out, and ask the question once. Someone will say something that is not an answer. The pot will go on the step, and I shall write the figure and leave a place the length of it. That is my trade: to enter what was drawn, and keep in the gutter what was only breathed.
