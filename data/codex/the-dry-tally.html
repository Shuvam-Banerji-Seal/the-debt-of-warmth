I buy eels on the marsh road and weigh peat at the dry, and I keep the tally under my apron. A figure named aloud in this country grows legs. It walks to Greybank before the man who said it has got his boots dry, and comes back wearing somebody else’s debt. I say mine into the apron, to the creel, to the board under my foot; said looking at a person it becomes a bargain, and I have made enough bargains to know which of them I wanted.

The stick is the honest witness. A notch is still there when the paper has gone soft. Fen Cant is not written, so I set what I can in the south’s letters, learned off an eel-trader who could tally and could not read what he wrote. Where a word will not come I tie a knot in the margin and cut a notch in the edge. A notch is not a letter and a knot is not a figure. Both keep longer than a name.

Nothing here is free. A thing that costs nobody in a marsh has usually cost somebody who cannot write.

In the narrow upright hand of a factor’s clerk out of Greybank, written above my first line: Coin and reckoning entered. Ember: five marks. Underneath, in mine: He has written an ember as though a man could carry one up the marsh road in his boot. I have carried peat in my boot and never an ember. He takes the verbs out because a thing always true has no need of anybody in it. I put them back because a woman standing in water to her calf wants to know who is doing the keeping.

Twelve copper bits to a silver mark; twenty silver marks to a gold crown. Crowns come north with the Sere factor and will not change at Greybank, so a man takes the eels and leaves the crown, which is neither generosity nor an insult but the only arithmetic the marsh has ever understood. The Court books in embers and calls five marks an ember; no man has ever exchanged one, and I have left no column for them on my board.

The market at the dry

What things fetched at the east-march dry between the last market of Stone and the Opening of the Slates. The left figure is what a man is told at Sill or the quay. The right is what is taken here, with the water still on the boots of the man who brought it.

Article — As told below — As taken here

Peat, a barrow at the dry — 2 bits in Grain, 5 in Stone — 1 bit in Grain, 3 in Stone; dear again at Greybank

Reed bundle, an armful, at the Reed Cut — not on any board — ask the arm that carried it; it knows weight and not cost

Hemp, a hank, from the east cut — not cried — 2 bits, and the hank goes soft if it has lain

Eels, a basket at the boards — 3 bits smoked, 2 salted — 2 smoked, 1 salted, named under the apron and never the same twice

Salt, a sack, up from the pans by Greybank — 3 marks at the quay — 4 marks; the extra mark is the road and nobody calls it a tax

Dried leeches, a dozen — 3 bits at the Fishstairs — 4 bits here, no questions; no slate asks where they came from

Grey, a phial, gathered and bottled at the dry — not sold in Grath — 4 bits, no name; the Court seals the same grey and sells it for sixty

Thin return, bought from a household — 30 marks, paid by the district — 10 marks, in peat and iron and labour, which is a third and still a return

Bought breath, a season — not spoken of below — paid in iron or in a term of peat; entered in months, not in marks

A child’s first bundle, cut in the dark at the Reed Cut — no figure — no figure; the child is not yet of the bog and goes again

Margin: The peat row at Greybank stands at five bits in Stone and the same barrow is three here. The carter who pays both says the coal did not change and the road did. I have not set his words on the board.

Margin: I scraped a word I wrote beside the grey row and left the bare square where it was, which is the length of it.

Paid in kind, and not entered

These stand on the board with a space where the figure should be. The factor’s clerk has more than once put a nought in the space. A nought is a figure and can be carried away; the things below cannot, which is why the marsh keeps them.

What passes — What is given — What I write

A knot of grey wool tied for a debt — untied when the debt is paid — the knot, in kind; a knot has no ear and no rate

A count-stick cut at the dry — a notch for each barrow — the notch, which the rain does not take out

A name said at home before the collector comes — said into the ground beside the hut — nothing; a name at home is a door and not a figure

A reed-name said three times to the ground — once to the child, once to the pot, once under the boards — nothing; the third saying is for the child who cannot yet say it

A gold crown that will not change at Greybank — left in the coat and carried home — nothing; a thing nobody can spend is a thing somebody has paid for

The dues of the roads

There are no roads in the Fen, only ways trodden long enough to be called roads by people who do not have to walk them. What follows is what the reeve’s man takes, or the factor’s, or the water. The difference between the two is the whole art of crossing a marsh.

The marsh road, east to Greybank

Peat and eels go up it and salt and iron come back, and the factor’s clerk arrives with his soft cuffs. A cart makes it slowly in Silt and not at all when the high way is under.

Due

Cart with a load — 2 bits at the dry, 2 bits more at the quay

Beast on the hoof — 1 bit

Foot passenger — nothing on a dry day; 1 bit in Silt, when there is a bank to walk on

A drove of bog-cows — 4 bits, and the hides counted twice, once at each end

The boardwalks to the Standing Post

The boards bear ten thousand feet and will bear a man’s if he puts his foot where the others went. Outsiders are put up on the walk and are not told which boards are safe, which is not cruelty but information nobody here has had reason to sell.

Due

A dry foot — 1 bit to the reeve’s man

A wet foot — a marked stick; a notch and no figure

A man who stands where he was shown to stand — nothing; the board minds him

A man from the Court asking which boards bear — 2 marks, paid before the question, not returned with the answer

The dry ways and the cuts

No gate stands at any of them. Where the cart goes twice, the peat is counted twice, which the factor calls a load and the cut calls weather.

Due

Barrow going out to a cut — no gate; weighed at the dry

The same barrow weighed again at Greybank — 2 bits, which is the second weighing and not a second barrow

A drainage cut — no due; the fever that comes up after it is not entered

A ferry over a cut — 1 bit for the boat, nothing for the water

The road over the Quiet Acre

North toward Mud End and Grath the way runs through the ground the Court calls the Empty Cantons and we call the Quiet Acre: wells with no hands at them and doorways in walls nobody lives behind. No reeve sits on that road and no due is asked, which is not the same thing as a road being free.

Due

Cart, beast or foot — nothing at any post, there being no post

A slate carried across the Acre — no fee; the figure arrives where the slate arrives

Margin: A due is a figure somebody will stand behind and this road has nobody on it.

The Peat Line, south

Sere’s northern advance stopped at the Line in Y.H. 634 and the reeves have paid tribute in eels ever since. A man goes down a neighbour and is counted a bowl when he gets there, and the crowns come back north and will not change at Greybank.

Due

Tribute in eels, at the Line — a basket of eels, paid by the reeves, entered by nobody

A man going south with a hide — 1 bit at the Acre end, and whatever the bowl asks at the other

Gold crown carried south — no due; the bowl takes it by emptying it

A man coming back from the Line — no due and no guarantee

The smuggler’s path, and the winter way

Due

The path through the reedbed — no due and no guarantee; a man comes back with wet boots and no figure

The bog when it bears ice — nothing; no reeve will stand on it

The post at the Standing Post — no gate, only a board and a post cut with reeves’ marks

What no road charges

These are not dues. No slate takes them and no factor enters them, and they are the reason a book like this has to be kept.

The mire-fever comes up after a drainage cut and burns a household in turns; a silt-leach draws as a collector’s tube draws, without the slate; a foot gone dark inside three turns of wool belongs to the woman who cannot stop working for it; a bought breath has a hook in the mouth that keeps a person from the word she would need to complain of it. No slate will stand behind these, and they are the only charges a household always meets.

The song the dry-keepers carry

Sung low through the nose on the marsh road, with the last word dropped into the mud and not finished. Never sung at the dry while the factor’s clerk is in hearing of the second part.

Twelve to the mark and twenty to the crown,

the crown stays north and the eel goes down;

a bit for the board and a stick for the wet,

and the dry foot pays what the dry foot gets.

A barrow at the dry and a barrow at the bank,

the factor counts twice and the reeve counts once;

a knot in the wool and a notch in the stick,

and the name said at home where the slate cannot pick.

Four for the boards and a fifth for the slack,

which is a place and not a toll to pay;

the Line takes an eel and the Court takes a figure,

and the mud takes the rest and wants no slate.

What the two borrowed hands did

The factor’s clerk strikes the verbs out. Where I write the marsh takes, he writes was taken, as though a cold had no hand in it. I put my words back underneath. A figure with nobody in it is a figure that will be paid twice.

The Sere factor writes in ink with a little gold in it and calls everything a rate: seasonal rate against the peat, market rate against the eels, figures where I had put an apron. I left both standing. A house of Sere has never stood in water to its calf at three in the morning.

At the head and in the gutter

The brown at the head is peat and I know it from Ash the way a woman knows her own debt from another’s: both stick to the hand, and one stays in the house after you have gone. I have not washed it off. The tideline took two words clean off the second leaf; I have not written them back, because a tally with two words gone is a tally said slowly, which is how everything here is said. The eel slime at the fore-edge is mine, from holding the leaf with the hand that held the creel. The fish-hook nick came off a man paying for six eels and charged for five, or the other way, which he never settled and I never entered. The press of sedge is from the morning we lay flat in the cut until a beast’s pushing had gone past. The knot through the hole holds a word that will not go into the south’s letters; I will not untie it. The small pale round place is where a collector’s cuff was laid while I told a girl to eat and she would not. There is no Last Day under a loose board at the dry.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The tally keeps what was weighed. The knot keeps what was owed. The road keeps the boot and gives back the man, or a thinner one.

I asked a reeve, an eel-trader come up from the quay, and a woman whose term is not out, and had three answers and no hand.

The leaf goes back into the sacking under the board. Tomorrow the carts come east with salt and a Sere factor with gold he cannot spend, and a number named under an apron will by evening be a thing that was true. I shall set it beside the figure they told and leave the space between them as it is.
