I keep the Slack Tide on the Quay. It is a different work from keeping a tally, though both begin with the hand on the board and end with a figure somebody wants to carry away. In the cold months the east wind holds, the Sallow skins over at the edges, and the galleys lie up on the pulled benches with their oars in, and there is nothing to do of an evening but ask one another what we already know and call the asking a riddle. A question with an answer nobody knows is a bet, and there are men on this Quay who would bet on which way the wind will sit by morning.

The door-post is tarred and the chalk shows on it. I score the night there and take the score off in the morning with a wet rope-end, which is the only erasure this house has ever needed. Nothing is carried away from the Slack Tide on a slate. A man who wants a thing entered can go up to the Council room and sit with people warmer than himself, and the paper there will rot before he gets home, which the Council calls a virtue.

The rules, as the house keeps them

  1. A riddle is put once. Ask for it again and you owe a quart, because the second asking is for your own pleasure and not for the post.
  2. Answer in one breath. A second answer is a different riddle and is not heard.
  3. Five are chalked. A sixth may be put and is not scored, being a thing said from the door and not from a stool.
  4. The house judges. I say whether an answer serves and I do not say why. A reason given is a riddle given away for nothing, and the giver can go home and sell it.
  5. No paper lies on the board. A contract left here overnight is a debt the weather has settled, and this house does not settle debts for the Council.
  6. A quart of small ale is set down before the first question and no one lifts it until the fifth. If a white bloom comes up on the board before then, the night’s answering will go badly. I have never known this to fail and I would not say so before a dipper.

The five that were chalked

The first went to a woman who holds a twelfth of a boat through her mother and will not say the fraction before company. She had her answer before the verse was fairly out, which is why she never takes the quart and is always asked first.

I go from mother to daughter and never by buying;

a man may come aboard and hold me in his hand,

and still his word upon the boat is counted half;

I am not the hull, and I am not the sea.

What am I?

“A share,” she said. “And the half is his opinion, not mine.” I gave her the quart and she drank standing with her sleeve over the rim, because she had been told not to spill. Nobody asked her how many twelfths she held, which is the proper way to ask nothing in this house.

The second went to Kett of the Quay, who reads the draws at the tide-tally house and keeps his cuffs dry by not touching anything.

I am set down to be kept and I keep by going;

the wetter the room, the less I owe;

the Council has made a virtue of my dying

and hired a man in a clean coat to write it.

What am I?

“Salt-paper,” he said, without looking up from his tally, which is how he answers everybody. The house allowed it. A man who reads the draws all day does not need to look at a person to know what that person has brought in.

The third was put to a dipper come in off the pans with salt dried white in the creases of her neck.

I run against the wind and am blessed before light;

the Council is surprised by me every year;

they tax me in a figure I never carried.

What runs seaward and is counted by men who never saw it?

“The draw,” she said, and said it to the pot and not to Kett, because a dipper does not argue with the tide-tally house before company. She took no quart. Dippers are paid by the pan-master, who pays in the weather and never in ale.

The fourth went to a boat-master who had lost a store below the waterline and had come back to sit down again, which not every man does after such a loss.

I knock where no hand can reach, below the waterline;

I take the store and leave the rope behind;

the nets are blessed at dawn and I come all the same.

What taps a hull and takes what the hull was holding?

“A salt-thing,” he said, and set the quart down again without drinking, which in this house is how a man says he has already paid for the answer. There was an argument about whether a blessing that does not keep a store is a blessing at all. I stopped it by touching the post, because there is no winning an argument about a blessing and there is only the thing that came in the night and the store that is not in the boat.

The fifth I put myself, because the room had grown noisy and wanted quieting, and a riddle put by the house is not scored against anybody.

I steal the warm and do not eat it;

I go up the wind with a coal under my crop;

no tally in this town stands one mark the poorer.

What carries the fire out of the account?

An old woman who mends nets said “a brine-gull” and said it low, the way people say things they have said to the fire at home. I passed her the pot. She set it down again and told me a gull had taken a coal off her brazier the week before and she had not gone after it, because a thing that goes up the wind with your coal has already been paid and there is nobody to charge. That is the best answer I have heard in nine winters and the only one that cost the teller nothing by telling it.

The sixth, which was put from the door

Toward the end of the night a girl came to the door and did not step over it. There was salt dried white on her shins and at the hem of her coat, as if she had come up off the pans and walked the Salt Road without putting her shoes back on, which nobody does who owns a pair of shoes. She asked whether anybody in the house had seen the tide she named at the Salt Walk. Nobody said yes, which is a way of saying no that costs nobody anything. Then she put the sixth:

I was named at fourteen with my feet in the pan

and written in salt where the dippers could read;

by noon there was no word, and the pan kept nothing;

I have carried the shape of it all winter, not the name.

What am I?

Kett said, “A tide not yet seen.” The girl said that was what the dippers wrote in the salt and asked what the house called it. The room went the way a room goes when everybody has the answer in their mouths and nobody will be the one to say it, which is the same in Vhal as in any town with a Council. She waited a little. “Then leave it where it is,” she said, and went back into the wind. Where she stood there was a small drift of salt on the boards. In the morning it was gone, and I have not swept the place where it lay, because the wind does the sweeping on this Quay and always has.

The score, in chalk on the tarred post

Question — Put by — Answer given — Score

The share — the woman with the twelfth — a share — one, drunk

The paper — Kett of the Quay — salt-paper — one, allowed

The draw — a dipper — the draw — one, to the pot

The tap — the boat-master — a salt-thing — one, not drunk

The coal — the house — a brine-gull — one, given low

The named tide — a girl at the door — none served — not scored

The Council’s figure for the draw went up on the tally board that morning and came down by noon, which is what the Council’s figures do. It went over the post with the ale and nobody looked at it twice, which is how figures go in this house.

What the clerk did

A clerk of the Council borrowed the leaf on a morning when the till was being counted and his cuff was soft at the edge from the damp, which is the only way a Council cuff ever gets soft. He set the riddles down as queries entered and the answers as responses entered, and put a figure of marks against each of the five, which was more than I had scored, because the house scores in whether a person sits down again and he does not. Against the sixth he wrote no answer tendered; struck. He asked who had put it. I told him a girl at the door. He asked for the girl’s name, and I said she had given none, which is a true thing and not the thing he wanted, and he wrote party unidentified; interest in a tide not entered.

  • Against a share, and the half is his opinion, not mine, he wrote one-half interest, contingent.
  • At the salt-paper he wrote instrument voided by damp, and underlined it twice, which is what the Council does when it wants a thing to look like a mercy.
  • Against the draw he wrote current taxed at source, which is true in a language nobody on this Quay speaks unless they are being paid to.
  • At the salt-thing he wrote loss not entered, and put a round nought in the place where the store had stood.
  • Against the brine-gull he wrote no value transferred, which is the only one of his answers I have ever wanted to keep.

I have put my words under his and left his standing, the way a tarred post keeps the chalk under the salt. He has entered five and struck the sixth, and the sixth was not struck: it went out on the wind with the girl and is still going.

From the boat-master

At the foot of the scored leaves, in the blunt hand of the man who had lost his store and sat down again:

A man’s word on a boat is counted at half. His loss is entered whole.

He says he wrote it about the salt-thing and not about his wife, and I have let him say so. A man who has married aboard is allowed one sentence in a book he did not write, which is more than the Council allows him anywhere else.

At the head and in the margins

The tar thumb at the head is mine. I held the leaf down on the Quay in the wind and did not know I had marked it; tar goes into the grain and will not come out, which is why the Council room uses it for a seal and this house uses it for nothing. The salt bloom along the gutter came up out of the paper on the third night, when I carried the leaf inside my coat and the coat crusted white at the fold. I have not brushed it off. A bloom written by the weather is the only writing in Vhal that a clerk cannot strike. The press of frozen rope across the first leaf came off the man who held the door while the girl spoke, and I have not written which man, because he would stop holding doors if I did. The green bead in the lower corner came off a string of cliff-glass a carrier left on the till; it has the colour of the Glaze Cliffs after rain and I have kept it where it fell. The fish-hook nick is from a hook set in the edge by a man paying for his quart, and he did not apologize, which is how hooks are paid for here. The coal-smudge near the fifth question is from the brazier, where the leaf lay too close while the net-mender was telling us about her gull; the coal was ours and the answer was not. The grey print along the outer fold is not mine and nobody has claimed it. Nothing on this Quay is swept until the wind does it, so the print is still there.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The pan keeps the shape and the sea keeps the name;

neither gives back what was made in it.

I asked the woman with the twelfth, Kett and the boat-master, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The leaf goes back in the tin and the chalk goes off the post with a wet rope-end, and in the morning the Council clerk will come for the till and find nothing in it that rots. Next winter the galleys will lie up again and the east wind will hold, and I shall set five questions on the post and leave the sixth standing at the door, for whoever it is to come in out of the wind or not, as they please.
