I count at the mouth and keep the keys, and have done it nineteen years. My father kept them before me. He went down at thirteen and came up with a different number in his arm, and the Warden gave him the keys instead of the pick, which is what Keld calls a kindness. He put them in my hand the next morning and said nothing about the arm. I have never found a figure for that morning.

A man from the Court came up the Rim Road with a soft cuff and asked what we said to a boy going down. I told him. He wrote it with the doing taken out and a figure put where a place should stand, and went off believing he had entered a trade. So I have set the words as they are said and put the doing back.

Say these low, to the rope, the stone, the iron and the count, and to a person last. Said looking at a person, a Keldish saying becomes a checking, and there is checking enough at the gate without carrying any up the shaft. Say the figure before the word, even in a sentence. A sentence that begins with a name is a sentence a man can argue with, and Keld has no appetite for arguments at the mouth.

Of the number, said before the name

  1. “Figure first. Name after. The name will keep.”

Gloss — At the gate, in the Warden’s hall, and here at the mouth. A lowlander who gives his name first is sent back to the foot of the post to begin again. I am not shaming him. A name is a thing a man can change at the next well; a number goes down with him and comes up as the mountain made it.

  1. “Say it once. The mountain keeps no second figure.”

Gloss — To say a number twice is to ask the stone to make it other than it was. The reeve writes down what he heard first and has never taken a figure back. A boy who corrects himself at the mouth will be checked, and a checked boy is entered in red.

In the narrow upright hand, against this saying: The measure is declared and verified. Underneath, in mine: He has put a clerk’s two words where I put a warning. Declared and verified is true of a figure and says nothing to the boy who dared not say his twice. I have put the mouth back.*

  1. “A thin man carries two counts. Only one of them is in the skin.”

Gloss — When a boy comes up with a number the mountain gave him, the old numerals are cut out and new ones cut in, and the earlier ones stand in the pale pucker round about. Nobody asks which count is his. The reeve enters the later. My father’s arm read like a slate with a correction nobody had agreed to, and the keys went into my hand the next morning.

Of the stone and what it keeps

  1. “Warm-stone keeps what was put into it and tells nobody who paid.”

Gloss — A block out of the Ninth holds its heat longer than anything else in Sarn and goes south for ten marks, and no man at the shaft asks what the gallery gave for it. A boy is given a shaving after the knife, and it is set against a newborn’s back up the terrace. Neither has a figure. I have kept the shaving in this fold thirteen years and never lent it.

  1. “The mountain sweats before it gives. Smell the lead and come up.”

Gloss — In the deep shafts the Warm comes up through the stone in a lethal flood, and the only warning is the smell of hot lead, ahead of the heat. A block nearly out is a wage not yet paid, which is why a man goes on cutting after he has smelled it. The reeve stops the shift anyway, and counts the lives going up against the iron coming down; the two never agree.

  1. “The Court’s fire is a candle lit from ours.”

Gloss — We say the true Hearth lies beneath this mountain and the flame behind the grating in the Hearthseat is a candle lit from it. The Court has never accepted the claim and has never bothered to argue it, which is a kind of answer. A man from Greybank wrote *unverified above this line. I have put the candle back.*

  1. “The mountain keeps its own. We do not go in after them.”

Gloss — The dead are walled into the mine facing the deep seam. The surveyors add a number to a slate nobody reads aloud, and the family is paid in iron. We do not say which part of them the mountain keeps. There is no broken pot at a Keldish door and no Last Day; there is a wall, a number, and a payment in iron.

Of the iron and the hand

  1. “One bite holds the vessel whole. One bite takes the hand. It is the same iron.”

Gloss — The clamp is mercy and mutilation in one cold bite. Held short, it stops the Warm from taking a man; held past the turning of the watch, it leaves the arm below the colour of old rope and it does not come back. The iron has never asked which mercy was meant. A cutter at the Floor told me this in four words and went back to cleaning his clamp.

  1. “Tap before you cut. A flaw answers dry.”

Gloss — A sound block answers full under the knuckle; one with a hair in it gives back a small dry sound. The same goes for an arm. A boy who cuts across a flaw he did not tap is sent to the wall to learn the note, and the wall does not answer him.

  1. “A way is a door cut in the wall of a man. It does not shut again.”

Gloss — The first way is cut across a surface way in the forearm and held open while it scars. It aches before the thaw and after. A fortnight is given to the arm, and a useless arm is not sent to the rack to hurry the scar. I have known a master to do it anyway, and pay for the hand with the life.

  1. “The slate keeps the honest answer. The palm keeps the burn.”

Gloss — Trial of hot iron is put to the palm when a man is to be trusted with the racks. He holds it and says whether he owes, and the honest answer leaves the hand dead. There have been a great many honest men at this Floor with dead hands. We have a column for the answer and none for the hand, and the Order has lived on that for a hundred years.

Struck hard in charcoal against this saying, by a cutter who would not sign it: Mine is dead and I answered true. They entered the honesty and never asked about the hand. In mine, underneath: I have left his line struck above mine and mine standing below, so a boy can see what was said and what was done to it. The charcoal has come off on the fold and I have not brushed it away.*

Of the going down and the keys

  1. “Go down with the number you brought. Come up with what the mountain gives.”

Gloss — At thirteen every Keldish child goes down the First Seam for one day. Some come back with the number changed, and a few do not come back, and the family is paid in iron. The Descent is not a feast and there is no clapping. A mother counts her child going down and coming up, and if the second count is not the first she says nothing, because she has been told what to say and it is not that.

  1. “A man who comes up thin is given the keys. Keep your hand on them.”

Gloss — He cannot descend again, and the keys are put into his hand instead, which is called a kindness. My father held them until the morning he put them in mine. He never went down after and never said why he had come up different. I have not asked the reeve’s slate; it has the later figure and nothing else.

  1. “The deep counts a life at eleven months. The twelfth is the mountain’s.”

Gloss — Condemned men and bought breath under-vessed in the deep seams are reckoned at eleven months. Nobody enters the twelfth and nobody has found it. A new man asks what happens at the end of the eleventh and is told to finish his cut, which is the whole answer the deep has given.

Of the road and the people above it

  1. “A Warden who cannot hold a pass is removed by his own captains. The pass stays.”

Gloss — Karric VII shut the Cold Pass in Y.H. 741 and the border was fixed in 747. There is a survey post and no gate, and our surveyors do not promise to bring a man back from it. The Warden changes. The pass does not. A man who goes past the post with a warm-stone and a figure in his head comes back with one of the two, and sometimes neither.

  1. “We clipped the ends off our words the way we clip the days off a winter. What is left is what was meant.”

Gloss — Keldish is a clipped speech with the number before the noun: forty of head, not capacity; two fingers, gone; one way, open. A lowlander hears rudeness in it and calls it brevity, which is the mistake of a man who calls a clamp an ornament. I have set the words in lowland letters and struck the endings off, so something of the hard edge travels down the road.

The mouth-song

Said at the mouth while the rope goes down and the boy after it, to the rope and not to the boy. One line is held: it is the number the mountain may give, and no singer has ever put a figure in it. Coming up, the song is said backward and the held line held again. A survey-clerk put a round nought in that place once and wrote *vacancy beneath it. A nought is a number. The place is not.*

One for the rope and one for the stone,

one for the count that the boy brought down;

tap it and cut it and hold it true,

and what comes up is the mountain’s own.

One for the shaving, one for the heat,

the pass stands open, the post stands bare,

and the Warden’s name is a thing that changes.

One for the keys and one for the hand,

and one held back where the number goes ---

we do not say it going down,

we do not say it coming up.

Twelve for the bit and mark for the mark,

the iron goes up and the figure goes down;

the slate keeps one and the wall keeps one,

and the rope keeps what it brought back.

Gloss — Children ask what the held line is and are told to ask the rope, an answer given by people who asked the same at their own Descent. My father never sang it. I have left the place the length it wants.

What the borrowed hands did

A survey-clerk of the Court’s factor borrowed the leaf at Greybank, ink in two fingers of a soft cuff, and struck the verbs out as though a verb were a thing a man could carry down a shaft and sell.

  • At the mountain keeps its own, he wrote interment recorded; no return arising, true of a slate and silent about the wall.
  • At the slate keeps the honest answer, he set trial by ordeal; evidentiary value nil, and the hand that held the iron has dropped out of the sentence altogether.
  • At the held line he put a round nought and vacancy. He has entered the place as nothing, which is the only way the Court knows how to enter a place.
  • At the keys, he wrote office transferred on medical grounds. I have put my father’s morning back underneath; there is no column for a morning in any College book.

The mine-reeve set his red checking point against the figure in saying three. I do not know which figure he was checking and have not crossed it out. A reeve who checks a leaf has already decided what it is worth.

At the head and in the gutter

The iron-red at the head came off the reeve’s checking point and will not come out of the grain, which is why I sign with a thumb and not with ink. The coal is mine, worked in from nineteen winters at the mouth. The shaving in the fold keeps its heat on the coldest morning; it has been asked for twice and never lent, and when it goes cold I shall not know who paid for the warmth I had off it. The lead bead came from a man who would not name his gallery, which is the honest way to give a gift in Keld. The filing along the gutter came out of the clamp-rack and I have left it where it fell. The frost-fern went to water before I had made an end of the first saying and I have not written the word back. The red figure in the margin and the grey on the outer fold are not mine and nobody has owned them. I have not swept it. There is no Last Day beneath this Keep, and the dust has nowhere to go.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The stone keeps the number. The wall keeps the man.

The slate keeps neither, and goes up the road.

The keys keep the door, and say nothing about who held them before.

I asked the reeve, a cutter of the Floor, a surveyor come down from the stones, and a woman of the terrace, and had four answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written. The cloth goes back into the tin by the key-rack, and tomorrow a boy will come up from his Descent with a figure in his arm that is not the one he took down, and I shall put the keys in his hand and say the sayings to the rope and not to him.
