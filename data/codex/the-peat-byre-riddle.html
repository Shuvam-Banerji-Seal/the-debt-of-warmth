I keep the byre at Mud End and the fire in it, and the measure we hide peat by, which is a different work from keeping an account, though both begin with the hand on the thing before the eye goes to the person standing there. There are nine houses here and a moot that sits in my byre because there is no other room in the hamlet big enough to hold a disagreement. In Stone the road goes under, the ford goes over, and there is nothing to do of an evening but ask one another what we already know and call the asking a question. A question whose answer nobody knows is a wager, and there is enough wagering in a peat village without a riddle in it.

I have set this down because a man came the next morning and asked whose hide was stacked against my wall, and I could not tell him. That is a thing a byre ought to be able to tell. I have put the tale as he told it and left the clerk’s figure standing above it, because a figure written over a tale does not take the tale away, and a byre with a tale in it keeps warmer than a byre with a tally in it.

The manner of the byre

  1. A question is put to the fire and not to the person beside you. Put to a person it becomes an argument, and an argument in a moot-byre is a debt with no date on it, which a peat house will carry longer than a debt with a date.
  2. Answer in one breath. A second answer is a different question and is not heard. A man who gives two has not understood the first, which is the same in Sill and in the marsh.
  3. The judge is the fire. I say whether an answer serves and I do not say why. A reason given is a question given away for nothing, and the giver can take it down the river road and spend it elsewhere.
  4. The wager is a peat-shaving laid on the measure. We count shavings by the thumb and not by the eye, because the eye listens to the face and the thumb does not.
  5. No slate lies on the bench. The moot has its slate on market-day and takes it away again on market-day; what is said at my fire goes off with the smoke, and neither is carried into a figure.
  6. The cup is set down before the first question and no one lifts it until the last. If a skin forms on it, the night’s answering will go badly. I have known this to fail twice and would not say so before a reeve.

The man off the marsh road

He came in at the going of the light with his boots dry, which a man cannot manage in Stone without having come a long way or else not having come from the weather at all. He would not state what house he was of, which is a thing a man in Grath states before he states his name. He set a peat-shaving on the measure, sat with his cap on his knees, and told us why he was dry.

He had been up to the Standing Post on a wager of a hide of peat. It was seven days into Stone, when the bog had skinned over and the reeves were choosing who had stood longest. He stood. He said the water went off him on the third day and did not come back, and that a man who has stood that long in a bog comes up with less of himself than he went in with, which is how they choose a reeve and why the chosen man is never in a hurry to stand again.

An old woman at the fire said the Fen have a saying for it: a dry man is a dangerous man. He said that was true and that he had heard it said of him before he had done standing, which is the worst way to hear a saying about yourself. Then he put the question. He put it to the fire, having been told twice, and said it as he said it, which is in the marsh speech with the endings left off.

I am cut wet and carried dry.

I am nine by the house and none by the road.

The more of me is spent, the less of me is kept;

the less of me is kept, the warmer you sleep.

The byre counts me. The slate has no column for me.

What am I?

The woman who cuts our peat said “peat” before he had finished the third line, which is why she never wins a shaving and is always first. A carter come up from Sill for the winter lease said “warmth,” and the man from the marsh said warmth was what a man bought with the thing in the riddle and not the thing itself, and the carter said that was a distinction without a difference and went on with his ale. A boy of the house said “a day,” which nobody heard, being said to his mother and not to the fire. The cup went round once and came back to its place.

Then the man said the answer the byre had given was the easy one, and that the true answer was what the bog had kept. He said it low, to the fire.

“This is what came up,” he said, and set his hand on his cap. “What went in is not here to be asked about. I won the hide. They set it against the wall at the Post and told me to take it when I had a house to put it in. I have no house. I put my name into the standing so the reeve would have something to write, and it is in the mud now with the rest of the water. A hide of peat without a name is a thing that warms whoever gets to it first, and that is not the same as a thing that is yours.”

Nobody answered him, which is an answer in a peat village. The old woman said the marsh keeps what it takes and gives back what it cannot use, which is a true thing and not a comfort. The reeve’s saying came up again and nobody said it out loud. He left the hide stacked against my wall, and it is there now, and I have not put a name to it because a name written by me would be a name I had made, and a foundling’s name belongs to whoever writes first, which in this byre would be me, and I have scratched the charcoal and not written.

The standing-song

Sung going round the measure in the first dark, one hand on the wall and the other at the small of the back. It is not a work-song; the peat gets cut whether it is sung or not. The last line is said to the fire.

One for the knee and one for the chin,

the bog takes the standing and gives back the thin;

nine by the house and one by the road,

the hide goes dry and the name goes wet.

Stand, stand, the reeve says stand;

the longer you stand the less of a man.

Bring up the peat and leave the name;

the byre keeps the warm, the mud keeps the claim.

The wager, as it stood at the fire

What stood — What the byre kept — What the Greybank hand wrote over it

One hide, won at the standing — One hide against the wall, no name on it — Fuel, unappropriated; value three marks

The standing, seven days into Stone — Nine standings and one man short — No account arises

The boots, dry in deep weather — Weather — Not entered

The name put into the bog — Nothing; the byre does not have it — Party unidentified; interest in a measure not entered

The shaving on the measure — One shaving, taken up by nobody — Gaming; not commercial

What the factor’s clerk did

He came up from Greybank on the first market after the thaw with a cuff soft from washing, and asked what the stack against the wall was worth. I told him a hide of peat was three marks and that the price was the price of a winter to a household that had one, and he wrote three marks and then wrote unappropriated under it, which is a word for a thing nobody has claimed and a thing nobody can have. He asked who had won it. I told him a man off the marsh road. He asked for the man’s name and I said the man had left it in the bog, which is a true thing and not the thing he wanted, and he wrote as you see.

At the slate has no column for me he wrote no charge arises. He has taken the peat away and left the man standing in the mud, which is what a figure does when it is done with the person it came out of. I have put his hand above mine and left mine standing underneath, the way the fire keeps its heat under a pot and is not asked where it got it.

In the margin, in a reeve’s sloping hand

The leaf went down the marsh road with a bundle of reeds and came back with a line in a hand that is not mine and not the factor’s. I have copied it as it stands.

At the hide goes dry and the name goes wet: I was at the Post the day he stood. He gave the name freely and we wrote it on the tally as the rule is, and on the third day the writing went soft and the name went into the bog with the rest. We did not scrape the mud for it. A reeve who scrapes the mud for a name has stopped being a reeve and become a man with a claim, and the Post has no place for a claim. Do not put a figure on the hide. A man with no name and three marks of peat is a dangerous man, and he is dangerous to nobody so long as nobody counts him.

I have not answered him in the margin. A thing written in a book follows a man home, and a reeve’s home is a boardwalk with nowhere to put a second opinion.

At the head and in the gutter

The peat at the head came off the hand I held the leaf down with in the draught that comes under the byre door, and it has gone into the grain so that no dry sleeve takes it out, which is why the byre signs in charcoal and not in ink. The tallow thumb at the fold is off the lamp I turn the leaf by. The press of frozen wool across the first leaf came off the carter when he held the door for the man from the marsh and did not let go when he was through, and I have not written down which carter, because he would stop holding doors if I did. The dark ring is small ale that stood from the first question to the last and was drunk by nobody; two men have claimed it and one woman has denied it and I have given it to none of them. The shaving in the fold is the one laid on the measure and taken up by nobody, kept where the children cannot find it, because a child who takes up a shaving has paid for the asking and got nothing. The strip of bog-fibre in the gutter came off the man’s cap and goes soft when the weather goes soft, which tells me when to shut the tin. The frost came up out of the paper on the carry up from the ford and is the only thing in this byre that entered without a hand. The grey along the outer fold came off a cuff that had no business lying where I keep the tin, and I have not swept it. There is a Last Day in Mud End and the corners are swept on the morning after and burnt and called dealt with. There is no Last Day in the marsh, and nothing there is swept until the water does it.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The fire keeps the tale. The measure keeps the count.

The wall keeps the hide. The bog keeps the name.

The factor keeps three marks and cannot say whose.

I asked the woman who cuts our peat, the carter come up for the winter lease, and the man who came in off the marsh road, and had three answers and no hand, as I have had before. I have left the lines where they were written. The leaf goes back into the tin behind the measure and the shaving goes back into the fold. Tomorrow the moot will sit and settle a fence and a pig, and the slate will come out and go back up the road, and the stack against my wall will stand where it stands, warming whoever is coldest first, with no name on it and three marks written over it in a hand that does not know what a winter is worth to a house that has nine of them and no more.
