To Bram at the Cross, who keeps the bell and will not be told what it is for. From Vesk of the foundry under the west wall of Keld Keep, who counts the metal before he counts the man.

I write number-first because that is how the foundry keeps its charges and how I was taught to keep them. Four figures to this letter. One, the metal. Two, the strokes. Three, what has come up the Clay Road about the bell at your Cross. Four, what I want from you, which is a number and not an opinion. I have no opinion. Opinions are what men sell when they have no figure to give.

One: the metal

I cast by the ear and not by the scale. A bell is a vessel with a mouth, and the note is the thing I sell; the metal is only the note made to stand still until a rope finds it. A bell with a flaw answers flat. I have cast thirty-one bells and never sold one that answered flat, and I have never once been asked to.

That is the whole of my trade and it is a small thing to put in a letter. The trouble began when a man came into the foundry off the Clay Road with a tale that the bell at Sill wants recasting with the fifth stroke put in, so that it may ring whole. He had the figure in a fist and would not say who had given it to him. He said a bell that rang whole would be worth a full note to the district. I asked him which district. He said all of them, which is an answer a man gives when he has never stood under a bell.

Two: the strokes as I count them

Stroke — What the hand does — What the district hears — What I was paid to make

First — Rope taken, weight given — A full note — Sound

Second — Rope taken — A full note — Sound

Third — Rope taken — A full note — Sound

Fourth — Rope taken — A full note — Sound

Fifth — Rope held; no stroke — A place where a stroke should be — Nothing

Four strokes I can cast. The fifth I cannot cast, because there is no metal in a silence. I asked the man from the Clay Road what metal he proposed to put into the fifth. He said that was a question for the founder. I told him I was the founder and the question was mine, and we sat a while without either of us getting the better of it.

I have struck through the figure nine above, where I wrote that I had cast nine bells this year. It is eight. The ninth was a pot-bellied thing for a shrine beyond the Cold Pass and went back into the pot before it ever went into a mould, which is a casting that does not count. I have put eight beneath in my own iron-gall so that you cannot say a stranger did it.

Three: what has come up the Clay Road

There is a bell you tell of on the Long Night and a bell you hear at the market. Which is it that wants mending? I am not asking to be difficult. A bell that is told of needs no metal and no rope; men sit in the dark and tell it, and it answers out of their mouths. A bell that is heard wants a hand on a rope twice a market-day, morning and afternoon, and wants it to go on wanting for as long as the market stands. Those are two trades and I keep only one of them.

I am told your bell is longer than a church bell and that nobody crosses himself at it. I have seen men cross themselves at a bell in the Bowl country; they do it quickly and with the whole hand, as if to brush something off the face. If your bell rang five, would they begin to do it there? That is the question I would put to a man from Sill if I had one in the foundry who had been to one of your markets. Give me the number and leave the reason at home. I have small use for reasons and a great use for counts.

Four: what I want

Send me a figure. If it is five I will cast five and thicken nothing. If it is four I will cast four and leave the lip thick where the fifth would have bitten, which is a thing I can do and still call the bell sound; a thick lip answers a little later than a thin one, and a late note is a note that can be stopped. Do not send me a saying. Sayings do not go into a mould.

The song I keep at the foundry, said to the metal and not to a person

Two to the fire and two to the floor,

the fifth to the place where the stroke was before;

the rope takes four and the hand takes none,

and the bell keeps the note that the hand never won.

Iron comes up and the warm goes down,

a flaw in the lip is a flaw in the town;

tap it at morning, it answers at night,

and the fifth is a hole that the market keeps right.

I have never written a verse in my life and I would not begin now if there were not a man at the other end of two roads who has heard a bell I have only made. Put it under the numbers where it belongs. If a clerk borrows this leaf, let him take the verbs out; he will have to take the metal out too, and then there will be nothing left for him to enter.

Vesk, at the foundry under Keld Keep, Bone of Y.H. 812.

In the margin, in a second hand, small, in charcoal, the letters round and pressed hard

You asked for a figure and I have set you one in your own table; look at the fifth row, where under what I was paid to make you have written nothing. That is the figure. Four strokes and a place. I have struck your five through with the point you left in the fold and put the other underneath, in charcoal, which does not keep; I could not find your iron-gall and would not have borrowed it if I had.

Do not recast it. A bell that rings five would set the market crossing itself, and this district does not cross itself at a bell. It has stood over the reading block these twenty years and nobody has once put a hand to his face at it. I have kept the rope through three Account-days in the wet and a fourth when the boards stood under brown water, and the gap has come back with the bell every time, the same finger’s width of nothing. I have never measured it and would not know how.

Your trade and mine are the same trade with the tool changed. You tap a bell where the lip is thick and I tap a pot where the crack was mended. A sound one answers full, a cracked one answers flat, and a mended one answers what it answers; I have never known a pot to lie about itself, which is more than I can say for the men who set them on the board. Strike our bell five times and the fifth would be a true note and the first four would be true notes, and the two truths would not agree. The yard has made its bed on the one that does not happen. Leave the lip thick. Leave it.

There is a bell we tell of on the Long Night and a bell we hear at the Cross. I keep the one we hear. The other is kept by people sitting in the dark, and when they have told it they go to their beds and it goes nowhere. If you must cast for someone, cast for them; they have no rope to wear out and no market to go up and down over the gap.

Four for the market and four for the bell,

the fifth for the place that the market knows well;

ask the rope what it wants, said the rope to the clay,

and the clay said nothing, and went on that way.

I do not write verses. I have set this one down because you set yours down and because a man who makes a thing and a man who rings it should not both be left with only figures between them. Put it under your eight where it belongs, and keep the eighth where you put it; a pot-bellied shrine bell that never went into the mould is a bell too, and there is no column for the ones that do not get made.

Bram, at the Cross, Sill, Reed of Y.H. 813.

What the Court hand did, at Greybank, in the second tend of Reed

A clerk of the factor’s house borrowed the leaf on a market morning; his cuff was gone soft at the edge from washing and he had the top sheet in his hand before he looked at either of us, which is the manner of that table. He writes in the neat upright hand they teach at the College, with the verbs taken out and everything in the passive. He took the verbs out of both hands as though a verb were a thing a man could smuggle in a cuff, and set a round figure against the casting, and struck the fifth row of the table and wrote fifth stroke: not entered above it. Against a place where a stroke should be he wrote vacancy. Where Bram wrote that the two truths would not agree he wrote figures reconciled, which is the Court’s own art in a small space, and left the charcoal underneath.

In the founder’s hand — In the ringer’s hand — In the Court hand

Four strokes cast; the fifth is silence — Four strokes and a place — Four strokes, sound

A gap is a fault in the casting — The gap keeps the bell honest — Vacancy; not entered

The lip thickened where the fifth would bite — A thick lip answers full — Material not entered

Two truths that will not agree — Leave it; leave it — Figures reconciled

Eight bells this year — The eighth was never cast — Castings entered: eight

He asked me what a verse was worth and I said nothing a clerk could enter, which is the first true thing he said all morning. I have left both hands standing, his above ours and ours beneath, as a repaired wall keeps the old break under the new keying and does not pretend the first hand never touched it. We have never entered one another. I would rather the foundry forgot my name than have it stand beside his on a slate with a figure after it.

At the head and in the gutter

The filings at the head are mine and came off the lip on the day I thickened the last one; I held the sheet down with my whole hand because the draught along the foundry floor takes paper before it takes a man. The tallow thumb is mine. The greenish shaving in the second fold is of the same metal as the bell at your Cross, or near enough; a shaving does not answer when you tap it, which is a thing I have often thought about and never said in company. The fibre in the stitch is your rope, not mine; it came away in my hand at Greybank and I sewed it back rather than let it go, because a rope that has been on a bell is not a rope to throw out. The ring is off a cup that stood from the first counting to the verse and was drunk by neither of us, which is how small ale is drunk in Keld when there is a thing to say and no proper word for it. The frost bloom came over the Cold Pass and has gone soft. The grey along the gutter is not mine and not the Court’s. I burn my grey on the morning after the Last Day like everybody else, and it is back on the bench by noon, which is why this leaf lives in a tin and not under a stone.

At the foot, in a hand nobody has claimed

Four, four, and the place kept clean.

The bell rings whole; the market does not.

Ask the metal what it wants: the fire.

Ask the rope what it wants: a hand that will stop.
