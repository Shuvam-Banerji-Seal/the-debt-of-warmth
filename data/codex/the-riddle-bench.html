I keep the bench at the Cross and the fire behind it, which is a different work from keeping an account, though both begin with the hand on the board before the eye goes to the person standing there. In the cold months the road shuts, the Ilm goes under its skin, and there is nothing to do of an evening but count what is left and ask one another questions we already know the answer to. That is a riddle. A question whose answer you do not know is a gamble, and there is enough gambling in Sill without ale in it.

The bench is black ash with the grain standing up where four thousand sleeves have gone over it. I score the night in charcoal on the end away from the fire, where the charcoal shows, and I rub it out in the morning with the heel of my hand. Nothing is carried away from this bench in a slate. A man who wants a thing entered can go up the street to Slate Row and sit with people warmer than himself, and he will not get a riddle there.

The rules, as they are kept

  1. A riddle is put once. Ask for it a second time and you owe a quart, because the second asking is for your own pleasure and not for the bench.
  2. Answer in one breath. A second answer is a different riddle and is not heard. A man who gives two has not understood the first.
  3. Four questions are scored. A fifth may be put and is not scored, being a thing said from a door and not from a stool.
  4. The judge is the bench. I say whether an answer serves and I do not say why. A reason given is a riddle given away for nothing, and the giver can go home and sell it.
  5. No slate lies on the bench. The score goes off with the morning, as the ale does, and neither is carried into an account.
  6. A cup is set down before the first question and no one lifts it until the fourth. If it answers flat when it is set down, the night’s answering will go badly. I have never known this to fail and I would not say so before a kiln-master.

The four that were scored

The first was put to the eel-woman from the Fishstairs, who smells of silt and answers a question with a question. She had given hers before the verse was finished, which is why she never wins anything and is always first.

Mouth and no lip to speak with,

belly that gives nothing back,

a door you cannot find from inside:

what goes in head-first stays?

“An eel basket,” she said, and laid a bit toward the pot though nobody had asked her to pay. The carter said a basket has no door. She told him to ask the eel, which is the whole of the answer and the proper way to give it.

The second was put to a girl who sweeps the yard for six bits a day.

Carried in a clay dish before the light,

given once and never twice;

every house has it by morning

and no house will say where it was lit.

What is it?

“The year’s fire,” she said, and I gave her a quart of small ale watered until it barely counted, which she drank standing with her sleeve over the rim because she had been told not to spill. Nobody asked her where she had learned it. A child of eight in this town knows which dish the coal comes in and which houses will not tell you who paid for it.

The third went to the man from the kiln, who had slip dried on both forearms to the colour of old bone.

I am not a warm-stone and nobody calls me one;

I am borrowed from a neighbour’s fire and carried in wool;

I am put under a head that cannot turn

and by morning I am a stone again.

What am I?

“A river stone,” he said, and there was an argument about whether a thing borrowed is the same thing when it is given back. I stopped it by setting the cup down. There is no winning an argument about a stone; there is only the stone, and it is cold by morning whichever way you decide it.

The fourth I put myself, because the room had got noisy and wanted quieting, and a riddle put by the bench is not scored against anybody.

Nothing at noon and plain by tallow;

I gather where a promise was not kept;

they leave me lying on the Last Day,

burn me in the morning and call it dealt with.

Name me.

No one answered for a while. Then a clay-hand from the pits, with the grey worked into his knuckles that no scrubbing lifted, said the grey in the corner, and said it to the bench and not to the room. I passed him the pot. He set it down again without drinking and said it was a thing his mother said to the skirting board, and that he had said it to his own daughter the week before and had not known he was going to. That is the best answer I have heard in eleven winters and the only one that cost the man something.

The fifth, which was put from the door

It was the middle of Stone, when the ice had set for good and the bit for a quart had gone up two and come down again. A man stood at the door and did not come in. He was dry, which a man cannot be in that weather without having come a long way or else not having come from the weather at all. He asked whether anybody here was warm. Nobody answered him, which is an answer in this town. Then he put the fifth:

I go back to a house that knows my face and not my name;

my mother is glad of me and does not know me;

I want a thing I never wanted

and cannot say what it is.

What am I?

The eel-woman said, “A thin return.” He said, “That is what the slate calls it,” and asked what it was to the house. The room went the way a room goes when everybody has the answer in their mouth and nobody will be the one to say it. He waited a little, and said, “Then you have not lost it,” and turned back into the cold. He did not come in and I did not ask him. Where he stood the boards were wet with the melt off every coat in the room, and in the morning there was a round dry place on the bench where no cup had stood. I rubbed the charcoal over it and it came back. I have stopped looking for the hand that did that.

The tally, in charcoal

Question — Put by — Answer given — Score

The basket — the bench — an eel basket — one, paid

The dish — the bench — the year’s fire — one, drunk

The stone — the bench — a river stone — one, argued and allowed

The grey — the bench — the grey in the corner — one, not drunk

The face and the name — a man at the door — none served — not scored

The figure read at the Cross that morning was one in twelve, as the notice says, and it went over the bench with the ale and no one looked at it twice. There is a pause in that figure the length of a held breath, which the clerk reads past and I do not, but that is not a thing for a riddle night.

What the narrow hand did

A clerk of the Court’s table borrowed the leaf on a market morning when the square was full and his cuff was soft at the edge from washing. He set the riddles down as queries entered and the answers as responses entered, and put a figure of marks against each of the four, which was more than I had scored, because the bench scores in whether a man sits down again and he does not. Against the fifth he wrote no answer tendered; struck. He asked who had put it. I told him a man at the door. He asked for the man’s name, and I said the man had given none, which is a true thing and not the thing he wanted, and he wrote party unidentified.

I have put my words under his and left his standing, the way a bench keeps a charcoal score under a spilled quart. He has entered four answers and struck the fifth, and the fifth was not struck: it stands where a man stood and did not sit down. I asked him what a figure was for and he said a figure was for carrying away. He has carried away four and left the one that would not go.

At the head and in the margins

The tallow thumb at the head is mine; I held the leaf down in the draught that comes under the door and did not know I had marked it. The ring came off a quart that stood from the first question to the fifth and was drunk by nobody; three men have claimed it and one woman has denied it and I have not given it to any of them. The press of wet wool across the first leaf came off the man who held the door while the stranger spoke, and I have not written down which man, because he would stop holding doors if I did. The barley grease is from a crust the sweeping girl set down on the leaf and took away again uneaten, which is how a crust is usually taken in this town. The grey in the gutter came off a cuff that had no business in the corner where I keep the leaf, and I have not swept it.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

Four went home warm. The fifth went home with them.

I have asked the carter, the eel-woman and the clay-hand, and had three silences and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The leaf goes back into the jar and the charcoal goes off the bench with the heel of my hand. The road will open at the thaw and there will be more to do of an evening than ask one another what we already know, and the bench will be wanted for cups again. I will set the fifth where it was and let the next man stand in it. If he asks whether anyone here is warm, I shall tell him to ask the pot.
