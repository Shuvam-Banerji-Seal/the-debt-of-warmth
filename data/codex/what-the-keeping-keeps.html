I copy the notices for the assessor’s house in Slate Row. Twice a tend I write the figure to be read over the basket, set it up where the moot can see it, and twice a tend I take it down. Six years I have done this and never once written please in a notice, because there is no such word in the hand I am paid to write, and no word for sorry either. There are eleven words for what is taken.

What follows is not what we say to a district but what we say to one another, in the room with the slate on the table and the door shut against the weather. A girl who has never had a figure read over her asked what her mother’s house said when the collector came, and I found I could tell her the ordinances and not one sentence anybody had spoken.

Say these at a table, in the clean voice, with the slate face up and nobody’s breath on it. Said at a fire, a sentence of ours becomes a threat with no speaker in it, which no household can argue with and no officer has to own. There is no doer in our sentences. That is not an accident of grammar but the whole of the craft: a thing with nobody in it can be done to anybody and arrives at the end of the Clay Road meaning exactly what it meant when written, which a sentence with a woman in it never does. I have put the doers back in the glosses; the Provost has taken some out again, and I have left his hand above mine, because that is the order things are kept in.

Of the account and the mouth

  1. “The account stands.”

Gloss — The first thing at a table and the last. It is not an answer and it is not a threat; it is a sentence with nobody in it, so there is no one to question and nothing to deny. A woman who hears it under her own roof has had something entered against her without a figure, and cannot find the place to argue. I have written the doer back below the Provost’s hand: the house holds this against her. He has taken it out again.

  1. “What is entered is kept. What is kept is not lost.”

Gloss — The whole of our mercy in three breaths, said over the pouring. A mother at a cradle wants to know whose keeping it is, whose hands, and for how long; the sentence has nobody in it and she cannot ask it. I have heard a keeper say it to a basin and then put a stone against the child, which is the only answer in that room with a hand in it.

  1. “An account is never closed. It is carried forward.”

Gloss — The rolls of the dead stay open; a man is entered and then the entry goes on speaking for him at every Weighing. We say this as a kindness, and it is one if you consider that a debt never closed is a debt that never has to be forgiven. A district that cannot pay carries the figure into the next year and the next, and the figure is patient, which people are not.

  1. “Consent is entered. Refusal is not.”

Gloss — The clip goes on the wrist until the wrist says yes. The form has a square for the first and no square for the second, and a clerk cannot enter what the form has no square for, which is the whole of the law as it arrives at a person. I have watched a woman say no all morning and be entered at noon as consenting, and the two things sat in one roll without touching.

  1. “The figure has no mouth. The hand that entered it does.”

Gloss — An assessor writes a figure and it is the figure that is read aloud, while the hand goes home to its tea. This is the one saying of ours with a doer in it and I have kept it so; a junior who copies notices ought to know which hand to watch when the figure goes twice over the basket.

Of the eleven words

  1. “There are eleven words for what is taken and none for what is given.”

Gloss — In Ledgerhand there are eleven ways to name a deduction and no word for *please or sorry. When a man asks what we call a gift, we tell him to go and name a tide in Vhalen or ask a pot in Grath, because we do not make gifts. What comes to a house comes as a return, and a return is an entry, and an entry has a figure.*

  1. “One in twelve is printed. The other goes where the ink has been.”

Gloss — Read twice over the basket on account-day, once for the moot and once for those who came late. Every district knows the figure read is not the figure paid; no table has ever had a square for the difference, and a thing with no square is a thing no officer can be asked about. I have put it here because a copyist who writes the notice ought to know the shape of the thing he is copying, even though he must copy it as though he did not.

Of the iron and the return

  1. “The iron is cold so the figure may be clean.”

Gloss — A warm tube would leave a mark in the skin that could be read as a number, and a number that cannot be wiped off is one the Court has not authorised. Cold iron takes the measure and leaves a small round place that fades by winter. We call this cleanliness, and it is our whole surgery.

  1. “The roll is carried forward. The person is not.”

Gloss — A name stays on the roll after the return and is never struck. A household gets a child with a liking for a cup, and the roll still reads the dead man’s figure, the only place in Sill where the two agree. I have kept both columns side by side and never joined them, the only kindness my office allows.

  1. “A whole return is twelve hundred and forty. The forty are not explained.”

Gloss — Booked at Greybank and at the Hearthseat, twelve hundred marks and forty unspent measures. I asked once what the forty were. I was told the account stands, which is an answer built out of the end of the question and contains no more than the question did.

  1. “Quiet ash is dispensed by a greyed hand.”

Gloss — Sixty marks a phial, and the hand that writes the licence is not the hand that greys. I have seen both in the same room and neither looked at the other. The previous life stops arriving in dreams; the hand that reaches for a cup reaches as before. We enter the phial and not the reaching.

  1. “The Kept are kept longest and feel nothing.”

Gloss — Fewer than twenty rooms near the fire in the Hearthseat. They hold three hundred measures and cannot feel the fire they are kept beside. We say it as a mercy and the word stands in the roll. I have never seen one and hope not to.

  1. “There is a word we have no column for.”

Gloss — When a man at a bed says it, the room goes quiet and the slate is turned face down. I have heard it once, from somebody who had come in from the Waste and did not know where he was. It is in no ordinance and there is no word for it in Ledgerhand, which is how I know it is not ours. I have never been told what it was and I have not asked.

The table-song

Said at the close of account-day, going out of the Court room with the slate under the arm, not at the Weighing. The fourth line of the second part is held, and the space after it is the length of a name. Nobody fills it.

Four for the sayings and one held back,

the figure goes up and the face stays down;

one in twelve is printed clean,

and the other goes where the ink has gone.

Count them entering, count them out,

a mark for the tube and a mark for the clerk;

the roll goes forward and the name goes round

*

The slate goes up. The hand stays here.

The figure is current. The person is not.

We keep what we cannot give back,

and give back what we cannot keep.

Gloss — The held line is not a fifth saying or a fifth deduction. Juniors ask what stands in it and are told to ask the slate. The Provost put a round nought in the space and wrote *vacancy beneath it. A nought is a figure. The space was a name.*

What the Provost set above

A Provost of the College borrowed the leaves on an account-day morning, with ink settled in two fingers of a clean cuff. He writes in the narrow upright hand and takes the doers out of the glosses as though a doer were a thing a man could carry off in a sleeve.

  • At the account stands I had written the house holds this against her. He set the clean sentence above and underlined it twice. He is right in the language of a table and wrong about the room it was said in; in that room there was a woman holding a basin.
  • At consent is entered, refusal is not, I wrote the clip is let go when the wrist says yes. He wrote consent is recorded, which leaves the wrist out.
  • At the held line he put the round nought and wrote vacancy. I have left it where he set it and put the name back underneath, the length it was.

I have left all of it standing above my words. He takes the doers out because a thing always true has no need of anybody doing it. I put them back because a junior wants to know which hand to watch, and whether it will come away from the wrist when the figure has been read.

In the margin, in a charcoal hand that is not a clerk’s

You write that the figure has no mouth. It has yours. I sat in the Waiting Room with a stone against my wrist and heard my number read by a man who had never held me and would not look at my face.

Below, in mine: she would not give a name and I have not asked. A margin is for what a table has no square for.

At the head and in the gutter

The ink thumb at the head is mine; I turn the leaf with the hand I write notices with and the black goes into the grain and will not come out. The barley-water ring went dark in the three days it took me to find a word for what a house does when a figure is read over it. The round scratch came off a tube-case set down hard on a dried correction and took the top off one of the Provost’s words; I have not written it back. The cut corner is where a figure stood and someone cut it away before I could copy it. I do not know who, and the figure is gone, which in this trade is the same as paid. The grey along the outer fold came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying in a room where the slate is kept. I have not swept it. There is a Last Day down in the district; there is none in Slate Row.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

We keep what we cannot return.

We return what we cannot keep.

The account stands between.

I asked a collector, a Provost and a woman who had sat overnight in the Waiting Room, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written. The leaf goes back under the loose board. Tomorrow is account-day; I shall write the figure again in the clean hand, send it twice over the basket, and put the doers back where no magistrate reads them.
