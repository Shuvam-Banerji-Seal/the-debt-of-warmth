I keep the drying frames at the third pan from the shore road, where the crust will bear a foot until the water comes over it. A Council clerk came down in the last tend of Ember with a slate and asked what the children counted when they jumped. I told him they counted the tide. He asked what figure the tide came to. I told him it came to nothing, which is the truth, and he went back up the coast pass and sent me a man with a better slate.

What follows is what the children said before the man came, and what they said again after he had gone back up the road. I have put his hand above mine where he set it and the children’s words underneath, as the pan keeps the salt and the water keeps the mark the salt made.

They play on the hard white crust above the high-water line, from the going of the flood until the crust goes soft under a foot. They do not play when the Sallow is over the second step of the tally-house. The rope is an old net-cord a dipper has given over, tarred once and gone soft in the hand; a child whose people are off the boats uses a twist of eel-grass, which is meaner to the ankle and counts the same. The children say a rope must have been in the water, because a rope that has never been wet does not know which way the tide goes.

The counter is chosen by the counting-out. She holds the ends of the cord and says the words to the rope and not to the children. Said to a child it becomes an argument, and there is always a child who will argue.

The counting-out

Right fists held out, knuckles up. The child who says it points at each fist in turn and does not look at the faces. The fist it lights on at the last word keeps the cord and turns the rope through. She does not jump first. She is the one who has to be fair, which the children accept as a punishment and the pans accept as practice.

Bit in the palm and bit in the shoe,

the dipper reads flood and the gull reads ebb;

a mark for the mother and half for the man,

and the last one named turns the rope through.

Say it to the cord. A cord that has been in the water tells your hands which way to turn; a child tells you nothing you can use.

The verse, as it is jumped

One line, one jump. Twelve bits make a mark, as the tally-man says. On the twelfth line the counter shuts her fist on the mark and keeps it there; a mark shut in a fist is a mark no slate can take. Then the cord is turned the other way. That is the turn of the tide, and the same twelve lines are said backward, one jump each, coming back to where they began. The Sallow runs against the wind and the dippers say it follows the Warm and not the moon; the children say the cord follows the hand that holds it. A child who jumps the ebb right has brought the count back to the pan it came from. The counter opens her fist and the salt that was in it has gone, which the Council calls a virtue and the children call the tide.

One for the crust that will bear a foot,

two for the net that comes up wet;

three for the green we get off the Cliffs,

four for the coal the gull has got;

Five for the bench that rows seven years,

six for the wine with salt on the rim;

seven for the share that goes down the mother,

eight for the word that is counted at half;

Nine for the pan where the water comes in,

ten for the step where it turns and goes;

eleven for the slate they keep in the house,

twelve for the mark that goes into the fist.

The half that is not a share

A child whose father came aboard jumps at the half. She goes on one foot and says half under her breath on every line, and the others allow it, because half is what he brought and half is what this house has always given her. She does not get to shut the mark in her fist at the twelfth; the mark is a whole thing and she has not got a whole one. She stands aside at the turn and comes in again on the ebb, which is the only tide that ever brought anything back to this coast. If she argues, they tell her the boat was here before her word, which is a hard saying to a child and a true one.

The gull’s edge

A child who puts a foot wrong goes to the gull. The gull is the far edge of the pan, where the salt is too thin to bear a weight; the child sits there and watches the others jump. She is brought back by three bits of salt put into her open hand, one after another, with nothing said. A child sent to the gull is brought back by the salt and not by a name, because a name called across the pans travels and comes back with a figure on it. This is the children’s own law and I have never once known them to break it. They stop when the crust goes soft or a dipper tells them off the pan, and the last thing said is always the bringing-back, never the count. A child left sitting at the gull’s edge when they stop is not counted as having played, which is a kindness the Council would not understand.

The verse they do not say while a man from the Council is on the Quay

I have it from the child who keeps the cord, with the space left before it. She would not say it to the clerk and would not say it with him in hearing. She said it to the crust with her back turned and made me put it down as I have it, with none of my verbs in it.

The draw takes the Warm and the tax takes the draw,

the rain takes the paper and calls it the law;

the pan keeps the salt and the tide keeps the count,

and the boat comes back on the water it spent.

One for the half that is not a share,

two for the mark that is not a debt;

say it to the crust and jump it through,

and no man in a clean cuff knows what it cost.

What the clerk did

A clerk of the Council borrowed the sheet on the morning he came down, being a man who cannot leave paper alone. He writes in the leaning hand they use in the Council room, where it smells of tar and wet rope, and the ink sits in two fingers of his cuff and will not come out. He numbered the twelve lines in the margin. At the mark that goes into the fist he wrote above, the measure is retained by the counter and not entered. At the tide takes it he wrote instrument void by exposure to water; no value attaches, and underneath, no claim can lie against a lost mark, which is fortunate for all parties. Against the half he set the fractional interest is rated at one half and does not constitute a share. At the foot of the count he put a round nought and wrote nil against it, where the children’s game ends with salt in an open hand.

I have put their words back underneath and left his standing above. He takes the verbs out because a thing always true has no need of them. I put them back because a child up to her ankle in the salt of the pan does not know she is jumping something always true, and wants to know which foot to lift. A nought is a number. The half is a place at the table. The fist is not a bank. He has put a figure where a place should be, which is the whole art of that hand.

He asked me what the children learned by it. I told him the count and the turn and the bringing-back, and he said it was the whole doctrine of returns taught before a child could hold a slate, and he entered it. The children stop jumping when a man from the Council comes onto the crust and take it up again when he is past the shore road, and they never once change the twelve.

In the dipper’s charcoal

At the foot of the first leaf, in thick charcoal that has rubbed onto the fold, in the hand of a dipper who borrowed the sheet while the net was mending:

We read the flood at dawn and tell the Council what it is worth. The ebb brings the boat back and nobody puts a figure on it. The children put a figure on it and then give it to the tide. That is why I let them have my old cord. A cord that counts and then loses is the only honest cord on this coast.

When I asked him why he wrote it on a children’s game he said a game is the only paper in Vhal that a Council man never thinks to read. I have left the charcoal where he put it and not washed the fold.

At the head and in the gutter

The salt crust at the head and along the gutter has gone into the grain of the paper and will not come out. The tar thumb is mine; I held the leaf down in a wind off the Sallow and did not know I had marked it. The fibre of net-cord in the second fold came off the cord the children use and goes soft when the weather goes soft, which tells me when to shut the tin. The green bead came off a girl’s hair as she leaned over to see the writing, and she did not ask for it back. The gull’s peck went through the word flood in the verse and I have left the hole standing, because a verse about the tide with a word taken out by a bird is in better order than I could have made it. The wet corner took two lines of the verse and they are void by the Council’s own law, which is a virtue; I have left the space the length of them and written nothing in it, because a verse with two lines gone is a verse said slowly, and all of ours are said slowly. The grey print along the gutter came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying; nobody will own it and there is no Last Day at the pans. I keep this leaf dry in a tin lined with rushes, which is a strange thing to do in Vhal, where paper is made to rot. A document the rain has not had is a document the Council can still read. That is the only reason I keep it.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The pan keeps the salt and the tide keeps the count;

the half is a share and the share is a debt;

the fist keeps the bit that the slate cannot keep,

and the boat comes back on the water it spent.

I asked the child who keeps the cord, a dipper, a woman who holds a share from her mother, and a Council clerk come up from the Quay, and had four answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The leaf goes back into the tin I keep on the shelf with the paper I mean to let the rain take. Tomorrow the flood will come in over the third pan and the crust will bear a foot again, and the children will jump the twelve and give them back to the water, and the clerk will put a figure against the one thing in this port that was never for sale.
