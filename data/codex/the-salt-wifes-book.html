I write on the back of a tide-table the Council left out in the rain. The figures have gone where the Council’s figures go, which is nowhere a man can put his hand on, and what is left is paper enough for a book nobody has asked to see. I am a salt-wife of the Quay. I go aboard when a birth will not wait for the quay, and I keep the households of the glass women, who are the only people in Vhal who sweat sweetness. I have set down what we do and what the lowland physician at Greybank told me to do, and where the two disagree I have left both standing. A remedy is a thing the sea has been paid for already. A woman who takes a line out of a book of physic is answerable for the next body that takes it.

My hand is not a clerk’s. I put the verbs in because a direction without a verb is a thing a Council clerk can read aloud at a table and a woman cannot use at three in the morning with a boat coming up under her. The green at the head is not tar. It is a bead of cliff-glass a girl pressed into the leaf with her thumb while I was writing, and she had sweetness at her mouth and said nothing. The tar is mine, off the oar I held down in the wind. The salt along the gutter came up out of the paper itself. I have not washed any of it.

The order of a sick-room aboard

Written small in the margin, with one word gone over in brine.

  • Get the brazier under the lee of the hold, not in the wind. A fire in a draught is a fire that has been taking warmth from somebody and giving it to the air. Nobody in this port will agree to pay for the air.
  • Set the sick one’s feet toward the ebb if the trouble is a chill, toward the flood if the trouble is a swelling. This is what we do. A lowland physician told me it should be toward the fire in either case, and both stand here.
  • Turn the slate face down before you go to the child, as the hearth-maids do in Grath. I have no slate-name and never shall; a salt-paper contract is a thing that rots in the damp, which is a virtue in this country and a folly in every other.
  • Salt the water before you wash a wound. The sea does it for nothing and we charge for it, which is the whole of our trade.
  • When the quay-crier calls the turn of the tide, stop and begin again after. He is counting something that is not in your room.

I have done three hundred births and I still look at the door before I look at the child. It is a trick the room plays on a woman. After the first hundred you stop calling it a habit and call it the order of things.

The remedies, as they are used

Trouble — What is set to it — The manner of it — Who pays

Glass-sickness (the sweetness) — A draught of seawater, and cliff-glass scraped into it — The glass women rub their gums with it and say the sweetness answers sweetness. A lowland physician says it is the lead in the glaze and that the draught makes it worse. I have seen the pale line at the gum and the hand that cannot hold a cup. I have not seen the lead. — The glass furnace, which cannot stop and does not ask.

A chill off the water — A common stone warmed at the brazier, in a fold of wool; the feet to the ebb — To the belly, never the feet. Change it when it goes cold in your own hand, not when the child stops crying. A Grathic hearth-maid says a stone is a stone and not a kindness, and I have let her words stand. — The brazier, and whoever paid for the coal.

A hot wound, or a fever that burns — A cold-iron knife blade laid across it — We say the iron draws the heat out. A Keldish man at the quay told me cold iron draws and gives nothing back, and that a blade on a wound takes the measure as well as the heat. I have set his words beneath mine. — The wound. There is no other answer.

A bone set wrong — Two hands, a splint of straight reed, a fold of wet cloth — Set it while the vessel is quiet, before the swelling takes; leave the reed on till the third day. A bone-setter from Nine Pots says leave it a tend and a half. Both stand. — The patient, in the holding still.

A birth-flower — Salt, rubbed in — We call it the tide-mark and rub it with salt, believing the sea set it there. A Grathic woman crossed two fingers at the corner of one and gave me a date instead of an answer. I have put the date in the margin. — Nobody knows. The child’s skin, perhaps.

Milk that will not come — Another woman to hold the child while the mother sleeps — The favour is paid out of the other woman’s own house, and I say so before I ask it. — The other woman’s child.

A cut from glass — Tallow first, then a clean fold — Do not salt a glass cut if you can help it. The glass women salt everything, and their hands are mapped in white lines. — Whoever opened the furnace.

The bite of a salt-thing — Nothing I know — Keep her from the water at the turn. Salt does not hold that bite, and a brine-gull will take a coal off a brazier but will not take this. — Nobody. This is the honest column.

A child emptied by leeches — Nothing. Do not set them. — They draw through a small wound and leave the body thin; a collector would call it a draw and ask for a slate, and I have never seen one ask. If you find them on a child, take them off and count them, and do not put them back. — Whoever put them there.

The third-night chill — Warmth, quiet, and a watch kept till the fourth day — Keep her against a living body under the quilt and give barley water by the spoon. Do not leave her to sleep it off, and do not call a collector for the worsening; it is the night’s own work and goes off by the fourth morning. — The household, one night’s sleep and a loaf.

A lowland physician came up the Salt Road in Y.H. 798 with a book that said the sweetness came from the lead in the glaze. He left a page of it in the glass-house and went off with a purse. The page came back to me with the word taken clean away, as though a word could be taken off paper the way salt is taken off a net. I have written the word here in my own hand, which is a thing a salt-wife is not supposed to do. I have also left the draught of seawater standing under it, because it is what I have seen done for thirty years and I have not yet seen what it does to a person beyond the month.

The full note

Tap a sound cup with the knuckle and it answers full. Tap a cracked one and it answers flat, a little dead sound that stops at the surface and will not go through. The glass women know it in their cups and I have borrowed it from them. A girl of the furnace told me her mother used to tap the ribs of a sick man and say he had gone flat. I do not do it. A body is not a cup, and a cup’s answer is none of a person’s business.

The dippers say the Sallow gives back a note like a cup three-quarters full, and that the Council enters it as full because the difference is not worth a figure. I have left the three-quarters where I heard it. A figure a clerk can carry away in a cuff; a note is not.

The tide and the draw

We say the draw runs seaward and a body empties into it. The dippers bless the nets at dawn so the eels will not notice, which is a kindness spent on eels. If the warm goes from the full to the empty, as the lowland books say, then a sick body turned to the ebb should lose what ails it. That is what I used to think. I have watched nine such bodies and eight of them went colder before the turn; the ninth did not, and she had been carried in the other direction by her daughter, who did not tell me. I have left the old line standing and set the nine beside it. When a thing has been done wrong nine times I want the ninth to keep her place.

There is a shape of salt and cold water in the Sallow that taps the hull and takes a boat’s store away. We call it the salt-thing. A healer who cannot tell a tide from a taking will set a sick man facing the water at the turn and wonder why he goes thin. I have done it. I do not say I was right.

The song for the third night

Sung low, to the child, while the watch is kept. The room says the last line with her.

The ebb takes and the ebb keeps,

the glass gives back what the furnace grieves;

a body is a boat with a seam

and the seam is where the warmth leaves.

Two for the brazier, two for the bite,

two for the hand that holds the light;

the twelfth goes down and the twelfth comes in,

and one goes out on a wind not seen.

Three nights, three watches, a hand kept true;

what comes back is not what went;

sing to the child, sing to the cold,

and come back on the flood. Come back as you can.

The twelfth is left where the twelfth should be. The Council would put a figure in it. A figure is a thing that can be written and then rained on and then entered again in a dry hand; a tide is not.

What the Council hand did

A clerk of the Council borrowed the leaf on a market morning in Ember. He writes on salt-paper, and the salt-paper has taken the damp and let his hand go, so that of his three corrections only one figure and half a verb survive. Where I wrote the draught of seawater, he set the measure is restored above it, and the damp has eaten restored so that only the measure is stands. He put a round nought in the place of the unnamed tide and wrote vacancy under it, and the vacancy has outlasted the nought, which is the Council’s own art in a small space.

I have put my words back under what is left of his. He takes the verbs out because a thing always true has no need of them. I put them in because a woman on a boat at three in the morning does not know she is hearing something always true, and wants to know which hand to hold the child with.

At the head and in the gutter

The green bead at the head was pressed in with a thumb and has left a sweet smear that will not dry. The tar is off the oar, and came off when I held the sheet down on deck in a wind that was taking everything else. The bloom along the gutter is salt out of the paper and not out of the sea, which is a thing only a person who has kept salt-paper knows the difference of. The ring of brine came from a cup set down while I was telling a girl to eat and she would not; the hook nick came off a man paying me with his hook still in his hand, and he did not apologize. The grey print at the foot is not mine and not the Council’s. I have not swept it. There is no Last Day in a tin.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The sea keeps what it takes. It has no slate and no clerk, and it does not call it a debt.

I asked the dippers, the glass women and a factor come up from Greybank, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The tin goes back among the boat’s things and the leaf goes aboard when the ebb comes. A girl will press another bead into it and say nothing, and I shall put the verbs back where the damp has taken them.
