I dress the hands of the eel-women and have caught their children under the north bridge for two-and-twenty years. Nobody asked me for this book. A girl came in the lean week with a foot gone dark inside three turns of wool and asked what I did for the cold that comes up out of the water, and I heard myself answer in my mother’s short way of speaking, and did not know whether I believed it. A remedy you have to say in your mother’s voice is a remedy nobody has looked at in a long while. I have looked at some. The rest I have left standing, because a woman at three in the morning wants a hand more than a correction.

I write on the back of a fish-bill that brine took clean. The figures went where the river goes, which is nowhere a man can put his hand on. I write in Grathic, the speech of the town above the bridge, and keep the short wet words of the stairs where they would not fit, as the eel-women keep what they will not say before a buyer. Where a woman gives me a date instead of an answer I have set the date down and left the question standing. Dates are safe. A date does not spoil in the mouth.

The silt at the head is mine. The grey along the fold came up out of the paper itself and has taken two words off the second leaf; I have not written them back. A remedy with two words gone is a remedy said slowly, and everything at the Fishstairs is said slowly.

The order of the fish-shed

Written small on the first leaf, with one word gone over in brine.

  • Turn the board face down before you go to the child. A board has a name chalked underneath that nobody reads aloud, and I have no slate-name and never shall. The hand that writes is not the hand that holds.
  • Keep the door toward the river but do not open it. A woman watched from the stairs holds her breath, and a held breath is a poor thing to bring a child on.
  • Set the child on dry sacking, never on a fish-board. A wet board takes the warm out of a side faster than the water, and the water has the decency to be cold all over.
  • Warm a stone at the stall lamp and try it on your wrist. It takes from the lamp’s account, and somebody paid for the oil.
  • When the collector’s boat comes round the bend, stop. He is counting something that is not in your room.
  • When the third night comes, count with her, not for her. A number counted for her belongs to whoever counted it.

An old woman of the Weir asked why I turn the board before I look. She gave me a date for an answer and I have put it in the margin, which is where she put it herself.

The remedies, as they are used

Trouble — What is set to it — The manner of it — Who pays

The cold in one foot — Take the leeches off, count them, salt the foot; a stone at the belly — They set their mouths to a way and draw as a collector draws, without the slate. A wader feels one foot go cold and it does not leave him ashore. The physician says the draw is real and salt only troubles the foot. I have taken them off eleven children and counted them; a twelfth came with three already gone. — Whoever put them there.

The glass-eel’s clamp — Belly down on a warmed stone; thumbs pressed from the hand toward the shoulder — The eel stops the ways for a moment in the water, like a clamp with no iron; enough to drown a wader, not to kill a man on dry ground. We push the warmth back the way it came. The physician says the body does it or does not. I have done it to four and not said which four got up. — The river, which enters nothing.

A fish-bone in the throat — A crust of brown barley bread, swallowed dry; then nothing — We say the bread carries it. Of nine, six were better by evening, two by morning, and one not at all. The physician says a bone wants the knife, and that an eel-bone has gone into the lung twice. Both stand. — The throat. The least honest row.

A cut from a fish-knob — Salt from the herring barrel, then a clean fold; never the silt — The women put silt on a cut to dry it, believing the grey is clean. I have dressed three and found grey under the skin in all three, a white line I could not get out. — The hand.

The child come up drowned — Roll on a warmed barrel; hold by the heels; thumbs at the ribs and count — The water comes up and then the child, if he does. The physician says a child under an hour is gone and no carrying warms what has let go. I held nine and three came back, and two of the three got worse before they got better. — Nobody knows. I have left the place where the name of that hour would stand the length a name of that kind wants.

The sweet at the mouth — Sour barley-water; a pinch of wood-ash at the gum — We say the sweetness answers sweetness. The girls come down from the Glaze Room with a pale line at one mouth-corner and give me a green bead and say nothing. The physician says lead, as he does in every country. I have seen the line go off one who left and come back on one who stayed. I have not seen the lead. — The glaze room, which cannot stop and does not ask.

The birth-flower — Nothing. Two fingers crossed at the corner, and the word the mother gives — A woman of Low Kiln washed one with salt and her child went flat by evening. I have set that beside the six I did not wash and not joined them. When asked what it is I give a date. — Nobody knows. The child’s skin, perhaps.

The third-night chill — Warmth, quiet, barley-water by the spoon, a watch to the fourth morning — It comes after the warming, not before. A hearth-maid from Low Kiln says a warmed body spends faster and the child should be held and counted with, not walked. I have walked eleven and held three; all saw the fourth morning, and one went thin. I have not written which was which. — The household, one night’s sleep and a loaf.

Nothing at this stair is given away. It is carried from one board to another and called a cure. That is the only sentence I will stand behind.

The note under the knuckle

Tap a sound pot with the knuckle and it answers full. Tap a cracked one and it answers flat, a small dead sound that stops at the lip and will not go through. The eel-women know it in the pots they salt in and I have borrowed it from them, as every trade borrows from the trade beside it.

I tapped a man once, gone thin after a thin return, and put my ear to the little flat I thought I heard. The flat was not in him. A body is not a pot, though we set pots on the boards in the shape of bellies and call them by house-names. The pot on the bench knows more about whether the shed held its warm through the night than the man does, and I set the stone by the pot, for there is no clock below the north bridge.

The eel-women say the river gives back a note three-quarters full and the tally enters it as full because the difference is not worth a figure. I have left the three-quarters where I heard it.

The hour on the far side

A child carried in out of the water and warmed before the fire will sometimes get worse at the moment he should be getting better. The limbs go first and the chest holds out, and there is an hour on the far side of the warming in which a body spends what it has been given faster than it takes in. I have no name for that hour. I have left the space where the name would stand, the length a name of that kind wants. A clerk would put a nought in it and call it nothing. I have seen the hour and it is not nothing.

The counting song

Sung low at the boards while the eels are sorted, to the water and not to the people. The twelfth is held in the singing, the length of a hand going into a basket and coming out with nothing. Nobody fills it.

Four for the steps and one for the short,

one for the board that will not bear;

salt on the hand and silt on the sleeve,

and the trap goes down where the eels were.

A shine in the water that clamps like iron,

the bone goes down with the brown crust

and the grey goes in where the cut was.

Three for the watch and one for the pot,

one for the child and one for the cold ---

what comes back is not what went,

and comes back as the wall can hold.

Eleven I counted and one held out,

and the twelfth goes down where the river keeps.

I have changed one line in two-and-twenty years. I sang the river keeps and now sing where the river keeps, and the women singing with me have not noticed, which is how I know the line wanted changing. A factor’s clerk put a round nought in the held place and wrote vacancy beneath it, which is a figure with the water taken out. A nought can be carried away in a cuff. The twelfth is a place and stays.

What the physician pressed in the margin

He came down from Greybank with a book that had the word lead in it and a hand that wrote without asking. Against the sweet he set lead, as in the north; against the cold in one foot, the animal draws; salt has no efficacy; against the drowned child, warming what has let go is a kindness to the living only. He was right in all three and had nothing a household here could pay for. Where I wrote we push the warmth back, he set no such transmission. I have put the warmth underneath.

What the factor’s clerk did

He borrowed the leaf when the winter eels were counted, ink in two fingers of a soft cuff. He struck out my column headed Who pays and wrote No cost to the house, true in a counting room and false on the boards. Where I wrote the river takes and enters nothing, he wrote no toll is recorded at the Fishstairs, the most beautiful sentence I have ever read in a hand that has never stood in water to the calf. He took the verbs out of the stone takes from the lamp’s account and wrote the measure is retained above, as though a thing always true had no need of anybody carrying it. I have put the verbs back underneath. A clerk can write without verbs because he is never at three in the morning with a child gone blue in a fold.

In the foot of the remedies, in charcoal, a second hand

Ask the pot what the night cost. Ask the cord what it counted.

I have sorted eels at these boards since before the flood and never had a figure tell me what the water took.

She wrote it while I was at the traps and set the leaf back with the fold changed, and would not put her name to it, because a name on a leaf is a name a clerk can carry up the bridge. A woman who has counted nothing is allowed one sentence in a book of physic. I have set her line above mine.

At the head and in the gutter

The silt at the head came off my thumb setting the bill down on the stall in a wind off the water and has gone into the grain, which is why the leaf smells of the river in any room. The grey tideline took two words clean off the second leaf; I have not written them back. The fish-scale was pressed in by a girl who went back to the creel. The brine ring came from a dish set down while I told a girl to eat and she would not. The tar is off the oar I held down in the wind. The green bead is not slip: a girl come down from High Kiln with the sweetness at one mouth-corner touched the leaf and went back up the bridge without a word. The scorch is the stall lamp catching the fourth leaf; the hand that put it out was mine and I have not entered the burn. The grey on the outer fold came off a cuff that had no business lying where I keep the tin, and I have not swept it. There is a Last Day in Sill and the corners are swept there in the morning and burnt and called dealt with. There is no Last Day under the north bridge.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The river keeps what it takes and has no slate.

The slate keeps what it cannot carry and calls it kept.

The child between them is warm or is not, and neither column asks.

I asked the physician, the clerk, and a woman who gave me a date for an answer, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The book goes back into the tin among the net-needles. Tomorrow a girl will come with a foot gone dark or a child that will not breathe, and I shall turn the board face down, put the verbs back, and leave the twelfth where it is, because a child is not an account and a woman at three in the morning wants a hand more than a remedy.
