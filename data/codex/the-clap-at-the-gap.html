I keep the step under the moot-post and sell barley water when the market stands. There are three mornings in every tend when the Cross is full of people and two hundred lean over the block and eight pigs go over the wet boards, and seven when it is only the step and the dust and a child or two waiting for a mother at the pot board. On those mornings the children clap the bell. They clap it when the bell is not rung, which is the whole of the game and the thing the clerk could not hear.

A man with a soft cuff came up from Greybank and asked what they counted. I told him the count and the gap. He wrote it down with the gap taken out and a figure put in its place, and went back over the north bridge with his boots clean. What follows is what the children did before he came and what they did again after he had gone. I have set his hand above theirs, as the step keeps the dust under the feet that bring it.

The slate dust at the head is mine. I sweep the step and I do not get it all out of the grain of the leaf, which is how I know I have had it in my hand.

The step and the hands, as the children keep it

Played on the seven mornings when there is no market. Not on Account-day, when the block is crowded and a child on the step is in the way of the reading. Not in the Five, when they are made to stand and listen. Not in rain, for a wet hand drops the corn and a wet step gives back no knock.

Two children sit facing each other on the reading-block step, with their feet on the lower stone. One is the bell and one is the gap, for as long as they sit there, and they are not called by their own names while they play, for a name said at the Cross goes up the clay road in a cuff and comes back written. There is no rope and no pot and nothing to buy. Each holds a dried barleycorn shut in one hand all through the game.

The bell claps four times on her own knee, then four times palm to palm with the gap. At the ninth the bell lifts her hand and gives no stroke, and the gap claps once with the shut fist into the open palm, so that the corn takes the sound and gives back a small dry knock. Then the bell brings her hand down and they begin again.

The verse is said low to the step and never to a face. Said to a face it becomes a question, and there is always a child who will answer. One line is one clap, and the last three lines belong to the gap alone.

If the corn falls out of the hand or is crushed, the round is spoiled and they begin again at the first knee-clap. Nobody keeps a count of spoiled rounds. There is no winning and no last round; they stop when a mother calls, and the corn goes back into a pocket whole, and is often eaten on the way down the clay road, which nobody mentions.

The counting-out, said before the corn is taken up

Open hands for this part, the corn still in the pocket. The child who says it taps each fist in turn and does not look at the faces. The fist it lights on at the last word is the gap, which is not a prize: the gap is the one who has to make the sound the bell leaves out. The bell is the one who gets to be silent.

Corn in the pocket and slate on the sill,

the bell goes up and the step stands still;

one fist, two fist, three and four ---

the one it lights on makes the ninth once more.

Makes the ninth once more means keeps the sound, in the children’s speech, though a bell will tell you it means makes it again; both sayings have stood on this step as long as I have swept it.

The clapping verse, as it is clapped

Four lines to four knee-claps. Then the refrain while the hands change. Then four lines to four palm-claps, and the refrain again. The last three lines are the gap’s, said with the hand still shut.

One for the dust on the reading-block stone,

two for the pig that went over alone;

three for the shade where the basket stood,

four for the gap where the bell should have stood.

Four on the knee, four across to the palm,

one dry knock where the fist holds warm;

the bell gives nothing, the gap gives one,

said to the step, and the step says none.

Five for the cup set down in the shade,

six for the figure the crier has made;

seven for the cuff with the ink in the seam,

eight for the count that is not what it seems.

Four on the knee, four across to the palm,

one dry knock where the fist holds warm;

the bell gives nothing, the gap gives one,

said to the step, and the step says none.

The bell lifts her hand and gives no stroke;

the gap claps once where the corn lies shut ---

a dry little knock, and the dust jumps up.

The fist opens. The corn is whole. Begin.

The children will not be told why the hand stays shut. Ask them and they say the corn tells them; ask what the corn tells and they ask you why a man wants to know what a corn tells.

What is said when the corn falls

Corn on the boards and a round gone wrong ---

four to the knee and begin the song.

Nobody keeps how often it fell.

It goes in the pocket and stays well.

The verse they do not clap while a man in a soft cuff is on the step

I have this from the child who was gap, with the space left before it, which is the space the bell leaves. She said it to the step with her back turned and made me set it down with none of my verbs in it. A verse clapped to a stone wants nothing added, and a verse clapped to a man wants everything taken away.

Four, four and a gap, says the bell on the tenth;

one in twelve, says the notice, read twice for the late.

We clap in the place where the Court leaves air,

and the corn in the fist has never been there.

The clerk takes the knock and sets it down nil.

The step keeps the dust and the dust keeps still.

Four are four and the ninth is none ---

but the corn goes home, and it is one.

I asked her what the one was. She said it was the corn in her pocket, and that it had been in her mother’s before her, and that she had never once cracked it between her teeth. I have put no word between them either.

What the clerk did

The factor’s clerk borrowed the board, with his cuff gone soft from washing and the ink sitting in two fingers of it and not coming out. He set the game down as a rhythmic instruction in reckoning, imparted to minors. Against four on the knee he wrote four strokes entered. Against the refrain he wrote four, four, rest; no charge arises. At the ninth he struck the children’s words through and set above them, ninth stroke not struck; vacancy; no deduction. Against the corn is whole he wrote one barleycorn retained; no debit to the district. At the foot, where the game ends with the corn going into the pocket, he put a round nought and wrote no result.

I have put their words back underneath and left his standing above. He writes of a thing that is always true and so has no need of a child with a shut fist on a cold step. I write for that child, who wants to know whether the sound she made is still hers once it has left her hand. He has put a nought where a sound stood. A nought lies where it is put and can be carried up the road in a cuff. A sound is gone the moment it is made, which is why no Court in Sarn has ever managed to keep one.

The children stop when a man in a soft cuff comes onto the step. They take it up again when he is past the bend toward Greybank, and they never once let the bell strike the ninth.

The gloss in charcoal, by the woman who sweeps the Cross

She has swept this step since before the Glaze Wars and does not write often, and when she does she writes small, the way you write where somebody is asleep. She pressed one line into the margin beside the refrain:

I have swept thirty years and the dust comes back with the market. The knock in the gap comes back with the children. Neither is entered and both are kept by the step.

I have left it where her thumb put it. She said the bell had been rung by men in clean cuffs for eight hundred years and had never once rung the ninth, and she had heard it every tenth morning of thirty and was not lonely for it.

At the head and in the gutter

The slate dust at the head came off the block where I set the leaf to copy; it goes into the grain and no damp takes it out, which is why the step takes a mark and the market board does not. The ring through the second leaf is a cup of barley water set down at the first verse and taken up at the last, by which time it had gone the colour of the dust it stood on; two children have claimed it and one woman has denied it and I have given it to none. The chip of tile in the fold fell out of a girl’s pocket on the third morning and she did not come back for it, so I shut it in the leaf, where it is lighter than a sound and about as useful. The frost-feather came off the north edge of the step and was gone before I had folded the leaf. The grey along the gutter came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying on a step where children sit; nobody will own it and I have not swept it. There is no Last Day at the Cross and nothing here gets gathered and burnt in the morning. The twist of grey wool through the hole is mine, tied for the word I could not put down. I will not untie it.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

Four and four and the clap under the fist.

The Court has the figure. The child has the sound.

I asked the bell, the gap and a woman at the pot board, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written.

The leaf goes back under the barley-water jar. Tomorrow there will be no market on the step, and two children will sit on it and clap the bell’s count while the bell is not rung, four on the knee and four across and one dry knock under the corn; and if a man with a soft cuff comes up from Greybank I shall put the verbs back and leave him his nought. The corn goes home in a pocket, whole and uncounted, and is eaten on the way down the clay road, which nobody mentions and no slate has a column for.
