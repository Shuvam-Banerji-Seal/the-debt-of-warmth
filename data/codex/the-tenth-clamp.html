I keep the irons. Nine clamps lie on the board at the mouth of the floor and the tenth hangs on its nail behind me, and has hung there since before I came down. A boy asks which is the tenth and is told: the one that goes to the stone. He asks what it does there. Nobody answers him twice, because the answer is a number and he will have it soon enough.

These articles were first gathered beneath the Keep in Y.H. 747, and have been copied forward from the board and not from paper. An article nobody has had to use is an article nobody believes. These have been used. Where a hand has corrected me I have left both readings, because a floor with one hand on it is a floor with one man in it.

The articles as they stand on the board

Of the taking

  1. The Order takes a boy at thirteen, on the day of the Descent, and not before and not after. A house gives him up for a day and has him back by dusk with a number, which may be the number it sent and often is not. The house does not ask for the old one back. The floor has never given one back.
  2. Number first, then the name. The clerk writes the figure before he has heard who the boy is, and if the boy gives his name first he is asked again. This is not pride. A name is a thing a man can change at a moot; a number is cut into the bone and stays when the skin is corrected around it.
  3. No boy is asked whether he wants the way. He is asked whether he understands what is bought: a cut across a surface way in the forearm, held open while it scars, a fortnight in which the arm is of no use, and a mark that will not close while he lives. The master cuts. The wanting comes after, or it does not come; the Order has never claimed to teach that part and has never stopped charging for it.
  4. A lowlander may be taken if he states a measure before a name and keeps his face while it is checked. No master now living has taken one, and no master’s master either, which is three generations and not a rule. The article stands because a rule forbidding a thing has to be read aloud every time the thing arrives, and the floor has better uses for a morning.

In the coal-pressed hand of the master of the floor, against article 2: Number first. Then the name. Everyone in Keld knows this. Underneath, in mine: I had written that the figure goes first so that a boy cannot change it with a smile. The master has struck the smile. There is no smile in this room after the first cut, so the rule is for the morning before, when a boy still thinks a face will do instead of a figure. I have left both.

Of the cut

  1. The way is opened across a surface way in the forearm and kept open while it scars. The master cuts it. No boy cuts his own and no boy cuts another’s; a way made by a friend is a way two men must answer for, and this floor has room for one answer.
  2. The arm is useless for a fortnight. On the fifteenth day the boy is given a clamp to hold. A boy who cannot hold it is given the keys instead, which is the kindness the Keep shows to men who cannot descend, and is not a kindness. He will keep the keys all his days and will know what they open and will never open it.
  3. Cold iron is set to draw the heat out of a cut that will not settle. The iron takes as well as it draws. A master who tells a boy the iron only draws is selling him the fortnight twice, and the boy finds it out in the sixth week when his fingers go the colour of skimmed milk gone off and he cannot feel the clamp he is holding.
  4. A way once cut is never closed. Men have closed them with grafts and with salt and with the long cold beyond the Pass, and the way comes back in the season when the stone sweats, and aches before the thaw and after it. A boy who says his arm aches is told the weather will have the last word. A boy who says it twice is given a warm-stone out of somebody’s fire, which is theft from an account the boy did not open and is entered nowhere.

In the leaning hand of a leech come up from the Ninth Gallery, against article 7: The hand comes back on the sixth day or not at all. Do not set the clamp again higher up and call it a second chance. In mine, underneath: I have set his line above the rule where the master can see it. He struck it out twice in the winter and wrote it again both times, which is how a leech argues with a floor.

Of the floor

  1. Nine clamps go to the floor and one goes to the stone. The tenth hangs behind the board and is not taken down. A master who takes it down must answer to the Warden’s roll for the answer, and the Warden’s roll asks its questions in a voice nobody down here has learned.
  2. A clamp is set across a way, never across a bone. A man who clamps a bone has stopped nothing and broken something, and the something does not grow back.
  3. The first man to open a way usually wins, because the other is busy bleeding. This is not a trick and is not taught as one. It is the whole of what the floor is for, and a boy who learns it as a trick will use it on a man who has already lost, and will not know why he won.
  4. A hand that goes the colour of old rope below a clamp is left alone. The clamp comes off; the hand is wrapped; nobody sets the iron again above the place where it failed. A man who has lost the use of a hand is still a man with a number, and the number stands whether the hand lifts a cup or not.
  5. Hot iron is put to the palm when the roll wants to know whether a man is honest. The honest come back with a hand gone dead. We have had a great many honest men under this Keep and not one of them lied, and nine in ten of them cannot lift a cup. No man is put to the iron for a question the roll has already answered.

The table of hands

Kept by the clerk at the mouth of the floor. A number is written before a name, and where the number has been changed the second is set beside the first without a mark, which is the only honest thing a clerk can do.

Condition — What is entered — What gathers — Discharged

Boy at the Descent — the number, before the name — a mark in the forearm — on the fifteenth day, if the arm answers

Boy whose number changes — the second number, no mark beside it — nothing entered — never; both numbers stand

Boy who cannot hold a clamp — the keys — a hand that does not close — never

Man gone cold in a season — the number in the floor’s roll — grey at the fingertips — not discharged

Lowlander taken — measure, then name — the master’s answer — as for a Keldish boy

The tenth clamp — the nail — nothing — never

  1. The roll is kept under the board and a Court clerk may read it but may not change the order of the two entries. Last winter one of them set a name above a number and wrote the vessel was re-entered in the margin, which is a true sentence in a language nobody on this floor speaks. I have put the number back above it. A name can be entered in a second column; a number cannot, because it is already in the arm.

Of faults and their correcting

Fault — As corrected on the floor

Name before number — Asked again; the number set above

A verb in the roll — Struck by the Court’s hand; set back underneath by mine

A clamp set on bone — Taken off; the boy set to counting irons for a tend

A way opened by a second boy — Left to heal as it can; both boys lose the fortnight

A hand gone rope-coloured — The clamp removed; no second chance above it

The tenth clamp taken down — The master’s own number entered against it

A question about what comes up through the stone — Answered once: the numbers are correct

  1. The last row is not a punishment and is not a rule about clamps. It is the only answer the floor has for the stone, and a boy who asks a second time is told to ask the Vorr, who will say it once in a voice like a man using a tool he has no use for, and will say it twice: the numbers are correct.

The iron-song, pasted inside the cover

Sung on the fifteenth day, in the room off the floor, with the clamp laid on the table and not in the hand. Nobody sings it on the stone and nobody sings it at the Descent. The boy whose fortnight is out says the middle verse, and nobody says his name.

Nine on the board and one on the nail,

the tenth is kept for the stone;

count the irons, count the head,

and give the number, then the name.

One cut across the living way,

a mark to keep and a fortnight’s cold;

the iron takes what the iron draws,

and the boy pays before the floor.

Four to the arm and a fifth gone wrong,

the hand goes rope and the rope stays put;

do not set it higher and call it a chance,

for a number stands and a hand does not.

Nine on the board and one on the nail,

the tenth is kept for the stone;

the master cleans what the master cut,

and the boy says his number, then his name.

What the borrowed hands did

A survey-clerk of the Court borrowed the leaf when the survey table sat at the Keep, ink settled in two fingers of his soft cuff. He struck the arm is useless for a fortnight and set above it the vessel was clamped, which is a thing always true and therefore true of every boy who ever came down. Against the nail he put a round nought and wrote vacancy beside it. I have put the nail back. A nail is a place and a place is not a number; he has spent his whole trade putting figures where places stood, and has never once been thanked for it.

The master of the floor struck the word taught out of article 3 and wrote above it that will hold. Don’t make me say it twice. I have left taught underneath, because a boy should be able to see the word the master would not let stand. He cleaned his clamp while he did it and did not look at me.

The leech’s objection has been struck twice and written twice; I have set the second above the first. A nail, a nought, a hand, a number: four things on one leaf, and no man has joined them.

At the head and in the gutter

The coal at the head came off the master’s sleeve when he leaned to read the third verse standing and would not sit down; he said a song that had never been in a mine was not worth the paper. The hot lead at the fold came off a cuff on the day a passing was measured two hours early and we all went up faster than we had any right to; it has burnt one word of the fifth article clean through and I have not written the word back in. The filings in the gutter are off the clamps on the floor. The scorch on the second leaf is a spark that caught while I was writing and went out under my hand; the hand was mine and I have not entered the burn. The tallow thumb is mine, from holding the sheet down in the draught that comes up the shaft. The frost on the third leaf came up out of the paper on the climb over the Cold Pass and is the only thing in this book that entered itself. The splinter sewn into the cover is warm-stone from the Ninth, and is why the leaf is a little warm in the morning; somebody paid for that warmth and his name is not in here. The grey along the outer fold is not mine and not the Court’s, and I have not swept it. In the clay country they leave the ash in the corners on the Last Day, burn it in the morning and call it dealt with. Under the Keep the dust has nowhere to go.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The floor keeps the number. The stone keeps the hand. Nobody keeps the boy who gave both.

I have asked the master, the clerk who keeps the numbers and the leech come up from the Ninth Gallery, and had three answers and no hand, as I have had before. I have left the line where it was written. The leaf goes back under the iron board and the tenth clamp goes back on its nail. Tomorrow a boy will be brought down at thirteen and will give his number before his name, and the master will clean the clamp while he asks the second question, and whatever he answers will be entered in the same neat hand as the boys who stayed warm. The nine will go out on the board and the tenth will stay where it has hung every morning since before I came down.
