I keep the room where the men drink after the lime draw. I take their names for their ale and give the names back at the end of the tend when the factor’s man comes up from Greybank, which is a way of keeping accounts without owning them. I do not sing. I write down what is sung, which is quieter work and needs a better ear than singing does, though no man has ever said so.

The white at the head of this leaf is lime, not Ash. There is Ash enough in this room without bringing it into a book, and I have not swept either. A woman who sweeps before she writes will write a different song, and it will be a true one and no use to anybody.

The manner of it

Written small at the head, in charcoal, with one word gone over in tallow.

  • It is not sung at the face. A man who sings at the face has not yet learned where to put his feet, and the bank-man will send him home.
  • It is called after the lime draw, when the kiln has given up and the men have washed as far as their wrists. It is not a song for the kiln month. In Ember the kiln is working and a man’s heat is already spoken for.
  • The bank-man says the first line of a part and the room says the line after it. The lift belongs to everybody and is said into the cup, not across the table. A man who calls a lift loudly has to mean it, and there is not one man in this room who wants to be meant.
  • If a man in a clean coat comes up the road from Greybank, stop at the end of the part. Begin again when he has gone back down it. Nobody in Stonemouth will say why we stop, and the man in the clean coat never asks, which is how we have kept the habit for thirty years.

The lift, which ends every part:

Lift to the stone; it outlasts the name.

White hands, full cup, and the rest goes round.

At the face

Seven to the hole and the powder in,

stand where the bank-man tells you to stand;

the stone gives a note that is not a note

and the dust comes up white on the land.

The rope burns true and the cart goes down,

the sandstone answers dull when the wedge is set;

Lift to the stone; it outlasts the name.

White hands, full cup, and the rest goes round.

The note is not the full note of a pot and no man here has called it that. A kiln-master from Sill come up to look at our stone tapped a block with his knuckle and said it answered dull, and went away with his hands in his coat. He was not wrong. We do not tap the wall to ask it anything. We stand where we are told to stand.

The lime draw

The kiln takes the hill and gives back the lime,

white in the creases and bitter to the touch;

you lift with your sleeve and you set with your hand

and you pay for the hand in the thaw, not now.

It burns in the nail and it burns in the cuff,

and a man comes out of it pale as a pot;

Lift to the stone; it outlasts the name.

White hands, full cup, and the rest goes round.

A pot is honest. I have set a pot down on this bench for twenty years and known by the sound of it what it would do. I have never known a man that way, and I have stopped wanting to. The lime takes the print of a hand and keeps it after the hand has gone, which is a trick the Court would like if it had thought of it.

The water

The Ferr comes down with the dust of the west,

cold in the throat and sweet in the sleep;

three hundred went thin on a stream with no name

and the Court wrote the figure and called it a war.

It ran then as it runs now, downhill and cold,

and the women of High Kiln drank it for years;

Lift to the stone; it outlasts the name.

White hands, full cup, and the rest goes round.

My mother was of High Kiln and she would not let me drink the Ferr, which I thought was a novelty of hers and am now old enough to know was a judgment. She never said why. When the physicians came down from Greybank in the year the sweetness was written about, she let them look at her gums and gave them a cup of barley water and sent them back up the road with nothing they wanted. She died thin but not sweet, and I have kept that as a thing to be careful with rather than a thing to be proud of.

The verse they do not sing before a man from Greybank

They take this one up when the clerk has gone back down the road with his satchel under his arm. I have it from the bank-man, with the space left before it, which is the space they leave in the singing.

One in twelve, says the notice, read twice at the Cross;

the cart counts four and the kiln gives back one;

and the one that is left is the one at the bench

where a man sat down and did not get up.

The cup goes round and the cup comes back,

the name goes up and the name comes down;

Lift to the stone; it outlasts the name,

and the place at the end of the bench stays set.

I asked the bank-man whose bench it was. He said every bench in Stonemouth has an end, and went to see to the kiln. There is a man who used to sit there whose name I still take for an ale on the tend’s last day and still give back when the factor’s man comes. I have not entered him anywhere else. A name kept in a room is a different weight from a name kept on a slate, and I have found that a woman can carry the one when the men beside her have put the other down.

What the face-man wrote in the margin

Against the verse about the Ferr, a man who has worked the face for thirty years wrote in a hand that shakes at the ends of words:

Two of mine went thin on that water. The slate says none. I have put the two where a clerk can see them if he ever looks away from his paper.

He brought me the leaf himself and stood at the door while I read it, and took it back to the face without a word about the ale he had not been given. I have kept his two in the margin where he set them. The room has never once sung that verse with the two in it, and the men know it is there.

What the clerk did

The clerk come up from Greybank borrowed the leaf on a market morning, being a man who cannot leave paper alone. He has a cuff gone soft from washing and the ink sits in two fingers of it and will not come out. He struck out it outlasts the name and set above it, the measure is recorded and the residue nil. Against the lime he wrote entered in kind, which is a true figure and a cold one. Against three hundred went thin he wrote one in twelve, as the notice says, and numbered the lines from one to four so that a man could read them without singing. Where the lift stands he put a small square rule and wrote no vacancy in the roll. He took the verbs out of two lines as though a verb were a thing a man could carry off in a cuff and sell at the next crossing.

I have put the words back underneath his and left his standing above. He can enter the lime in kind; the kiln has given back the same weight for twenty years and nobody has disputed it. He can enter the stream as a war, because there is a war on a slate somewhere and it saves a man the trouble of looking at the water. What he cannot enter is the place at the end of the bench, and he has not tried. A clerk knows the difference between a figure and a place better than a woman who keeps a room has any right to, and he has made his living on it.

He scraped a word off the lower leaf and would not say which. I have left the bare square where it stood. A word can be taken off paper; the space it leaves is harder, because the paper remembers where it was and the eye keeps going back to it. This is the whole of the account between this room and Greybank, and neither of us has settled it.

At the head and in the gutter

The lime at the head came off my own cuff when I held the leaf down in the draught under the door, and it has worked into the grain where nothing will get it out. The blue-grey along the fold is the Ferr: a man came in from the weir head with wet sleeves and set his cup on the leaf while he was telling the bank-man something about the upstream water, and the tell was lost when the water dried and the blue stayed. The ring is mine, from a cup I set down before the first question and took up after the last, which is how this house measures a conversation. The tallow is off the lamp I kept low while I wrote, so that the men at the table would not know I was writing and change the words for me. The grit in the gutter is coarse sandstone and came out of a boot, not a pocket; no man in this room carries stone away, and I have never asked the boot. The grey along the outer fold came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying. I have not swept it. There is no Last Day in a cracked pot behind a door.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The bench keeps a place and the cup keeps a ring;

the stone knows the both and says neither thing.

I asked the room whose it was and had three answers and no hand, as I have had before about the grey print and the scraped word. I have set it down as it stands. The leaf goes back into the cracked pot behind the door, and tomorrow the draw comes again and the men lift to the stone, which does not answer and asks nothing of anybody, and the place at the end of the bench stays set.
