I keep the south bridge and the road-rail at its foot. I render a sum to the moot at the Opening and keep what is over it, which is my wage, and make good what falls short, which is my debt. This book is not a board; a board is for a man who has not paid. I set down what the market cried at the Cross, because a load is worth what it fetched before it came to me, and what I gathered at the rail, and the two columns do not agree, and the moot’s figure agrees with neither.

Twelve copper bits to the silver mark; twenty marks to a Sere crown that comes north and cannot be spent here. The Court books in embers and calls five marks an ember. I have no column for embers, because no ember has ever crossed my bridge and a column for nothing is a thing a man cannot render to a room with the door shut.

In the moot clerk’s narrow hand, above my first line: Coin and reckoning entered in the ordinary way. Render of the bridge: as assessed. Underneath, in mine: There is nothing ordinary about a bit a man carried over wet boards in his boot. As assessed is a phrase with nobody in it. I have put the lamp and the stick back.

The market I price by

The left column is the figure cried under the moot-post on Account-day, when the bell goes four, four and a gap. The right is what I can take from the same load when it reaches me. I do not say these are the same thing, and the moot does.

Article — Cried at the Cross — What I can take at the rail

Brown-edged barley loaf — 1 bit — nothing; it comes in a basket on a woman’s arm

Salt herring — 2 bits — nothing; it comes off the north bridge, where the Fishstairs argued it and won

Smoked eel — 3 bits; salted, 2 — nothing at this rail; the eel-women pay at the boards

Coarse pots, the score — 4 bits — 1 bit on the cart that carries them; the pots themselves are not counted

Tile, the hundred — 9 bits — 1 bit on the cart, and not on the tile

Peat, a barrow — 2 bits in Grain, 5 in Stone — 1 bit if it comes on a cart; in Stone I have taken it off a man on foot and not entered him

Pig, over the weighing board — 6 marks — 4 bits for the drove and a bit to the weigher to have it cried twice

Salt, a sack off the pans — 3 marks — nothing here; the coast pass takes its mark where I cannot see it

Iron, a bar, Keldish — 2 marks in the lowlands — nothing; the Rim takes its mark at the pass and the bar comes down unweighed

Rope, a coil — 1 mark — 1 bit where it rides on a cart; it has left tar in this book

Barley gruel at a house door — not sold; given, and not entered — nothing, and the only thing I have never tried to count

Margin, in mine: A thing carried on a person pays nothing; a thing carried on a cart pays a bit. That is not a law about value. It is a law about wheels. A man with a pig on his back is worth less to this bridge than a man with an empty cart, and I have entered it as it stands.

In the moot clerk’s hand: What I can take is struck and Rate of the gate written above it. In mine: A rate sounds like a thing decided in a room where nobody stood in the rain. I have put my words back underneath and left his above.

The tolls of the roads that come down here

Four roads come to Sill and a fifth which is the river. I do not gather on all of them; a toll is only as good as a man’s legs, and mine end at this rail. What follows is what I took, and beside it what the moot expected me to take when I accepted the bridge, which the moot calls the render and I have called the bet in this book and nowhere else.

Road and place — Due — The moot’s figure — What I rendered

South bridge, loaded cart — 1 bit — 900 bits — 611 bits; the steep street carries the rest past me

South bridge, foot passenger — 1 bit; nothing on Account-day — 300 bits — 94 bits; the most people come on the days I may not charge

Drove of pigs, going up to the block — 4 bits — 240 bits — 176 bits, eight pigs on a market-day and none on some

Clay Road, cart with a load, at this end — 2 bits — the weigher’s — not mine; taken at the block before the cart comes down to me

River road, a beast bound for the Weir — 1 bit — 120 bits — 71 bits, gathered by a boy I pay out of my own column

Plank walk at the Weir — 1 bit — 80 bits — 33 bits; the boards move after rain and he will not stand on them

North bridge, any load or passenger — nothing — nil — nil; the Fishstairs argued it and won

Nine Pots road, west from the Cross — no gate; a pot set down at the feud post — 6 marks — nothing; neither village will let a collector stand on it

Fen boardwalk to the Standing Post — 1 bit for a dry foot; a marked stick for a wet one — not entered — none; the Fen reeves keep their own count

Winter ford, when there is water to cross — 1 bit — 180 bits — 41 bits; there were days the river was a road of ice and no collector could stand

Hearth Road, at the barrier — none; there is no barrier — none — I have kept the empty row so a carter can see I am not hiding it

Margin: The moot’s column is a sum made with the door shut. Mine is a sum made with wet feet. The difference is not theft; it is the steep street, which carries half the carts past my rail while the moot counts every cart as though it had come over the boards.

In the moot clerk’s narrow hand, against the render at the north bridge: Nil, as agreed, the fish-trade being exempt by long usage. Underneath, in mine: I did not agree. My father did, and I took the bridge with the argument already lost. A wrong figure agreed to by a man who is dead is the strongest figure in the moot’s book.

The surety’s note, in a leaning hand with a little gold in the ink: The sureties stand to the render, not to the gathering. In mine: They stand to it with my house. I have set this above the table so that it is read before the figures and not after.

The charges of the farm

These are not tolls. No slate takes them and the moot’s book has no column for them. They go out of my pocket before the render is counted.

For — The figure

A boy at the plank walk, the season — 40 bits, and he keeps what he can from the wet boards

Oil for the lamp at the rail, the year — 2 marks; the wind off the river takes half of it

A bit to the weigher when a pig is cried twice and the seller has only a half — I pay it and never ask it back; a pig not cried comes back over my bridge at dusk

A pot set down at the Nine Pots feud post — 4 bits the score, and nobody will say whether it is an insult or a toll

Word of which boards bear at Reedholt — 1 mark to a man who told me and then went over them himself in the dark

Margin: The last row is the only thing in this book I have ever paid for and been given. The man would not say how he knew. I have entered the mark and left the knowing out, because a figure with an answer attached gets copied, and a copied answer gets read aloud.

The song at the rail

Sung when the lamp is lit and nobody is crossing. Never at the Cross, and never while a man from Slate Row is on the bridge.

The moot wrote a sum and I rendered less,

and the steep street took what the bridge forgot;

a cart has a wheel and a man has a shoe,

and the wheel pays a bit and the shoe pays nothing too.

Four roads come down and the river comes down,

and the river goes under and never comes round;

the north bridge is nothing and says so aloud,

and the grey goes out north with no toll and no crowd.

In the moot clerk’s hand, against the last line: No figure arising from weather. In mine: He is right. The sleet is not a figure and the oil is. I have kept both rows.

In the head and in the gutter

The tar at the head is bridge tar, from leaning on the rail while the lamp was lit; no washing takes it out. The silt in the gutter came in on the boots of every cartman since the thaw, and is the same silt that lies under the sill, which the moot does not count and the river does. The pig bristle in the second leaf came off a drove that went over with a man holding one ear and my boy the other; it points east, the way the figures went. The salt bloom along the fore-edge is off the Sallow wind and eats the moot’s column faster than mine. The green fleck in the lower corner is lead glaze brought down on the wind off High Kiln; a woman leaned to see what her pig had fetched, with the sweetness still at one corner of her mouth, touched the leaf, said nothing and went back up the steep street. The grey in the fold is off my own thumb; I have swept it four times and it comes back with the cold. The bare square is a figure I scraped out, which was the true one and would not agree with the render. There is no Last Day at a bridge. The tar stays, the silt comes back with the thaw, and nothing here is burnt and called dealt with.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The moot counts what crossed. The bridge counts what stayed. The river counts neither, and takes the road in winter.

I asked the weigher, a carter who takes the steep street, and a man who would not say which side of the rail he stood on, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written.

The quire goes back into the tin under the loose board. At the Opening the clerk will read the render in a clean voice and nobody will ask how the carts got past me. I shall take the bridge again on the same terms and keep what is over, which is sometimes a wage and sometimes a debt, and the roads will yield what they yield, which is neither figure. Tomorrow a man will come down off the Cross with a pig and ask what the bridge costs. I shall tell him a bit if he rides and nothing if he walks, and by evening both will be things that were true.
