I cut numbers at the Descent and cut them again at the mouth. The first knife is at thirteen; the second is after the bank, when a man comes back with the old figures still on him and wants a place he cannot name. I have set two counts where a name would stand, because here a man states what he holds before he says who he is, and I am not going to break that rule in my own hand. Which of the two I answer to depends on who is calling.

This is what we say over the returned arm. We do not say it to the man: said to a man it becomes a question, and there is no figure for a question at this mouth, so the question goes away unanswered and the figure stays. The clerk from Greybank has been at the leaf with his verbs taken out, and his hand stands above mine where he set it. The iron-red hand in the margin belongs to a woman cut twice who keeps both counts in one skin. I have left all of it standing, as the earlier numbers are left showing under the later ones.

The manner of it, cut small at the head

  • Bare the arm before the words. A man who cannot speak can still state what he holds, and the arm does it for him.
  • Do not cut the old numbers out until the answer has been said. They came back with him. They have a claim, even if the bank has entered them closed.
  • Give the warm-stone after the knife and never before. A stone given before the knife is a bribe to the arm, and the arm remembers who bribed it.
  • Where a man carries two sets, cut the later deeper and leave the earlier showing. The mine-reeve enters the later. We cut both.
  • The Vorr come down from the Stones and do not answer. Leave the length of their silence standing. Do not put a nought in it; a nought is a number, and they have refused the number.
  • Say it to the arm, low, the way you would speak to a pot before you knock it. If an officer of the Court is at the mouth, give him the new count and not the leaf. He can enter a figure. He cannot enter an arm.

The litany

The cutter reads from the arm. The order answers by number. The answer is the same at every station, which is why it is a litany and not an argument.

I. Of the number before the name

The number goes on the arm before the name goes in the mouth.

A lowlander gives his name and waits for us to ask the other thing.

We send him back to the foot of the post to begin again.

The dark does not care what a man calls himself.

It cares what he carries into it.

Here I turn the arm so the figures stand up. Nobody looks at the face.

The order answers: Cut the number. Keep the hand. Do not ask the stone what it kept.

II. Of the mountain

The Hearth is under this mountain, as our people say.

The Court’s fire is a candle lit from it, they say, and say it softly.

A man came up with a slate and took the word out of our sentence

and the sentence stood up without it,

which is the kind of standing a slate likes.

Here the mine-reeve touches the iron-red cross on the post. Neither man looks at the other.

The order answers: Cut the number. Keep the hand. Do not ask the stone what it kept.

III. Of the first knife

At thirteen the knife goes in and the numbers come up on the skin.

The boy holds the stone after, never before.

He holds it and does not know what has been cut into him.

Nobody tells him twice. A thing cut once is told.

The boy is not in the room. He is at the Descent, holding the stone, finding out what the knife gave.

The order answers: Cut the number. Keep the hand. Do not ask the stone what it kept.

IV. Of the second cutting

He comes back with the old figures on him

and a want for a table he never sat at.

We cut the old ones out. The new ones go in deeper.

The mine-reeve enters the later. We leave the earlier showing in the skin.

Two sets on one arm is not two men.

It is one man counted twice, which the Court has always liked.

Here I cut. There is no ceremony in it, only a small knife, a hand that does not shake, and the time the figures take to come right. The arm knows the knife. The man does not watch.

The order answers: Cut the number. Keep the hand. Do not ask the stone what it kept.

V. Of the eleven months

A bought breath is entered at eleven months,

whether he lasts them or not.

The figure is for the life bought and not the life lived.

A man who lasts fourteen is entered at eleven and is not corrected.

We do not correct him either. We give him the stone.

Here the under-vessed man at the back of the order puts out his arm. His figure was bought before he came up. The figure is not his and the arm is.

The order answers: Cut the number. Keep the hand. Do not ask the stone what it kept.

VI. Of the clamp

One iron holds and one iron takes. Here they are the same bite.

A man who holds a clamp to his palm without flinching

is called honest, and this Keep is full of honest men with dead hands.

Say the number over the hand. It has earned the saying.

Do not ask the hand whether it was worth it. It will not tell you.

A man who held a clamp at the Warden’s test lays his palm on the board. We say the number over the hand and not over the man. The hand knows the difference.

The order answers: Cut the number. Keep the hand. Do not ask the stone what it kept.

VII. Of the sweating

The mountain sweats in the deep.

The smell of hot lead gives whatever warning it gives.

Nothing was said in the Ninth Gallery that could be written down;

a man went down and the lead told him, and he came up or did not.

What the lead tells is not entered. What the lead takes is entered by nobody.

I stop while the rope moves over the mouth. There is no saying for the deep that has not had a man in it. I have tried to write one and left the place the length of it.

The order answers: Cut the number. Keep the hand. Do not ask the stone what it kept.

VIII. Of those who do not answer

Across the Rim they sit against the Stones and come away with numbers.

They do not come down to the knife and do not answer at the mouth.

They say they were never owed.

We have left the length of their silence standing.

Do not fill it. A filled place is an entered place.

Here the order turns toward the Rim. At this station the answer is said by nobody. I say it once into the arm, which is not the same as having it answered, and close the space the length of a man sitting against a stone.

The order answers: Nothing is said here. The place is kept.

IX. Of the stone after

The stone is given after the knife and stays warm longer than the man expects.

He will get worse before he gets better. That is the rule here as everywhere.

If he comes back wrong, keep him. Keep him anyway.

Do not ask the stone what it kept.

It will tell you the weather and nothing else.

I put the warm-stone in the hand that has just been cut. It is the whole of the mercy and the whole of the theft. Somebody paid to have it warm, and the man holding it does not ask, which is the only courtesy the mountain has ever been shown.

The order answers: Cut the number. Keep the hand. Do not ask the stone what it kept.

What the clerk did

A clerk of the factor’s office came up with the lead cart and borrowed the leaf at the mouth on a morning when the rope was going down and nobody had a hand to spare. He writes narrow and upright and takes the verbs out as though a verb were a thing a man could carry past a weigh in his boot. He laid his hand on the leaf before he looked at me, which is the manner of that office.

  • Against the number goes on the arm before the name, he wrote: vessel identified by measure at entry. He has left the mouth out, and the mouth is where the number has to go before anybody will hear it.
  • Against the Hearth is under this mountain, he wrote: claim not admitted; the Hearth is entered under the Keel. He has not argued with the mountain. He has entered somewhere else and gone away.
  • Against we leave the earlier showing in the skin, he wrote: superseded entry retained, not current. True in the figure. I have seen a man with two sets on one arm and both hands answered when his mother called, and there is no figure for that in a counting house.
  • Against two sets is not two men, he wrote: duplicate entry. One to be struck. He strikes men readily. The skin does not agree with him and has never been asked.
  • At the eighth station he put a round nought in the space and wrote no return required. This is the truest thing he has written and the cruelest. A nought is a number. They refused the number. I have left his nought standing in the space and my space standing around it, as a crack is left showing under a keying.

I have left all of it standing, his hand above mine and mine under it, as an arm keeps the first figures under the second. He takes the verbs out because a thing always true has no need of them. I put them back because a man at the mouth wants to know which hand to hold out.

What the other hand put in the margin

The iron-red hand is Nessa’s, of the Duelling Floor, cut at thirteen and cut again after the bank, who keeps both counts and has never said which she answers to when somebody calls across a room.

At IV: “My second was cut deeper and I still turn to the first when my mother calls. The mine-reeve entered the deeper. Both hands turned. I have never known which one she was calling to.”

At V: “My brother was entered at eleven and lasted fourteen. On the last morning he asked for the stone and they gave him the figures. I have set this here and not in the stations, because the stations are said over an arm and this is said over a man.”

At VI: “My father held the clamp and was called honest. He ate with the other hand for nine years and never once said the word they used of him.”

At VIII, against they were never owed: “I asked one of them at the Stones whether they were cold. He asked me whether I had ever been cut. I did not ask him anything else.”

At the answer: “Say it over the hands. The rest of us are standing here and can hear.”

At the head and in the gutter

The smut at the head is mine; I hold the leaf down with the hand I cut with and do not know I have marked it, and coal gets into the grain and no wet sleeve takes it out. The red cross is iron oxide off the mine-reeve’s point and stays red in frost. The warm-stone shaving in the fold came off a piece set down and not taken up, and is still warm on the coldest morning, which is the whole argument about where warmth comes from and who paid. The lead bead rolled out of the Ninth Gallery and a surveyor laid it on the leaf and went away without it. The frost-feather grew on the survey stone after a warm night and was gone by noon. The clamp-scratch across the answer was made by a man showing his brother how a clamp bites; both were called honest afterwards and one has a dead hand. The grey on the outer fold came off a cuff that lay where no cuff has business lying, and I have not swept it. The thumb in coal dust at the foot is not mine and not the mine-reeve’s. There is no Last Day at a shaft mouth.

At the foot

In a hand that is not mine, not the clerk’s and not Nessa’s, struck once and written again underneath, very small:

The knife gives before the bank takes.

The arm keeps two counts. The slate keeps one. The stone keeps none, and is warm.

I asked the cutter at the Descent, a woman cut twice, a man entered at eleven who lasted fourteen, and one of the Vorr at the Stones, who answered me with a question, and had four answers and no hand. I have left the struck line where it was struck. The leaf goes back into the tin at the mouth. Tomorrow a boy will go down at thirteen and hold the stone after the knife, and a man come back thin will hold out his arm, and the old numbers will be cut out and the new cut in deeper, and the mine-reeve will enter the later and leave the earlier showing in the skin, and I shall say nine stations to the arm and keep the eighth place the length of a man sitting against a stone.
