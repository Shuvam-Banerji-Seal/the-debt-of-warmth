To my sister of the Ember house, who keeps the seventh name and will not say which of the seven she kept. From the north, from a room with good light that I am not permitted to enjoy, in the counting house of the man who brought me.

Sister, I have taken the first ring off before I began, and the second is off and lying by the ink, and the third I have left on because I have not yet decided whether what follows is true enough to cost it. You know the habit. You have watched me remove them one at a time since we were girls, and you have never once asked why, which is a kinder thing than asking and being told.

I write on the back of a grain docket from the factor’s granary, because there is no paper in this room that is mine, and a letter on a man’s paper is a thing a man may read if he has a mind to. The smell on it is myrrh. I have begun to smell of it myself, which in the Bowl would make you laugh and here makes the serving women put a hand to their mouths, the way these lowland folk cross themselves when a thing is said badly. I have not crossed myself. I am a woman of the Ten Bowls and I know what a bowl is for.

It is the third day after a feast and my hand is not steady. Look at the line that runs out toward the gutter and then stops. I have not straightened it, and I have not written the word again. The priests at home call the third-day tremor the price of beauty. Here they say nothing at all, because nobody in Greybank has ever seen a beautiful return and would not know a price if one were laid in their hand. The wine on the leaf came from the public bowl and went cold while I was finding the word I wanted, which is the whole history of warmth in the north in one small circle on a counting-house docket.

Why I am writing, which is not the ring on my finger

There are three of me and there may be no fourth. A woman with three deaths ought to set them down in her own hand before a clerk sets them down in his, because a clerk will write them as returns entered: three and leave the deaths standing out of the sentence where they cannot be read. I was returned in the year of the green bowl. No --- the third one. I was returned in the year of the green bowl and I have had two since. You were at the first feast. You stood where the fire in the court lit the whole bowl of us, and you clapped for the debt and not for the girl, which was correct, and which I have never quite forgiven you for being correct about.

The name I answer to — The year — The death that belonged to it — What the new walls let me keep

Solene, the one I chose out of seven — Y.H. 799, the green bowl — the Spending fire, in the open court — the taste of the wine, and the applause for the debt

Ilyra, which was our mother’s — Y.H. 804 — the fall from the balcony at the feast of Grain; nobody pushed, which is why it was entered as a kindness — the fall itself, and the noise the bowls made below me

Vey, the shortest of the three — Y.H. 809 — quiet ash, given by a woman who smiled the whole while and said it was a mercy — the smile, and nothing at all of the face above it

The third one is the one I remember least and therefore the one I fear. When Valen Openhand asked me whether I was grateful, I said yes three times, and on the third I took a ring off before I said it, and he saw me do it and did not mention it, which was the only mercy in that court that had nobody’s name on it.

When you write back, ask me which death I mean, the way you used to. If I do not say the third one inside a year, I shall know that the part of me that knows the difference has gone, and you may have the room and the light and the rings and welcome to them, but you will not have me to write to.

The song, as we sang it at the end of the feast

They burn the debt-slates of one household by lot when the eating is done, and then they make new ones. I have set the song down as it is sung, going out from the bowl rather than coming back from it. The ends of the lines fall away in the court; I have put them back where I could hear them and left them gone where I could not.

We emptied the bowl and the fire said yes,

we burned the slate and we made it again;

the debt we carried went up with the smoke,

and the bowl stands full for the next of the house.

Seven names at the naming, one kept at the Bowl,

and the one that we kept was the one we let go;

the wine goes down and the fire goes up,

and the slate goes where the slate is owed.

That is how we sang it. I used to think the last line was a courtesy. I have since seen a slate carried out of a room in this country with the same face on the man carrying it, and I no longer think it a courtesy.

What I ask you, which I ask everybody now

What debt do you intend to carry? I ask it of everyone. In the north they think it is a question about money. It is not. When I asked it of a man who sits at a table in this house, he turned a slate face down before he answered me, so that I should understand I was not being entered, and then turned it face-up again when he had done, so that I should understand I had been. He has eleven words for a deduction and not one for sorry. I have tried to teach him the word. He said it was the same absence wearing a different coat, and asked me to write the sentence again without the verb in it, which I did, and which is the most Sarnish thing that has ever been said in Sere.

There is a custom here of leaving the grey Ash in the corners on the last day of the year and burning it the next morning and calling it dealt with. In the Bowl we burn the slate and make a new one, and call that glory. I have stood between the two and cannot tell you which of us is the more honest. A woman here broke a pot at the door of a death-house so that the heat might get out. We would have poured a bowl. I have watched both and I have not decided which lets go the better.

What the factor’s clerk did to this leaf

A clerk of the factor’s office borrowed the leaf on a market morning, being a man who cannot leave paper alone. He writes in the narrow upright hand they teach in the College in the Hearthseat, with the verbs taken out as though a verb were a thing a man could smuggle in a cuff. He set his hand on the top sheet before he looked at me, which is the manner of that table, and said that it was entered as it stood and there was nothing in it I need correct.

  • Against the three names he wrote returns entered: three; fourth not entered.
  • Against the deaths he set a figure for each, as though a fall from a balcony and a smiling woman with quiet ash were the same article at two prices.
  • Where I wrote that I am kept in a room with good light, he wrote the party is accommodated, and underlined it twice.
  • Where I wrote that I have three deaths, he wrote measures current.
  • Beside the name I left empty for the fourth he put a round nought. A nought is a number. The fourth has not come back to be one.

I have left all of it standing, his hand above mine and mine under it, as a repaired pot keeps the old break under the new seam and does not pretend the first hand never touched the clay. He took the verbs out because a thing always true has no need of them. I put them back because a woman writing home wants to know who did what to her sister, and the answer is in the sentence if the sentence has someone in it.

In the margin, in a second hand, warm and sloped, the long vowels open

You always did name the death before you named the guest. Do not tell me you are kept. Tell me which room has the light and whether the window opens, and I shall know whether you can bear to sit in it. We burned the green bowl’s slate at the feast of Smoke and made a new one, and I put your name in it, which is no loss, because a slate that burns is a slate that cannot be read aloud by a man who never met you.

I have asked Valen Openhand what debt I intend to carry and he said the house’s, which was correct and not an answer. Ask me again when the bowl is empty and I will tell you something else. Ask me on the third day and I will lie to you in the warmest voice I have, which you will believe, because you always do.

Keep the third ring. It was our mother’s before it was yours,

and a thing that goes through a mother’s hands

does not stop being hers because a clerk has entered it.

Name the fourth when you have seen it. Not before.

A tide that has not come is still worth more

than a figure written in the rain.

The last of that is not mine; I copied it off a salt girl in a counting house and she charged me nothing and said so at once, so that I should know it was no favour. She is the only person I have met in the north who understands that a debt can be paid and still be owed.

At the head and in the gutter

The wine ring at the head is mine; I set the cup down to find the word and left it standing until it went cold. The myrrh has come up out of the paper itself, or out of the cloth it was folded in, and I have not washed it. The grey bead in the fold is from the public bowl at Greybank, pressed there by a woman who leaned over to see what I was writing and said nothing; it is Ash, and in this country Ash is swept up and burned, and here it is in a letter going south. The green at the lower corner is not myrrh. A girl with the sweetness of the glaze still at one corner of her mouth touched the leaf with her thumb and went away, and I have kept the mark where she put it. The greenish print by the ink is where the third ring lay while I wrote the name Vey, and I have not lifted it or covered it. The line that runs toward the gutter is the third-day hand and I have left it running, because a letter written by a steady hand would be a letter from someone else.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

Three deaths entered, four names carried, and the bowl stands where it stood.

The fire under it is paid for by somebody who will not be named at the feast.

Ask the fourth one when it comes what debt it intends to carry, and listen to what it says before you applaud.
