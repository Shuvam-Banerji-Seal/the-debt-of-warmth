A woman of this house asked me to set down what I do, and I have set it down in the courtesy we use to people we would not disagree with, which is the same courtesy we use to the returned. I have left the errors standing. A physician come down from Greybank pressed his objections into the margin with a hand that writes cold, and I have put my words beneath his where the two disagree, as a bowl is set under a shelf to catch what the shelf will not hold.

The gold at the head is leaf off the tally. It gets into the creases and stays, so that after a year the Court’s figures and my cures look alike. The ring is warm wine from the public bowl, set down while I told a girl to drink and she would not. The myrrh came off the fold the tablets slept in. I have washed none of it away.

The order of the sick-court

Written small in the first leaf, with the word for the guest gone over in wine.

  • Set the sick one’s face toward the fire in the middle of the court, not toward the door. A person who faces the fire can be seen by everybody, which is how we know whether they are going.
  • Ask what debt they intend to carry before you ask what ails them. A person who has named a debt will tell you where it hurts. This is not kindness. It is the order of the room.
  • Turn the slate face down before you go to a child. I learned it from a northern bought breath who put her hand on a slate, turned it, and would say nothing more. I have done it at forty births and never asked her again.
  • Give the guest a place by the door and no bowl of their own. A guest who is given a bowl will ask when the debt falls due.
  • Do not look into a bowl that has a name written inside it. The house puts a name there that the bowl’s owner may never read, and a leech who looks has taken what the household has not spent.

Against the first line the physician wrote, in the cold hand: A vessel overheated in a cold room loses faster when turned to the fire. I have set my line above his. Both stand.

The remedies, as they are used

Trouble — What is set to it — The manner of it — Who is emptied

The third-day tremor — A second bowl emptied in the returned one’s name, and applause — It comes on the third day after a beautiful return, when the hand will not hold the cup. We say beauty must be paid for, and pour again. The physician says it is the head rising after too much was poured, and that wine adds to it. I have poured and watched the hand steady for an hour and worse by evening. Both stand. — The household, in wine; the returned one, in another measure.

The sweet (the pale line at the gum) — Scrape the green off a bowl into wine; a sweet answers a sweet — The green comes north from the Glaze Cliffs in bowls, and the women here taste the mix for colour. The physician says it is lead in the glaze and that scraping a bowl into wine takes more of it. I have seen the line go off a girl who left the furnaces and come back on one who stayed. I have not seen the lead. — The furnace, which cannot stop and does not ask.

The fever that cooks from within — Bleed at the elbow, to let the heat out — The Spending says a vessel emptied is a vessel saved. The physician says bleeding lets the measure out and not the fever. I bled four; two were quiet by evening and did not wake. Quiet is not better. I have written it and gone on bleeding. — The patient, in the arm.

The guest that wants another house — Seven names spoken at the door; a bowl set where the old bowl stood — She calls a name not given in this house and reaches for a door that is not in the room. We say a returned one has brought a guest, and give the guest the name until it goes. The physician says a spoken name puts no shape back, and he is right as a man who has never held one of these. One went off by the seventh naming; one kept it a year. — Nobody. The house, in a year it cannot spend.

A graft that will not take — Tallow-wasp resin at four marks a nest; a donor out of the same house if one can be found — The resin holds the wall until the vessel takes; a nest is four marks in the south and is burnt in Grath. The physician says resin holds a wall but not a want, and a wall from a donor shaped otherwise takes anyway and wants. I have seen it take and want, and never a donor who did not go thin. — The donor, in a head wine will not bring back.

The northern cold that stays — A bowl-fire at the belly, warm wine, and work carrying bowls — The bought breath comes south with a foot gone dark inside three turns of wool, is shown at a feast and given small coins, and is then brought to me. The extremity goes first and the heart holds out, and there is an hour on the far side of the warming. The physician says a cold vessel cannot clot and that a foot set to the fire is a road to losing it. I set them to work and have watched toes go, and gone on. — The bought breath, who cannot stop. This is the row where I have been least honest.

A cut from the public bowl — Wine and myrrh, then a clean fold; never the grey out of the bowl — The priests put cremation ash from the public bowl on a cut, believing a vessel spent is a vessel cleansed. I have found grey under the skin in three such cuts and could not get it out. A family buys its ash back at the price of quiet ash, and a feast can ruin a house. — The cut, in a white line it keeps.

The name that will not come — Seven names on a glazed tablet in gold ink, read at the door — After a return the name comes late and then wrong. Gold ink outlasts the name on the tablet, not in the head. The last of the seven goes first; I have left the space where it stood the length a name of that kind wants. — Nobody. The tablet, which keeps what the head cannot.

In this house nothing is given. It is emptied out of one vessel into another and called generosity. I will stand behind that sentence and behind no other in the book.

The note under the knuckle

The bowl-surgeons tap green ware before they set a graft, and a bowl that answers full will take a wall where a flat one will not. The priests tap the public bowl at a feast and say the emptier it is the truer it rings. I have tapped a bowl before wine and after wine, and the note was the same: a sound bowl answers full and a cracked one answers flat. I have not put this to the priests. A leech who corrects a priest at a feast is a leech who will be tending nobody by the next one.

I tapped a returned woman once, gone thin after a third pouring, and put my ear to the little flat I thought I heard. The flat was not in her. I have written it here, where a woman with a slate may read it; saying it in a room with a slate in it is another matter.

On the beautiful return

They come back calm. The returned one sits in the good light, wants nothing, takes the cup when given and applauds the debt when the bowl is emptied. On the third day the hand begins to shake. The priests call it the price of beauty, and I have called it that for nineteen years. It is a name that lets a household pour the second bowl without asking what the first one took.

I asked the physician what he called it. He said the head rises when too much has been poured, that memory goes before appetite and appetite before the walls, and that calm of that kind is a burn. He wrote it in the margin and went off with a purse, which is the way physicians go. I have set his sentence above mine and left the second bowl standing underneath. A woman at three in the morning does not want a true sentence. She wants a hand to hold the cup, and I have held it.

The song for the third day

Sung low, to the returned one and not to the guest. The room says the last line with her. There is a place after it the length of a seventh name, and no singer has yet filled it.

Seven for the names and one for the guest,

the gold keeps the word that the head forgets;

the green bowl is sweeter than the wine,

the third-day hand will not hold mine.

Empty the bowl and the debt is named,

the house applauds and the fire is the same;

the northern foot goes dark in the wool,

and the leech sets the bought breath to work.

Two for the ash and two for the resin,

two for the hand that carries the glass;

the guest comes in by the door uncalled,

and the name goes out where the gold ink was.

Sing to the fire, sing to the flat bowl,

sing to the woman the guest would know;

what comes back is not what went,

and comes back as it can ---

A factor’s clerk put a nought in the place after the last line and wrote vacancy beneath it. A nought is a figure and can be carried out of the room in a cuff. A seventh name is a place and stays where it is.

What the physician pressed in the margin

He came down from Greybank in Smoke of Y.H. 811 with a book that had the word lead in it and a hand that wrote without asking. Against the seven names he set no efficacy. Against the guest he set a disorder of the memory, not a visitor. Against the Spending he wrote, the measure goes out and the head does not, which is a true thing in a cold language and of no use whatever to a woman whose sister has come back calm and will not say whose child she is.

He was right about the lead and wrong about the guest. I have left his three sentences above mine, so the next reader can tell which is which.

What the factor’s clerk did

A clerk of the factor borrowed the leaves at Greybank when the glass was being counted. His ink sits in two fingers of his cuff and the cuff was soft at the edge from washing. He took the verbs out of we empty the bowl and call a debt forgiven and wrote the debt is discharged above it, as though a thing always true had no need of anybody doing it. Against my column headed Who is emptied he wrote No cost entered, which is a true thing in a dishonest place. Where I had set the donor going thin, he wrote the account is retained in the nest, which is the most beautiful sentence I have ever read in a hand that has never held a donor’s arm.

I have put my verbs back underneath his. A clerk can speak without verbs because he is never in the room. A woman on the third day wants to know which hand to hold, and a woman whose term is not out wants to know who paid for the wine.

At the head and in the gutter

The gold at the head is mine, off the tally. The ring of wine is mine, set down while I told a girl to drink and she would not. The myrrh is off the fold the tablets slept in and has gone into the grain, so the second leaf smells of a feast in the middle of a sickness. The green bead in the lower corner was pressed in by a girl of the furnaces with sweetness at one mouth-corner; she would not say which bowl it came from, and I did not ask, for a bowl with a name written inside it is not a bowl a leech looks into. The grey bead in the gutter is public-bowl ash, caught when a woman crossed the court with a cut on her thumb. The pale crescent at the foot of the remedies is tallow-wasp resin off my thumb, setting a wall in the house of Vey; it keeps a little warmth of the nest, warm at dawn and cold by dusk, entered in no account. The line that runs out toward the gutter and stops is mine. The square cut out with a knife is not mine, and I have not written the word back. There is no Last Day in this book: the grey is swept into the public bowl at a feast and called generosity.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The bowl keeps what is poured into it. The slate keeps what is taken out.

The guest between them is kept in no house.

I asked the physician, the factor’s clerk and a bought breath who would not look into her own bowl, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The tablets go back into the silk and the silk into the tin, and tomorrow a woman will come into the court with a hand that will not hold a cup, and I shall pour the wine and turn the slate face down before I look at the child, because a child is not an account.
