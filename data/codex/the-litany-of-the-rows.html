I have laid out two hundred and eleven of them and I have never once been asked what I said over the rows. The Court has an order for the draw and an order for the pressing and a verse for the pot at the door. It has nothing for the place where the body goes after the iron has been and the slate has been read twice, because there is no table at the orchard and nothing there to enter a figure on. I wrote this so that the women who carry them out would have a sentence to say with their hands full of clay.

It is a prayer for the clay. The child is already in the account, and the account is a room I have never been in.

A woman who has just set a child down cannot hold a slate and will not argue with a priest. She wants a line she can say with her knees wet. I have said this one nineteen years and have never made it longer.

The manner of it, written small at the head

  • Say it at the setting-down, before the spade goes in, not after. After the spade a woman wants to go home and will not stand.
  • Do not read it from a slate. There is no slate at the row. If somebody brings one, lay it face down on the stake and stand on the other side of it.
  • Stand with the north road at your back. The road goes on to Cold Acre and does not stop for a child.
  • The answer is said by whoever carried. If nobody carried, the woman nearest the stake says it. A child who came back thin may say it too, and generally does, which is a thing I have set down and not explained.
  • Break the pot at the east ground after the eighth station and before the gap. The breaking is for the house and is not written down.
  • If the bell comes up the road from the Cross, stop at the gap. It is counting the market, which is a different count and a loud one.
  • Never say it over the same grave a second year. The clay has had the first saying and does not want it again.

The litany

The woman who carried says. The women at the stake answer. The answer is the same at every station.

I. Of the carrying

The arm that knew the household brought you a league.

It did not ask the road what you were worth.

The road would have said nothing. The road is asked nothing.

You are set down without a figure, which is the only figure

the orchard has ever been given.

Answer: Set it down. Keep what you have.

II. Of the iron

The collector came before the women came.

He set the cold rim to the way near the skin and waited for the hiss.

What went up the road in the iron is not what lies under this stake.

I have laid the two things down and have not made them one.

Here the woman who carried puts her hand flat on the stake. This is not part of the litany. It is the part before.

Answer: Set it down. Keep what you have.

III. Of the slate

The slate was read twice: once for the moot,

once for the households that came late up the north road.

The writing was put into the vessel before the vessel could refuse it.

Here there is a stake and no writing.

Here the account comes to an end in clay and says no more.

Answer: Set it down. Keep what you have.

IV. Of the pooling

They say many went into you and that I should not ask how many.

I have not asked. A figure asked at a grave is a figure that stays at the grave.

You were made of more than one and you will not be one again.

What comes back does not come back into this row.

Answer: Set it down. Keep what you have.

V. Of the pot

The pot broke at the east ground and the shards lay where they fell.

A whole pot answers full under the knuckle. A broken one answers flat.

This one was carried out whole and broken on purpose at the threshold,

which is the only honesty this house has paid for this year.

Answer: Set it down. Keep what you have.

VI. Of the grey

The barrow goes out on market-day and comes back empty.

The grey it tips at the south edge is not dirt and not the child.

We do not sweep the corner. We do not ask the dog.

What is left of a household is left in more places than one.

Answer: Set it down. Keep what you have.

VII. Of the count

Four in the row and four beyond it, and a stake with no name.

Count them going and count them back.

The count comes out even only if the gap is left in it.

I have never made it come out even by filling the gap.

Answer: Set it down. Keep what you have.

VIII. Of the coming back

They tell us the child comes back. They do not tell us into which house.

They tell us nothing is lost that we have kept.

I have kept this row nineteen years and have not seen one come back into it.

That is not an argument. It is a thing I have noticed.

Answer: Set it down. Keep what you have.

Here the leaf leaves a space the length of a stake. It is not a station forgotten. There is no ninth saying and the women have never made one. When a child is set down and nobody in the house has a sentence, the space does the work. I have known a woman stand at it a whole winter and say nothing, and go home warm, which is more than this litany can promise.

The verse at the breaking

Said low at the east ground while the pot is struck. It is said to the pot and not to the child. A woman of the rows who has lost a child will say it to the pot anyway, because the pot will stand still and a person will not.

Break it at the threshold, not at the row.

Let the house hear it and let the row be quiet.

One for the hand that carried and one for the hand that let go;

one for the warm that went up the road

and one for the clay that stayed behind;

four in the row and a fifth not ours to name.

The pot does not know whose it was. The pot is honest.

What the clerk set above

A clerk of the factor came up from Greybank on a market morning with a cuff gone soft from washing and ink in two fingers that would not come out. He borrowed the leaf while a pot was being broken at the east ground and gave it back with the verbs left in and the women taken out, which is the stranger thing to do to a thing written at a grave.

  • At what went up the road in the iron is not what lies under this stake, he wrote: The measure is banked. The residue is entered as vacancy. Mine stands beneath. His has a figure in it and mine has a woman.
  • At they say many went into you, he set a thin return is entered; one in twelve is kept for the fire and underlined the figure twice. It is the figure they like to see. Both stand.
  • At the grey it tips at the south edge is not dirt, he wrote: Ash is not a vessel. Nothing arises. He did not say what it was, which is the only merciful sentence he wrote all morning.
  • At the space where a ninth station would stand he put a round nought and wrote vacancy beside it. A nought is a figure. The space was not.
  • At I have not seen one come back into it he wrote not a claim; not entered, which is true in the language of a room with a table in it.

He asked what a woman learned by the litany. I told him the order of the sayings and the breaking of the pot. He entered both and went back down the north road with a figure he had come for. The leaf came back with his ink wet on it. I have kept the ink. A man who cannot help writing on a thing has let it be seen that he wrote.

What the woman put in the margin

The letters are thick and lean and were made with a stick of charcoal held in a hand that had been in clay. She came up from Low Kiln with a pail and set it down outside the gate before she touched the leaf, because a woman who touches a paper in a burial ground will be asked what she had to do with it.

At II: “He came to our house in the morning and I gave him the arm without looking at his face. I have looked at the face since. It is not in this row and it is not in the cradle two doors down.”

At IV: “They told me she would come back as a stranger with a taste for a table she never sat at. There is a child two doors down who goes toward a cup before the cup is shown. I have not told her mother.”

At VI: “I have swept the corner three times and it comes back. I have stopped sweeping.”

At VIII: “I asked at the Weighing whether my son was whole. They said the account stands. They did not say which account.”

She would not give a name and I have not asked twice. There is always a woman at a burial ground with a slate she has carried a long way.

At the head and in the folds

The grave-clay at the head is mine and I have not knocked it off; a leaf that has never been wiped on a sleeve was never taken into an orchard. The filings came off a tube-wrap set down hard on the shed bench and worked into the grain, and iron has shown this leaf one kindness: it has never drawn the warm out of it. The grey thumb nobody will own came off a cuff at the south edge and lies with the clerk’s nought where neither is entered. The pale ring is a pot lifted off the leaf and not set down again. The flower was dry when I found it on a mound and I do not know who put it there. The tideline came from a pail going over and took two figures with it, and I did not write them again. The awn is barley from the meal mill, stiff where the fold has kept it dry through a wet tend. The tallow is mine. The ale stood from the first station to the eighth and went dark in the time it takes to set a child down.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The orchard has no column and no clean hand. It keeps what the bank has no place to put: the body, and the woman who carried it.

Struck once and written again underneath, very small:

It is a prayer for the clay. Say it and go home.

I have left both. The leaf goes back into the wool and the wool into the breast of the coat, and the coat goes home ahead of the women, who stay a little longer at the stake, which is the whole reason the words were written down and the reason they will not bear being read in a room.
