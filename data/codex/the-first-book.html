I keep the first book, which is the book the others are copied from and the one no man asks to see. The chronicle of the Keeping has a continuator who writes the later years in the roll-room under the narrow window; I write the beginning in a box on the inner side of the Weighing Hall, where the heat comes up out of the Keel and goes into the paper and will not come out again. There is a grating between me and the fire and a rule that no priest touches it, and I have sat here copying for eleven years and have never once been asked to move my table.

This book has been copied more times than any other book in Sarn, and every copy is smoother than the one before it, as a pot is glazed again and again until no clay can be seen. I have done the copying as I was told and I am good at it. I can take a rough year and lay a clean one over it so neatly that a man reading Standing here would not know there was a year underneath. It is only that the brown ink comes back in the thin light before Kindling, when the fire is low and the vellum cools enough to show what is under the glaze. I have kept what shows. I have not put it in the year.

The founding as the book has it

Copied from the vellum. The smooth hand is above; where the brown shows through I have set it beneath, in the order the words stand on the leaf and not in the order I would put them.

B.H. 400 to 1, which the temple calls the Broken Years and the people of Keld the Long Want. The temple tells that the dead went into the Waste and did not come back, and that vessels broke and nothing mended them. The book enters almost nothing, which is the easiest way of entering a thing. What survives out of that age is bowls and slates. The bowls are well made. The slates are accounts in a hand no living scholar can read, though the Vorr claim to recognise the numbers. I have entered the bowls first, because a bowl is a thing a man can hold and the claim of the Vorr is not.

In the upright hand of the College, against this entry: The long want is a mystery of the faith. The bowls are not entered.

I have left the bowls standing under the mystery. A bowl that held soup in a country with no Return is a bowl that held soup, and there is no hand in the College clever enough to take the soup out of it.

Y.H. 1, the lighting. The temple says the Nine Who Kept lit the Hearth with a coal brought from the first fire. The oldest hand in this book says only: the chamber was found already warm. The entry has been copied four times and each copy is smoother than the one before. I have copied it five. In the fifth the two sentences stand together. No copy before mine has done it and none after mine will, since I am the last hand that reads the brown by the low fire.

There is a coal shut in a nail-hole of the cover. It is cold. It has been cold every time I have opened this book for eleven years, and no man will say whether it was lit. I asked the continuator when he came up to borrow the leaf. He said a coal eight hundred years in a box is a coal and nothing more, and wrote nothing about it, which is his craft and not a lie.

Y.H. 3. The first Returns are recorded. Entered as the mercy of the Hearth made known in the keeping of the dead. Under the brown: a collector drew the first measure with a tube of cold iron and a woman stood by with an infant and did not cross herself, being paid by the hour. I have set both sentences down. The mercy entered the book in a clean hand on the third day of the year; the tube entered it under the glue of the binding, where the College hand has never thought to look.

Y.H. 8 to 12. Three offices are set up: assessors to value a life, collectors to draw the measure, bankers to press it into an infant. Entered as the several offices of the Keeping established in order. There is no order in the brown. The three come on the same leaf, written at one sitting, and the word several has been put in over a word scraped away, of which a square is left. I have not written the scraped word back. A square in this book is a thing a reader can put his finger on, and I have no licence to fill a man’s finger.

Y.H. 40. The deduction is fixed at one part in twelve and printed on the Weighing notices, where it stands after eight hundred years. The figure in the book is in a round clean hand, larger than the hand about it, so that it can be read from the door. I once struck it and wrote one in three beneath, having heard the figure spoken in the yards on a market day. I struck my own figure out again the same tend. This book holds what was entered, and what was entered was one in twelve. The other figure has no row here and never will, and I have left the place where I struck it as near as I could to the gutter, where the light from the grate does not fall.

Also in Y.H. 40: four of the Nine Wells are sealed in the Keel and the keys are kept. Five are left for the living. This is entered as four reserved to the Hearth. The brown says sealed, and the word was written hard enough to have gone through the leaf if the vellum had been paper. A well that is reserved is a well a man may stand beside; a well that is sealed has a stone over it and a key in a room he may not enter. Both are true at the door of the room.

Y.H. 41. Sixty leagues south of Sill the province called the Empty Cantons appears in the ledger as a line of figures and then as a line of empty squares. The temple says the people were returned elsewhere. No record says where. I have counted the squares on nine copies of this book, and there are forty on every one. The continuator says a square is a place where a figure was left out in copying. I have asked a reeve come up from the Fenmarch what his people call that ground, and he gave me a name I have set in the margin and will not put in the year, because the Court can figure a thing once it has been named in this hall.

Y.H. 120. Here the first book ends. The clean roll goes on in its smooth hand. Another book, kept behind the winter tallies in the lower room at Greybank, takes up the years from this one, and puts the verbs back where they were taken out. I have never seen that book and have no wish to. Two hands correcting the same sentence in one week is a near thing, and this world has accounts enough.

The three offices, as they stand

The list from the Y.H. 8 entry, set beside what each room is called by the men who work in it. The right-hand column is not in any copy but mine.

Office — As entered in the first book — What the room is called here

Assessors — To value a life — The room where a man is told what he was worth before he is asked how he died

Collectors — To draw the measure — The table with the iron and the tube, which is set up before the person comes in

Bankers — To press the measure into an infant — The room below, where an infant is carried in and a person is carried out

The assessors sit highest and write least. The collectors carry the iron. The bankers have the warmest room, being nearest the Hearth, which is a kindness of the building and not of the office. I have set the three in this order because that is the order on the leaf. A man who puts the bankers first has begun an argument about where the warmth goes, and there is no end to that argument in this Hall.

The kindling song

Taken down from a woman who has sung at the grate on Kindling for forty years and will not sing it while the figure is being read. She said the song was older than the book and then said that songs about fire are made by the fire, which is the custom of this city and not an answer. Sung low, with the last line left to the room.

The chamber was warm before we came,

the bowl was whole before the crack;

nine went after what had run away

and nine came back with something on their hands.

They set the figure on the board, one in twelve,

and called it mercy in a clean round hand;

they sealed four springs and kept the keys

and left five open for the living.

Sixty leagues of road and well and door,

and never a name entered after the fortieth year;

the slate says they were returned elsewhere,

and the squares say nothing, and are believed.

Kindle the fire and do not ask

who kept it before the Keeping was;

the flame has no mouth and the grate no tongue,

and the book by the fire has learned to be quiet.

What the upright hand did

The continuator borrows the first book on the tenth of every month and takes the verbs out of it as though a verb were a thing a man could smuggle past the grating in his cuff. He writes with care and has never in eleven years changed a figure.

  • Against the chamber was found already warm he set the Hearth was lit in the first year, and drew a small rule under it. I have left both. The lighting is an event; the finding is a state of the room; and a room that was warm before anybody came into it is not the same thing as a fire that somebody made.
  • Against four wells sealed he set four wells reserved to the Hearth, which I have set in the year already, so that the two readings stand together where a reader can find them.
  • Against the line of empty squares he wrote figures not legible in this copy, which is the truest false sentence in the book. I have counted forty squares on nine copies. A figure not legible has a figure somewhere behind it; a square has nothing behind it and was made empty on purpose.
  • Against no record says where he wrote nothing at all. His hand passed over the line without a stroke, which is the readiest way of correcting a sentence I have yet seen.
  • Against the kindling song he wrote no entry arises, and then, underneath in mine, I have put the room sang the last line, which is a record of the room and not of anybody who can be entered.

At the head and in the gutter

The head of every leaf is brown and brittle from eleven hundred years of warmth coming up through the grate, and the ink there has gone the colour of tea; the gutter is still black and the figures in it read as if they were written yesterday. A keeper of this book reads the end of a line first and the beginning last, which is not a skill and cannot be taught. The soot at the head of the first leaf is off the grating and was there before I came. The coal in the nail-hole is shut in with wire I have not touched. The slate dust in the fold came off a surveyor’s slate; I told him to move it and he moved it onto the book. The tallow thumb at the second corner is mine. The ring at the foot is small ale, set down by the continuator on a tenth-day when he had taken three verbs out of the founding and would not say what he had put in their place; he stood a long while with the cup in his hand and then set it on the leaf and went away without drinking. The square of bare vellum is where I scraped a word away. The grey along the outer fold is not mine and not the College’s. I have had this book eleven years and have not swept it. There is no Last Day in a box beside the fire.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

They found it warm and wrote it lit,

and the figure went up clean;

forty squares and a line of road

with nothing entered in between.

Four springs are sealed and four keys kept

in a room the reader does not know;

ask the chamber what it held before

the first book told the chamber so.

I asked the continuator, a woman who sings at the grate, and a reeve out of the Fenmarch who would not give me his name twice, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The book goes back into its box on the inner side of the Hall, where the warmth comes up through the grating and settles into the vellum and no brush takes it out. On the tenth the continuator will borrow it and take the verbs out again, and the clean copy will go on in its smooth hand, and this one will go on underneath it with the brown showing through where the fire is lowest. Next tend I copy the first Returns out of the year Three and set the tube beside the mercy, where a man must move the book to reach them. The notice by the door will still say one in twelve. The squares will still be forty. I have put both down where they stand.
