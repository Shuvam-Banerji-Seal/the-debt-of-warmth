To Holl at Mud End, who keeps the eel baskets by the landing and has not written in two tend. From Wenna at the Post, who lays the peat blocks in the niche and does not say whose shape they are.

I write on the back of a weigh-slip from Sill. It came up the mud road under a carter’s boot and was given to me for nothing because the figure on it had been read twice and was not the figure paid. Paper from Sill is too clean for this country; it takes the peat badly. I have gone over every second word with the stub of a reed-pen and tied a knot in the margin where the word would not come. Fen Cant is not written and never will be. A word set down in the south’s letters is a word a man can carry off in a cuff and read aloud to people who have never stood in water to their calf.

Nib stood on the boards in the last tend of Silt and came back, and I laid a block for him in the niche. It is the shape of where he stood and not the shape of him, and I have never once mistaken the one for the other. Our father stood in the year of the Long Cold and did not come back, and there is no block for him and never will be, because the niche takes only a stand that ends with a man walking back. I have kept a dry block in my hut against the day somebody tells me different. It has gone hard at one corner. I have not shaped it.

You were away when she tied the knot. Six marks and a knot for a season of bought breath, she told the eel-trader, and he wrote it on the back of his hand because he could not write at all. The season is out. The knot is not untied. I have kept it in the fold of my apron two winters and taken it out on the first morning of every tend to see that the wool has not gone soft on me, and put it back. I do not know whose hand I would be undoing if I undid it. That is the asking I have written this for.

What went down and what came back by the peat cart

Set down on the slip as the cart went down on Fifthday and written up again on the return on Eighthday. The left is what I sent; the right is what came back. Where a thing was not sent I have left the place as a place and not put a nought in it.

Went down in the cart — Came back on Eighthday

A dry block for the niche, wrapped in reed — The same block, carried back; wet at one corner

A letter on a Sill weigh-slip — The same slip, with an answer in the margin

Six marks for the season, tied in a fold of apron — Four marks, and a word about the other two

Nothing — A little salt, gone damp in the fold of the slip

A word for our mother — A word about the knot, which is not the same word

A place where the fifth mark would stand — The place still standing

In the margin, small, in mine: The cart went down with six and came back with four. He has not stolen the two. He has left them standing on the other side of the water, where I cannot put my hand on them. A mark left standing is not a mark paid, but it is also not a mark that has gone into anybody’s pocket, and I have set both readings here.

The reeve’s stone has no name cut into it and has never counted a man. Nib’s block went in because Nib walked back. There is a woman at the Post who says a block is not the man and a block is not the shape, and that the stone keeps neither; she has said it so often that the young say it before her, which is how a saying starts to be a law. I did not ask her about our father. I asked the stone, and the stone kept its own counsel, as it does.

The ask

Come back before the Reed. The young cut their first bundles in the dark and come back wet or dry, and if the bundle comes back wet they go again, and nobody tells them why. I want you standing at the end of the boards where I can see you put your foot where the others went, and I will not point to the board. If you come back after the Reed I shall not be at the landing and shall not send the block up again.

Bring the salt if you can carry it; salt is four marks up here and three at Greybank, and the extra mark is the road and nobody calls it a tax. Do not come by the smuggler’s path. A man comes back that way with wet boots and no figure, which is a cheap way to travel and a dear way to arrive. The winter way will not bear a cart yet, and no reeve will stand on it, which is the only honest thing I know about that way.

Untie the knot yourself if you are coming. Do not send it back tied and do not send me the marks in place of the hand. I have kept two winters of a thing that belonged to her hand, and I want the undoing to be done by the person it was tied for, so that the wool has been in somebody’s fingers who remembers why it was tied.

At the foot of the page, in a second hand, hard and salt-stiff, written small along the margin where the peat would not take a full line: I have sent four marks and the salt is damp because the sacks at the landing are not what they were. The other two I have not got. I have kept them where a man keeps a thing he owes and cannot pay, which is in front of his eyes and not in his pocket. I am not coming before the Reed. That is not a refusal and I will not have you read it as one. Send me the knot and I will send it back tied the way she tied it, which I have not forgotten though I never asked her to show me. A thing tied by her hand and untied by mine would be two hands and no debt, and I do not know a man who can live in that country. Holl.

Underneath, in mine, written on the white of the slip where the figure had been read twice: He has not said he will come after the Reed, and I have not asked him a second time. Four marks stand on one side of the water and two stand on the other and the knot stays in my apron. I have taken it out to look at it this morning and put it back. The block is still hard at one corner.

The song said to the boards

Said low through the nose while the blocks are laid, never looking at the stone and never while a reeve is standing within hearing of the second part. The last line is said to the water.

Four for the stand and one for the slack,

the block goes in where the man came back;

the reed says wet and the reeve says wait,

and what was lent goes back to the water.

Six for the breath and a knot in the wool,

the knot keeps the term and the term keeps the hole;

two marks stand over and two marks stay,

and the hand that tied is the hand gone away.

Salt comes up and the peat goes down,

the cart keeps the boot and the mud keeps the man;

we do not shape it and we do not say,

and the stone keeps the place where the shape would lie.

In the margin: He will say the second part is a complaint about the Court. It is a complaint about a knot. I have let him say what he pleases and tied another knot in the margin where the word should be. There is a place where the fifth stand would go and I have not put a block in it and have not called it a fifth.

What the factor’s clerk did

A clerk of the factor at Greybank borrowed the slip when the eel-trader carried it on to the quay, being a man who cannot leave a written thing alone. He writes in the narrow upright hand they teach, with the verbs taken out and everything done to the paper. Where I wrote I laid a block for Nib, he set a block was entered above it, and Nib went out of the sentence as though a man could be carried off in the fold of a weigh-slip. Where I wrote the knot stays in my apron, he set the obligation remains current. Against the two marks standing on the far side of the water he wrote outstanding, which is a word with a cuff on it. He put a round nought in the place I had left for the fifth stand and wrote vacancy beside it.

I have put my words back underneath and left his above. He takes the verbs out because a thing always true has no need of anybody in it. I put them in because a woman standing in water to her calf wants to know whose hand tied the wool and whose hand has not come back to undo it. His nought has outlasted the place it was meant to cover, which is his trade and not mine. The nought has kept better than the two marks. That is the whole of what is wrong with it.

At the head and in the gutter

The peat at the head is mine; I held the slip down on the boards in a wind off the bog and did not know I had marked it. Peat goes into the grain and will not come out, which is why we sign with it. The tideline has taken two words clean off the second page and I have not written them back, because a letter with two words gone is a letter said slowly, which is how all of ours are said. The eel slime at the fore-edge came off the hand that carried the creel down to the landing; the hook nick came off a man paying for six eels and charged for five, or the other way, which he never settled and I never entered. The pale round place is where a collector’s cuff was laid while I was telling a girl to eat and she would not; it is not a wound and there is no blood in it, and I have covered it with the fold and written nothing over it. The grey along the outer fold is not mine and not the factor’s. I have not swept it. There is no Last Day at the Post and nothing under a loose board gets burnt in the morning.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

A block is the shape of a place where a man stood. A knot is the shape of a breath somebody bought. The stone and the wool keep both, and neither of them knows what it cost.

I asked a reeve, an eel-trader come up from the landing, and a woman whose term is not out, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written.

The slip goes back into the reed wrapper under the loose board with the sayings and the dry stick. Tomorrow the carts go east with peat and come back with salt, and four marks will stand on one side of the water and two on the other and I shall not add them. If he comes after the Reed I shall be at the landing and shall not point to the board he is to stand on. The knot stays in my apron until a hand comes for it that I can recognise.
