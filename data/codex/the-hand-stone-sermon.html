I keep the meal-book at the mill under the north bridge, and nobody asked me for this. I write because my father spoke to a stone and the stone gave nothing back, and I did not want the words to go the same way the note goes, into the water and out of sight. He stood with his palm flat on the hand-stone and spoke to the stone, the river, the mill, and last of all to us, which is the order a miller uses when there is work waiting and somebody may be listening who wants a figure. I have set his words down in Grathic. Where he left a verb out I have left it out, and put it back underneath in my own hand, because a saying said over a stone is a thing a household has to use.

The white at the head and in the gutter is mill-dust, not salt; I write at the mill and the dust gets into everything. The brown line along the foot came up the Fishstairs in the flood and stayed when the water went. The ring was a cup set down while he was speaking of the hour and taken up before he saw it. The grey at the corner came off a cuff and nobody will own it; I have not swept it. The barleycorn shut in the fold was in the leaf when I took it from the spoiled quire and I have left it where it lay. The nick is from the dressing-pick, which I use to open the book when my hands are wet.

The manner of the listening, as the hand-stone has it

Written small, from watching, not from any order the Court has printed.

  • Come at the hour the note is due, not before. A man who comes early hears the water going over the ledge and calls it the note, and is wrong all day.
  • Stand below the bridge with your palm flat on the hand-stone. The note comes up through the stone into the hand before the ear places it out in the water. If you feel it in the roots of your teeth and cannot yet hear it, you are standing in the right place.
  • Do not say the hour aloud. An hour said aloud becomes an argument, and there is argument enough at the Cross without carrying it down to the river.
  • Leave what you brought on the ledge behind the stone: a crust, a bit, a knot, a sherd. Take nothing back. The river takes what is set on its bank and gives back silt, which is an honest exchange and entered nowhere.
  • If the note comes before its hour, do not correct it. It has not gone wrong. We are the ones who are early or late.
  • When the bell comes down from the Cross, stop. It has counted something that is not your river. Begin again after. Four, four and a gap, and the gap is not one of yours to fill.
  • The mill keeps one hour and the stone keeps another. Do not lay one over the other. A man who does will grind his grain at the wrong time and blame the mill.

He said the last of these twice, once to the stone and once to a woman who had come down from High Kiln to hear him and was writing nothing.

The sermon, as he said it

Of the two stones

He put his palm flat on the hand-stone and turned his head toward the mill behind him.

“There are two stones under this bridge doing the same work and not agreeing,” he said. “One turns and grinds and gives me a measure of meal I can carry to the Cross and sell. One lies under the water and gives one note and goes on giving it and never gives a measure at all. I have stood between the two for two and thirty years. The millstone feeds the body. This one feeds the day.

“You will ask which of them the town could do without. I have asked myself on mornings when the hopper was empty. The answer changes with the hour, which is a thing the Court does not like in an answer. At the mill we grind with what the river sends and give out what the mill can hold, and we never ask the grain whose house it came out of.”

Of the hour

“There is a clock in the square that takes this note and gives out an hour,” he said. “Nobody has ever told me what the note counts. A man come up from Greybank told me it was a mercy of the Keeping, so that the world might be counted in its hours as it is counted in its measures. I asked him how much of an hour the Hearth kept back. He looked at the stone and gave me the printed figure, one in twelve, as though a twelfth of an hour were a thing a man could put in his pocket and carry away.

“Here is what I know of hours. A day at the mill is the time it takes to grind what is brought, and the time is never the same twice, and the meal is never the same twice, and nobody has ever entered either. When the note comes I set the mill by it and grind. That is all the preaching I have, and I have had two and thirty years to find another sentence and could not.”

Margin, in my hand: He used the phrase lawful tide twice, once of this stone and once of the Weighing, and I have not put the two sayings together. A man who uses one word for two things has a use for the word.

Of the early note

“It came a week before its hour this morning,” he said to the stone. “I felt it in my teeth before I heard it out in the water, and when I looked up there was still skin on the Ilm and the frost-bloom not gone off the well-lip up the yard. A man come back from looking said the water ran fast under the skin. The yard said weather, and then said the thaw that was, and went home warm.

“A thing that comes early is not a thing gone wrong. The eels run early and late and nobody fines them. The lambs come when they come and no slate is set at the byre. But let a note come a week before its hour and a man with a slate has a figure for it before the water has finished. I have heard the figure go from a fist to a mouth to a shoulder and arrive at no door in this town.

“In the Long Cold the ice held ninety-four days and this stone kept its note under the skin of the river and the town kept its hours another way, by the bell and by the loaves and by the light going off the terrace. When the ice went the note came back as though it had never stopped, and nobody asked who had kept the hours in between. I have asked. I got a date and then a figure and then weather. A man who keeps an account does not answer a question about what he did not enter.”

In my hand, at the foot of this section: He said the paragraph about the eels twice. The second time he said it to the mill and not to us. I have set the two sayings down as one because the mill does not answer.

The low-note verse

Sung low, standing, with the palm on the stone. The row says the last four lines with him. There is a gap before those four, the one the bell leaves, and I have left it here as it is sung.

The stone gives one note and goes on giving,

low as a man’s throat under water;

the clock takes it and says nothing,

and the mill takes it and grinds.

Four for the steps and four for the boards,

and a place where the note comes through;

the bell keeps a gap and the stone keeps a note,

and neither is entered by you.

It came a week before its hour and we called it weather;

it came under the ice and we called it the Long Cold;

say what it gave and not what it meant,

and leave the place where the answer stood.

The note goes on and the clock goes round

and the mill goes round with it;

the ninety-four days are kept

by nobody this town can name.

A girl from the Fishstairs asked what the space before the last four lines was for. Nobody answered her. The clerk put a round nought against it later, which I have left where he set it.

The early notes, as the meal-book keeps them

I keep this tally because a figure that arrives at the mill in a fist goes out again in the meal and leaves nothing on the leaf. These I have set down where they stood.

When — Where it was felt — What the yard called it — What was entered

Ilmen 7, Y.H. 813 — up through the boards of the well-platform — weather — nothing

Ilmen 9, Y.H. 813 — up through the boards of the Weir — weather — nothing

Ilmen 25, Y.H. 813 — up through the boards of the Vail kiln-house, spoken of from the Weir — weather — nothing

Silt 7, this morning — in the roots of the teeth, out in the water after — the water running fast under the skin; then weather — nothing

The ninety-four days, Y.H. 811 into 812 — under the ice; not heard — the Long Cold — a line of figures

There were others before these, and my father could set a year to each of them, and I have not written them, because a row of years is a thing a clerk can carry away and a note is not. The line of figures for the ninety-four days stands in the meal-book as it stood in the mill: a row with nothing under it.

What the clerk did

A factor’s clerk came up from Greybank on the morning after with a cuff gone soft from washing and ink in two fingers, and borrowed the leaf while I was at the hopper. He gave it back with the verbs taken out.

  • Where my father said the millstone feeds the body and this one feeds the day, he wrote retention and conversion are lawful conditions of a vessel above and left mine below.
  • Where he said the note came a week before its hour, he wrote the tide arrived in advance of the reckoning and underlined it twice. At the end of the row he wrote nil.
  • At the ninety-four days he wrote a vacancy in the account, carried forward, which is a true thing said by a man who has never stood under this bridge.
  • At the gap in the verse he put a round nought and wrote vacancy beside it. When I asked him what a gap was worth that had no note in it, he said nothing a clerk could enter, which was the first true thing he said all morning.

I have put the verbs back underneath and left his hand standing above, as the mill leaves the old grinding showing under the new. He takes the verbs out because a thing always true has no need of them. I put them back because a man with his palm on a cold stone wants to know who is doing the giving, and whether it was his own hand that set the hour.

At the head and in the gutter

The mill-dust at the head is mine and will not wash out of the fold; my father’s hands were white to the wrist and he never noticed he had marked the leaf. The brown tideline is from the morning the flood went over the Fishstairs and I carried the leaf under my apron to keep the figure in it dry, though there was no figure in it. The wet ring is not ale; a woman set her cup down while he was speaking of the hour and took it up before he saw it. The grey at the corner came off a cuff at the hand-stone and nobody will own it. The barleycorn was shut in the crease when I took the leaf from the spoiled quire; it has not sprouted and I have not moved it. The nick is from the dressing-pick. The clerk did not notice any of these things, which is how I know he is a good clerk.

At the foot

Struck through once and written again underneath, very small, in a hand that is not mine, not my father’s and not the clerk’s:

The clock takes the note and gives the hour.

The clock was given by a hand.

I asked my father, the woman from High Kiln, a man come up from Greybank, and a barge-man waiting below the bridge, and had four answerings and no hand. I have left the line struck through and standing, which is the only honest way to keep a sentence you cannot sign.

The leaf goes back in the toll-bag with the barleycorn in its fold. Next Silt the stone will give its note when it gives it, early or late, and the town will set its clock by it and say nothing about what it counts, and the clerk will come up from Greybank and take the verbs out. I shall put them back as I did this time, and leave the nought in its place and the gap the length it is.
