I keep the fire in the open court of the third bowl and read the names when the eating is done. There is no grating between me and the flame. A man may come close enough to singe the hair on his wrist, and often does. This is a thing I did not understand until I saw the north. A man come up from Greybank stood at our feast with his hands behind him, the way men stand in the Weighing Hall, and would not bring them out to take the cup. He told me that in his country the fire is behind iron and no priest may touch it. I asked him what kind of fire was afraid of a hand. He said that was not the question the Court asked, and gave me the printed figure instead, which was one in twelve, and went on not drinking.

What follows is what we say when the household’s slate is burned and before the new one is made. I have set the rubrics underneath as the court actually has them, because a rubric written in a counting house is a rubric for a room in which nobody empties a cup. The factor’s clerk has been at the leaf with his verbs taken out, and I have left his hand above mine where he set it. A woman who has had three deaths has written in the margin, and I have left that too.

The ring at the head is mine. I set the cup down to find the word and left it standing until the wine went cold, which is the whole history of warmth in our court in one small circle on a counting-room tally. The myrrh is out of the cloth and I have not washed it. The scorch along the upper edge is from the night a draught off the court carried a spark against the leaf while I held it to the lamp; a prayer with a burn on it has been used, which is more than can be said for a clean one. The grey grain in the fold came up out of the public bowl under a girl’s nail. The green at the lower corner is not myrrh. A child with the sweetness of the glaze still at her mouth leaned over and touched the leaf, and I have kept the mark where she put it. The thin gold between the last leaves is not leaf from our bowls, which are glazed.

The manner of it, written small at the head

  • The fire is not covered and not approached with a slate. A man may stand as near as his courage and his sleeves allow. If he burns himself it is his sleeve, not the court’s account.
  • Burn the old slate before the litany. Make the new one after it, while the wine is warm. A household without a debt on a fresh slate is a household with no place at the next feast, and we do not leave our places empty.
  • The cup is emptied at every station. A man does not fill his own cup; his neighbour fills it, so that each station is carried by two hands and nobody pours for himself.
  • The answer belongs to the whole bowl, including the child who has not yet had a name-day and the one who has no measure in law. The child says it badly. The one with no measure says it loudest. We do not correct either.
  • When the slate of a household is burned by lot, do not say which household it was. The applause is for the debt and not for the house. A house that hears its own name in the applause has been given a thing it cannot spend.
  • If an officer of the Court is in the court, give him the new slate and not the leaf. He cannot enter a debt that has been burned, and he cannot burn what he has entered. We have tried both with the same man.

The litany

The keeper reads from the lip of the bowl. The bowl answers. The answer is the same at every station, which is why it is a litany and not an argument.

I. Of the making

The bowl was made with a lip and a hollow,

and the hollow was the point of it.

A cup that is never drunk was never made;

a life kept whole in a cupboard is a life mislaid.

The world was fired whole and the lid lifted in one anger,

and what poured out of it is the Hearth’s own giving.

Answer: Empty the bowl. Name the debt. Come back to the fire.

II. Of the fire in the court

We do not keep our fire behind a grate.

A fire a man cannot come near has stopped being a fire

and become a figure in a room where nobody is warm.

The north says no priest may touch the flame.

In this court the flame is touched by everybody

who has hands to hold a cup and a debt to name.

At this station a man from the north once put his hands behind him and would not bring them out. I have left that standing.

Answer: Empty the bowl. Name the debt. Come back to the fire.

III. Of the names

Seven names are given and one is kept,

and the one that is kept is the one let go.

A name given at the naming is given to be spent.

A name kept too long grows heavy in the mouth

and the child who carries it cannot lift the cup.

Name the debt before you name the guest. That is the courtesy here.

Answer: Empty the bowl. Name the debt. Come back to the fire.

IV. Of the burning

The slate goes up with the smoke and the figure goes with it.

What was written stands no longer and is not therefore untrue;

it was carried, and carried is a kind of kept.

Do not say whose slate burned. The lot drew it and a child drew the lot.

Then make a new slate while the wine is still warm,

because a debt not carried is a debt not forgiven.

Here the household’s slate burns in the court and the bowl waits. Nobody reads the old figure. The new one is written in a clean hand and the applause comes before the writing, which is the order the court has settled on and no court in the north has ever understood.

Answer: Empty the bowl. Name the debt. Come back to the fire.

V. Of the pouring

One measure into one vessel: he comes back with his name,

with the hand that held the cup and the debt it meant to carry.

Many measures into many: they come back as they can,

with a taste for a table they never sat at,

and are shown at the feast and given small coins.

Both are poured. Both are applauded.

The applause does not ask which one it is clapping for.

Answer: Empty the bowl. Name the debt. Come back to the fire.

VI. Of the figure

Count embers, not years. A year is a bowl nobody weighs.

Twenty marks to the crown and the crown buys no hour.

One in twelve the north prints on its notices,

and the north is welcome to its figure.

Measure for measure is what the speaker has already decided.

Write the new debt small. It travels better small.

Answer: Empty the bowl. Name the debt. Come back to the fire.

VII. Of the beautiful

Blessed are the calm who want nothing.

They come back from the pouring without the tremor,

and are shown with the coins laid on a clean cloth,

and are called beautiful, which is a word for a debt spent in full.

The third-day tremor is the price of beauty.

Somebody pays it. The cloth is laid out before the feast.

Answer: Empty the bowl. Name the debt. Come back to the fire.

VIII. Of the coming back

The bowl goes round and the fire goes up.

A man who has been emptied is a man who has been carried.

Come back to the fire. Come back as you can.

If you have no name, leave the place where the name would stand

and let the next cup be set down in it.

Empty the bowl. It was made for this.

Here the leaf leaves a space the length of a name. It is not a name forgotten. It is the seventh, which the house keeps and the bowl keeps and nobody says at a feast. We leave the length of it standing. Nobody says the name into it.

Answer: Empty the bowl. Name the debt. Come back to the fire.

What the clerk did

A clerk of the factor’s office borrowed the leaf at Greybank with ink in two fingers and the cuffs of his coat gone soft from washing. He writes in the narrow upright hand they teach in the College, and took the verbs out as though a verb were a thing a man could smuggle past a weigh.

  • At the hollow was the point of it, he wrote: retention and conversion are lawful conditions of a vessel, above my words. Both stand. He would have a bowl that holds; I have known the kind that cannot be got at when it is full.
  • At we do not keep our fire behind a grate, he wrote: the Hearth-fire is behind iron and may not be approached. True in the Hearthseat, nowhere in this court.
  • At the burning he wrote: debt extinguished by lot; new entry required. It is the whole of what we do with the sentences taken out. He has entered the burning and not the going.
  • At they are shown at the feast and given small coins, he set thin return: exhibition, value nil. It is true in the figure. I have watched a man hold a coin flat in his palm and not spend it, and there is no figure for that in any counting house I have seen.
  • At the answer he put a round nought and wrote return recorded under it. His four sayings have no room in them for a cup. I have set the cup back underneath.

I have left all of it standing, his hand above mine and mine under it, as a repaired bowl keeps the old break under the new glaze. He takes the verbs out because a thing always true has no need of them. I put them back because a woman at a feast wants to know whose cup she is emptying.

What the woman with three deaths put in the margin

The warm sloped hand is Meline’s, of the house of Ardel, returned whole three times and keeping the years of her deaths in a cloth she will not open. She keeps three names and will not say which of the three she answers to in the morning. I have copied her as she wrote it.

At the burning: “They burned the slate of the house that had paid the most into the bowl. The lot was drawn by a child who could not yet hold a cup. Both of those things are said to be chance, and I have heard chance defended in a finer room than this.”

At V: “There was a northerner at the feast with the look of a thin return on him. They gave him a coin and he held it flat and did not spend it, and afterwards a girl took it from him to buy a crust and he let her. I do not know which of us that shames.”

At VII, against the price of beauty: “My third came back calm and would not lift the cup. We called it beauty for six years. I would call it something else if I could find the word.”

At the answer, where I had set eight answers, she wrote: “Say it for the ones who have no measure in law. They are the only ones in this court who say it as if they meant it.”

At the head and in the gutter

The wine ring at the head is mine and has gone from warm to dark and will go no further. The myrrh is in the grain and not on it; I have tried to brush it off and it comes back with the weather. The scorch has worked into the fold and goes on when the leaf is shut. The grey grain came out of the public bowl on a girl’s nail and is the only thing in this court that nobody has tried to sell me. The green at the lower corner is the sweetness, and a child who licks the glaze room door is either very young or has been warned too often. The gold will not lie flat. The hair is not mine; a hair in a fold means somebody leaned over to read, and at this feast only the keeper may lean. The line that runs toward the gutter ran while the wine was warm and stopped when it reached the grey. I have not straightened it. A prayer written by a steady hand would be a prayer from somebody else.

At the foot

In a hand that is not mine, not the clerk’s and not Meline’s, struck once and written again underneath, very small:

The bowl is ours. The fire is not.

The fire under the bowl is paid for by somebody who will not be named at the feast.

I asked the keeper of the fire, a woman with three deaths, a northerner exhibited at the feast and a child who had not yet had a name-day, and had four answers and no hand. I have left the struck line where it was struck. The leaf goes back into the tin under the myrrh cloth, and at the next feast the household’s slate will burn by lot and a new one will be made in a clean hand, and the applause will come before the writing as it always has, and I shall read the eight stations with the wine warm and put the verbs back where the clerk took them out, and leave the seventh place the length it is.
