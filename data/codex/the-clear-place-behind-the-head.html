I trap eels at the Weir and fish the first warm silt below the north bridge. That is my trade and the whole of what I know, and I have set down only what I have put a hand to. A man who writes what he has heard at a hearth is writing a hearth, not a beast. I have known both kinds of book and this is the sort a man can use with wet hands.

The slime on the fore-edge is eel and the silt in the gutter is the Ilm. I have not cleaned either off. A book that has never been down to the Fishstairs was never taken on watch.

The run

They come up in Ilmen when the water turns and the ice is gone from the middle of the river, and they come up out of the Sallow, which is a thing every trapper on the Ilm knows and no clerk has ever had to enter. The traps at the Weir are set with their mouths upriver, so a thing going with the water goes in and cannot find the way back out. On Ilmen fifteen the hearth-maids stand on the flat stone and bless the weirs and the traps, and speak the words over the boards; the eels run on that day and they run on the day after whether the words were spoken or not. I have set down both things and joined neither.

In the first warm days they lie in the silt of the Fishstairs, under the north bridge, where the water goes flat and brown and glossy. At night they come to a lamp and to a stone that has held warmth. This is the whole of the hunting: a lamp on a stone, a trap with its mouth upriver, and a man who keeps his hands out of the water until he knows where the eels are.

I tap a basket the way a potter taps a pot. A basket with eels in it answers full; an empty one answers flat and small. It is the only account on this river that I trust, and it cannot be carried into a counting room because there is nothing on it to write.

What it is

It is an eel. I will set that down first, because the old women will not. It has an eel’s length and an eel’s way of going, and it is the colour of the Sallow on a dull morning, grey with a thin glass shine along the back. In the hand it is cold and slippery and stronger than a thing of that thickness has any right to be.

I have opened nine. In each I found an eel and nothing else, and behind the head a small clear place, no bigger than a pea split in two, with a little warmth shut in it. I have no name for that place and I have not asked for one. A man who writes a cause into a book he keeps in a clay tube is a man who will be asked for the cause by somebody with a slate, and the answer will be entered and the eel will go on being an eel.

The stop

The danger is not the bite. It has no bite worth writing of. The danger is the stop it puts through the water.

A man wading at night with a lamp will feel it before he sees anything: the ways in him stop as though a clamp had closed on them, and there is no iron to see and no mark on the skin. It lasts a moment on dry ground. In water that is up to a man’s calf it lasts long enough. He cannot lift his foot. The water does the rest, quietly and without haste, the way the Ilm takes a step at the Fishstairs and goes on being a river.

I have seen it once only and I will set that down plainly. It was Ilmen twenty-two, a man of the Weir wading below the bridge with a lamp on a stone and his creel at his hip. He was a man who carried more than his natural measure — a Held man, the sort who can stand a great deal — and I thought he would stand this. He went down at the knee in three feet of water and his hands came up empty and would not close. We had him out in the time it takes to cross oneself, which was not a short time. His fingertips were grey for a tend after and he did not get his warmth back all at once. He has never said what it felt like and I have not asked him. A physician at Sill would call it the cold that stays. I have called it the stop because that is what it did.

A Thin man in that water would not have come up. I have no doubt of it and no wish to test it. The thing has no malice that I could see. It stops what is in front of it, the way the draw stops at a sluice, and goes on with its own business.

The lamp and the trap

Set at dusk, mouth upriver, the lamp on a stone to the lee side and never against the chest. A lamp against the chest brings the eel to the man instead of to the stone, which is a distinction a new man learns once. Work in pairs where the silt is deep. Smoke what you take at the Fishstairs and sell it for three bits a basket; salted it goes for two, and the women there set their own figure and do not say it aloud.

The skins go a different way. A man comes up from Greybank in a coat with the cuff gone soft from washing and buys the skins dried, a mark apiece, which is four baskets of eels for the skin of one and a figure I have never been able to argue with. He told me the dried skin carries a flow a little farther than the hand can carry it, which is a trade I do not follow and have no wish to follow. A mark is a mark and a question is a question with a figure hanging off it. I have written his price where he set it and have not added a reason to it.

Pressed in the blocky hand of the Greybank buyer, in the margin beside the price: A mark a skin, dried whole, no holes. Twelve to the bundle, entered at the tally house. The flow is not explained at this price. --- G.

I have left his hand standing. He paid and I have no quarrel with a man who pays.

The song

Sung setting the traps at dusk, not lifting them at dawn. The third line is the old line and the men at the Weir sing it wrong on purpose, because a song that tells a man why the eel comes is a song he can carry home and set by his own fire. I have set it down as it is sung.

One for the Sallow and one for the draw,

one for the run that comes up in the thaw;

it comes to the lamp and it comes to the stone

and takes the warm that the stone has known.

Four in the silt and four in the run,

the mouth upriver and the lamp on the bight;

if the stop comes, let the man go down ---

the river keeps what the river has found.

Count them going and count them back,

three bits a basket and a mark for the skin;

we sing the old line wrong and leave the gap

where the sixth eel rose and no hand went in.

The gap is where the sixth eel rose on Ilmen twenty-two. The men know it and sing it and have never once said why it is the sixth. There is a man at the Weir who sings the last line twice and will not say what for, and I have let him keep it.

The tally

These are the nights I kept a true account of. There were others I did not write because my hands were full and one I did not write because I had drunk. I have set down both sorts as they stand. The last column is the clerk’s, not mine.

Night — Place — Lamp — Taken — Stopped — Entered

Ilmen 3 — Weir, west mouth — one — four — none — 12

Ilmen 8 — Silt below the north bridge — one — seven — none — 21

Ilmen 13 — Fishstairs, second step — two — five — none — 15

Ilmen 15 — Weir, after the blessing — one — nine — none — 27

Ilmen 22 — Below the bridge, deep silt — one — two — one man — 6

Ilmen 27 — Weir, east mouth — one — six — none — 18

In the narrow upright hand of the factor’s clerk at Greybank, struck through the “Stopped” column: Persons stopped: not entered. Stop not a vessel. No deduction arises. Account stands.

I have left his hand standing. A clerk’s hand has its use: it keeps a man from arguing with what he cannot change. But the man on Ilmen twenty-two was grey at the fingertips through the whole of Ilmen and went on selling eels over the top of it, and the clerk has put nil against him with a neat instrument and no name on it. I have written the grey in the margin below, where his column cannot reach, and set my own mark beside it in charcoal.

Below, in my hand: One man, grey ten days. Paid for at the Weir and not on any slate. Missed by nobody but me.

What the old women say

They say the glass eels are the shadows of star-rivets that fell into the river, and that each one is the dark of a rivet that could not bear the light where it was driven. There are songs for it with a woman’s name in them and a wrong done at a weir. I have heard them and I have no quarrel with a song. I have opened nine and found an eel each time, and a small clear place behind the head with a little warmth in it, and nothing that would cast a shadow in daylight.

The stars are rivets in the great repair, so the old women say, and I have no learning to set against it. But a rivet that fell into the Ilmen would not come up as an eel. It would come up as a thing a man could not close his hand around, and there would be a figure in the slate for it before the water had gone down. I have written that sentence once and crossed it out twice, because it is the kind of sentence that gets a man called a natural philosopher, and natural philosophers are not set to trap eels at the Weir in Ilmen.

At the head and in the margins

The slime at the fore-edge is mine, from holding a basket still while I wrote with the other hand. The silt in the gutter came up over the second step below the north bridge and has never come out. The salt bloom at the head came up out of the paper on a wind off the Sallow and comes and goes with the weather, which is more than can be said for the clerk’s figure. The scorch on the second leaf is where the lamp went over on Ilmen twenty-two, while I was down at the man in the water; the hand that set the dish upright was mine and has not been entered. The hook nick is from a creel at the Fishstairs. The scale in the fold came off a fish that went into the basket and not into the song. The tar is off the wrap. The tideline is the Ilm’s own hand on the leaf, and it has taken two of my words and left the clerk’s figures standing, which is a small injustice in the ordering of things. The green bead is lead glaze off a woman who leaned to see the tally with the sweetness at one corner of her mouth; she said nothing and went off with the light. The grey print nobody will own I have not swept. There is no Last Day on a river, and nothing here is burnt and called dealt with.

In pale ink on the corner of the fourth leaf, in the hand of a hearth-maid who stood on the flat stone at the blessing: We say the words over the traps on Ilmen fifteen. The eels run whether we say them or not. I have spoken them for two and thirty years and have never once been told what they do, which is a mercy, because a blessing that does something has a price, and there is no house in Sill that wants a price on a blessing.

I have put her line above my own. She wrote it at the Weir and I wrote mine in the silt, and neither of us has asked the eel anything.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The river keeps what it is given. It gives nothing back.

I asked a clerk, a buyer, a hearth-maid and a man who went grey at the fingertips, and had four answerings and no hand. I have left the line where it was written.

Next Ilmen I will go down to the Fishstairs before the blessing with a fresh lamp and two folds of eel-skin, and I will count them going and count them back, and if the clear place behind the head is full in one and empty in another I will set that down too, in charcoal, with the verb left in it and no figure at the end of the row.
