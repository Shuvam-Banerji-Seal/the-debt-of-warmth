I keep the boardwalk at the Standing Post and set the peat-blocks in the niche when a man stands. There is no desk in the Fenmarch that will hold a ledger upright, and the reeve’s count-stick has no place on it for a pair of feet, which is why this shrine has kept what it keeps and Greybank has never kept it right. I write on the back of a bog-tally spoiled by a wet figure, because a paper that will not lie flat is a paper nobody can borrow for a column, and I have had enough of columns.

She stood with her back to the post and spoke first to the peat, then to the board under it, then to the water in the prints of her feet, and last of all to us, which is the order the marsh uses when a man is standing and somebody may be listening who wants a figure. I have set her words down in Grathic, which is not the speech she used; the marsh bites the ends off its words. Where she left a verb out I have left it out and put it back underneath, because a saying said over a block is a thing a man has to stand on.

The brown at the head is peat from her palm when she brushed the sedge from the stone. The tideline is marsh-water that stood on the leaf through a night; the writing there is thin and I have not gone over it, for a word written thin is a word said quietly, and most of ours are. The grey came off a cuff and nobody will own it; I have not swept it.

The manner of the standing, as the stone has it

Written small, from watching, not from any order the Court has printed.

  • Come at first grey, when the water in the prints is still and unstirred by anybody’s shadow. A man who comes at noon stands in water another man has stood in and calls it the same stand. It is not.
  • Stand where the post points, facing the bog, not the road. The reeve faces the direction the bog came from. We have had two men face the road and neither came back to the choosing.
  • Do not say how long you have stood. A stand said aloud becomes an argument, and there is argument enough at the Post over the winter peat without bringing a man’s feet into it.
  • Set a peat-block in the niche for every stand that ends with a man walking back, and none for a stand that ends otherwise. The block is not a man; it is the shape of where he stood, which is the only thing this shrine pretends to keep.
  • When a factor’s clerk comes with a slate, stop, and begin again after. His figure is for another room, and there are ten leagues of dry road between it and this water. Do not lay the two together.
  • A reeve is not chosen by the block but by the standing, which the block cannot hold and the count-stick cannot hold either, and the Court has never asked to see.

The sermon, as she said it

Of the post

She put her palm on the post and left it there while the water filled the prints behind her.

“It does not count,” she said. “It has stood here longer than any of us and never once counted a man. No notch, no name; the reeve’s stick has notches enough for all of us and still cannot tell you who stood. I have laid a block here one and twenty times and never seen the post take notice of one.

“A man from Greybank measured the post with a cord and asked how old it was, and I gave him a year, because a year is safe and does not spoil in the mouth. He asked what it was for and I gave him the same year. The year went back in his coat and the post stood where it had stood with no figure on it. That is the whole of my preaching and I have had one and twenty standings to find another sentence and could not.”

Margin, in my hand: She gave the same year twice, once to the post and once to the clerk. I have set it down and left the question standing. A date is the only answer this shrine gives out, and it is given so the other question may be left where it was asked.

Of the feet

“The marsh keeps the shape,” she said, looking down at the water in her own prints. “You stand and the water comes up around the boot and holds it a moment before it lets go. Then the print closes and there is nothing but water with the look of any other water. The bog has kept your stand and cannot tell you whose it was.

“They say the Marsh Mother keeps her own down there, and I have no quarrel with a saying that gives a woman a reason to put her hand on the ground. I have laid my hand on the peat where a cow stood the week before and it was warm there and cold a spear-cast off, and the warm place had moved by the next tend and the cow with it. That is all I know of her keeping. It is a place and not a person, and it moves.

“A Drawn man came out from Reedholt and stood in the black stand as long as any of us, and came up with his fingers the colour of old rope and called it an obstacle in the ground. I have set that beside the man who braced and did not come back. Both are true.”

Of the block

She took a peat-block from the niche and held it in both hands, as a woman holds a loaf before she cuts it.

“This is not the reeve,” she said. “It is the shape of where he stood. A man goes into the black ground and comes out with a year taken off him and a stick that counts his term; this is a lump of peat the size of two fists that anyone could have cut. I have set the two side by side one and twenty times. They have never agreed and were never meant to.

“The Court reads a whole return in a clean voice at the Weighing Hall for a person not in the room. Here a man comes back thin and the north buys him cheap, and the post has no word for cheap. You will not get an argument out of peat: only a block in a niche and a man sent home with boots full of water, which is the most this shrine can do.”

Of them that do not come back

There was a stand in Silt that ended with four men going into the east cut and three coming up, and the old woman set no block for the fourth. She said the name once to the stone and again to the water and would not say it to us.

“Said looking at a person, a name becomes a bargain,” she said. “Said to the ground it stays where the man is. I am not saying the Return is a lie. I am saying I have stood at the choosing since before some of you had feet and never seen a block laid for a man who did not come walking up out of the cut. The Court can read him whole in a room I have never stood in. This niche cannot, and I will not teach it a lie to make the row look better.”

Of the figure

A factor’s clerk come out from Greybank stood at the end of the row with a slate held inward to his forearm. He asked what the blocks were worth.

“Nothing,” she said. “Nobody bought them and they do not travel. A man who cuts a block and lays it in a hole has done something with his hands before the day took them from him. If the Hearth wants the stand, let it come down here and stand in it. It has not come yet, and I have been laying blocks one and twenty years.”

“The measure is retained in the peat,” the clerk said.

“The water closes over the print,” she said. “Those are two sentences. I will not have one put over the other.”

“No account arises,” he said, in the voice that takes the speaker out of his sentence.

“I have kept no account,” she said. “I have set a shape in a hole and gone home to dry my socks.”

The standing-song

Sung low, standing, a boot on the niche stone. The row says the last four lines with her. There is a gap before those four, the length of the sound a boot makes coming up out of the peat, and I have left it as it is sung.

The post stands and the water stands,

the foot goes down where the water went;

the block goes in and the block stays in,

and the reeve keeps what the reeve has kept.

Three for the marsh and three for the board,

and one for the sound the boot leaves;

the clerk writes one and the house writes none,

and both go down in the same wet hand.

They read a whole in the Weighing Hall;

here they sell what the bog gives back;

say what it gave and not what it cost,

and leave the place where the standing was.

A child from the upper board asked what the space was for. Nobody answered her. The clerk put a round nought against it later, which I have left where he set it.

The row, as the niche keeps it

A figure that comes into the marsh in a coat goes out again in a thin return and leaves nothing in the niche. These I have set down where they stood.

When — Who stood — What the water gave — What was entered

Reed 4 — a man of Reedholt — stood to the knee, walked up, gave his name to the stone — nothing

Reed 11 — a woman of the east marsh — stood to the thigh, came up thin; north bought her cheap — one thin return, entered at Greybank

Silt 9 — four men of the black cut — three walked up; the fourth’s name said twice to the ground — nothing; no block set

Reed 20, this morning — the new reeve — stood to the belt; the post did not move — nothing; the block laid in the niche

There were stands before these that the old woman could set a season to, and I have not written them, because a row of seasons is a thing a clerk can carry away and a print in the peat is not. The niche has never agreed with the factor’s book and has never been asked to.

What the clerk did

He borrowed the leaf on the morning after while I was laying the new block in, and gave it back with the verbs taken out.

  • Where she said the water closes over the print, he wrote the measure is retained in the peat above and left hers below.
  • Where she said the post has never counted a man, he wrote the post is a lawful marker of the term, a true thing said by a man who has never stood with water in his boots.
  • At the Tallow stand he put a round nought against the fourth man and wrote third party unidentifiable; vacancy, and when I asked what a stand was worth with no man standing up out of it, he said nothing a clerk could enter, the first true thing he said all morning.
  • At the gap in the song he wrote vacancy again. He did not ask what sound had been in it.

I have put the verbs back underneath and left his hand standing above, as the board leaves the old print under the water. He takes them out because a thing always true has no need of them. I put them back because a woman standing up to the thigh wants to know who did the closing, and whether it was the marsh or her own foot.

At the head and in the gutter

The peat at the head is hers and mine and will not come out of the grain, which is why the marsh signs with a thumb and not a name. The sedge press has left the first two words soft; I have not gone over them. The tideline is marsh-water dried dark. The awn is sedge and the horn-shaving came off my own stick when I used its point to open a word I could not spell in charcoal. The grey came off a cuff and nobody will own it; there is no Last Day on this boardwalk and nothing gets swept. The ale ring stood from the first voice to the fourth stand and was drunk by nobody, which is how small ale goes at the Post when there is a thing to say and no proper word for it. The beetle’s wing-case lay on the niche stone and I have pressed it flat where it fell. The clerk did not notice any of these things, which is how I know he is a good clerk.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

Struck through once and written again underneath, very small:

The block in the niche is not the man who stood.

The whole is read for a person not in the room. The thin one is sold.

I asked the old woman, the woman of the east marsh, the man who brought the reeve up out of the cut, and the clerk, and had four answerings and no hand. I have left the line struck through and standing, the only honest way to keep a sentence you cannot sign.

The leaf goes back in the sacking under the niche stone. Next choosing the marsh will stand for a man, the water will close over the prints, the old woman will lay a shape in a hole, and the clerk will come out from Greybank and take the verbs out. I shall put them back and leave the nought in its place and the gap the length it is sung.
