I wind children’s feet in wool below the sill and I have set this down because a man from the Court’s table at the Cross came into the shade with his cuff wet at the edge and asked what the children counted when they jumped. He wanted a figure. I told him there was a count in it and no figure, which is the truth of the thing, and he sent me a clerk instead of an answer, which is the way of that table. What follows is what the children said before the clerk came and what they said again after he had gone back over the north bridge. I have set his hand above theirs where he put it and their words underneath where they stand, as the landing keeps the foot and the water that came up over it.

The game is played on the flat place at the foot of the Fishstairs, which has four steps down, four steps, and a landing a hand’s breadth out of line where a step should have been. At high water it is played on the boards under the shade. It is not played in the Five, because in the Five the children are made to stand and listen and there is nobody to turn a rope. The rope is a plait of reed-fibre or a length of eel-sinew, and it is never knotted. A mother will untie a knot in it before she will let a child jump, because a thing with a knot in it is a thing that will be asked what it counts, and a skipping rope is not an account.

How it is played

  1. The choosing is said to the rope and not to the faces. Said to a child, it becomes an argument, and there is always a child who will argue. Right fists held out, knuckles up; the child who says it points at each fist in turn and does not look at anybody. The fist it lights on on the last word belongs to the one who will hold the rope at the hold, which is a duty and not a prize.
  1. Two children turn and one jumps. One line of the verse, one jump. The line must be crossed and never touched: a child who touches the rope is banked, and sits on the landing edge with her hands folded while the others go around her.
  1. The hold is the fifth line. When it comes, the two turners stop the rope hanging slack between them and the jumper must jump once over the rope where it lies still. That is a jump over nothing. A child who steps into the slack, or who says the held word while the rope is still turning, is banked as above.
  1. A banked child is brought back when three children say her name. A child whom nobody brings back is thin until the next round. The children will not say why they call it that, and I have not asked twice.
  1. The counter says the count at the end. She says nine. A second child counts on her fingers underneath and gets ten, because she counted the hold as a step. The argument is settled the same way every time: the hold is a place and not a jump; but the children keep both counts, one in the mouth and one in the hand, and have never made them agree.
  1. The last child still jumping takes the dry end of the shade, where no slate lies. That is the whole of what is won. It is a place to set a basket down and nobody has ever asked a figure for it.

The choosing

Eel in the creel and coal in the gull,

silt in the cuff and a scale in the fold;

step and step and step and step,

and the one it lights on comes up with the hold.

The verse, as it is jumped

One line, one jump. At the fifth line the rope is stopped and held still. Say it to the rope.

One for the step that goes down to the water,

two for the step that comes up the same;

three for the eel at the outer board,

four for the coal that the gull took wrong;

Hold --- for the place where the foot must not go.

Five for the cuff that is washed every morning,

six for the slate that is held to the arm;

seven for the hand that goes down to the stone,

eight for the figure that goes up the road;

Nine for the landing at the foot of them all.

They call it nine and the hold. Ask a child what the hold is and she will tell you it is the step you do not take, and if you ask whether it counts she will ask you whether a rest counts. I have put that down as it was said to me, with the question left in it, because a question said to the ground is the only answer a child here will give a woman with a slip in her hand.

The verse they do not say when a man with a soft cuff is on the landing

This one I had from the girl who holds the rope, with the proper space left before it, which is the space the turners leave in the jumping. She would not say it while the clerk had his pen out and would not say it to him at all. She said it to the rope with her back turned.

One for the man set down before he was called,

two for the cuff that goes grey by noon;

his hand to the slate and his eye to the stall,

and the number goes up and the landing stays down.

Four for the boards and four for the post,

and the place where the fifth should be --- even here;

the gull has the coal and the river the eel,

and nobody counts what the water keeps.

I have set it down with none of my words in it. A verse said to the rope wants nothing added, and a verse said to a man wants everything taken away.

What the girl who holds the rope put in the margin

The letters are hard at the th and one of them backward, which is how a child writes who has learned off a slate and not off a book.

At six for the slate that is held to the arm: Not to the arm. Where nobody can read it.

At Hold --- for the place where the foot must not go: It’s a rest. A rest is what you get for jumping and not going anywhere.

At eight for the figure that goes up the road: He said it twice. We counted it once.

At nine for the landing at the foot of them all: There’s a step gone off this and nobody’s come down to see which.

A line from the Weir

A woman come down with eels from the Weir, who keeps a string of knots at her waist, read the slip over my tub and put one line in the margin in the small nasal hand she uses when she is tired. I have copied it as it stands.

Up at the boards we tie it in wool. A knot stays where you put it. A jump goes off with the foot.

She would not say what her own knots counted, and told me to ask the cord, which is an answer given by people who asked the same thing at their own cutting. I have left it where she set it and put no gloss beside it.

What the Court hand did

The clerk of the Court’s table borrowed the leaf in the shade on an Account-day, being a man who cannot let paper lie. He has a cuff gone soft from washing and the ink sits in two fingers of it and will not come out. He numbered the nine lines in the margin, struck through the hold and wrote above it, the fifth is a vacancy; enter nil. Against the place where the foot must not go he wrote no passage; no entry. Against the number goes up and the landing stays down he wrote the measure is carried forward. At the foot of the count he put nine and a round nought and wrote one vacancy beside it, which is a true figure and a false place.

I have put their words back underneath and left his standing above. He has taken the feet out of the game and left the figures, which travel well and arrive with nobody in them. I put the feet back because a child up to her ankle in silt on the out-of-line landing does not know she is hearing something always true, and wants to know which foot to lift. A nought is a number. The hold is a place to stand in for a breath. He has put a figure where a place should be, which is the whole art of that hand.

He asked me what the children learned by it. I told him the count and the fault and the bringing back. He said that was the account of a Return taught before a child could read a slate, and he entered it. The children stop when a man with a soft cuff comes down the steps. They take it up again when he is past the bridge. They have never once changed the hold.

At the head and in the gutter

The eel slime at the head is off the hand that held the slip down while I wrote; it goes into the grain and will not come out, which is why the shade uses eel-sinew for rope and not string. The silt is along the fore-edge and through the second leaf, where the water came up over the flat place and went back down again the way it does. The tideline has taken two words off the back and I have not written them in again, because a rhyme with two words gone is a rhyme said slowly, and all of ours are said slowly. The fish scale in the fold came off a creel set down on the leaf while a child was told to lift her own bundle and let her arm tell her what went into it, and did. The smoke on the corner is off a coal a brine-gull carried upriver from a banked fire that was not yet cold; the bird went over with it and the corner took the soot. The hook nick is not mine and nobody has claimed it. The salt bloom is on the leaf that lay nearest the creel, where the eels went in and stayed. The grey in the gutter came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying; I have not swept it and nobody will own it, and there is no Last Day under a tub.

The oiled cloth goes back around the two slips and under the outer tub. In the morning the water will be down or it will not, and the children will draw the same flat place with their feet and jump the same nine and the same hold, and the counter will say nine in the mouth and ten in the hand and the two will stand side by side as they stood today. I have kept their words and I have not joined them to anything.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

They bring her back by saying her name. Nobody brings back the hold.

I asked the girl who holds the rope, the woman come down from the Weir, and a man going past with a creel, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written.
