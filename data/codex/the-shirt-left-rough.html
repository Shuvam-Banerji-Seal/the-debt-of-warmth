To Sanna at the washing bench beside the lime kilns in Stonemouth, from Milla at the hearth off the Clay Yard in Low Kiln. Written in Stone of the Thin Year, on the ninety-fourth morning of the ice. I have written on the back of a tally because the front is all soap and no sentence will take on it. Do not open it on the second crease; that is where I sewed the thread through the hem and the word goes over double there.

The water came up this morning with a skin on it that broke round the rim of the pail the way a pot answers when it has cracked all the way through, a small flat sound and then nothing. I have kept this hearth eleven winters and I do not know whether the river is under the ice or the ice is under the river; the bucket comes up the same either way and I have left the question where it stood. Lye will not take in this cold. I set the ash in warm water and it goes sour before it goes strong, and the women tell me the ash from your lime is the wrong ash and the right ash comes from the kilns five leagues upriver, which is a thing I mean to ask you before I am done.

Here is what I am writing for. A woman of the yard came to me on the third morning of the ice with a child’s shirt in the fold of her apron and asked me to wash it. She did not say whose. I knew whose, because the whole yard knew and nobody had said it in my hearing since the collector came in the first week of the cold. The child went in at three days old and came back on the second day of Stone with grey at the fingertips and a red place at the hairline that the room called a graft settling and I did not name it. The woman set the shirt on my bench with her thumb still on the fold, and she said Wash it clean, and then, so low I nearly missed it, but not the last one. I said, What is the last one. She told me to ask the water, and went back up the yard with her apron empty.

I washed the shirt. Twice in lye, once in water warmed at my own fire, and I wrung it and held it to the light from the door, and there was a grey place at the left shoulder the size of a thumb-nail that would not come out. I have not tried a third time. A thing that will not come out stays where it was put, and I have no trade that takes it out of cloth, whatever the physicians from Greybank say about the corners of rooms. I have folded it with that side turned in and I have not sent it back, and that is the whole of what I have done, which is little for a letter of this length.

The shirts I have washed for houses that got a child back, set down here and not on any slate

When — What was brought — What I did — What the house entered

The first cold, Y.H. 811 — A shirt with milk at the breast — Washed in lye, rinsed in well-water, set on the rack — Nothing

The thaw before Stone, Y.H. 812 — A shirt too small for any child in the yard — Washed, carried away, not worn so far as I saw — Nothing

This morning, Stone, Y.H. 812 — A shirt with grey at the left shoulder — Washed twice, held to the light, stopped — Nothing

There were other shirts and I have not set them down, because a column of shirts is a thing that gets carried up the road and read over a basket, and a shirt folded with the grey side in stops at my bench. When a woman asks what a washing cost I tell her to ask the pot. When she asks what the shirt cost I have begun to say the same and then kept my mouth shut, for a pot holds water and a shirt holds something else and I will not put them on one shelf.

What I want to know from you

At Stonemouth, when a man is brought down off the bank with the white cough, does his house wash his shirt before it is given away? I do not want a belief. I want the doing. Who sets the water on, is it lime water or well water, and is the last shirt left rough as the women here say, or rinsed like the rest? You will know that the well beside the Ferr is not the one your people drink from, and I am not asking about that well; I am asking about the water you put a shirt in.

Then a second thing, kept behind the first because I wanted it answered by a woman who has washed for men who come back up the bank. When a shirt has been washed clean, does it still know the body it was washed off? A child of three days came back into a house I know and would not wear the shirt her mother had washed for her. She wore the other one, which had never been near my bench, for three days, until it was taken off her, I do not know by whom. That is an observation and not an argument; I have put it on a place of its own. Tell me whether your quarrymen’s shirts behave in the same way, and if you have no answer, tell me what you do with your hands when you come to the last one.

The washing-song

Said low, to the water and the shirt, and to the woman waiting only after those. The last four lines are held the length of the wringing and nobody fills them.

Lye in the pail and ash on the lip,

the water comes up and the ice lies flat;

wash for the living and wring for the dead,

and leave the last one as it sat.

Three at the bench and one on the rack,

a grey at the shoulder the size of a thumb;

the bell goes four and four and a gap,

and the gap is the only part of it ours.

Salt for the dish and wool for the wrist,

a stone warmed at somebody’s own fire;

count with her, count with her, one and again ---

[held the length of the wringing; not filled]

I have never put that held place into words and I have never once heard anybody fill it. A clerk at the Cross would set a nought in it and call it vacancy, and a nought travels and a wringing does not. They crossed two fingers at the shirt when I held it up and gave the woman a date for it, which is what we do here when a girl asks what a sign is for. A date does not spoil in the mouth and has no figure in it. The dish went out overnight with water in it and came back with a skim on it that I did not taste, and nobody in that house has sat down since the cough came on her, though nobody will say the two things together.

In the margin, in a second hand, broad and lime-pale, with the ends of words let fall

The thaw shirt was not returned. It went to a house below the Ferry and was given away there, and you have put returned where given away should stand. I have struck it and written the other underneath in your own charcoal, so that you cannot say a stranger wrote it. This is the whole of my correction to your count, and it is a large one, for a shirt given away and a shirt returned are two garments and no slateman alive can make them one.

We rinse the shirt and leave the last one rough. It is not for the man. It is for the hands that must give it to the next house. A clean shirt is a gift and a gift can be refused, and a man who refuses a shirt on the morning he needs one has paid for it somewhere where nobody stood to see. A rough one is only a shirt. I have carried two of them down the bank and did not ask the third time because the third time would have wanted a name.

On the white cough: the shirt goes under the pot-breaking at the door, unwashed, with the man. There is a saying on this bench which I did not make and cannot trace to anybody: A name kept in a room is a different weight from a name kept on a slate, and a woman can carry the one when the men beside her have put the other down. The weigh-clerk said it over a tooth-tally without knowing he had said it, and set his cuff on the leaf before he looked at the man who brought it. I have written it here because it is the only answer I have to your second question, and it is not one.

Three at the bench and one gone up the bank,

the lime takes the hand and the cough takes the breath;

rinse what will rinse and leave what will not,

and give the rough one to the house that’s left.

I have washed for sixteen men off this bank and had one shirt come back to me with the grey still at the shoulder. I did not take it out and neither did the next woman. The child wore what she would wear. Do not make a rule of it: a rule is an account that has found somebody to pay it.

--- Sanna, at the lime, in Stone.

What the clerk did, at the weigh-clerk’s bench, on the back, in narrow upright Ledgerhand

He borrowed the leaf for a morning. His cuff was gone soft from washing and he had ink in the creases of two fingers; he laid his hand on the top before he read a word of either hand, which is the manner of that bench.

  • Against the tally he set garments laundered: three; returns entered: three; discrepancy nil, joining the washing to the returning and taking both women out of the sentence as though a woman were a thing a man could carry off in a cuff.
  • Where I wrote that the child would not wear the washed shirt, he set attachment not entered above and left mine underneath.
  • Where I wrote that a thing which will not come out stays where it was put, he wrote residue retained; material not entered. He did not ask to see the shirt.
  • At the held place in the song he set a round nought and wrote vacancy beneath it.
  • Against leave the last one as it sat he wrote garment retained; no further entry, and underlined it twice, as though an underline were an argument.
  • Against the levy week in the first cold he wrote one in twelve, as the notice says, which is true of the notice and of nothing else at this hearth.

I have put the verbs back underneath where I could still read them. He takes them out because a thing always true has no need of anybody doing it. I put them back because a woman at a wash-hearth at three in the morning wants to know whose hands were in the water, and there were hands. I have left his figures standing on the back, where they belong, and the shirts on the front, where nobody has asked for them.

At the head and in the gutter

The white at the head is lye and has got into the grain of the paper and will not be brushed out, which is a thing I noticed too late to care about. The tallow at the second fold is mine; I held the lamp low while I wrote so the women at the rack would think I had gone to bed. The blue-green speck came off a cuff that brushed the leaf as I carried it through the yard, and there is a kiln up the terrace where that colour is made and a girl who wears it at the corner of her mouth and calls nothing by its name. The wet ring is the dish, which stood here from the first line to the song and was taken away still full. The frost-crisp along the gutter came up out of the leaf itself in the carter’s coat; the writing there has gone thin and I have not gone over it, because a word written thin is a word said quietly and most of ours are. The word struck through at the foot of the margin is returned, struck with my sister’s lye-sour thumbnail and written again under in the same charcoal; I have not covered the first word, which is how a seam keeps the crack under it. The grey along the outer fold is not mine and not my sister’s and not the clerk’s. I have not swept it. There is a Last Day in Sill and the corners are burned there in the morning and called dealt with; there is no Last Day at this hearth and the grey lies where it fell.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The shirt knows what the slate forgets.

The grey keeps the shoulder; the house keeps the date.

Three went through the water and one was left rough,

and the fourth has no name and no column.

I asked the woman who brought it, Sanna at the lime, the weigh-clerk and his clerk, and had four answerings and no hand. The shirt goes back tomorrow with the grey side turned in. If the child will wear it she will wear it rough, and no figure, collector or slate will know what it cost, which is the ordinary way of things and not a doctrine. The leaf goes back in the tin. Next winter the ice will come on the Ilm and the water will come up the same and I shall put the ash in it and it will go sour before it goes strong, and my sister will strike another word of mine and write the true one small underneath.
