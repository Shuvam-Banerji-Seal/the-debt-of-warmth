I went south with pots. That is the whole of my reason and I will not dress it as a pilgrimage. Nine Pots has four kilns, a shared clay pit and a quarrel with Sill about a road neither town has mended. A journeyman who can throw a jar in four beats hears that a house in the south will pay for a coarse jar to hold oil, and he hears it the way a man hears water in a dry well. He goes.

I left in Seed of Y.H. 812 with a cart, two bales of wool off the Tallow side and nine bars of Keldish iron got honest at Stonemouth, and came back in Ash with a purse of gold crowns that no man at the Cross could change and a figure in my head that would not leave. This is the figure. I have set down what happened and what did not. A man who writes only what happened writes a ledger, and I have read enough ledgers.

The road down

The Clay Road ends at Greybank and the south begins somewhere past it, though not all at once. The Empty Cantons come first: wells with the rope still on the windlass, doorways with the thumb-polish worn into the lintel, and nobody. A Fen man told me the people were taken in the first Returns and paid for by a line of figures. I asked him where the line went. He said a line goes where it is written and a figure stays where it is put, and went back to his eels.

At the Peat Line the wet begins. A reeve took a mark off me for standing on a board he had not pointed to, which was within his right. The Fenfolk say a dry man is a dangerous man, put us up on the Standing Post and did not tell us which boards would bear us. We learned. Sere’s northern advance stopped here in Y.H. 634 and the Fenmarch has paid tribute in eels ever since, which tells a man more about the south than any temple tells him about the Hearth.

Past the Line the dust comes up off the roads and lies on the pots like grey slip. The burning winds take the skin off a man’s lips. I had been told the south was warm; it is warm the way a kiln is warm, with the door open and the breath coming out, and a man goes thirstier there than he does in a Grathic winter.

The bowl at the third hour

The Bowl of Sere is three hundred leagues from Sill and has no wall worth the name. Its courts are open and each has a fire in the middle. Men sit in the heat of them with their coats on the ground beside them, and the dust settles in their hair, and nobody brushes it off.

At the third hour of the day a public bowl of wine is emptied, and that is how the Sun-Emperor’s acts are announced. I stood in a court and watched a man in a pale coat tip the bowl out onto the stones. The crowd leaned in. I asked the man beside me whether a debt had been forgiven or a province ruined.

He said, “Yes.”

“Which?”

He looked at me as though I had asked a pot which side was the bottom. “Measure for measure,” he said, and went back to his wine.

I have since learned that the phrase means whatever the speaker has already decided. It is a fine phrase for an empire. At home a man says one in twelve, as the notice says, and the notice and the man between them keep a whole district warm for a season. Here they empty a bowl and call it the same thing as a number.

They sing this at the wine-presses. I have set the Grathic words under the southern tune, which loses something, as Grathic always does when it is made to carry a tune.

Fill to the lip and let it go,

the pot was made to pour;

the cup that keeps is the cup that sours

and cracks along the floor.

One for the house and one for the fire,

one for the slate we burn by lot;

and one stands full and is not poured ---

ask not whose debt. Ask it not.

The burning of slates

Valen Openhand, whom they call the Bowl-Priest, preached in the court while I was there. He teaches the Spending: that a vessel was made to be emptied and a life hoarded is a life wasted. He is not a thin man, and neither are the priests who say it after him, which a traveller notices after standing in a court all morning.

At the end of a feast they burn one household’s debt-slates, chosen by lot, and then make new ones. I saw it done. A woman whose slate went into the fire wept, and the crowd was kind to her, and then the new slate was brought out and the crowd applauded that too. I have not got used to the second applause.

The warmest funerals in Sarn are in Sere. A house will burn a year’s grain under a dead man to keep him warm, and the Court taxes the ash, and the house is ruined by degrees and called generous. A poor funeral is a small fire in an open court. A great funeral can empty a province, and the province is then bought cheap, which is the sort of arithmetic the south has made its own.

A girl of sixteen emptied the bowl at her Bowl feast and named a debt she intended to carry. The applause was for the debt, not for her. She had seven names by then and would keep whichever her household liked. I asked a bowl-surgeon beside me how a person becomes one person out of seven. He said a vessel keeps the shape it is poured into, and sold me a nest of tallow-wasp resin for four marks. He held the nest up to the light and listened to it the way a potter listens to a pot, and asked whether it had been taken in a frost. In the Clay Yard we burn the stuff. Here it goes into a graft.

People with no measure

Slavery is open here and legally simple. A slave’s warmth is the owner’s holding, and a slave has no measure in law. This is not an oversight. It is the whole of the law. A beautiful house-slave is given a whole return and exhibited at feasts as proof of the owner’s generosity; a slave who runs is returned thin and set to the grain mills. I heard the mills grinding at night, a low sound under the wind, and was told that the people in them had once been someone.

At a feast I saw a northerner exhibited for small coins. He was thin-returned and could not say where he came from. He looked at me the way a man looks at a wall he half remembers, and said a word that might have been slip, or might have been a bit of the Ferr water coming up in him. I did not go to him. In a southern court the man who buys a curiosity and the man who claims a countryman are the same man until he has paid.

I have written this plainly because it was plain. I have not written what I think of it, which is a thing a traveller keeps to himself if he wants to travel back.

What I fetched

Twelve bits to a silver mark. Twenty silver marks to a gold crown, which is a Sere coin and does not stay in Grath. The Court books in embers and will tell you an ember is worth five marks; no man has ever exchanged one for the other, and I left them off.

I carried south — Sold for — I carried north — Cost me

Nine bars of Keldish iron, Stonemouth — 2 crowns and a half — One green glazed bowl — 3 marks

Two bales of wool, Tallow side — 1 crown, 9 marks — A skin of southern wine — 6 marks

A score of coarse jars, Nine Pots — 11 marks — A phial of oil for a lamp — 4 marks

One nest of tallow-wasp resin — 4 marks — Four crowns, changed at the quay — would not change at the Cross

Written across the wool row in the leaning hand of the Sere clerk who borrowed the leaves at the market: Wool entered at the crown rate. The figure is current.

Underneath, in mine: It was current until he went home. In the morning the same buyer wanted two marks less for the same bale, and the clerk was not there to enter the difference.

Against the last row, in the same leaning hand: Coins entered as four. The bowl not entered.

And below, in mine: He entered the coins and left the bowl, which is the whole art of that country in one line of writing.

The green bowl cracked on the third morning after I got home. It gave a small flat knock when I set it down and went from lip to foot without anything falling out that I could see. A southern glaze will not take a northern frost. In Sere they would have told me what it meant. In Nine Pots we swept around it and called it a bowl gone wrong.

The bowl that stands

At every feast a fourth bowl is set down full and not emptied. It stands through the feasting and is carried away still full. Nobody pours it and nobody says whose debt it holds. I asked three times and got three different silences, the last of which was the kindest.

This is the figure I brought back. There is a place in every Sere account where a bowl should be emptied and is not, and the clerks go around it the way our yard goes around the space under the drying rack. I have set it down because I saw it and not because I understand it. A hearth-maid would cover it with a date. A Court clerk would give it a number and the number would be nothing entered in the wrong column.

At the foot of this section, in a narrow upright hand added at Greybank, the verbs left out as though a verb were a thing a man could hide in a cuff: No fourth bowl. Not carried forward.

In mine, underneath: I have left his line standing. If it is not carried forward then it stands where it is, which is what I said before he wrote it.

Coming back

I came back in Ash with the dust still in the seams of my coat and a crown pressed flat between the third leaf and the fourth. The year before I went down had been the Long Cold. The barley was thin, the fuel dear, and a potter with nine bars of iron was a man with a reason to leave. I found the reason had followed me home: the same levy, the same printed figure, the same men going around a place where a line should stand and does not.

The crown buys nothing at the Cross. In Sere a coin is a thing a man can empty into a bowl and call it generosity. Here it is a thing I keep because I cannot spend it. I have not spent it and I have not entered it. The fourth bowl stands where I left it.
