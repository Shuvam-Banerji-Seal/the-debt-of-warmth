I clear the steps at the Fishstairs after the water has gone down, and I have counted the loads going up for nine springs. The river comes up the same steps every year and lays silt on them, and we shovel it off and carry it to the high ground above the bridge until the stone shows its own colour. There is a fourth step from the bottom that we do not clear. No spade goes there and no number is said over it where a man with a slate can hear, because a thing that has no number cannot be entered, and that is the only part of this work the river is allowed to keep.

I set this down in the last tend of Silt in the year the sill gave its note a week early, when the Ilm came up over the Fishstairs and laid silt waist-deep in the lower town. A man who keeps the landing tally stood at the out-of-line step and asked what the women said while they cleared, and wrote the answer down while I was still saying it, with the doing taken out and a figure put where a step should stand. So I have written it as we say it, to the step and not to the river, and put the verbs back where he took them out.

The silt at the head is mine. I held the leaf down with the hand I shovel with and did not know I had marked it. It goes into the grain and no washing takes it out, which is why we sign with it here and not with ink.

How it is cleared

  • Work from the top step down, never from the bottom up. A bank cleared from below slumps in the night and refills the step you had finished, and a woman who has done a step twice does not call it two loads, though her back knows better.
  • Keep the barrow on the high stone. A barrow left on the low step fills with river faster than a woman can tip it, and there is no trade in carrying water up a stair to pour it back.
  • Cut along the grain of the silt, not across it. The water lays it in thin leaves, one on one; a blade across the leaves lifts the whole bank, and a blade along them takes only what the step was holding.
  • Count the loads going up, never the loads coming down. A load that has reached the high ground is a load the river cannot take back. A count coming down is a count of empty barrows, which a clerk could enter and nobody else wants.
  • Say the words to the step, not over the water. Said over water they go where the silt goes and come back as weather, and a man at the Cross will give you the weather and the price of barley when he wants to know what the flood cost.
  • The fourth step from the bottom is the river’s. No spade goes there. If a girl asks why, tell her to count the loads going up and find out; if she asks again, do not answer her, because the answer is a place and a place cannot be told to a person who wants a figure.
  • Do not sing it on an Account-day. The bell goes four, four and a gap, and the gap is the fourth step. A shovelling-song set against that gap comes home wrong in the shoulder.
  • Keep the last part back until the boots have gone back over the north bridge.

The song

Said low into the heap, with the barrow steady on the high stone. One verse to one load going up. The refrain is said into the step and not to the women beside you; said to a person it becomes an account, and there is enough account at the Cross without bringing it down the stairs. One line of the refrain to one stroke of the spade.

The water laid it in thin leaves, one on one,

and it lies the way it laid, with the grain to the stone;

cut across and you lift the whole bank with the blade,

cut along and the step comes clean and stays.

Heave it and lay it on the high dry stone,

three to the barrow and the fourth its own;

the river keeps the step that no spade finds,

and sends up the silt with a door in it.

Count them going up and never count them down,

a load on the high step is a load the river cannot drown;

the barrow on the low step fills as fast as you tip,

and the fourth is the river’s and stays where it is.

Heave it and lay it on the high dry stone,

three to the barrow and the fourth its own;

the river keeps the step that no spade finds,

and sends up the silt with a door in it.

Tap a cup on the stair and it answers full,

tap the silt in the trough and it answers dull;

a house is a cup with its rim in the flood,

and the note it gives back is the note it had.

Heave it and lay it on the high dry stone,

three to the barrow and the fourth its own;

the river keeps the step that no spade finds,

and sends up the silt with a door in it.

It came up the same steps and took a different door,

which is the whole of its manner and has been since the First;

the silt on the third step came out of a floor

in a house down the row where the water went in.

Heave it and lay it on the high dry stone,

three to the barrow and the fourth its own;

the river keeps the step that no spade finds,

and sends up the silt with a door in it.

One in twelve is printed and read twice at the Cross,

and the other goes up the Clay Road in a box;

the flood comes in Silt and the levy comes on the tenth,

and neither of them asks which house it will take next.

Heave it and lay it on the high dry stone,

three to the barrow and the fourth its own;

the river keeps the step that no spade finds,

and sends up the silt with a door in it.

The sill sang a week before the ice was gone

and the town set its clocks by a stone under the water;

we set our count by the silt on the step instead,

which does not tell the hour but tells where the water has been.

Heave it and lay it on the high dry stone,

three to the barrow and the fourth its own;

the river keeps the step that no spade finds,

and sends up the silt with a door in it.

You hear the boot on the stone before the slate comes in sight,

dry at the heel, clean at the cuff, and the figure written tight;

he takes what the river left and calls it a measure of warmth,

and the silt on the step has no name for the house it came from.

Heave it and lay it on the high dry stone,

three to the barrow and the fourth its own;

the river keeps the step that no spade finds,

and sends up the silt with a door in it.

There is a space before the last refrain in the singing, the length of a step that is not shovelled. I have left it on the tally. A clerk put a round nought in it once, which is a thing you can hold up and look at; a step is not.

The part they keep back

Not sung while the landing-clerk stands at the out-of-line step, and not while a man from Greybank is on the bridge. Said to the barrow and not across the stair.

The fourth step from the bottom is the river’s. No spade goes there.

A pot is broken at a door and the sherd is set on the stair,

and a child come back from the bank has a house in its mouth

and cannot say which house, and the river can, and will not.

We go around the fourth with the barrow, the way a house goes round

what it will not sweep. The clerk put a nought in the place where it stood;

a nought is a figure and travels, and a step is a place and does not.

What the landing-clerk did

He keeps the tally at the out-of-line step and writes in broad charcoal on the back of his own spoiled leaves, the way a man writes who has had wet hands on a board all morning. Against three to the barrow and the fourth its own he set: Four loads entered. The fourth is not a load. No deduction arises. Against the river keeps the step that no spade finds he wrote: step submerged; no entry lies. He did not look at the fourth step while he wrote it, which is the only honest thing his hand did all morning.

Underneath, in mine: He is right that the fourth is not a load and wrong about what keeps it. He has counted the silt and left out the water, which is the whole art of that board. Both stand.

What the factor’s clerk did

A clerk come up from Greybank with a cuff gone soft from washing borrowed the leaf on an Account-day, with ink settled in two fingers of it that would not come out. He took the verbs out and set his figures above mine, and I have left his hand standing and put ours underneath, as the step keeps the old silt showing under the new.

  • At the water laid it in thin leaves, he wrote alluvial deposit, unassessed. True in the figure and false on the stair, where a leaf of silt is a floor that a woman swept before the flood.
  • At it came up the same steps and took a different door, he wrote loss not attributable, and underlined it twice. He attributes the door to nobody, which is a kindness in a language that has no word for a house.
  • At the levy comes on the tenth, he wrote deduction current; one part in twelve, and set the printed figure against it. He has entered the number that was read and not the number that was paid, which is the difference between a clerk and a woman carrying a barrow up a stair.
  • At the space before the last refrain he put a round nought and wrote vacancy. A nought is a figure and can be carried over the bridge. The fourth is a place and stays where the water put it.

What the woman of the eel-stalls put in the margin

The thick charcoal belongs to a woman who keeps the stalls below the bridge and has sold eels at dawn since before the Glaze Wars. I have copied it as it stands.

At the silt on the third step came out of a floor: It came out of my sister’s floor in the year of the flower. She swept that floor on the Last Day and left the grey in the corner as we do, and the river came and took both and laid them on the third step. I have shovelled my sister’s corner up the hill twice. I have not told the clerk and I have not put a name in the margin.

At a child come back from the bank has a house in its mouth: Mine came back and would not say whose. I asked the water and the water told me where it had put it, which was nowhere I could go. Both my answers stand.

At the head and in the gutter

The silt at the head is mine and has gone into the grain; there is no Last Day on a stair and nothing here gets gathered and burnt. The tideline along the gutter came up over both of us on the second load and took a word off the fifth verse; I wrote it back from my own mouth, which is how I know I was still saying it. The chip of sandstone in the fold is off the third step and came away under the spade with the silt still on it, which is the whole of the lesson and no use at all to a clerk. The fish-scale came off a basket set down on the leaf while a man counted six eels and was charged for five, which he has never settled and I have not entered. The gull pecked through one word of the sixth verse while the leaf lay on the stall and took no word, which was a mercy and cost somebody a crust. The green bead came off a girl with sweetness still at her tongue who leaned to see the writing and touched the leaf and went back to the stall; I have not washed it. The sour ring is small ale set down at the first verse and taken up at the last, by which time it had gone the colour of the step it stood on. The grey along the outer fold is not mine and not the clerk’s; nobody will own it, and the river will not take it, which is the only thing in this leaf the river has refused.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The steps go down and the silt comes up.

Nobody enters the fourth and it is still kept.

I asked the woman of the eel-stalls, the landing-clerk and a barge-man waiting below the bridge, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written. The tally goes back under the eel-stall board. Tomorrow the loads will go up as they went up today, three and three and the fourth where the river put it, and if a man with a soft cuff comes down from Greybank to ask what the flood cost, I shall give him the weather and put the verbs back when he has gone.
