I keep the dispensations in the factor’s house at Greybank. The office is on the roll and the hand is not, and when the factor wants a name for the hand he writes the counter, which is as much as a man who sells other people’s quiet is allowed to have. This book holds what was given out and what was paid for it, which is a small account and a clean one. I have copied it for nine years and have never once had to enter a name in the purchaser’s column, because the people who buy quiet ash come from a long way up the Hearth Road and do not wish to be found by what they have forgotten.

The year 766 is the only year in the book that gives me trouble. It has no war in it, no levy and no figure worth reading aloud. It has a child, a page from a physician and a phial with nothing under the buyer’s name. I have set the three where they stand and have not joined them, because a clerk who joins three things has begun a sermon, and a sermon gets copied further than an account and lasts longer.

The years as the counter has them

Copied from the tin. The entries are in the tally hand; where the physician’s page differs I have set it beneath.

Y.H. 763. A thin return entered for the district of Greybank, thirty marks, paid in labour and not in coin. Entered as a provision of the poor. Nothing unusual. The child came from the bank with the faint interest in a life it could not name that all thin returns bring, and was named at the moot and given a house and went about.

Y.H. 766, the third working week of Stone. A child returned thin in the year before, then about six years old, spoke words in a voice the district did not know. The hearth-maid who was called wrote them down as she heard them and could not write the voice. The physician at Greybank wrote a settling of the graft, which is the ordinary account of the second chill and is usually true, and then wrote another phrase underneath and struck it through. The struck word is still legible where the leaf has been folded. Entered as an irregularity following upon a thin return.

Y.H. 766, the last tend of Stone. One phial of quiet ash dispensed at the counter, sixty marks, entered under dispensed by the physician, the purchaser’s column left bare and the word charity written above it in a hand that is neither the physician’s nor mine.

Y.H. 812. Copied forward in the Thin Year, with the verbs put back where I found them taken out. The phial is empty. The cork is in the gutter.

In the narrow upright hand of a clerk of the College, against the second entry: An imagination of the third day. Strike spoke in a voice the district did not know.

I have not struck it. He has put imagination where the voice stood, and an imagination is a thing a child makes while a voice is a thing that comes to it, and the difference is the whole of the entry. He has never in this book changed a figure, which is the only mercy a clerk of that hand is capable of.

What the child said

Taken down at the hearth from the woman who was called, in her Grathic, of words she says were not Grathic when they were first said. She would not say them again after the physician came, and has given me the lengths of the pauses instead.

There was a bowl at the third hour and it went round.

A slate was burned for a house I don’t belong to.

I had seven names. I kept none of them.

The fire was for a debt. They clapped when it took.

She said the child used the word for a public feast and the word for a debt-slate and the word for a name-day, and that it had no word for its own mother, whom it looked at as a person looks at a wall it half remembers. It could not say where the words came from. Asked what country it was in, it said the country with the fire in the middle of the court, and then said it was cold, and then said nothing more that day.

A thin return is a pooling. Many measures go into many infants, and what comes back is a stranger with a faint interest in a life it cannot name. That is what is taught and that is what the book holds. A child who speaks of seven names and a bowl at the third hour has either more than a faint interest or an interest of another kind, and the physician’s page is where the difference went to be settled.

The physician’s page

Copied out of a leaf lent to me across the counter and returned before noon. The struck word is left struck.

The woman’s account — The physician’s hand

Spoke in a voice not of this district — A settling of the graft; the second chill

Named a bowl and a burning slate — a memory of the south, struck through

Could not name its mother — no remark

Asked to be made quiet — one phial, quiet ash, at the licence price

Underneath the last row, in the physician’s own hand, there is a line that is not a diagnosis: the child is not in pain and does not say it is frightened; the mother is. I have kept it, because a physician who writes about the mother has stopped writing about the graft, and I would rather have the thing he actually saw.

The phial

The licence price is sixty marks, which is a great deal of warmth for a small glass stopper, and the counter holds the tally. I have set it here as the book has it, with the empty column showing.

Year — Phials dispensed — Marks entered — Purchaser

Y.H. 764 — 2 — 120 — named

Y.H. 765 — 1 — 60 — named

Y.H. 766 — 1 — 60 — —

Y.H. 767 — 2 — 120 — named

The dash is not mine. It is in the book. Where the factor’s clerk came through in Ash and set charity above the empty place, he put no name into it, and there is no name for a giver anywhere in the leaf. Sixty marks went out of this house and came back as a clean column, which is the readiest way of balancing an account I know. The phial went up the Hearth Road in a pannier with two loaves and a packet of salt, which is how quiet travels in this country: under bread.

The cork shaving in the fold came off the cork when the phial was opened, not when it was sold. It was drawn across the place where the purchaser’s name would stand, the way a man draws a cork across a table while he is deciding what to leave out. I have not taken it off. There is a small square of bare paper beside it where I scraped a word away and put no other in, which I have done once in nine years and will not do again.

The corking verse

Sung at the counter by the women who bring the phials down from the Hearthseat, while a clerk writes the figure. They say a verse with the last line left out is a verse that keeps its stopper, which is the custom of that road and not an answer.

Cork the glass and shut the eye;

what came back may go back by.

Sixty marks for a quiet night

and a slate that never reads it right.

Do not ask who paid the score;

the count goes up and comes back four.

Keep the mouth shut, keep the name,

and the child will answer to the same.

The last line was sung very low and then not at all, and I have set it down as I heard it. A verse that is sung while a figure is being written is a verse about work, and work is a thing a woman can remember without being told to, which is more than can be said of an entry.

What the second hand did

The College clerk came through after the Long Cold and corrected the leaf while I held it against the counter. He is careful and he is clean. He has never once changed a figure in this book.

  • Against spoke in a voice the district did not know he set an imagination of the third day.
  • Against the country with the fire in the middle of the court he set no place is entered.
  • Against no remark, which the physician left against the child’s not naming its mother, he set not relevant to the dispensation.
  • Against the sixty marks he wrote nothing. The figure stands as the figure stood.

He took the country out of the sentence and left the phial standing in it. That is his craft, and it is a craft worth having: a child with no country is a child who was never far from home, and a phial with no purchaser is a mercy with no giver and no debt. I have put his hand above mine everywhere and left mine beneath it, as a potter leaves the old break showing under a new seam.

At the head and in the gutter

The barley dust at the head came off a measure that went over in the granary yard while I held the quire open; it goes into the paper and no wet cloth takes it out. The salt bloom along the gutter comes and goes with the weather off the Sallow and has never once been entered, though it takes the corner off a figure every winter and leaves the figure. The husk of southern grain stuck fast in the fold came up the Hearth Road in a convoy two years after the phial and has no business in this book, which is why I have kept it. The grey along the outer fold is not mine and is not the College’s. I have had this leaf six years and have not swept it. There is no Last Day behind a counter, and nothing here is burnt in the morning and called dealt with.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

They poured him out of many bowls and one of them remembered.

A silence was bought and no giver stands under it.

The phial is entered. The voice is not.

The hand wanted to be found and did not want to be known, which is the state of most hands that write in the margin of a book like this. The leaves go back into the tin behind the counter. Next tend I copy the licences for the winter and after that the granary tallies, which are clean and have no voices in them. The child has a slate-name in this country now and answers to it, which is the only answer this house requires, and the phial is empty and the cork is where it fell. I have kept the voice where it stood, in the margin, where a voice is allowed to stand.
