I keep the sick-bowls of this house. That is my office: to set a bowl of barley water at the head of a bed, to carry it away when it has been drunk, and to know which of a girl’s seven names to say when the household asks for somebody who is not in the room. I have kept these bowls eleven years and nobody has ever asked me which of the seven I meant, which tells a person how much a name is worth here and how much a bowl.

I write in the thin gold because thick gold is for contracts, and a contract is a thing said to a man who is not in the room. A remedy is said to the bowl. If the bowl answers, that is between them. The tally-clerk writes above my lines and a northern woman writes underneath, and I have left both standing, because a house that keeps only one hand in a book of physic has stopped learning, which is the same as a bowl that has stopped pouring.

The thin gold has gone off half the remedies, and where it has gone the uncleared contract shows through: nine marks, a date, a house-name grander than the house. I have not written over it. A physician who covers her leaf will soon be covering a patient.

The order of a sick-room

Written small in the thin gold, with one word gone over in resin.

  • Set the bowl at the head, never at the feet. A person takes from the top down, as a bowl empties from the lip.
  • Leave the court door open. A sick one who cannot hear the third hour will die of wondering what the bell said, which is the more expensive death.
  • Ask whose bowl it is before you fill it. A slave’s warmth is the house’s holding and a free servant’s is her own, and a physician who gives the same physic to both has made a gift of goods that were never hers.
  • Pour for the house, not for the person. The person belongs to the house and the house knows what it wants. I have set down what I was told, not what I would choose, which is a physician’s whole art here.
  • When the burning winds come off the east, wet a cloth and lay it over the lips. The wind takes what is loose, and a man’s lips are the loosest thing he has.
  • At the third hour, stop. The bowl is being emptied in every court in the city and our small room is not behindhand.
  • Do not tap a person to hear whether they hold. A bowl-surgeon taps the clay; a man who taps a person is asking a question whose answer belongs to the owner.

I have set down more bowls than I can count, which is why I count them in sevens and leave the eighth place standing. A girl here may have seven names by the time she is grown and keep whichever the household prefers, and a book of remedies ought to be at least as honest as a household.

The seven remedies, as they are used

Trouble — What is set to it — The manner of it — Who is emptied

A fever that burns — Bleed at the elbow; a bowl of wine at the head — Take from the arm while the head is still clear, and give the wine after, to fill what was taken. A physician of the north would leave the blood in and give water. I have set her words underneath mine and both stand. — The patient, in the arm, by the house’s leave.

The thirst of the south — Water, and a wet cloth over the lips — Not wine. Wine is for the third hour and for the emptying, and a man who drinks it in a sick-room is being emptied without a slate. The water-carrier brings it up from the court well and is not thanked. — The water-carrier, who carries it and is not entered.

A hand taken by the grain mills — Tallow, a clean fold, resin if a graft must hold — A nest of tallow-wasp resin costs four marks and holds a patch until the vessel takes. The bowl-surgeon lifts the nest to the light and listens to it before he buys, the way a potter listens to a pot. In Grath they burn the nests. I have never asked why. — The mill. It takes no account and names no names.

The stone-cough of the dust — Steam of hot water under a cloth; honey if the house has it — To the mouth, not the face. The burning winds put a yellow grit in everything, and a man coughs it up in the morning in a colour he will not discuss. The woman of the mills writes that no steam will bring it up and that I am treating the symptom of a wind. Both stand. — The wind, which does not answer.

A wall that has given way within — A living patch, held under pressure with resin — Take the wall from a donor whose measure was shaped like the sick one’s. A patch from a donor shaped otherwise takes anyway and goes on wanting, and a person who wants a life that was not theirs is unsafe to exhibit. The bowl-surgeons know this and the buyers do not. — The donor, first, and the buyer afterward.

A child that will not take milk — A wet-nurse come up from the mills, for a season — She was returned thin and has milk enough for one; the house has bought her for two. Give the child barley water when she sleeps and do not tell the house how little there is. — The other child at the mill, who was not bought.

A wound that will not close — A pinch of quiet ash, then a clean fold — The house buys back its own funeral ash, a year’s grain burnt under one person and the remainder sold at the price they ask. I have set a pinch in a wound gone three days yellow and watched it go quiet by evening. The Court licenses such ash only to the rich; the penalty for selling it unlicensed is a greyed hand. I have never sold any. I have set it in wounds. — Nobody. This is the row I keep turned down.

There is an eighth place in this book where a remedy would stand for the trouble a person has when they remember who they were. I have left it the length of a name. The tally-clerk has put a figure in it; the figure stood and the place stood beside it and the two did not touch.

On the full note

Tap a sound bowl with the knuckle and it answers full. Tap a cracked one and it answers flat, a small dead sound that stops at the surface. The bowl-surgeons listen to the clay before they pour a graft into it. A green-glazed bowl bought from a northern potter gave a flat knock in its first frost and went from lip to foot without anything falling out that I could see. In the Clay Yard they would call it a bowl gone wrong and sweep around it. Here the house calls northern clay inferior, and I have not corrected the house.

A northern potter tapped the ribs of an exhibited man at a feast and said the man had gone flat. He was a guest in that court and I did not correct him; in this country a guest who is corrected stops being a guest and becomes a witness. I have written his words without his name, which is the only mercy a physician here can practise without a slate.

Of the beautiful return

This is the part of the book the house would have me leave out, so I have set it in the middle where nobody skipping to a remedy can miss it.

When a person has been emptied and brought back thin, the bowl-surgeons warm them and pour until the head rises. The memory goes first, then the appetite, then the sense of self; what comes back is calm and wants nothing. The house calls this a beautiful return and exhibits it at feasts as proof of generosity. A woman with seven names came back from one and kept the name the house preferred, saying she had always been that one, and the house was well pleased.

A physician of the north wrote that the calm is a burn. The copy that came down to me had the word taken clean away, as though a word could be taken off paper the way wine is taken off a bowl. I have written it back in the thin gold. I have not seen the burn. I have seen the calm, and a woman who said she had always been the name the house kept. I have set both sentences down, because a book that holds only one of them is a ledger, and I would sooner be wrong in a book than right in a ledger.

The song for the third hour

Sung at the wine-press, not at the feast, with the vowels open and the ends thrown into the fire. The last line of the third part is said to the bowl and not to the company.

The wind comes yellow and takes the lip,

the mill turns under and takes the hand;

a bowl goes round and a name goes round,

and the one that is kept is the one that stands.

Pour for the house and pour for the heat,

pour for the ash that the house buys back;

the calm comes first and the wanting goes,

and the bowl goes dry and says no lack.

Three at the board and a fourth on the shelf,

seven on a girl and one on the slate;

the ash comes back and the wine comes round,

and what went out . . . . . . . . . . .

There is a space before the last line where a name would stand. I have left it the length it is. The tally-clerk has tried twice to fill it; both times the space stood under the figure, which is the only argument this house has ever lost and never admitted.

What the tally-clerk did

He borrowed the leaf after the third hour, being a man who cannot let paper lie, and writes in the leaning counting-hand with the gold gone thin.

  • At a house-leech’s mercy he struck out mercy and set above it return completed, current.
  • Against the quiet ash he wrote licensed residue, market rate, and put a figure in the margin where I had put a pinch.
  • Against the eighth place he set a round nought and wrote vacancy underneath, as though a place where a name had been were the same as nothing.
  • He took the verbs out of two lines. A sentence with no verb travels through a counting room without catching on anything, which is what a counting room wants of a sentence.

I have put the words back underneath his and left his standing above. He can enter the ash at the market rate, for the house paid it. What he cannot enter is the space at the end of the song, which tells a person he knows the difference between a figure and a place better than a physician has any right to.

What the woman of the mills wrote underneath

She is a bond-woman come up from the grain mills, returned thin, and she writes in charcoal on the backs of barley ration tallies with a hand that shakes at the ends of words. She would not let me put her name.

Against the bleeding: I have seen four bled. Two were quieter by evening. Quiet is not better.

Against the beautiful return: The calm is not peace. It is a room with the door shut and nobody in it.

Against the eighth place: That is where my name was. I have not forgotten it. I have only stopped saying it where a clerk can hear.

I have left her words without a name, which is the only kindness in this house that a clerk cannot enter. She took her charcoal away and did not ask for the leaf back, which is how I know she meant it.

At the head and in the gutter

The olive thumb at the head is mine; I turn the leaves with the hand I carry the bowl with. The dust of the burning winds has worked into the sewing rather than lying on the face, which is how a person tells a book carried in a coat from one read in a court. The resin smear caught dust in the third year and will not come out, and I have stopped trying. The wine-ring at the foot belongs to the third hour and is small. The scorched corner is where the leaf lay too near a court fire and went brown without burning through, so the writing there is thin and I have not gone over it, because a word written thin is a word said quietly and most of ours are. The grey print along the gutter came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying, and I have not swept it. There is no Last Day in a house of the Ten Bowls and nothing gets swept. The grain of wheat in the resin came from a loaf sent north and returned uneaten. The pale hair in the fold is not gold and is not mine.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The fire takes the grain and the house buys back the smoke;

the bowl takes the wine and nobody buys that back.

I asked the bowl-keeper, the tally-clerk and a woman who would not give her name, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The book goes back under the fourth bowl, where I can find it in the dark. Tomorrow the third hour will come, and I shall set a bowl down at the head of a bed and carry it away when it has been drunk, and if a girl has seven names I shall say the one the house keeps and leave the other six standing.
