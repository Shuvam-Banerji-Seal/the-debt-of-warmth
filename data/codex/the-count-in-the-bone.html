I keep the survey at Keld Keep, in a room of slate and lead with its door facing the shaft. Five leagues higher there are standing stones older than the Return, and the Vorr sit against them for days and come down with numbers. I have sat within sight of them three seasons and have never been asked to move, which in that country is as near a welcome as a man from below is given. What follows is what they said to the stone while I was near enough to hear.

The manner first, because a saying said wrongly is a different saying. Say them slowly, with the vowels opened and the ends left in the air, once, and to the stone or the ground beside it. Do not say them looking at a Vorr. Said to a man, one of these sentences becomes a question with an answer owed, and there is no sentence here with an answer owed in it. Ask the same thing twice and you will be told the same thing twice, which is not anger but the exactness of a people who give a name once in forty years. A third asking is the fault of the asker. I have set the words in Grathic letters with the Keldish endings struck off, because Vorr speech is not written and bone-notches travel badly in a pocket. Their counting-stones are marked with grey dust off a blighted seam, which they do not sell and which has rubbed into the head of this leaf.

Of the number and what it is for

  1. “The numbers are correct.”

Gloss — What a Vorr says when a man asks what the oldest slates say. It is not a translation, and he will say so himself if he is in a patient mood, which is a mood waited for the way weather is waited for. The surveyors of Keld call the oldest marks Ash and the Court calls them language; a Vorr says the numbers are correct. I have set the three answers one under another and made none of them a sentence of mine.

  1. “A stone is kept. A number is sold.”

Gloss — They sell nothing else. In Y.H. 758 they stopped selling warm-stone to the Court and gave no reason, and the Court entered it as a cause with no effect, which is the figure it keeps for a thing it has been told and does not like the telling. The counting-stones stay with those who marked them; the numbers go down the road on a surveyor’s slate. This is not a saying about trade. It is a saying about what may leave a house.

  1. “The count is in the bone. The tube finds nothing there.”

Gloss — The collector’s cold iron tube takes what is in the blood, and a Vorr keeps his measure in the bone, which makes him difficult to draw and impossible to bank in the ordinary way. They do not undergo the Return. When one dies the others seat the body upright facing the direction it came from and leave it uncovered, and say we were never owed. I have seen such a body on the high path and did not go near it. A Grathic hearth-maid would have covered it with a fold and crossed two fingers. Both acts are kindnesses; only one has a number in it.

  1. “The number after a sitting is not the number before.”

Gloss — At forty a Vorr sits against a stone for a season and comes down with the number changed. What he heard is not recorded. The Court, which employs them as surveyors and watches them carefully, enters the change because it must, and has never asked to hear the reason, which is the only thing I have seen the Court do with a Vorr that a man could call restraint. A lowlander calls this a rise. A Vorr says the number is different, the way another man says the weather has turned, and goes on with his sitting.

Of the sitting and the hearing

  1. “The stone does not answer the first asking.”

Gloss — Names come one at a time and many years apart, and a Vorr is known by the stone he keeps rather than by a family. The first sitting gives a number and not a name. I have known one through two sittings and still do not know what to call him except by his stone; he has not offered the other word. A man from the lowlands takes this for a riddle. It is an arrangement of years.

  1. “The hearing divides the work. The body does not.”

Gloss — They do not divide labour or inheritance by sex as we do below. They divide it by whether a person has sat against a stone long enough to hear it, which takes most of a life and is not permitted to the impatient. This is not a kindness. One who has heard does the counting, and a woman who has heard counts as readily as a man who has, which is a fact the Court’s roll has never found a column for.

  1. “What is heard is not written. What is written can be carried away.”

Gloss — The Vorr keep the long listenings in the head and write none of them. A clerk once asked one to set down what the stone had said and was given its height, which was true and useless and which he entered. There are perhaps four thousand Vorr, most of them here in Keld and almost none in Grath, and I have never heard of one writing a sentence. A thing said to a stone stays where it was said, which is more than can be said for a thing said at a table.

  1. “The impatient hear the stone scratch.”

Gloss — They will not let an impatient person sit, and there is no argument about it. A man who cannot sit still has spent his season and brings back a number he could have had at home, and a temper besides. I watched a young surveyor try it in Ash and come down with the figure unchanged. He has not been up since, and neither has his temper.

Of the questions men carry up the stones

Men walk five leagues of scree with a question and bring back the same answer in different words. I have set four here, with what was said on the stone and what was entered at Greybank.

What was asked — What the Vorr said — What was entered

What do the oldest slates say? — The numbers are correct. — Figures confirmed by the party.

Why is there no return for your dead? — We were never owed. — The party is outside the roll.

What did you hear in the season? — The sitting continued. — No proceedings to record.

Why was the warm-stone stopped in Y.H. 758? — The number is sold. The stone is kept. — Supply discontinued, cause unrecorded.

In the narrow upright hand of the Court’s clerk, across the foot of the table: Four answers reduced to entries. Underneath, in mine: One is a refusal and two are silences, and a table with four rows is a fine thing to carry into a room where nobody has climbed the five leagues.

What they say at the turning of a sitting

Said low, facing the way the last Vorr died, with the hands flat on the stone. The young say the first part before a sitting and the old say the second after it. Nobody says both in one breath.

Sit to the stone and the stone to the cold,

one for the name not yet bestowed;

the count goes in where the bone is old,

and the bone keeps what the blood let go.

Say it once. It is said.

Say it twice. It is the same.

The stone was here before the sitting.

The number changes. The stone remains.

We were never owed. We do not ask.

Gloss — The third line is the only one that names the bone, and it is said to the stone, not to the person sitting. Children ask what the name not yet bestowed is and are told to ask the stone, which is the answer their elders were given. A clerk at Greybank put a round nought after the last line and wrote exempt, not discharged below. A nought is a number; the line was a refusal, and the two have never touched.

Of the dead and what is not owed

  1. “We were never owed.”

Gloss — A Vorr death has no slate, no deduction and no return. They stop. The others seat the body upright facing the direction it came from and leave it, and say this once. I asked what becomes of the measure and was told there is no measure to become anything, which is not an answer in our tongue and is a complete one in theirs.

  1. “He came from there. He faces there.”

Gloss — Said over the body before they leave it, with the chin set toward the way it came. It is not a prayer. There is no fire carried up from the Keep and no one reads anything aloud. A man who washes bodies at the Keep asked me whether they were sending the dead home, and I said no, and he wrote nothing down, which was the only honest thing he did that morning.

  1. “The drawer has borrowed from a debt that was not his.”

Gloss — Said of one who draws from a well or a seam without owning it. The warmth comes out of somebody’s account in another district and the district pays in cold, and the man who took it calls it a current. A Vorr calls it borrowing, which is the word the Court takes out of every sentence it writes. I have set it here because it is the only Vorr saying I know with a person paying in it.

What the borrowed hand did

A survey-clerk of the factor at Greybank borrowed the rubbing in Ilmen. He writes upright and narrow and takes the verbs out as though a verb were a thing a man could carry off in a cuff.

  • Against we were never owed he set the party is outside the roll, which leaves the owing out and the party in.
  • Against the numbers are correct he wrote figures confirmed by the party and underlined it twice, as though a man confirming a number had said something about it.
  • Against the count is in the bone he set the measure is retained, not deposited, which is true in a counting room and false on a high path, where a bone is neither a bank nor a box.
  • Against the number after a sitting is not the number before he wrote revaluation after lapse of time, and put the season in a column where a season has never stood.

I have left his hand above mine everywhere, as a newer repair lies over an older one in the seam. He strikes out the doer because a thing with nobody in it cannot be argued with. I put the doer back because a boy at forty with a stone before him wants to know which way to face.

In the hand of a Vorr

One of them wrote across the lower margin while I turned the leaf, in letters so large and slow that the line took the better part of an afternoon. He used grey dust off a blighted seam and pressed the doubled joint of his last finger into the tallow beside it. The dust has worked into the grain and does duty for a signature.

You have written much. The numbers are correct.

The first sentence is a judgment on this leaf and the second an answer to the whole of it. I have not glossed either. A man who glosses a Vorr’s two sentences has begun to teach the stone his own trade.

In the margin, in charcoal, a clamp-master’s hand

A master off the Duelling Floor borrowed the leaf below stairs and wrote, with the charcoal he marks hands with:

Ask a Vorr what the clamp took and he will tell you the number. He will not tell you the hand.

I have set that where he wrote it. Both sentences are true, and the second is the reason the first was written. He did not sign it, and the charcoal has rubbed thin where the fold is carried.

At the head and in the gutter

The grey at the head is counting-stone dust off the finger that wrote the two large lines; it goes into the grain and no damp takes it out, which is why they use it for marks they mean to keep. The coal smut at the fold is mine, off the sleeve I wipe the survey with. The warm-stone shaving was left on the leaf by a Vorr who went away without it; it held its heat one night on the stone floor and is cold now, which is the order of things here. The frost-feather came off a stone at the high path and was gone before I folded the leaf. The lead bead rolled out of the Ninth Gallery, where the rock sweats and the smell of hot lead gives what warning it gives; a surveyor laid it down and did not take it up. The clamp-scratch across two glosses was made by the master showing a boy how iron bites; both were honest men afterwards and one has a dead hand. The grey along the outer fold is not mine and not the Court’s. I have not swept it. There is no Last Day above this Keep.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The stone has kept more counts than the Court has written.

It has never entered one.

I asked a surveyor who carries the rubbing up, a clamp-master off the floor, and a Vorr come down with his number changed, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written. The rubbing goes back into its wire fold in the survey room and the grey dust onto the high shelf where they keep it and nobody buys it. Tomorrow I shall go up five leagues and sit where I am allowed, and if a Vorr says the numbers are correct I shall write it once and not ask him twice, which is the whole of the trade above this Keep.
