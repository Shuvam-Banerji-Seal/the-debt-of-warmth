I took lime up the Rim Road in Ash, when the frost had not yet closed the high places and a cart could still make the Keep in nine days. The load was mine and the horse was not. A man who owns the horse does the arguing, and I was paid to carry stone and not to argue. At Keld Keep I gave my measure before my name at the Warden’s hall, which is their custom and not a rudeness, and the clerk wrote the figure first and asked the name second, as though a name were something a man might keep back and use again.

I bought a warm-stone there, a piece the length of my palm, and paid six marks, which is a great deal of lime. The man who sold it said it had come up out of the Ninth Gallery and had kept a fire since before his father. He did not say whose fire, and I did not ask. A warm-stone is a piece of somebody’s account that you can hold in your hand, and the man who sells it will tell you the price and not the debt.

The post, and the road that ends

The survey post stands ten leagues beyond the Keep, where the Rim Road goes through into the cold. There is a post and no gate, which is the first thing a man from the clay countries asks about and the last thing anybody tells him. I asked the keeper what the post was for. He said it was for counting. I asked what it counted. Men, he said. I asked where the gate was. He looked at me a while and said that a gate is a thing you put up when you mean to stop somebody, and the Warden’s surveyors do not promise to bring a man back.

That is the truest thing I was told on the whole road, and I have set it above the rest.

Beyond the post the road goes on for a day as a track, and then it goes on for another day as a place where a track once was, and then it stops being a road and becomes the ground again. A surveyor come up from the shaft had drawn the way for me on a slate before I left the Keep. He drew as far as the post and stopped, and turned the slate face down. I asked him why he had not drawn the rest. He said that a line drawn past the post was a lie told with a ruler, and that his office had never yet been thanked for a lie that could be measured.

The frost came up out of the paper on that climb. It is in the head of this book still, or was when I copied it, and was gone by the middle of the day. I set down that much and no more, because a man who writes about frost as though it meant something has already begun a sermon.

The days that held

I kept the days in charcoal on birch bark, which is what a quarryman keeps anything with that he means to carry up a mountain. Charcoal rubs off. The first day beyond the post I wrote the date and the word track. The second I wrote stone, no road. On the third I could not find the bark, and when I found it under a bank of snow the writing had come away on my thumb and there was no date to put back. After that I set down what the days had in them and not which day it was, which is a poor way to keep a reckoning and the only one that country allows.

In the bark — What I entered at Greybank — What stood underneath

The day of the post — post, no gate — the same, struck and written above

A day of track — track — track

A day of stone — stone, no road — stone, no road

— — nought — a space the length of a thumb

A day with the sitting man — man seated, not covered — the words not covered scraped away

— — nought — a space, and a second space beside it

A day a man gave me a name — name given, not retained — the letters of it, with no sound

The climb back — strap let out two holes — the horse’s name, which I had forgotten

I have left the spaces where the clerk put his noughts. A nought is a figure. A space is a day I cannot spend. The two have stood side by side in this book for a year and nobody has asked me to make them agree, which is the only mercy I have ever had from a counting room.

What was given, and what was taken

A Wasteling gave me a stone on the second or third day and asked nothing for it and would not take the bowl I offered. I carried the stone back and sewed it into the corner of this book, where it sits now, grey and cold and worth nothing. It is the only thing I brought home that I can still account for.

Another told me his name. I wrote it on the bark in charcoal, and when I came to copy it at Greybank the marks were there and the sound of them was gone, so that I had a name in a hand I could not say aloud. I have left the marks where they stand. A man who cannot say a name has still had one given to him, which is more than I can say of the reason I went.

A third said he would give me a day of his warmth. I put out my hand. He put his hand on mine and let go, and I was no warmer, and yet I came back a day short somewhere that I have never been able to find. I have entered this as it happened and not as a bargain, because a bargain is a thing two men remember and I remember only the hand.

They say out there that the cold is not empty but is full of what nobody kept. I asked a surveyor what that meant. He said it was a saying, and that sayings are what men keep when they have no figures. I asked a Wasteling the same thing and he asked me if I was warm. I told him I had a stone in my coat. He said, then you have not come far enough.

The sitting dead

I came on a man sitting with his back against a stone, facing the way he had come, which was south-east, toward the Keel. His knees were drawn up and his hands lay on them. Nothing had been put over him. Frost had made a white seam along one shoulder and a little on the sleeve, and there was no drift against him, because he had been set there to be seen and not to be hidden.

I stood a while. A man who carries lime does not often have time to stand, and I have thought about that standing more than I have thought about the road.

A Wasteling came by and I asked whether they set the dead like that for love or for fear. He asked me if I was warm. I gave him the answer I had given the other, and he said that a warm man who asks that question has not yet understood the answer, and went on.

In Grath we break a pot at the door of a death-house to let the heat out. Out here there are no pots and no doors, and a man is left facing the road. I have set the two customs side by side and joined neither, because a custom is a thing a man does with his hands and not a thing he can explain to a clerk.

The song of the sitting dead

Taken down beyond the post, from a man who would sing it only while he was walking and not while he stood still. It is said to the ground and not to a person. The last line is said twice; the second time is for those who went in and did not come back to say why.

The cold is not empty;

it is a room with a name in it.

We set the dead to face the road

and put no cloth on them;

whatever they came for, they can see it still.

A stone is given and no thanks entered.

A name is given and no man keeps it.

Ask the warm man what he carries ---

he will carry it back.

There is no Last Day out here.

Nothing is swept. Nothing is owed.

The cold keeps what nobody kept.

He would not give the name of the man who made it, and said that songs from that country are made by the country, which is an answer of the kind the coast gives and the clay countries do not. I have put the song where I heard it, walking, and away from the post.

What the clerk did at Greybank

The factor’s clerk borrowed the leaves on a market morning. He writes upright and narrow and has ink settled in two fingers of his cuff that will not come out. He laid his hand on the top sheet before he looked at me, which is the manner of that table.

  • Against a space where a day stood he wrote nought, and beneath that, no entry lies. I have put the space back under the figure. A nought can be carried into a court room; a space cannot, which is why one of them is honest.
  • Against I do not remember why I went he wrote motive not entered, which is true in the language of his office and false in mine. I did not fail to enter it. It is not in me to enter.
  • Against they gave and asked nothing for it he wrote no consideration recorded; gift void. Perhaps it is void. I have kept the stone sewn into the corner, and it has never once been entered.

I have left both readings everywhere, the thin one under the neat one, as a quarryman leaves the old cut showing in a face of stone. A clerk who takes the man out of a sentence leaves the road standing by itself, and a road standing by itself has carried nobody, cost nobody, and has no figure in it to argue with.

At the head and in the gutter

The frost at the head came up out of the paper on the climb and was gone before noon; it kept no figure and asked for none. The lime in the gutter is mine, off the load I took up and did not bring back. The tallow at the fold is my lamp, set too near the leaf because the light goes early up there. The grey stone in the lower corner came from beyond the post and has never been warm and never will be. The flake in the fourth leaf was warm-stone once and is cold now, and I have kept it because a man should be able to see the price he paid even after the price has gone out of it. The grey along the outer fold is not mine and not the factor’s. I have not swept it. Beyond the post there is no Last Day on which a man might sweep, and I have not found one since I came back.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

A post and no gate, and a road that ends in stone;

a man gave me a name I cannot say.

I carried warmth up and came back with a cold stone;

the reason went somewhere I cannot find.

Ask the man seated what he waits for.

I asked a Wasteling, a surveyor come up from the Keep, and a clerk at Greybank, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The leaves go back into the tin, into the pocket the strap has worn. Tomorrow I take the cart down to the lime-yard and enter nothing, which is the only entry the road beyond the post has ever asked of me.
