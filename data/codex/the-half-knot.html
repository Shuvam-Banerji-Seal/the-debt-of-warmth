I coil and mend the ropes for the boats that lie under the tide-tally house, and I have set this down because a girl of the Quay asked me why the half in the rope was for a man who never held a share and never would. Her father came aboard, which is what we call a man who marries into a boat-owning house: his word on the boat counts at half, and has counted so these nine years, and he knows it and does not say it where a clerk can hear him. The girl put him into a skipping rope. I have written it as the children keep it, with the verbs left in, because a rhyme said to a rope wants a hand to lift the next line, and a rhyme said to a Council man wants nothing but a figure.

The leaf is the back of a tide-draw the Council left out in the rain. The figures on the face of it have gone where the Council’s figures go. The green at the corner is a bead of cliff-glass pressed there by a girl of the furnace who leaned over to read and did not ask for it back; the tar is mine, off the coil I was holding when the wind came up off the Sallow; the salt bloomed out of the paper itself, which is a virtue in this country and a folly in every other. The grey along the gutter came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying on a Quay, and nobody will own it. There is no Last Day on a Quay.

How it is played

  1. The rope is an old tow-rope gone soft, tarred once and rained on since. Eleven knots stand in it. The twelfth place is where a knot has been untied; the cord there is frayed and lighter than the rest. The two ends are called the flood and the ebb. Two children hold the ends and turn; the rest are shares, and skip in the middle. They are the flood, the ebb and the shares, and are not called by their house-names while the rope is going round.
  2. Play it on the boards under the tide-tally house at the head of the flood, or on the Salt Pans at dawn before the water has crusted. Never at the ebb, and never when the quay-crier calls the turn: the rope stops then, because a count is being taken elsewhere and two counts in one morning make a debt. Not in the Five, and not while a boat is going out with a dead man aboard, which is a watching and not a game.
  3. Say the words to the rope, not to the faces. Said to a face it becomes a bargain, and in Vhal a bargain has to be kept, which no child wants at a rope.
  4. One line, one skip. The eleventh line is skipped like the others. The half is not skipped. At the half the two ends stop with the rope knee-high, nobody crosses it, and every hand goes to the frayed place where the knot came out. A child who skips the half is come aboard: she takes the ebb end and turns until three others call her by the name she has at home. Then she comes back into the middle. That is the whole of the bringing back.
  5. If the rope catches a foot, the same thing: she goes to the ebb end, and stays there until she is called back. Nobody calls her quickly. The ebb end is not a punishment anyone names, and a child asked why she is standing there will say she is turning, which is true.
  6. There is no last round and nothing is won. The child who has kept the half the most times gets to say the verse about the man aboard, and says it to the rope with her back turned to the Quay.

The counting-out

The rope lies on the boards. A child lays a finger on each knot from the flood end, one knot to a name, and does not look at the faces. The finger’s knot turns the flood end; if it lights on the frayed place, that child turns the ebb. This is not a prize. The ebb end is where the rope goes out of the hands.

Flood and ebb and coil and cleat,

the share goes round and the half keeps its seat;

mother’s mother, aunt and kin ---

the finger stops where the knot has been.

The eleven lines, as they are skipped

One line, one skip, over the rope as it comes round. The tenth line is skipped whole. The eleventh is skipped whole. The half is kept.

One for the keel and the cord that binds,

two for the share that the mother finds;

three for the aunt who carries it down,

four for the coil that goes soft in the wet;

five for the tar that stays on the hand,

six for the gull that steals from the brazier;

seven for the pan where the water stands white,

eight for the bench where the seven years sit;

nine for the paper the rain has made nothing,

ten for the crier who calls what has not come;

eleven for the word we say at the board,

and half for the man who came aboard.

The second line is the share that passes through the mother, and the third is the aunt who carries it down to the boat when the mother cannot. There is no fourth line for a husband. A man who comes aboard is put in the half, which is the last line and the one nobody skips. Ask a child why the half comes last and she will ask you why a man should be counted before a boat.

What is said at the half

Hands on the frayed place. The rope is held still, knee-high, and nobody crosses. The words go down to the cord and not across to the children.

The knot’s gone. The place is kept.

The cord holds what no mother had.

Say it to the cord and not to a face ---

a half is a share with nobody under it.

The verse they do not say while a man of the Council is on the Quay

I have this from the girl who keeps the half, with the space left before it, which is the space the children leave when the rope stops. She said it to the boards with her back turned and made me set it down with none of my verbs in it.

The share goes down the mother’s row,

the half goes nowhere and is not signed;

the Council writes on paper made to rot

and calls the rotting law. We call it weather.

Eleven whole and a half kept out;

the crier turns and the rope turns too,

but the crier’s turn is not the rope’s,

and the half stands where the knot came through.

The draw follows the Warm and not the moon; every dipper knows it and no Council man will say it in front of a factor. The children know the crier’s turn and their own are two turns, which is a thing the tally-house has never entered.

What the woman at the pans put in the margin

The charcoal here is hers, pressed hard and leaning, the way a woman writes who has dipped at dawn for thirty years and does not want a sentence read by a man who has never stood in salt water.

At the half: He came aboard and counted at half for nineteen years before he died, and never once said the word in his own house. The children have put him in a rope. It is more than any of us could do with him.

At the cord holds what no mother had: A share goes through the mother. That is the only road it is allowed. My half had no road and went nowhere all his life, and the rope has given it somewhere to stand. Do not make a proverb of this before you have kept a half yourself.

What the Council clerk did

A clerk of the Council borrowed the leaf on a market morning, being a man who cannot let paper lie still. He writes in the leaning hand with the salt in the nib, and the damp has had at him since. He struck out the half keeps its seat and set above it, half-interest unenumerated. Against a half is a share with nobody under it he wrote no entitled party appears, which is a true thing in a language nobody on this Quay speaks at a rope. At the frayed place, where the children put their fingers, he drew a small circle and wrote nothing inside it, and the rain has gone round the circle and left it standing, so that a place where a knot came out is now a figure that can be copied into a column.

I have put the words back underneath and left his hand above. He writes so a thing can travel up the Salt Road; I write so a child knows which hand to lift. A circle on a damp leaf is a figure. The frayed place is where somebody’s father stands, and it will not travel, and the Council has never had a column for a man standing at half. He asked what the children learned by it. I told him the count, the stopping and the bringing back. He wrote rudimentary instruction in shares, and went away with his cuff gone soft at the fold, which is the only thing about him the weather has improved.

At the head and in the gutter

The salt bloom at the head came out of the paper and has gone white in the grain where the leaf was doubled; it will not wash out, which is why we keep such leaves in tarred tin and not in a house. The tar thumb is mine and came off the coil I held down in the wind; a coil paid for with a wet hand goes soft when the weather goes soft, and I know by it when to shut the tin. The rope fibre in the second leaf came off the very rope the game is played with, and goes frayed at the end like the eleventh place. The brine-gull peck went through one word of the sixth line and nobody has claimed the bird; the word was brazier, and a rhyme with a word gone is a rhyme said slowly, and all of ours are said slowly. The hook nick came off a man paying with his hook still in his hand, and he did not apologise. The rent at the lower corner is where the old rope frayed before I cut it down; I have kept the corner open at it. The green bead came off a girl of the furnace who touched the leaf and went back up the shore road with the sweetness at her mouth. The grey in the gutter came off the cuff and has not been swept. There is no Last Day on a Quay; the grey stays where it fell and goes into the next year under the oilcloth.

At the foot, in grey chalk, in a child’s hand

I do not jump the half. It is where my father stands.

The letters are hard at the th and one of them is set backward, which is how a child writes who has learned off a tally and not off a book. I asked the girl who keeps the half, a woman who dips at the pans and a carter waiting with his load off the Salt Road, and had three answers and no hand, as I have had before. I have left the line where it was written.

The leaf goes back into the tin under the coil. Tomorrow the boats come in at the flood and the crier will call a turn that is not the rope’s, and the children will stop their rope for it as they always do and begin again after, and the half will stand where the knot came out with nobody jumping it and nobody entering it. The Council clerk may have his circle. The girl will put her finger on the frayed place all the same.
