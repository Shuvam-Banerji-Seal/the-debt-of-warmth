I twist warp and mend nets under the net-rack on the Quay, and I have set this down because a man come down from Greybank with a clean cuff asked a girl on the bollards what the children counted when they skipped, and wrote her answer in a book before she had finished saying it. She told him a share and not a figure, which is the truth of it, and he sent me a clerk, which is what happens to a share in this country. What follows is what the children said before the clerk came and what they said again after he had gone back up the Salt Road with the verbs taken out of it. I have set his hand above theirs where he put it, as a new splice lies over an old one and shows both ends.

How it is played

Played on the flagstones between the third and fourth bollards, below the tide-tally house, in the warm months when an old warp is supple enough to turn. Not in hard frost: a frozen rope bites, and the salt on the stones takes the skin off a knee. It is played while waiting --- for the ebb, for a cart off the coast pass, for a boat that is late. Nobody plays it to pass the time. The time is passed already and the game is what fills the standing.

Two children hold the rope, one at each end, and are called the ebb and the flood, after the two ways the Sallow runs. The child inside is the boat. There is no prize in being the boat: the boat is the one who has to jump, and the others only swing. Nobody says the children’s own names while the rope is turning. A name said over a quay rope travels up the Salt Road in a cuff and comes back entered.

The rope is a cast warp gone soft in the lay. A new rope is too stiff and takes the ankles off; one that has been out twice is the only kind that will do. The turning hands do not count aloud. The boat says the verse, and the verse is said to the rope and the stones, never to a face; said looking at a person it becomes an argument, and there is always somebody on this Quay who will argue.

One line is one turn of the rope and one jump. When the rope catches an ankle the boat is cast off and goes to take an end, and the turning hand comes in. There is no winning. The game ends when somebody calls the ebb, which is any voice at all that says the boats are in.

The counting-out

Said over fists held knuckle-up, the speaker touching one fist for each word and not looking at the faces. The fist it lights on at the last word is the boat; the two after it hold the rope. The children call this a fair casting of lots, which it is not. The same child is boat three turns running and nobody on the Quay can say why.

Salt in the knot and tar on the thumb,

ebb to the left and the flood to the come;

one for the bollard, two for the stone,

three for the rope that will never be new ---

the one it lights on goes in alone.

The share-rope, as it is skipped

The boat says the first line as the rope goes over her head, and jumps on the last word of it. The ebb and the flood say nothing until the half. One line, one turn, and the verse is kept low so the tally-men at the house cannot hear the numbers coming off the stones.

One for the mother that holds the shares,

two for the mother that held them before;

the rope goes over and under the stone

and the boat comes up and comes back for more.

Three for the boat and the salt in her hold,

four for the warp that has been out twice;

a half for the man that married aboard ---

hop it. Don’t jump it. He’s not whole in the boat.

On the half line the boat makes a half: a little check of both feet, no more than a finger’s width off the flagstone. The turning hands lift the rope a hand’s breadth higher on that line, so that a full jump would carry the boat into it and cast her off. A child who jumps the half whole is not cheating and is not confused; she has given the man aboard a whole share, which is a thing no boat on this Quay has ever given him. She goes to take an end of the rope.

Five for the bollard that stands on the quay,

six for the ebb that takes what it sees;

there’s a share for the boat that went out on the tide ---

stand over it. Don’t set your foot where it was.

The last line is said to the stone and is not skipped. The rope comes all the way over and the boat lifts her feet and lets it pass beneath her, standing with her heels together on the flagstone. This is called the empty bollard. If the rope touches her she is cast off, the same as if she had jumped the half whole. Ask which boat went out on the tide and the children say the rope knows and the tally knows and neither of them says. A child new to the Quay asks again and is told to ask the ebb, which is the answer her mother was given.

The verse they do not say while a man in a soft cuff is on the Quay

I have this from the child who was boat, with the space left where she left it. She said it with her back to the tide-tally house, and made me set it down with none of my verbs in it. A verse said to a rope wants nothing added; a verse said to a man wants everything taken away.

Three shares and a half and a share with no name,

the rope keeps the count and the Quay keeps the same;

the figure goes up in a clean cuff and comes down a figure,

and the half comes back as a hop and the empty comes back as a stand.

The sea doesn’t care who married aboard.

It takes the boat and it gives back the rope.

I asked her what the last two lines were worth, and she said a rope was not obliged to be worth a figure just because a clerk had written one against it. Then she put her tar thumb on the corner of the leaf to keep it from the wind off the Sallow, as the net-women do.

What the clerk did

The factor’s clerk came down the Salt Road on the morning the ebb was late, being a man who cannot leave paper alone, with a cuff gone soft from washing and the ink sitting in two fingers of it and will not come out. He set the game down as a division of a boat’s shares among the persons holding an interest, and against the children’s count he wrote this:

The children say — As he entered it

One for the mother, two for the mother before her, three for the boat — Three shares, held, entered

A half for the man that married aboard — 0.5 share; opinion non-voting

The share for the boat that went out on the tide — Vacancy; nil; no balance outstanding

He struck out hop it, don’t jump it and set above it, the fractional interest is not exercised. Against stand over it, don’t set your foot where it was he wrote no person enters, which is true of his table and false of the flagstone. At the foot, where the children stand with their heels together and let the rope pass, he put a round nought and wrote nil.

I have put the hop back underneath and left his standing above. He takes the feet out of a thing and so has no need to say what the feet do. I put them back because a child on the flagstones does not know she is holding a fractional interest; she wants to know whether to lift both feet or one. A nought is a number. The empty bollard is a place to stand with your heels together, and no figure on this Quay has ever had a place to stand.

He asked what the children learned by it. I told him the count and the half and the standing over, and he said that was an account of inheritance taught before a child could read a slate, and he entered it. The children stop when a man in a soft cuff comes down the steps. They take it up again when he is past the tide-tally house with his book under his arm, and they never once jump the half whole.

What the tally-man put in the margin

The lean hand belongs to a man of the Council who reads the draws at the tide-tally house and will not write upright, which he says is a hand for people who have never wet a cuff. He wrote three lines against the verses:

The tide follows the Warm, not the moon. The water goes where it is told.

A half share goes with the boat. It does not go with the man.

A contract written on this paper is a contract that has changed its mind by morning, which the Council calls a virtue.

I have left it where he set it. He wrote it of the Sallow and it is true there, the dippers say. It is a hard thing to argue with a man who has watched a document disappear in a night and called it good weather.

At the head and in the gutter

The white at the head is salt bloom, off the leaf that lay three tides in a coil on the net-rack; salt goes into the fibre and will not come out, which is why we use it to mark a warp and not a page. The tar in the fold is mine, off the stick I write with; I twist and mend and do not wash before I sit down to this. The brine-gull feather came off a bird that stood on the fourth bollard with a coal not cold in its beak and dropped it when the rope came round. The green bead is Glaze-Cliff glass, rolled out of a broken flask and put in the fold by a girl who would not say where she got it; the sweetness of the cliffs is in it, and the sweetness is not a kindness. The grey along the gutter came off a cuff that had no business lying on a quay where rope is laid, and I have not swept it. There is no Last Day on the Quay and nothing here is burnt in the morning.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The rope keeps the half. The stone keeps the stand.

The slate keeps neither and is read aloud all the same.

I asked the child who was boat, a tally-man who left his book open on a bollard, and a woman who has twisted warp here for thirty years, and had three answers and no hand, as I have had before. I have left the lines where they were written.

The leaf goes back into the coil in the nail-keg under the net-rack. Tomorrow the ebb will be called at the usual hour, three shares will be held in a house on the Quay, and a man come aboard will have his half. If the children are not chased off they will hop it and stand over the empty bollard and go on keeping the boat until somebody says the boats are in. If the clerk comes again with his pen I shall put the verbs back. He may keep the nought. The rope comes back to the coil at the end of every count, and no tally in this house has ever had a coil for it.
