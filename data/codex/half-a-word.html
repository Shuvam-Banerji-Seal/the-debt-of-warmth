I mend nets on the Quay and hold no share in anything that floats. I set this down because a man come up from the counting house stood at the net-bench one Ember morning and asked what the children chanted when they turned the rope. I told him they counted a boat. He asked whose boat. I told him it was every boat and no boat, and that the count had a half in it which was not the half of anything he could enter. He went away and sent a clerk, which is what a man does when he has been given an answer he cannot use.

They play it on the open boards between the net-racks and the tar-shed, where the planks keep a little warmth after the sun and the salt has eaten the splinters off. In Ember the boards are too hot by noon, so they play in the early hours or after the boats have gone out on the draw. They do not play in rain, and they do not play when a boat is casting off on the ebb, because a rope stretched across the Quay is a rope that has to be stepped over, and somebody will lose a finger to it. Two turn the rope and one goes in. The one who goes in is the boat.

There is no slate on the Quay that a gull will not carry off, and no paper that will last a morning in the salt except the paper the Council writes its contracts on, which is made to go soft at the right moment. I have written with a tarred needle-point on the back of a tide-tally gone soft on the face. Tar is the only ink here that the weather cannot read and cannot take.

The counting-out, before the rope is taken up

The children stand in a row and hold out their right fists, knuckles up. The one who says the words touches each fist in turn with the untarred end of the rope and does not look at the faces. The fist it lights on at the last word takes the coil: she will turn, not skip. She is the one who has to keep the half true, which the children accept as a punishment and the Quay accepts as practice. Say it to the rope. Said looking at a child, it becomes an argument, and there is always a child who will argue.

Mother and mother and share and share,

the boat goes out on the draw;

touch the knuckle, not the face,

and the one it lights on holds the coil.

The rhyme, as it is skipped

One line, one jump. The tenth line is the half. At the half the rope is held still at the skipper’s hip and she stands on one foot without hopping. The half is not jumped. It is stood in. The refrain is said together while the rope is held.

One for the mother that kept the share,

two for the aunt that the share passed through,

three for the mast and the rope it bore,

four for the pan where the crust came to;

Five for the glass that sweats in the tin,

six for the rain the captain keeps in a barrel,

seven for the coal that the gull went off with,

eight for the eel-bone laid on the board;

Nine for the mark and the binding word,

and a half for the man they let aboard.

Hold the half and set no foot down;

the share goes down the mother’s line, the word goes round;

name me a tide and I’ll come back in

on the turn of the water and not the wind.

Said to the rope. Said to a person, it becomes a bargain, and there is always a person who will take it as one.

The held turn, and the going aboard

If a child hops at the half, or sets her foot down while the rope is held, she is aboard wrong. She goes to stand with her back to the game, facing the Sallow, until somebody names a tide that nobody has seen. The children have a hundred names for tides that have not come and not one of them is written anywhere; a name said on this quay is like a name written in salt up at the pans, which the sun takes by noon and the Council has never found a way to tax. If she cannot name one she stands until the rope turns again, which the children call the ebb, and the ebb takes a while.

No one explains why the naming brings her back. Ask a child and it will tell you to ask the water, which is the answer its mother gave it. I have known a child go aboard wrong on purpose, on a morning when there was no fire at home and the boards held what the house did not. I have set that down and have not mended the sense of it.

The verse they do not skip when a man from the Council is on the quay

I have it from the child who holds the coil, with her back to the counting house. She would not say it to the clerk and would not say it within sight of his turned cuff. She said it to the rope.

One for the mother and one for the boat,

one for the man they let aboard;

his word’s half a word and his debt’s a whole,

and his name goes on no mother’s line.

The share goes out and the share comes back,

and what was kept is called a lack;

the Council writes the half as nil

and the water goes where no clerk has been.

Name me a tide and I’ll come back,

not to the chapel but to the coil.

I asked her what the half was. She said a child who had never been given a share would not know where to stand, and went back to the rope. That is the answer the Quay gives to everything it does not want entered, and I have set it down as she gave it, with nothing added.

What the Council clerk did

A clerk of the Council borrowed the leaf on a morning when the tally-man had his figures out to dry, being a man who cannot leave paper alone. He writes with the cuff turned back twice and the ink on the first joint of two fingers, the way a man writes who is paid by the line. He struck out and a half for the man they let aboard and set above it, one half-share acquired by marriage; opinion weighted at one half; no vote entered. Against hold the half and set no foot down he wrote rest nil. At the foot, where the children name a tide, he put a round nought and wrote tide not entered.

I have put the children’s words back underneath and left his standing above, as the rope keeps the tar on one side and the salt on the other. He has made a share of a stance and a figure of a coming-back. A half in the mouth is a place to stand; a half on a slate is a debt. He has set the debt where the standing was, which is the whole art of that hand. The children stop when the turned cuff comes along the boards. They take it up when he is back over the quay, and they never once change the half.

In the margin, in the leaning hand of a boat’s clerk

We count a boat by who bails. The Council counts it by who signed. Both are true, and only one of them floats.

He wrote it in the sloped letters of tallies, never of decrees, and did not put his name to it. I have left it where he set it.

At the head and in the gutter

The salt bloom at the head has eaten two letters of share, which I have not written in again; a word the salt has half taken is the only honest spelling of a half-share. The tar thumb is mine: I turn the leaf with the hand I mend nets with. The green bead came off a coarse pot carried down from the pans, and it is sweet and it is lead, and I have not put it in my mouth, whatever the children say of the sweetness. The fish-hook nick is from a man mending a line beside the bench who did not apologize. The rope-burn went along the outer edge where the leaf lay coiled. The soft corner is the wet: the tide-name in it went with the wet and did not come back, which is the condition I have described in the game itself and have now been given in my own paper. The grey print came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying on a Quay; I have not swept it. There is no Last Day here, and the salt does the sweeping for us.

At the foot, in grey chalk, in a child’s hand

I held the half. They went on without me.

No hand will own it. The letters are set down hard at the th and one of them backward, which is how a child writes who has learned off a slate and not off a book. I have asked whose it was and had three answers and no hand, as I did with the grey print. I have left it where it was written.

The leaf goes back into the coil under the bench. Tomorrow the boats go out on the draw and the children will stretch the rope across the boards and count a boat that is every boat and no boat, and the tenth line will be held where it is held now, and the ones who go aboard wrong will be brought back with a name, which is more than can be said for the district that has a figure for everything else. I have kept their words and I have not joined them to anything.
