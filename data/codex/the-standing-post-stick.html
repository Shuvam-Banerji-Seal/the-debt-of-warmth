I keep the dry stick at the Standing Post. A man comes up out of the bog with a bundle and asks what it will fetch, and I tell him what the arm that carried it says, because the arm knows the weight and not the cost. I cut a notch and let him touch it; a notch is still there when the stick is wet, which is more than can be said for the paper they use at Greybank. A man from the factor’s house asked for a figure that would stand still. I told him a figure that stands still is a man who has forgotten the water. He told me to cut it in a better stick. I have cut it in the same stick.

Twelve copper bits to a silver mark; twenty marks to a gold crown, which comes north with the Sere traders and will not change at this Post, so a man may be paid in one and go wet all the same. The Court books in embers and calls five marks an ember. No man at this Post has ever held one, and I have left no ember column on the reed. A column with nothing in it is a thing a man can see.

In the narrow upright hand of the factor’s clerk, written above mine: Coin and reckoning entered. Ember: five marks. Underneath, in mine: He has written it as though an ember were a thing a man could carry in his boot across a bog. I have put called back. It is the Court that calls it five marks. The coin does not agree.

Say the figures slowly, and say them to the board rather than to the man asking. Said looking at a man, a price becomes an argument, and an argument on a Post is a debt with nobody’s name on it, which is the one debt we know how to leave in the mud.

The rates at the Post

Cut on the stick, the first column what is taken here at the dry boards, the second what is said of the same thing upriver. Both were cut between the last market of Bone and the first eels of Ilmen.

Article — Taken at the Post — Said upriver

Peat, a dry barrow — 1 bit and a measure of eels in Grain; 2 bits in Stone — 2 bits in Grain; 5 bits in Stone at Sill

Reeds, an armful cut in the dark — 1 bit the armful, counted in the arm that carried it — half again at Reedholt, where they will not say which arm

Eels, smoked — 2 bits — 3 bits at the Fishstairs before the bridge shade goes off

Eels, salted — 1 bit — 2 bits; a third when the boat has been out longer than anyone says

Eel skin, dried — half a mark — 1 mark; it has been down the water to be sold back up it

Ash, by the thimble, in a jar with a Fen name — 4 bits — the same grey in a jar with a Court mark is sixty marks

A thin return — 10 marks, or the same in peat and eels by the load; the source is not asked — 30 marks, paid by the district in labour

Bought breath, the season — 6 marks and a knot — not spoken of in the kiln towns

Bought breath, the year — 18 marks and two knots — not entered, though the boys are entered elsewhere

Warmth in an unmarked jar — not rated here; asked for among the reeds after dark, paid in marks, never in kind — not on any board I have been shown

Margin: A man from Sill paid the Grain figure here in Stone and asked why the barrow had doubled going nowhere. I told him the barrow had not changed and the weather had. He called it a robbery done by a bog, which is true and not an entry I can make.

Margin: A man asked why the four bits and the sixty marks stood on one reed. I told him the four bits had a Fen name and the sixty a Court mark, and went on cutting. He wanted the likeness written down. A likeness written down is a debt with two names, and a debt with two names gets paid twice. There is a knot in the margin where the word would go.

An eel-bone laid across the bought-breath rows: The hook is in the mouth, so a man cannot say the word he would need to complain of it. I laid the bone across instead of writing it, and it has lain there since Ilmen.

What a fine becomes

The reeves settle fines and feuds standing up on the Post. Nothing here is a price. A fine is a thing that stands until the mud calls it paid, and the standing is the whole of the law.

The thing heard at the Post — Paid in — If not paid

A feud between two houses — a knot of grey wool tied before both — the knot stays tied and the standing goes on

A fine left out over the tend — a term of bought breath — the term becomes a measure

A wrong done in a hut and heard here — entered only where a household’s account has suffered — otherwise left where it was said

A man who stands longest in the bog — nothing; the standing is the fee — nothing

Margin: A child here can tell a collector by his boots before she can tell you her own name. I have never seen one pay a due at these boards, or step on the loose one. The stick keeps what men pay; it has no place for what they take.

The dues of the ways

There are no roads in the Fenmarch. What a man pays here is for a board somebody laid, a cut somebody cleared, or a strip of ground the water has not yet called in. A road in the south is built and then charged for; a way here is allowed by the water and taken back.

The Standing Post boards

Due

A man put up on the boards — nothing; nobody tells him which boards are safe

A stranger who steps where nobody pointed — the water to the calf; no figure

A peat-block set on a board overnight — 1 bit; the board bears it or the block goes in

A barrow of grey going out by the boards — not weighed, not entered; the water takes what it wants

Margin: The boards are shown by stepping first, and whoever steps first does not say which board he chose. A man from Greybank asked me to mark the safe ones on the reed; I told him the boards move after rain and a mark does not. He wrote vacancy in the empty place. A vacancy is a thing the Court can enter. The boards are not.

The Reed Cut

Where the young cut their first bundle in the dark. A bundle is judged by the arm that carries it, and the arm tells what went into it and not what it cost.

Due

Going in at the dark — nothing

Coming back with a bundle — counted in the arm; no figure

The dry boards shown to a man from the south — 1 bit for the showing; shown by a child who stands on them first and will not say which is which

A second cutting owed on a stick — one notch, untied at the next cut

A man who asks what the hands will be paid — the weather and the river; never a figure

The ways up into Grath

East by the mud road to Mud End, three leagues downriver of Sill; the south bridge into the town; north-east by the Tallow road; and west the Quiet Acre that lies between us and Grath.

Due

South bridge into Sill, loaded cart — 1 bit, paid going up

The same on foot — nothing; a foot sinks and pays in water

Barrow of peat going up to Sill — 2 bits at the bridge, and the same peat is 5 bits in Stone; the difference is the road

Tallow road, going up with wool — no due; a sheep’s fibre from such a man is in the fold of this reed

The Quiet Acre — no toll; the wells have water and the doorways have no hands to open them

A man who stops at a well there — nobody tells him which house will answer

Margin: The Empty Cantons are what the Court calls the ground between us and Grath; we call it the Quiet Acre and leave it alone. A doorway is built for coming back through. I have not written what hung in the bare clay above one of them, because a thing a man can name is a thing the Court can figure.

The Line

South to the Peat Line, where Sere’s northern advance stopped in Y.H. 634. We have paid tribute in eels ever since, by the year and not by the cart.

Due

Tribute in eels at the Line — the same since Y.H. 634; nobody has told me it was raised

A cart going south with peat — 2 bits, or a pot out of the south, whichever the reeve’s stick says

A gold crown offered at the Line — taken and not changed, which is a toll the coin pays by being useless

A man who goes south and comes back — nothing coming back; the Line has taken its eels and does not ask twice

Ditches and water

Due

A ditch between two dry places — no toll; the ditch takes what the man leaves in it

A peat raft out into the reedbed for the dead — no fee, and no fetching back

Dry ground built on — borrowed; called in when the water pleases; nothing entered

The water under the boards — no figure; the water keeps no stick and does not say what it took

What the borrowed hands did

The factor’s clerk writes upright and narrow and takes the man out of every sentence. Where I wrote I cut, he set was entered above; where I left the place for the water he put a nought and then vacancy. He numbered the ways as three, leaving the ditch and the water out, which is the readiest way I know of counting a road without counting who walks it. I have put my words back underneath and left his above, as a board keeps both the man’s weight and the water’s mark.

The other hand is a reeve’s, and it is mine to know and not mine to name. He cut a notch through the bought-breath figure and cut it over again, so that the first shows under the second, and would not say which way he meant it. A notch cut over a notch is a correction a man can read with his thumb, which is how most things are read here.

The song said to the boards

Said low at the Post while a stick is being cut, never to a man from Greybank, and never while a reeve is standing within hearing of the second verse. The last line is said to the water.

A bit for the board and a bit for the showing,

a knot for the term and a stick for the load;

four for the ways and a place for the wet,

and the fifth is the water and takes what it wants.

The peat goes down and the eel goes up,

the fine stands longest and is called right;

a man comes over with dry boots and goes back wet,

and the Post keeps the notch and the mud keeps the rest.

In the factor’s narrow hand, against the fourth line of the first verse: Four ways entered. In mine: There are four ways and a place; a nought in that place is a figure paid, and the water has never paid. The man who sang it would not sing it facing the boards.

At the head and in the gutter

The peat at the head is mine; I hold the reed down in the wind and do not know I have marked it. Peat goes into the grain and will not come out, which is why we sign with it. The reed-fibre in the second fold came off a wet bundle set down while a man counted his marks and went away without his change. The tide-line has taken one word off the peat row; I have not written it in again, because a row with a word gone is a row said slowly, and most of ours are. The eel-bone across the bought-breath figures was laid there by a woman who would not say why. The wet boot over the dues came off a man who stepped on the loose board while asking what the safe ones cost; he paid and would not say whether he stepped before he paid or after. The grey along the margin is not mine and not the Court’s. I have not swept it. There is no Last Day on a Post.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The stick keeps the weight. The knot keeps the term.

The mud keeps the man who stood longest, and none of them says what it cost.

I asked the eel-trader, a reeve of the Post, and a man come down from Greybank, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The reed goes back into its sinew under the loose board. A man will come up and ask what his armful fetches, and I shall tell him what the arm says, and the water will take what it wants, without a figure and without a hand to write one.
