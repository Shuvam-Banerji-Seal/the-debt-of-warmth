I keep the fourth bowl at the Ember house. That is my whole office: to set a bowl down full at the third hour, and carry it away full when the feasting is done, and answer no questions about it. A boy come down from Greybank sits at the lower table and watches my hands to learn when to applaud. Last Grain he clapped at the new slate and the man beside him told him to clap at the old one too, which he did, and he has not been right since. I have set these sayings down for him in the thinner gold. The thick gold is kept for contracts, and a contract is a thing said to a man who is not in the room. A saying is said to the bowl. If the bowl answers, that is between them.

The manner of them first, because a saying said wrongly is a different saying. Say them with the vowels open and the ends thrown into the fire, so that the listener supplies the last of the thought. Said looking at a man, a saying becomes a lawsuit; said to the bowl, or to the fire under the bowl, it stays a saying. There are words here that are only spoken at the third hour, when the wine is up and an act of state is being announced. Write them at another hour and they look like anything. A northern clerk copied one into a slate once and was polite about it for a week, which is the longest politeness I have ever seen a northern man sustain.

Of the bowl and the pouring

  1. “The pot was made to pour. The cup that keeps is the cup that sours.”

Gloss — Valen Openhand says it at the third hour, and the priests who say it after him are among the fullest vessels in the empire. A visitor notices this; a child of the house is taught not to. A vessel was made to be emptied gloriously and a life hoarded is a life wasted. Say it over the wine, never over the accounts, where it would be a very different sentence and might cost somebody a province.

  1. “A debt carried gracefully is an ornament of a house.”

Gloss — What is carried gracefully is still carried. At sixteen a girl emptied the bowl at her feast and named a debt she intended to carry, and the applause was for the debt and not for her. Three houses asked for her in the same hour. They were not asking for the debt. I have put that in because the boy will see the applause and want to know who is being honoured, and the answer is not the person.

  1. “Burn the slate by lot. Write it again before the wine is cold.”

Gloss — At the end of a feast one household’s debt-slates go into the fire and new ones are made. A woman whose slate burned wept, and the crowd was kind to her, and then applauded the new slate. I have set both sentences here. The boy will want one of them to be the true one; both of them are true, and the second one is the one that is copied.

  1. “A year’s grain under the dead is a warm send-off. Buy the ash back at the price they ask.”

Gloss — The warmest funerals in the world are ours. The Court taxes the ash and the house buys it back as quiet ash, and a great funeral can empty a province, which is then bought cheap. We call the house generous. I have not written what I think of this, because an under-steward who writes what he thinks is an under-steward looking for another house.

Of courtesy and the face

  1. “There is no rudeness in Sere. There is only a person who has not yet been told.”

Gloss — Our courtesy is long and open, and a man who does not know it is never refused; he is confirmed in being a stranger. The boy has one name and is ashamed of it. A child here has seven by adolescence and keeps whichever the household prefers. He asked me why one name is not enough. I told him a bowl holds one pouring and a house holds seven, and let him make what he could of it, which is how courtesy is taught.

  1. “Say yes. If they ask which, say measure for measure.”

Gloss — At the third hour a public bowl of wine is emptied and an act of the Sun-Emperor is announced. A potter from the north stood beside me and asked whether a debt had been forgiven or a province ruined. The man beside him said yes. When the potter asked which, he said measure for measure and went back to his wine. I have learned that the phrase means just what the speaker decided before he opened his mouth. It is a fine phrase for an empire. It is not a fine phrase for a contract, which is why it is never written in gold.

  1. “A woman may own the bowls and the slaves and a husband’s debt. She may not enter the room where her numbers are read.”

Gloss — Inheritance goes to the eldest surviving child regardless of sex, and a woman of a great house may hold a great deal. The room where her measure is counted has a door in it, and her numbers are read to her by a man. This is a courtesy with a door in it. The boy asked why a woman who owns a house may not count it. I told him a door is not an argument and will not fight him, and he let it go, which is a courtesy he has learned from us already.

  1. “Concubinage is a public measure. Do not call it a kindness.”

Gloss — Returned slaves are kept openly and are counted as part of a household’s standing, the way a good fire or a row of bowls is counted. A kindness that can be seen and admired is not a kindness but a receipt. This is said in the court and not in the counting room. The slaves know it before the boy does; he will learn it from them, which is the ordinary order of learning in this country.

Of dust and the open court

  1. “The courts have no walls. The dust settles in the hair and nobody brushes it off.”

Gloss — Men sit in the heat of the court fires with their coats on the ground beside them and let the dust lie where it falls. A northern man brushes himself and is noticed. We say a man who brushes the dust off has decided he is only passing through, and a man only passing through may be charged for the hour.

  1. “The south is warm the way a kiln is warm, with the door open. A man goes thirstier here than in a northern winter.”

Gloss — The potter who asked about the bowl said it first and got it right. I have kept his words because our own say the warmth and leave out the thirst, which is the whole art of a southern saying. He had been told the south was warm and found it no comfort.

  1. “The burning winds take the skin off a man’s lips and leave the grain standing.”

Gloss — The winds come yellow off the east and a traveller’s lips go first. The grain stands through them, which is why the empire has grain and the traveller has a skin he does not recognise. We say the wind takes what is loose. It is a saying about lips and about households, and neither of them is safe in it.

Of the fourth bowl

  1. “Three go round and are poured. One stands and is carried away full.”

Gloss — At every feast a fourth bowl is set down full and not emptied. It stands through the feasting and is carried away still full. Nobody pours it and nobody says whose debt it holds. I asked three times and got three silences, the last of which was the kindest. The boy asked once and I told him to ask the bowl. He did. The bowl did not answer, which is the answer they gave me in finer words.

  1. “There is a place in every account where a bowl should be emptied and is not. Go around it.”

Gloss — The clerks go around that place the way our yard goes around a thing set down where a child might knock it. I have set the bowl in the margin where a figure should stand, because the bowl will not lie in a column. The tally-clerk has tried twice to give it a figure. Both times the figure has stood and the bowl has stood beside it, and the two have not touched.

The second — the fire — the act announced from the third hour

The third — the slate — the burning by lot and the new writing

The fourth — nobody — nothing

What they sing at the wine-presses

Sung at the tread, not at the feast. The third verse is said to the bowl and not to the company. He has learned to keep his hands off the fourth bowl, which is more than I managed at his age.

At the press we tread and the wine comes up,

a neck for the house and a neck for the fire;

the third goes down on a slate burned by lot,

and the fourth goes back to the shelf.

We clap for the debt and we clap for the slate,

we buy back the ash at the price of the grain;

the year that we burned under one we loved

was the year we had meant to spend anyway.

Seven names on a girl and the house keeps one;

the others go into the fire with the slate.

Three go round and they all go dry,

and the fourth stands full and is carried away.

What the tally-clerk did

A clerk of the house borrowed the leaves after the third hour, being a man who cannot leave paper alone. He writes in the leaning counting-hand, with the gold gone thin and the verbs taken out as though a verb were a thing a man could spill out of a cuff.

  • At the cup that keeps is the cup that sours, he wrote vessels are made for use, and underlined it twice.
  • At a debt carried gracefully, he set obligation acknowledged, current, which leaves the ornament out and the obligation in.
  • At measure for measure he wrote equivalence recorded, and underlined the equivalence.
  • At buy the ash back at the price they ask, he set quiet ash, market rate, and put a figure in the margin where I had put the price.
  • Against the fourth bowl he wrote a reserve retained, no charge against it, which is a true thing in the language of a counting room and has the bowl standing in it still.

I have left all of it standing above my words. He takes the verbs out because a thing always true has no need of them. I put them back because the boy wants to know whose hand poured, and there is always a hand.

What the northern hand did

A factor’s clerk at Greybank corrected the copy on the back of the voided warrant in the narrow upright hand. He has never been south and has strong views about dust.

  • At the courts have no walls, he wrote the premises are unenclosed.
  • At the dust settles in the hair, he wrote atmospheric deposit, not entered, which is a description of a room after everybody has left it.
  • In the margin beside the fourth bowl he put a round nought and wrote vacancy underneath.

I have left his hand standing over mine, the way a new slate is laid over the burned one. A nought is a number. The bowl was not a number, and nobody has poured it to find out.

At the head and in the gutter

The gold at the head has worked into the grain and will not come out, which is why we write contracts in it and sayings in the thinner mixture: a contract is meant to last and a saying is meant to be carried. The dust in the fold came off the court bench and I have not shaken it, because the boy will one day ask what the yellow is and I should rather he asked than knew. The wine-ring is small and belongs to the third hour. The olive thumb is mine; I turn the leaves with the hand I carry the bowl with. The husk of grain stuck in the gutter came from a loaf sent north and returned uneaten, which is a journey I could enter if I chose and have not. The green shard in the fold came off a bowl the house bought from a northern potter and cracked in the first frost; the tally-clerk has asked for it twice and has not had it. The scorched corner is where the leaf lay too close to a court fire. The crown pressed flat between the fifth and sixth leaves is one the boy was paid in and cannot spend here, which makes it the only coin in the house nobody has tried to enter. In Sere the ash is bought back and burnt in a bowl; that is another way of saying nothing is left where it lay.

At the back, in a hand nobody will own

Three are poured and the fourth stands.

The house does not know whose debt it holds

and would pour it if it knew.

It is the only thing in Sere nobody has spent.

I asked the bowl-keeper, the tally-clerk and a factor come down from Greybank, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The leaves go back into the oiled wrap under the fourth bowl; tomorrow the third hour will come, and I shall set the bowl down full and carry it away full, and the boy will watch my hands to learn when to clap.
