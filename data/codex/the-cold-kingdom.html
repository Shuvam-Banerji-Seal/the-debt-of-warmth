I throw pots. That is the whole of what I am, and I went up the Rim Road because a man who throws pots is always short of iron. I took nine coarse bowls and a letter from the factor at Greybank. Four came back whole. Two came back in pieces. Three I sold at the Keep for less than the cord they were tied with, and the cart came home lighter than it went and heavier by a thing I have never entered.

The mountain is a kiln with the door shut. I have said that to nobody, because a man who says it in Keld is taken for a spy and a man who says it in Nine Pots is taken for a fool. Put a fire under clay and it holds. Put a fire under stone and it keeps. The Keldish have a word for the keeping and no word for the heat, which tells you which of the two they have learned to trust.

The road, and what it took

Nine Pots stands eight leagues west of Sill, and the Rim Road passes north of it at the Ferr ford, so a man can sleep at Stonemouth with his pots unbroken if he wraps each in straw and does not let the ass choose the ford. I let the ass choose. One bowl went in the Ferr and came up in three pieces the colour of the water, blue-grey off the old working, and I entered it at Stonemouth and not again, because a bowl that goes into the Ferr does not come home, and a bowl that comes home in pieces has cost the journey its only honest figure.

We made the Keep in nine days up and seven down, and the seven were colder, which I did not expect and have not found a clerk who will enter. I carried a warm-stone in my coat on the advice of a lime-man who had been up twice and come back with all his fingers. It cost six marks, which is a great deal of pots. He told me the price and not the debt, which is the manner of that country. It kept my hand warm on the climb and was cold the morning I got home.

What the road took, and what it gave

What I entered at Nine Pots, what stood at the Keep, and what I wrote on the back of the docket at Greybank, where the factor’s man puts a figure against a thing whether or not the thing has a figure in it.

Article — Entered at Nine Pots — Given at Keld Keep — What I set on the back

Coarse bowl, straw-wrapped — nine, at four bits — three sold, four whole, two broken — A bowl that breaks has no price and still cost the road.

Iron clamp, small — — — 1 bit and a favour — The favour is not current. I have entered it anyway.

Iron clamp, for the wheel-shed — — — 2 marks at the Keep, 2 marks again at Greybank — The second was for the road. The road has no slate of its own.

Warm-stone, a palm’s length — — — 6 marks — Sold, not lent. Cold by the seventh day home.

Lead, a blue-grey chip — — — given, not sold — Off the Ferr, not out of the Ninth Gallery. I have kept the two apart.

A number, from a Vorr — — — 1 bit — The numbers are correct. I have not entered what it means.

Twelve bits to a silver mark and twenty to a crown, if you can find a crown in Keld, which I could not. The Court books in embers and tells a man an ember is worth five marks. Nobody has ever exchanged one, but the figure travels faster than the coin, which is the whole art of that house.

The weigh, or the number before the name

At the Warden’s hall I gave my name, and the clerk looked at me until I understood I had begun wrongly. He said nothing. A man beside me said, “State what you hold, then say who you are,” in the manner of a man repeating something he has said to fools before. I went back to the foot of the post and came again with two-and-forty, which is what I judge I carry, and the clerk wrote the figure first and asked the name second, as though a name were a thing a man might keep back and sell later.

A name is a thing a man can change at the next well, the beside-man told me, and it is the only explanation I was given on the whole road that I could carry home. The number goes down the shaft with a man. The name stays in the room where he said it.

The mine-reeve checked me in red. He set his point where I had given two-and-forty, drew a stroke through it and wrote the same two-and-forty above, which is how a clerk tells you he has found no lie without saying anything a man can thank him for. The red is at the second corner of this leaf now, and I have not wiped it off and have not entered it.

What is sold above ground

Iron is common in Keld and dear in Grath, and the difference is the price of the road, which the road itself will never enter. A good axe is half a mark up there and two at Stonemouth, and a man who carries one down is paid for the carrying and not for the iron.

Warm-stone is sold and is not lent, said twice to me by two men who had no reason to agree. A thing that can be lent has a place in somebody’s account; a thing sold is paid for, which is cleaner. The shavings go by the half-mark, and a potter from Nine Pots will buy a shaving before a stone, because a shaving will keep a cup warm through one night.

There is a Vorr at the weigh who sells numbers. I bought one for a bit and asked what it was for. He said the numbers were correct, and would not say it twice.

The mouth, and the boy going down

The Descent is at thirteen. I stood at the mouth with the old women who count and watched a boy go down with a number cut into his arm and a warm-stone put into his hand after the knife. His mother said nothing. One of the old women said the last part of a saying with him, and the boy said the first part before he went down and the women said the second, and nobody explained it to me, and I did not ask.

Two go down and the count comes two. That is what they say at the mouth, and it is not a promise that two come up. It is a promise that the count will be made. I have written it as it was said, with the thing that is not a promise standing in the middle of the sentence, which is where the Keldish put their mercies.

A man at the mouth had two sets of numbers in one arm, the later cut deeper. The mine-reeve entered the later and left the earlier standing in the skin, and told me both stood. A man who comes back thin has the old numbers cut out and new ones put in, which is a kindness done with a knife and entered as a correction.

I asked if I might go down. They said a lowlander goes down as tribute or as a spy, and often as both, and I was neither, so I stood and counted the boys. What I know of the First Seam is told to me, not seen.

The dead are walled into the mine facing the deep seam, and the surveyors add numbers to a slate nobody reads aloud. They do not fetch them back and do not say they have. The mountain keeps its own, which costs less than a kindness. There is a cup at the end of the Duelling Floor that nobody takes, set for a man whose number was changed twice. I did not see the cup.

The song said at the mouth

Taken down from the old women who count, said once to the boy going down and once to the ground after he has gone. The second part is said with a line left out.

One for the arm and one for the stone,

one for the pair that goes down together;

the knife goes in and the warm goes home,

and the dark keeps what the mother never entered.

Hold the rail, it is iron and it takes;

hold the stone, it is sold, not lent;

count them going and count them back.

Two go down and the count comes two;

the figure stands though the arm may not;

the mountain keeps what the mountain took,

and the slate writes once what the mother forgot.

The fourth line of the second part is left out. I have set the space where it should stand and have not filled it, though I know what word would go there. A song that tells a boy what the count is for is a song he cannot carry down with him.

The Sweating, and what they reckon a life at

In the deep seams the rock sweats and the Warm comes up through the stone in a flood, and the only warning is the smell of hot lead. I did not smell it. I have set that down first so that no reader thinks I have been where I have not. A shift-man at the mouth told me he had smelt it once and come up, and that men who smelt it twice did not come up, and then asked if I had a stone.

Below the floor they work the stone with under-vessed slaves, condemned men and bought breath, and reckon a life at eleven months, a year with the month of the buying taken off. A man who sells a life by the month will tell you the months and not the month that is missing, which is the same trick as the price of a warm-stone.

The Keldish claim the true Hearth lies under their mountain and that the Court’s fire is a candle lit from it. A surveyor come up from the shaft, with drink in him and no officer in the room, told me the rim-seam shows repairs laid one over another and that the oldest are not stone and not clay, and then turned his slate face down. The Keldish call those repairs Ash. He said the Court’s copy has another word and that I would not be shown it, which I have set down as heard and not as known.

What the borrowed hands did

A Court survey-clerk at Greybank borrowed these leaves on a market morning. He writes upright and narrow and takes the verbs out as though a verb were a thing a man could carry off in a cuff.

  • Against I gave my name and was sent back to the foot of the post he wrote the person was identified after the measure was declared, underlined twice.
  • Against two go down and the count comes two he wrote two persons entered the shaft and two were recorded, which says nothing about the coming up.
  • Against they shut the Cold Pass in seven hundred forty-one he wrote the frontier was closed by treaty in seven hundred forty-seven, which is the agreement and not the shutting, and has a date a clerk can copy.
  • Against the mountain keeps its own he wrote interment underground; no return entered, which leaves the mountain out and keeps the entry in.

The mine-reeve’s red stroke stands at the second corner. It is a check and not a correction. He found the figure I gave stood, and I have kept his mark where it fell, which is more than I can say of the factor’s marks.

I have left his readings above mine everywhere. A clerk who takes the doer out leaves the mountain standing by itself, with nobody kept and no count in it to argue with. I put the doer back because a potter who carried nine bowls up a road wants to know whose hands they were in when they broke.

At the head and in the gutter

The coal at the head is mine, off the sleeve I wipe the pots with; no wet cloth takes it out. The iron-red at the second corner is the mine-reeve’s point. The chip in the gutter is Ferr lead, off the bowl that went in the ford; the bead in the fold came from the Ninth Gallery. I have never joined the two, because they are two different debts, and a man who joins them has drawn a conclusion, which I have no licence to draw. The shaving held its heat one night on the stone floor and is cold now, which is the order of things up there. The frost-feather lay on the well lip and was gone before I folded the leaf; the yard at home calls that weather and the Keldish call it a map of the leak. The tallow print is my own lamp set too near the leaf. The square of bare paper is the word I scraped away and put nothing in its place. The grey along the outer fold is not mine and not the clerk’s. I have not swept it. There is no Last Day above this Keep.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The cart went up with nine and came back with four;

the stone went in warm and came home cold;

the figure stood and the name went nowhere,

and the count came two where the cup stood still.

Ask the mouth what it counted.

I asked the beside-man at the Warden’s hall, a woman who counts at the mouth, and a Vorr who sells numbers, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. Tomorrow I take the four bowls that came back whole down to Stonemouth and sell them for what they fetch, and when the weigh-clerk asks me what I carry I shall say four and not my name, which is a habit I have brought home from a country that does not care what a man calls himself.
