To Lett, who counts the lamps in the low houses below the sill. From Orra at the tryworks, two leagues up the north-east road. Written in the last tend of Bone.

I write on the back of a weight-chit the factor’s man spoiled with his figure, because there is no paper at the works that is mine and a sheet that has carried a number is a sheet a man will not read twice. The fat has got into the grain and the charcoal would not take on it, so I have gone over every word in iron-gall and the ink beads where the thumb was. Do not hold the leaf to the fire to read it. It goes soft and smells, and a letter that smells of rendering is a letter somebody will ask about.

The north wind came on Bone 26 and carried the smell down into Sill the way it does, and a woman at the well below the bridge said the whole terrace had been rendering sheep all morning and would not believe it was us, two leagues off. A smell travels without a slate and arrives before the man that sent it. By midday it was in the pottage. Nobody thanked us and nobody stopped eating.

We rendered thin this year. The Long Cold took the grass before the flocks were in, and the sheep came up to the works with their bones showing through the wool like a slate with the figures rubbed off, and what fat there was lay pale in the pan and set before it could be poured. A pound of it will not stand in a cup the way it stood last year. I have set the tend’s count below and put in the last column what the factor’s man took out.

Fat rendered in the last tend, and where the light went

Written in charcoal first, then gone over. The figure column is his. The last column is mine and has no standing at any Weighing.

When — Fat rendered — Price asked — Who bought — What the light stood beside

Firstday — Seven pound, pale — Nine bits the quart — A woman of the low houses — A child’s bed, lit until the mother came in

Thirdday — Four pound, set hard in the pan — Nine bits, and a crust thrown in — The man at the Cross — Nothing; he carried the quart past the bridge

Fifthday — Six pound, sweet at the smell — Not asked — Nobody — The pan itself, which smoked all afternoon

Seventhday — Three pound, dregs — Six bits, taken in ale — A woman from Cold Acre — A window set for a man coming on the Clay Road

Ninthday — Eight pound, the best of the tend — Nine bits — The Vail house, by a girl with slip in her wrists — The shelf where the bread is kept

Nine bits the quart is what the notice in the factor’s window says, and it is what I asked, and it is not what a woman below the bridge pays when she has had three days without work. She pays in small ale and a promise, and the promise gathers grey in the corner the way a debt gathers, and I have never once entered it. A promise is a debt without a figure, which is the only kind this side of the works can afford.

A lamp is a small fire taught to sit still, the way a hearth-fire is taught, and tallow is what it eats. There is no note in a lamp. Tap a pot with the knuckle and it answers full; tap a cracked one and it answers flat, a little dead sound that stops at the surface; tap a clay lamp and you get only the dull sound of the thing you tapped, which tells you nothing except that it is clay. I have learned to read a lamp by the smell. A lamp that smells of the wick is a lamp eating its own hemp. A lamp that smells of nothing has gone out and will be cold by morning. There is a woman three doors below the bridge who sets her lamp in the window when her man is due off the Clay Road, and the lamp is not for her and she will not say so, and the fat in it is bought with the same bits that buy the bread. I have put her in the last column, where the clerk will not let me.

Every light somebody keeps burning is paid for by somebody. This is not a saying of the Keeping and it is in no ordinance; it is what a woman learns who has stood over a try-pot and watched a length of yellow fat come up out of a sheep that ate a field that somebody held the deed to. The Court will tell you one in twelve, and the notice says it, and Berr Clay reads it over the basket twice, once for the moot and once for them that came late, wiping his thumb twice before he touches nothing. The bell went four, four and a gap and nobody crossed himself. He never asks what a lamp cost. A figure read aloud is not the figure paid, and a light in a window is not a light anybody has entered.

So I ask you two things, and I have put them where you can answer without a slate in the room.

The first: what is in the cup of the lamp by the bed in the Vail house? I am not asking who pays for it. I know that answer and so do you, and it is the house and never a person, as it is everywhere below the sill. I am asking what is in the cup, because a woman come up from the pans says a child upriver turns his head toward a light a breath before it is lit, and I want to know if that is a thing they say at the Fishstairs or a thing you have seen. Do not give me a name. A name in a letter is a thing a clerk can enter against a person before she has taken her coat off.

The second: is the wick hemp from the fen, or the thread they spin at the Quay? I have a mind to send a wick up with the next fat and I will not send the wrong one, for a wick that draws too fast eats a household’s winter in a night and there is nobody in the low houses who can afford it. Answer on the back and not in the gutter, where the Ash has got into everything.

The lamp-song

Sung low over the try-pot while the skimmings are lifted, never to a person and never while the factor’s man is in the room. The fourth line is said to the wick. Where the light goes out is left as it is sung.

Fat comes up pale and the pan goes black,

the sheep ate grass on a borrowed ground;

nine bits the quart and a promise besides,

and the light in the cup keeps what it found.

Wick from the fen or thread from the Quay,

draw it too fast and the winter goes;

four in the window and a fifth never lit,

and the hand that set it is the hand that knows.

The north wind carries the smell to the town,

the smell goes in and the figure stays out;

a lamp is a wound that is fed with a spoon —

ask who paid, and the room goes dark about.

What the clerk did

A clerk of the factor’s house borrowed the chit on the morning after it came up the quay, being a man who cannot leave paper alone. He writes in the neat upright hand they teach at the College, with the doers taken out. Against a woman of the low houses pays in small ale and a promise he set consideration arranged and the woman went out of the sentence the way a neighbour goes out of a tally. Against the light stood beside a child’s bed he set domestic consumption, and against the girl with slip in her wrists he set household of standing, which is a phrase that means the house has pots and says nothing about the girl. Against the question of who pays for a burning he wrote no deduction arises, which is the Court’s own art in four words. He put a round figure in the last column where I had put a woman and a window. The figure has outlasted the fat it was meant to explain.

I have put the verbs back underneath where I could still read them, and left his figure standing above. He takes the people out because a thing always true has no need of verbs. We put them in because a woman at a tryworks at the going of the light wants to know whose bed the quart went to.

In the margin, in a second hand, small and set close to the writing

The quart went to the low houses and the ale was watered and the promise was kept in the corner and not in the cup. I do not know what is in the lamp by the bed and I have never looked into it, which is the same as knowing in this house. The child turns his head at the pot before the knuckle falls, and before the light is set down, and I have seen it four times and will not make a fifth of it, for four are four and a fifth would want a payer. It is not quickness. It is not a sign. It is a thing a child does in a room whose floor his own floor does not touch.

It is hemp from the fen. I know the smell of it when it draws, and I have kept yours these three years without asking what you wanted it for.

I have struck your nine bits and put eight under it in your own iron-gall. The woman below the bridge paid eight and a crust, and the crust was the browned one and went to the child and not to her, which is the kindness of the low houses and is not entered. Put the figure where the figure belongs and the woman where the woman stood. I will not write the name of the girl with slip in her wrists, and not because I do not know it.

A lamp kept in a cup is a light somebody ate in a field.

The field keeps the figure. The cup keeps the dark.

At the head and in the gutter

The tallow at the head is mine, off the thumb I held the chit down with while the wind came through the door; it has gone into the grain and there is no washing it out, and I have stopped trying. The grey along the gutter came off a cuff that had no business lying where the weights are kept, and I did not make it and have not swept it; there is Ash enough in this house without bringing a stranger’s in. The wick through the tie-hole is hemp from the fen, and I have tied it there so that I cannot send it with the fat and lose it. The salt bloom on the outer fold came off a woman’s cuff that had been at the pans on a flood tide, and it has eaten one letter of the date so that I cannot now read whether it was Bone 25 or Bone 26 the wind came on, which is a small loss and the only one I mind. The scorch is where the leaf lay too near the try-pot on Ninthday and took the corner off the price without taking the sense. The brown at the second fold is from a crust set down by the woman who pays in ale; she laid it there while she told me where the child had been and would not say the name of the house, and the grease has eaten two letters and left the sentence standing. The clerk’s correction stands above my words and my words stand under it, and neither has been struck out.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The smith mends the mouth that takes the house.

The render feeds the light that shows the crack.

Nine goes in the slate and eight comes in the cup,

and the fifth stays dark where the fifth stood.

I asked a woman at the pans, a man come down from Cold Acre with a hare, and a girl who keeps the factor’s tally, and had three answers and no hand, as we have had before. I have left the line where it was written.

The chit goes back in the tin beside the wicks. In the morning the fat goes down to Sill on the cart, and the woman below the bridge will pay in ale and a promise, and the lamp in the window will be set for a man who is not yet on the road. That is the whole of the letter, and it goes on.
