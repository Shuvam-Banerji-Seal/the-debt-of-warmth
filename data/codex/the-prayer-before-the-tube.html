I have drawn measure on this road for nine years and I have never once said the four sayings in the order they are printed. They are printed for a room. They are said in a clean voice by a clerk who has never held the tube, with the slate face up and the man already on the pallet and the door shut behind him. On the road we turn them end for end. It is not doctrine. It is the order in which a collector comes to them, which is a different thing and the only one I can answer for.

There is no name on this leaf. A collector’s name on a prayer is an entry, and an entry can be produced at a Weighing by a man who was not there and had not held the iron. I have set a crossed tube in the corner in charcoal, which is a mark and not a name. If the factor asks me what it is I shall tell him it is a stain.

I wrote this on the back of a district tally voided for a figure entered in the wrong column, between Greybank and Sill in the last tend of Ash, Y.H. 812, the Thin Year. It rode in the wool sleeve of the tube, which is why the iron is in every fold and why the leaf goes grey before the words do. A clerk of the factor set his hand above mine at the head; a hearth-maid come up from Low Kiln with a birth-slate put four lines in the margin, in letters pressed hard enough to tear the paper. I have left both where they were put. The leaf has never been read at a Weighing, which is the whole reason it has lasted.

The manner of it, written small at the head

  • Wrap the tube before you say the prayer, not after. A man can hold iron while he speaks, but he will not speak well with his fingers going the colour of the sky before snow.
  • Stand with the road at your back and the house in front. The house is what you are about to take from. The road has no opinion and will not follow you home.
  • Do not look at the face. Look at the arm, where the ways run near the skin. A face will tell you nothing you can use, and a thing you cannot use is a thing you will remember, which is worse.
  • Say it into the wool, not into the air. Words said into the air go up and get entered somewhere. The wool keeps them one draw and lets them go, which is as much as any prayer of mine is owed.
  • Count the draws on the fingers afterwards, never before. A figure counted before the draw is a figure you have already decided, and a decided figure is the same thing as a false one with the ink dry.
  • If the bell comes off the Cross four and four and a gap, stop at the gap. The gap belongs to somebody else. Begin again when the bell has finished counting what it counts.
  • Never say it twice over one man. A second prayer argues with the first, and the first was honest, being said before I knew how much he had.

The prayer

I say it into the wool. There is nobody present to answer. The answer is made with the hands after every part: wrap the iron, turn the slate, wipe the hand. I have written the answer down as it is done, because a thing done is the only answer this road gives.

Before the iron

Iron, you were cold when I took you out of the wool.

You will be cold when I put you back.

What you take does not stay in you. It goes where it is entered.

I am the hand that holds you to the way. I am not the way,

and I am not the taking, whatever the slate may say later.

Here I draw the tube through the wool again, so the sleeve keeps its shape for the next man. A small care, and costs nothing, which is why I keep it and enter it nowhere.

The answer is made: wrap the iron. Turn the slate. Wipe the hand.

Of the arm

The ways run near the skin here. A man’s face is no use to me.

The arm tells where to set you; the slate tells what stood there.

I do not ask the arm whether it consents. Nobody asks it in this country.

A good arm gives full and gives quickly. A worn arm gives flat,

and the figure is smaller, which is not smaller to the man.

Here I set the cold rim to the way and wait for the small hiss. There is iron, a smell like wet wool near a fire, and the time it takes to write a figure down.

The answer is made: wrap the iron. Turn the slate. Wipe the hand.

Of the figure

Count embers. A year is a thing the household remembers

and the slate has no column for.

One in twelve, as the notice says. Printed. Read twice,

once for the moot and once for those who came late up the road.

I write the printed figure. What the district pays is not my hand,

and the man who set the other figure has gone up the Clay Road with it

and does not carry it in the open.

Here I read the figure twice, low, into the wool. The second is for the person at the door who came too late to be counted and stands in the draught.

The answer is made: wrap the iron. Turn the slate. Wipe the hand.

The four sayings, turned end for end

Your return is recorded. I say this first, so that it cannot comfort at the end.

Nothing is lost that we have kept. Kept is not the same as held. I have held the iron and know the difference.

The account stands. It stands after the man sits down. It does not stand while he is standing.

The measure is banked. Last, because it is the true one and the one I do the most.

The clerk set these in their proper order above mine, with as appointed against them. Both stand. His is the order a clerk prints for a room; mine begins with the record and ends with the taking. I have put my hand beside his, not under it.

The answer is made: wrap the iron. Turn the slate. Wipe the hand.

Of the cradle

I never see the cradle. It is a room I am not paid to enter.

One into one: the name comes back with the shape,

with the price of bread and the way a hand closes on a cup.

Many into many: what comes back wants a table it never sat at

and goes toward a cup before the cup is shown.

Both are poured. Both are entered in the same clean hand.

I have seen such children at the Cross and cannot tell you

which draw of mine went into which of them.

Here I leave a space the length of a name. It is not a name forgotten. No name of mine belongs in it, and the clerk’s round nought is a figure, and a figure is a thing I have already used too much of this morning.

The answer is made: wrap the iron. Turn the slate. Wipe the hand.

Of the hand after

The iron takes from the drawer as well as from the drawn.

By the third draw the fingers go the colour of ash water;

by the fifth they are as they were, or they are not.

A collector who says the cost is nothing

has not looked at his own hand, which is the only looking

he does not do.

I marked forty draws on the bench in charcoal one tend

and washed the bench before the factor came,

which is not the same as not having made the marks.

Here I set the tube back in the sleeve and shut the slate. Collectors say the grey comes off in a day. I have had it last six, and no clerk has asked after the sixth.

What the clerk set at the head

A clerk of the factor borrowed the leaf while the tube was being rewrapped, with a cuff gone soft from washing and ink in two fingers. He did not take the verbs out. He took the man out instead, which is the stranger thing to do to a leaf written on a road.

  • At I am the hand that holds you to the way, he wrote: the draw is performed by an officer of the Keeping. Both stand. Mine has a hand in it, his an office, and the man on the pallet will be told about the office.
  • At I do not ask the arm whether it consents, he wrote: lawful draw; consent recorded. Underneath, in mine: Recorded by whom? The slate was face down and the man was looking at the wall. His above, mine below; that is the order this road is read in.
  • At the figure is smaller he wrote the printed figure above it, larger. It is the figure they like to see. Both stand.
  • Against the grey fingers he wrote no cost to officer, true in the figure and false in the hand I wrote it with. I have not struck it out. A clerk’s false line is what the next reader ought to see.
  • In the space where a name would stand he put a round nought and wrote vacancy. A nought is a number. The place was not.

He asked what a collector learned by the prayer. I told him the order of the sayings and the wrapping of the tube. He entered both and went up the road with the tally he had come for. The leaf came back with his ink wet on it. I have kept the ink; a man who cannot help writing on a thing has let it be seen that he wrote.

What the hearth-maid put in the margin

The letters are thick and lean, made with a charcoal stick and not a pen. She came up from Low Kiln with a birth-slate under her shawl and stood at the Cross until the pigs were weighed before she would touch the leaf, because a woman who touches a paper in the middle of a market will be asked what she had to do with it.

At the arm tells where to set you: “It tells you nothing I have not told it. I held that arm last winter while it was still warm. You came up the road after I had carried the slate down it.”

At one in twelve, as the notice says: “The notice is not in our house. We give the other figure there, and you do not write it, and the house is cold in the morning. I have said this to a woman who was holding a child and had no word for the cold.”

At I never see the cradle: “A child came back two doors down and went toward a cup before the cup was shown. I have not told you which of yours it was. I never shall.”

At sit down — for she wrote this where I had set the fingers go the colour of ash water — she put: “Sit down. I have kept a stone warm since before you came. It is not for you. It is for whoever comes in behind you.”

She would not say who that was, and I have not asked twice. There is always somebody at a market with a slate.

At the head and in the folds

The filings came off the tube when I set it down hard on the assessor’s step at Sill and went in without apologising; they have worked into the grain and have never once drawn the warm out of the paper, which is the one kindness iron has shown this leaf. The grey wool is the same colour as the fingers it wraps, which I have noticed and not written into the prayer. The green bead is lead glaze from a girl at the Cross with the sweetness still at her mouth; she touched the corner and went back to her pot board without saying what she had seen, and the corner tastes sweet in wet weather, and I have not washed it. The frost along one edge is from the Rim Road in Stone, when the leaf lay against my coat and the writing went thin; I have not gone over those words, because a word written thin is a word said quietly, and most of ours are. The tallow thumb is mine. The ale ring stood from the first part to the seventh and went dark in the time it takes to draw and be corrected. The silt came up from the step on a wet morning and lies in the gutter with the clerk’s nought beside it, neither entered. The grey along the foot came off a cuff that had been in a corner where no cuff has business lying. There is no Last Day on this road, and nothing here is swept up and burnt in the morning and called dealt with.

At the foot

In a hand that is not mine, not the clerk’s and not the hearth-maid’s:

The room hears them in the right order and is comforted.

The road hears them backwards and goes on working.

Struck once and written again underneath, very small:

Neither order is said to the arm.

I asked the clerk, the hearth-maid and a slate-holder at the Cross, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the struck line where it was struck. The leaf goes back into the wool, the wool over the tube. At the Hearth Drawing the first measure of the year will come off this iron on this road, and I shall say the four sayings backwards into the wool, and the clerk will set them right above my words, and the hearth-maid will come up from Low Kiln with a stone in her hand and will not give it to me until I have finished, which is the only order of service I have kept nine years without correction.
