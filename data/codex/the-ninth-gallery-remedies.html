I keep the leech’s box in the Ninth Gallery, where the rock sweats and the smell of hot lead gives the only warning a man gets down here. I have dressed the hands of the Duelling Floor and caught the children of the survey families for seven-and-twenty years. Nobody asked me for this book. A boy came up in the lean week with the way in his forearm gone rope-coloured below a clamp, and asked what I did for it, and I heard myself answer in the hard short speech of this place, and did not know whether I believed it. A remedy you have to say in your mother’s voice is a remedy nobody has looked at in a long while. I have looked at some. The rest I have left standing, because a man at the changing of the watch wants a hand more than a correction.

I write on the backs of survey sheets the slate-damp spoiled. The rim-seam figures went up to Greybank where figures go, and what is left is paper enough for a book nobody asked to see. I write in the Grathic letters a clerk taught me and keep the Keldish endings where they will not fit. The coal at the head is mine. The grey in the gutter came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying. There is no Last Day under this Keep; the dust has nowhere to go.

The order of the leech-room

Written small on the first survey sheet, with one word gone over in slate-water.

  • Lay the warm-stone face down when you go to a child. A stone with its warm side up takes from the room, and a room in the Ninth is spending already.
  • Try the stone on your own wrist before it goes to a belly. It came out of somebody’s fire and the man who carried it up before me is in no roll I have read.
  • Set the number above the name, as they do on the floor. A man down here gives his number before his face, and a leech who writes the face first has written the wrong man.
  • Never set cold iron to a burn to draw it. Iron takes as well as draws. I learned that above me on the floor and have not unlearned it below.
  • Do not set a second clamp above a hand gone the colour of old rope. I wrote this on the iron board; the master struck it twice; I wrote it twice.
  • When the second chill comes, count with her. Do not count for her.

I asked a woman of the survey families what she saw when she crossed two fingers over the birth-flower, and she gave me a date. A date is an answer that does not spoil in the mouth. I have put the date in the margin and left the question standing where I asked it.

The remedies, as they are used

Trouble — What is set to it — The manner of it — Who is spent

The sweet at the gum — Sour barley-water; wood-ash at the gum; a tend above ground — We say a sweet answers a sweet. The physician says lead, and here he is right: the rock sweats hot lead, and a man in the lower galleries tastes it before he sees it. I sent nine up; four came back with the line gone. — The Ninth, which cannot stop and does not ask.

The rope-coloured hand (below a clamp) — Take the iron off; wrap in wool; set nothing above — The floor says a clamp draws heat out of a way that will not settle. I set one again higher on a boy in Y.H. 811 and by evening the arm had the colour of the stone. — The hand, and the number that stays when it does not.

The Descent boy’s fortnight — Warm-stone in wool after the knife; a clamp on the fifteenth day — We say the stone gives back what the knife took. The physician says a warmed arm spends faster and that a stone from another man’s fire is that man’s heat in a pocket. I gave the stone to eleven; ten held the clamp and one was given the keys. — Somebody paid for those coals. His name is on no slate.

The warm-stone burn — Tallow, then a clean fold; never cold iron — The floor says iron draws the heat out of a burn. I set a cold clamp on one and watched the skin go the colour of old rope; I have not done it twice. His sentence stands above mine. — The burn, and the hand if the iron stays.

The member gone dark — Three turns of wool, a stone at the belly, and work — The extremity goes first and the middle holds out, and there is an hour on the far side of the warming when a body spends faster than it takes in. I have no name for that hour and have left the place where the name would stand the length a name of that kind wants. — The man, who cannot stop working. This is the row where I have been least honest.

A cut from a seam-shard — Tallow, then a clean fold; never the grey — The men put grey from the corner in a cut to dry it, believing it clean. I dressed three and found grey under the skin in all three, a white line I could not get out. Above ground they sweep their corners after the Last Day and call it dealt with. — The cut.

The second chill — Warmth, barley-water, a watch to the fourth day; we hold them now — It comes after the warming, not before. A hearth-maid from Low Kiln says a warmed body gives heat away faster and walking faster still, and that the child should be held and counted with. I walked nine and held four; of the walked two went thin, of the held none. I kept the counts on birch bark and did not add them. — The household, one night’s sleep; sometimes the child.

The birth-flower — Nothing. Two fingers crossed, and the word the mother gives — I rubbed one with salt, one with slip and one with nothing, and the thing kept its shape in all three. A woman of the Weir washed one and her child went flat by evening; I have not joined the two. When asked what it is I give a date. — Nobody knows. The child’s skin, perhaps.

Nothing under this Keep is given away. It is carried from one hand to another and called a cure. That is the only sentence I will stand behind.

The note under the knuckle

The surveyors tap a piece of warm-stone before they set it in a tin. A sound piece answers full under the knuckle; a flawed one answers flat, a small dead sound that stops at the surface. It is the one thing in the Ninth that costs nothing and is still allowed to be true, and no clerk has thought to enter it.

I tapped a man once, gone thin after a thin return, and put my ear to the little flat I thought I heard. The flat was not in him. A body is not a stone, though we cut the number into it and call the cutting honest. I did not say so then, because a survey-clerk was in the room with a slate. I have written it here, which is a different matter.

The Sweating

The stone sweats in its season and the old ways ache before the thaw and after it. A boy who says his arm aches is told the weather will have the last word; one who says it twice is given a warm-stone out of somebody’s fire, which is theft from an account he did not open and is entered nowhere. The stone comes up already warm and the carrying takes the name off.

The Vorr say the count is in the bone and that the tube finds nothing there. I have seen a collector go away with a blank slate beside a man who had worked here thirty years. When one of them dies they seat the body facing the direction it came from and say they were never owed. A woman of Low Kiln would cover such a body with a fold and cross two fingers. Both are kindnesses; only one has a number in it. I have set them side by side and joined them to nothing.

I have no remedy for the Sweating and have left the place where one would stand the length a remedy of that kind wants. A clerk would put a nought in it and call it empty. I have seen the place and it is not empty.

The watch-song

Sung low at the changing of the watch, to the stone and not to the men. There is a place after the third verse where the tenth should stand, and no singer has put a word in it. The tenth is kept for the stone.

Nine on the board and one on the nail,

the tenth is kept below;

give the number first, then the face,

and wrap the hand in wool.

The rock sweats and the lead smells sweet,

the way goes rope at the second clamp;

a stone comes up already warm

and nobody asks whose.

Hold the child and count the breath,

do not walk her, do not ask the slate;

what comes back is not what went,

and comes back as the seam can.

Nine I counted and one held out ---

the tenth goes down where the stone keeps.

A survey-clerk put a round nought in the held place and wrote vacancy beneath it, which is a figure with the stone taken out. A nought can be carried away in a cuff. The tenth is a place and stays. I changed one line in seven-and-twenty years: and nobody knows became and nobody asks whose, and the men singing with me did not notice.

What the physician pressed in the margin

He came down from Greybank in Ilmen of Y.H. 812 with a book that had the word lead in it and a hand that wrote without asking. Against the sweet at the gum he set lead, as in the glaze rooms, and he was right in a gallery where the rock sweats hot lead and gives a man nothing but his tongue to warn him. Against the rope-coloured hand he set no second clamp, no such transmission, true in the language he speaks and of no use to a floor with one answer for everything. Against the child come up cold he set the air warms the child, not the stone. He was right in all three and had nothing a survey family could pay for. I have left his sentences above mine and my verbs underneath.

What the Court survey-clerk did

He borrowed the sheets when the winter seam was counted, ink settled in two fingers of a soft cuff. He struck out my column headed Who is spent and wrote No cost entered above it, which is a true thing in a dishonest place. Where I wrote that the boy’s fortnight was paid out of somebody’s fire, he set the measure is retained in the stone, taking the hands out as though a stone could carry itself up a shaft. Against one in twelve he wrote the deduction is constant, which lets the figure stand and the gallery go on spending. I have put my verbs back underneath. A clerk can write without verbs because he has never held a warm-stone with a man’s heat still in it.

In the hand of a clamp-master, in charcoal

It’s the draw, not age. I have seen a man grey in one winter at the tube and could not get the colour back with a week at the fire. Ask a Vorr what the clamp took and he will tell you the number. He will not tell you the hand.

He wrote it while I was at the shaft mouth and set the leaf back with the fold changed, and put no name to it, because a name on a leaf is a name a clerk can carry up the road. A man who has counted nothing is allowed one sentence in a book of physic. I have set his line above mine.

At the head and in the gutter

The coal at the head came off the master’s sleeve. The slate-water took two words clean off the second leaf and I have not written them back: a remedy with two words gone is a remedy said slowly, and everything down here is said slowly. The bead of lead was shut into the sewing by a girl who would not say which gallery it came off. The scorch is a spark off the hot-lead corner that caught while I wrote and went out under my hand; I have not entered the burn. The warm-stone shaving in the fold held heat one night and is cold now, which is the order of things here. The ring of barley-water came from a cup a boy would not drink from. The tallow is mine, from holding the sheet in the draught up the shaft. The grey along the outer fold is not mine and not the Court’s, and I have not swept it. There is no Last Day beneath this Keep, and the dust has nowhere to go.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The floor keeps the number. The stone keeps the warm.

The man between them is spent in no house.

The tenth is kept, and nobody says for whom.

I asked a physician, a survey-clerk of the Court, and a clamp-master off the floor, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The book goes back into the oiled cloth in the leech’s box. Tomorrow a boy will come up with the sweet at his mouth or a hand gone the colour of old rope, and I shall take the iron off, set the stone face down, put my verbs back where the clerk took them out, and leave the tenth where it is, because a child is not an account and a man at the changing of the watch wants a hand more than a doctrine.
