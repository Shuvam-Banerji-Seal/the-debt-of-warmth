I am a house-leech of the Ember bowl-house and I have caught the children of the quarters and laid out the bowls at the feasts for three-and-twenty years. Nobody asked me for this book. A girl came in the hot week with a hand gone pale inside seven rings of gold wire and asked what I did when a returned woman began asking for a door that was not in the house, and I heard myself answer in my mother’s long vowels, and did not know whether I believed it. A remedy you have to say in your mother’s voice is a remedy nobody has looked at in a long while. I have looked at some of mine. I have left the rest standing, because a woman at the third watch wants a hand more than a correction.

I write on the backs of feast-orders spoiled by wine. The lists of bowls to be emptied went where the house’s figures go, which is into the fire, and what is left is paper enough for a book nobody has asked to see. I write in the northern letters, learned off a factor who could count but could not read his own tallies, so that a girl of the Quay may read this if it goes north. The southern vowels I have left in the words that will not fit; a word that will not fit stays where it was said.

The oil at the head is mine. The wine in the gutter came up out of the paper itself. Nothing in this house is free. Here a thing that costs nothing has usually been emptied out of somebody who has no measure in law.

The order of the bearing-room

Written small on the first order, with one word gone over in wine.

  • Turn the bowl face down before you go to the child. I have no bowl-name and never shall. There is a name written inside a bowl in this house which its owner is never allowed to look at, and I have no wish to write one in a leaf a factor can carry.
  • Keep the shutters toward the court but do not open them wide. A woman watched from the court holds her breath, and a held breath is a poor thing to bring a child on.
  • Oil the wrists and the lips, never the belly. Oil on a belly is for the feast, and a child is not a guest.
  • Say the clay-name the house has given her. If she gives you the old one, write it nowhere: not on the leaf, not under it, not in the crease where a clerk does not look. A name written down is a name a man can carry out in a cuff.
  • Give the mother wine diluted and the child nothing. The physician from Greybank says wine thins what the mother needs to keep. I have given it to nine and both stand.
  • When the third-day quiet comes, count with her, not for her; a number counted for her belongs to whoever counted it.
  • Do not tap a person to hear them. A bowl is not a person, though we glaze ours with eyes so that the guest may be looked at while he drinks.

I was at the pouring of the lady Solene Ember in Y.H. 809, at the edge of the court. They said the return was whole. I have watched her at three feasts since and have not asked what she remembers.

The remedies, as they are used

Trouble — What is set to it — The manner of it — Who is emptied

The feast-sickness (after the public bowl is emptied) — A cold cloth at the brow; wine with water, not neat — We say a vessel emptied gloriously must cool in its own time. The physician says it is the wine and the heat of the court, and that water only makes the draught longer. I have set the cloth on eleven; eleven sat up by morning. — The household, which must empty another bowl the next day.

The sweetness at the gum — Sour wine; green glaze scraped from a broken bowl and rubbed on the gum — We say a sweet answers a sweet. The physician says lead, as physicians do in every country I have worked in. I have seen the pale line go off a girl who left the glaze court and come back on one who stayed. I have not seen the lead. — The glaze court, which cannot stop and does not ask.

The mill-cough (a returned thin set to the grain mills) — Oil and honey in warm water; a fold across the mouth at night — We say the dust of the mill is the dust of the old debt and must be coughed out. The physician says grain dust in the lung and no physic but departure. I sent two north; both came back worse, for the road has dust of its own. — The runner, returned thin and set to the mill for running.

The bowl-burn (a hand into a hot glazed bowl at the feast) — Oil first, then a clean fold of linen — The bowl-surgeon says cold iron to the burn, that iron draws the heat out. I set a cold iron ladle on a girl’s wrist and watched the skin below go the colour of old rope. I did not do it twice. His words stand above mine. — The wrist.

A graft that wants — A new clay-name, wine poured out on the court stones, a second wall over the first — In Sere we say a graft takes and then asks for the house it came from; we give it a name and a wall and call it settled. The physician says the donor’s measure was shaped otherwise, and that a second wall wants as the first did. I have set three second walls; two wanted the same thing after. — Nobody. The house, in a wanting it cannot enter.

The quiet on the third day (after a whole return) — The clay-name nine times; a bowl of wine under the bed, emptied at dawn — She calls a name not given in this house and reaches for a door the room has not got. We say the guest has come and must be seated. The physician says a spoken name puts no shape back, right in the manner he means. One went off it by the ninth speaking; one kept it two years. — Nobody. The house, in a year it cannot spend.

The name inside the bowl (a woman who will not take the clay-name) — Pour wine into the bowl and let her drink from the other side; never look in — The old name is written on the inside and forbidden. We say the wine carries it down where it cannot be spoken. Of six women I did this for, two stopped saying the old name, three stopped saying anything, and one went on saying it. I have entered two and not the three. — The woman, in a voice she does not use afterward.

A cut from a bowl-shard — Oil, then a clean fold; never the grey — The women put grey from the ash jars on a cut to dry it, believing grey is clean. I have dressed three such and found the grey under the skin afterward, in a white line, and have not got it out. — The hand.

Nothing in this house is given; it is emptied, and the feast is called generous. I will stand behind that sentence and no other.

What the bowl knows

Tap a sound bowl with the knuckle and it answers full. Tap a cracked one and it answers flat, a small dead sound that stops at the lip. The bowl-surgeons tap before a pouring and know whether it will hold a whole measure. It is the one thing here that costs nothing and is still allowed to be true.

I tapped a woman once, gone quiet on the third day after a whole return, and put my ear to the little flat I thought I heard. The flat was not in her. A body is not a bowl, though we glaze ours with eyes and set them on the table as guests. I did not say so, because a factor was in the court with a slate. I have written it here, which is a different matter.

The Spending

The Bowl-Priest says a vessel was made to be emptied gloriously, and a life hoarded is a life wasted. At the end of a feast they burn one household’s debt-slates by lot; by morning the slates are made again in a better hand. I have stood at eleven burnings and never seen a debt go where the paper went. The paper goes up; the figure gets a new coat.

When a northerner with a thin return is exhibited and given small coins, we say the house has been generous to a curiosity. I held such a man’s hand once while the coins were thrown. He was cold; the coins were warm from other hands, and the warmth did not go from the coins to him. This is as much as I know.

The song at the third watch

Sung low in the quarters, to the woman and not to the child. There is a place in the third verse where the old name would stand, and no singer has put a word in it.

Four for the fire in the open court,

one for the bowl that is turned face down;

say the clay-name, not the other,

and pour the guest a place in the ground.

The mill takes dust and the court takes light,

the north sells what the house will spend;

a graft takes hold and then takes a want,

and a second wall does not make it mend.

Three for the watching, three for the day,

and one for the name on the inside kept;

what comes back does not come as it went ---

come back, come back, as the wall can.

I have changed one line in three-and-twenty years. I sang the house will spend and now sing the north sells what the house will spend, and the women singing with me have not noticed, which is how I know the line wanted changing. A line said over a hundred feasts belongs to the room.

A factor’s clerk wrote above the empty place the former name is not a party to the contract, in a hand with a little gold in it. I have left the place. An empty place cannot be emptied twice, which is the only thing in this house I know of that cannot be.

What the northern physician pressed in

He came down from Greybank with a book that had the word lead in it and a hand that wrote without asking. Against the sweetness he set lead, as in the north; against the mill-cough, grain dust in the lung; against the graft that wants, donor mismatch. He was right about all three and had nothing a household here could pay for. I have left his sentences above mine, where a woman at the third watch will find them if she wants them. Where I wrote the guest has come, he wrote no efficacy. I have put the guest underneath.

What the factor’s clerk did

A clerk of the factor borrowed the leaves when the winter grain was counted; the ink sits in two fingers of his cuff, with a little gold in it that comes off on the paper and stays. He struck out my column headed Who is emptied and wrote No cost to the house, true in the counting room and false in the quarters. Where I wrote she asks for a door that is not in the room, he wrote the subject expresses a preference, a sentence with no door and no woman in it. He set the measure is restored above the house gives back, as though a thing always true had no need of anybody giving it.

I have put my verbs back under his. A clerk can write without verbs because he is never in the quarters. A woman at the third watch wants to know who poured, who drank and who paid.

At the head and in the gutter

The oil at the head came off the wick I held low while a girl was telling me something I wanted the end of. The wine came up the second leaf and has taken two words clean off it; I have not written them back, because a remedy with two words gone is a remedy said slowly. The flake of gold in the gutter is off the clerk’s cuff or a bowl’s rim; I cannot tell the two apart, which a goldsmith would not say. A chip of green glaze was pressed in by a girl who said nothing. The scorch is a spark off the court fire, caught while I wrote and put out under my hand; I have not entered the burn. The black hair bound with a bronze bead came out of a woman’s braid when she leaned to see what I was writing; she took the braid away and left the hair. The pale round place at the foot is where a bowl-surgeon set a pad greased with beast-clay while he read the remedy for a graft that wants. There is no Last Day in a bowl-house; at the end of a feast the slates are burnt and new ones made by morning, which is the same thing in a better hand.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The bowl keeps the name it is never looked into.

The slate keeps the debt that was burnt by lot.

The woman keeps the door that is not in the room.

I asked a bowl-surgeon, a factor’s clerk and a woman who gave me the old name and would not take it back, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The book goes back into the linen in my sleeve. Tomorrow a girl will come with a hand gone pale or a child that will not breathe; I shall turn the bowl face down before I look, say the clay-name and not the other, and put my verbs back, because a child is not a bowl and a woman at the third watch wants a hand more than a doctrine.
