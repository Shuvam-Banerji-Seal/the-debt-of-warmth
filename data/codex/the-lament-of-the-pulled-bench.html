I keep the oar-store on the Quay. That is my office: to count the oars in at the ebb and out at the flood, and to see that the leather sleeves at the rowlock are mended before a boat goes down. An oar comes back with a shine on the handle where a hand has held it through a term, and I can tell a new man’s oar from an old man’s by the shine before I can tell it by the wood. The Council counts the boats. I count the oars. Nobody counts what the oar took from the man who held it, which is not the same thing as saying nobody knows.

I set this down because a clerk of the tide-tally house stood at the store with a cuff gone soft from washing and a leaf in his hand, and asked what the women sang when the boats came in off the pull, and wrote the answer down while I was still saying it, with the doing taken out and a term set where a place should stand. So I have written what we said before he came and what we said again after he had gone. His hand stands above mine and the damp is taking it, and a shaving of oar-wood lies in the fold where a sixth verse should stand. A shaving is not a figure and cannot be carried away in a cuff.

The bloom at the head is salt. I held the leaf down on the oar rack in a wind off the Sallow and did not know I had marked it. Salt goes into paper the way seven years go into a hand, and nothing takes either out.

How it is sung

  • At the oar-store, over the oars as they are counted in, never aboard and never while a boat is going out. Said aboard, it becomes a prayer for a passage, and we have no prayer in this port that the Council has not entered against somebody’s term.
  • Standing, with the hand on the oar-handle before it goes to a woman’s arm. Said with a hand on a person, it becomes an argument, and there are arguments enough at the Quay without carrying one three leagues up the Salt Road.
  • To the wood and not to the company. The last word of every verse is let fall into the salt on the floor and not finished. The oars keep the refrain: one voice begins it and the rest come in on the second line.
  • Tap the oar-handle once for each stroke while the refrain is said. The tap is not a number. A clerk can carry a number off in his cuff; he cannot carry off a tap on a piece of wood, and if he could he would not know what to enter it as.
  • One verse for the oar, one for the hand, one for the term, one for the sea, one for the coming home. There is a place where a sixth should stand and it is not scored and not filled. Where the sixth should stand I have laid a shaving of the handle, cut with a knife where the wood has been worn smooth. The Council clerk has written against it, and his word is going soft with the damp. His word is his and the shaving is ours.
  • Not sung at the launching and not sung for a man who died at sea. There is a different thing said for him and it is said to the water, at the pushing-out of the boat, and watched until the boat is out of sight, which is held here to be enough.

The song

Said low to the wood, standing. The oars keep the refrain. The hand goes to the handle before the first line.

The refrain, as the oars keep it after every verse:

Pull to the ebb, and the oar keeps the count.

Pull to the flood, and the hand pays it.

The Council keeps seven. The sea keeps none.

The wood keeps the shine where the hand has gone.

I. Of the oar

Count them in, count them out, count them on the rack;

an oar comes back shining and a man comes back slack.

The sleeve at the rowlock is cut to the wrist;

the oar’s entered serviceable, the wrist isn’t.

Pull to the ebb, and the oar keeps the count.

Pull to the flood, and the hand pays it.

The Council keeps seven. The sea keeps none.

The wood keeps the shine where the hand has gone.

II. Of the hand

There’s a pale ring on the palm where the handle wore;

the fingers shut on nothing when the boat came in.

A hand that cannot close on an oar

cannot close on a cup, and won’t be asked to.

Pull to the ebb, and the oar keeps the count.

Pull to the flood, and the hand pays it.

The Council keeps seven. The sea keeps none.

The wood keeps the shine where the hand has gone.

III. Of the term

Seven years the notice says, or till the hands give out,

whichever comes first, which is never the one you want.

The Council writes discharged where we write come home;

the same man stands in both and neither asks him.

Pull to the ebb, and the oar keeps the count.

Pull to the flood, and the hand pays it.

The Council keeps seven. The sea keeps none.

The wood keeps the shine where the hand has gone.

IV. Of the sea

If he dies at the bench the sea has him returned;

no slate goes down and no collector goes with it.

We seal him in a salted boat and push him on the ebb

and watch till he’s out of sight, which is enough.

Pull to the ebb, and the oar keeps the count.

Pull to the flood, and the hand pays it.

The Council keeps seven. The sea keeps none.

The wood keeps the shine where the hand has gone.

V. Of the coming home

We set the cup to his mouth and don’t ask his hand;

a man can still be thirsty with his hands on the bench.

He sits where the oar rack was and says nothing of the pull,

and the shine on the wood goes on keeping his count.

Pull to the ebb, and the oar keeps the count.

Pull to the flood, and the hand pays it.

The Council keeps seven. The sea keeps none.

The wood keeps the shine where the hand has gone.

VI. The place that is not a verse

Here a shaving of the oar-handle lies in the fold, cut with a knife where the wood has been worn smooth. There are no words to the sixth place. The taps on the wood go round once more with the shaving under the thumb.

In the leaning hand, against the shaving: nil, term served.

*

Underneath, in mine: A nought is a figure and can be carried away. The shaving is a place and stays where it is. I have not moved it and will not.

What the Council clerk did

A clerk of the tide-tally house borrowed the leaf on a morning when the galley stores were being counted. He writes in the leaning hand, on salt-paper, and the damp has taken half of what he set down, so that of four corrections two are figures and one is the ghost of a verb. He took the verbs out of the verses and set his words above mine.

  • At an oar comes back shining and a man comes back slack, he wrote oar returned serviceable; person discharged; no claim arises.
  • At a hand that cannot close on an oar, he wrote term completed by infirmity, which is the same sentence with the man taken out of it.
  • At we seal him in a salted boat, he wrote return at sea presumed; no slate required, and underlined it twice, which is a great deal of ink for a thing nobody pays for.
  • Where the shaving lies he wrote nil, and the damp has eaten the word down to a figure with no verb.

I have put my verbs back underneath and left his standing above. He takes the doing out because a thing always true has no need of it. I put it back because a woman at the oar-store does not know she is hearing something always true, and wants to know which hand held the oar.

What the boat-mother put in the margin

The charcoal belongs to a woman who holds a share from her mother and has a man come aboard whose opinion on the boat is counted at half. She writes on the backs of spoiled tallies in a hand that presses hard at the ends of words. I have copied it as it stands.

  • At the Council keeps seven: Seven is a term and a term is a thing with an end to it. His hands gave out in the fourth year and the Council called it mercy. A mercy with a date on it is a fine thing for the man who wrote the date.
  • At a man can still be thirsty: I set the cup to my brother’s mouth for eleven nights. On the twelfth he took it with his own hand and we all went out of the room, which is how a house thanks a hand for coming back.
  • At the shaving: Cut it from where the wood is smoothest. That is where the most hands have been. I will not tell you whose.

At the head and in the gutter

The bloom at the head came up out of the paper itself and is the only thing on this leaf that entered without a hand. The tar thumb is mine, off the sheet I held down on the rack in the wind. The rope fibre in the second leaf came off a coil paid for with a wet hand and goes soft when the weather goes soft, which tells me when to shut the tin. The ring at the foot is brine, set down at the first verse and taken up at the last, by which time it had gone the colour of the floor it stood on. The fish-hook nick came off a man who set his hook down to count oars and charged the pulling of the boat to the wrong house, which is a thing the Council can settle and I cannot. The green bead fell out of a girl’s hair; she had been to the Cliffs and had sweetness at her mouth and said nothing, which is how the Cliffs are spoken of by those who have licked them. The grey along the margin came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying; nobody will own it and I have not swept it. No Last Day is kept at an oar-store, and nothing here is gathered and bottled and called dealt with. The shaving lies in the fold where the sixth verse should stand and I have not lifted it and will not.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The oar keeps the shine. The sea keeps the boat.

The slate keeps seven and nothing of what they paid.

I asked the clerk, a dipper come up from the pans, and a woman whose man came home in the fourth year, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written.

The leaf goes back into the tin under the rack. Tomorrow the boats go out on the flood and I shall count the oars in at the ebb, and the wood will keep the shine and the clerk will keep his seven, and if a man comes home with his hands left on the bench I shall set the cup to his mouth and not ask him to lift it. There is a place in the song for a sixth verse and it stays where it is, under the shaving, with no figure and no hand.
