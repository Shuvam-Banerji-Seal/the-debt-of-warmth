I keep the tide-roll in the Council room, which smells of tar and wet rope and has smelled so since before any of the men now elected were born. The Council is chosen for a year. The roll is not. Five Councils have come and gone since I was set to it, and not one of them has signed it, which is a kind of honesty in a port where a document is only as good as the weather it has survived.

This is not the history of Vhal. It is the list of what the Council entered, and the space between the pot and the slate, which is where I live. Everything in it is written on salt-paper that I keep dry, against the practice of the country and with the knowledge of the folly of it. A contract left in the rain is no contract here, and that is called a virtue. A history left in the rain is no history either, and I have met no man in this port who sees the virtue in that. So I have kept the roll dry and put the damp where it belongs, in the margin.

The years as the roll has them

Copied in the flat scrivener’s hand out of the tin under the table. I have put nothing in from the temple tells, which have their own years and a comfort in them.

Y.H. 611. The first contract for cliff-glass fine ware goes south by way of Greybank. Entered as a trade in sand.

Y.H. 626. The three furnaces stood. No glass came down to the Quay for a year and the Council suspended contracts written on salt-paper, which in Vhal is the same as suspending the year. Entered as a cessation of the glass work; contracts in abeyance. I have struck rose above the entry and set stood in its place, because the roll holds what was entered and not what a man might have shouted at a pot.

In the narrow upright hand of the Council, against the word I struck: A temporary irregularity in the delivery of glass. No contract was impaired. Strike suspension.

I have not struck it. A contract in abeyance is a contract a man cannot bring into a room and lay on a table, which is precisely what suspension means in this port and what the word irregularity is for. The hand has never in this chapter changed a figure. It takes the doer out and leaves the pot standing cold by itself.

Y.H. 627. The masters of the furnaces put the stopping into articles, so that a refusal would have a place to be entered. Entered as a regularity in the keeping of the furnaces. A master who cannot enter a refusal will enter the man who made it, which is a different thing and a longer one.

Y.H. 798. A physician come up from Greybank leaves a page saying the sweetness at the gum comes from the lead of the cliffs. The word goes out of the page before the year is out, as though a word could be scraped off paper the way slip is scraped off a bench. Entered as an opinion received and not proceeded with. I have put the lead back underneath, where a clerk can see it and choose not to read it.

Y.H. 811, the Long Cold. No boat crossed the Sallow and no glass went south. The roll was shut in the tin and the tin under the table and the table against the wall where the brazier stood, and a corner of this leaf took the heat and did not recover. Entered as a visitation of the weather; account standing.

Y.H. 812, the Thin Year. The cold broke and the contracts came back on paper the damp had never been asked to settle. I have entered both the reopening and the suspension, which the first keeper of the roll did not, because he entered openings and not closings, as a man enters what he can see from the Quay.

The figure for Y.H. 627 stood at nine hundred in the clean hand, because the clerk who made it counted pots the way a potter counts a row of wet pots, by the places and not by what was in them. I have struck it and written six hundred and ten underneath, by touch as it were and not by eye, because a man writes differently when he is setting down what happened from what was entered. Two hundred and ninety sheets went south that year by a boat the roll does not name, and are in the gutter under the round sum where a factor from Greybank will not think to look.

In the narrow upright hand of the Council, against the struck figure: Deficit not entered. Delivery discharged at the furnace door. Account standing.

The hand is careful and it has never once changed a figure. It has made a year without glass into a year in which glass was delivered, which is a cleaner thing to carry into a Council room. We have eleven words in this port for a contract that has gone wet and not one for a pot that stood cold with a man beside it who had wanted to work.

The hands

The Council’s figure is set first. What stands beneath it is not a figure and was not entered.

Refusal in Y.H. 626 — irregularity, not entered — The pot left standing cold

Hands named after the rising — seconded to the pulled benches — Seven years, or until the hands gave out

Heat-name at the first gather — one decorative appellation, not a vessel — A name said to the pot and kept in the mouth

Green at the gum — occupational mark; no deduction arises — Lead, paid out of the hand

The men named after the rising went to the pulled benches, seven years or until their hands gave out, whichever came first, and the roll enters them as seconded, which is a word for sending a man somewhere he has chosen to go and is used here because no other word in Ledgerhand will carry a refusal. A man who melts sand for a living has hands, and a man who has been at the oars for six years has hands that give out. I have set both facts down in the column where they can be read and left the Council’s seconded standing above them where it belongs.

One of them was a girl of the outer furnace who put her hand to the gum with cliff-glass and would not say whose hand had taught her. She was not sent to the benches; she was kept at the anneal and given seawater, and a green bead she pressed into this leaf is the only thing in the roll that has never been entered. I have not asked her name and the Council has not asked either, which is the same thing said about a person and said about a room.

The anneal-song

Taken down at the third furnace, from a woman who had been at the gather in Y.H. 626 and had not been at the benches. It is sung at the anneal, standing, with the pipe across the knee. It is not sung at the Council table and the third verse is said to the pot and not to the company.

Sand from the bay and lead from the blue,

the sea takes the writing, the lead takes the hue;

hold the end up and never point it at a man,

take what comes up and not what you began.

The year the paper rotted the pot stood still,

the clerk wrote nothing and the Council set nil;

seven years at the oar for a hand that would melt ---

the sea keeps the figure and the hand keeps the heat.

Green at the gum where the pale line was,

the master calls skill what the girl calls hers;

ask the pot what it held when the firing was stopped,

and the pot says the heat and the heat says the hand.

She would not sing the third verse while a man from the tide-tally house stood by, so we went and stood at the anneal. She said songs about glass are made by the glass, which is the custom of that shore and not an answer. I have set it where I found it, away from the table.

What the borrowed hands did

Two hands have been at these leaves besides mine.

  • The Council clerk, in the narrow upright hand, took the verbs out. Where I wrote the furnaces stood he set a cessation of work occurred; where I wrote the men were sent to the benches he wrote hands were seconded, no deduction arising; where I wrote a girl pressed a bead into the leaf he wrote an object of no value was affixed. I have put my words back underneath. A verb is the difference between a thing that happened to a person and a thing that happened in a room with a table in it.
  • A factor come up from Greybank, in a leaning counting-hand and pale ink that has gone soft where the damp took it, wrote figures of account, not of loss against the table of sheets and set a round nought beside the year without glass. He has never stood at a furnace and has strong views about sand. A nought is a figure; the cold pot was not, and no man melted anything in it.

I have left both readings everywhere, the cold one under the neat one, as a furnace leaves the flaw showing under a gather and does not pretend the sand was ever clear.

At the head and in the gutter

The white at the head is salt bloom off the dockets and comes and goes with the weather; it has taken the last letters off one word in the second year-entry and left the rest readable, which is the only honest thing the damp has done to this roll. The glass dust in the grain is off the third furnace and no brush takes it out. The tar at the fold came off the rope under the table and is mine; I have never got it out and have stopped trying. The ring at the head is small ale, set down by a ship-master who would not drink with dippers and did not mind drinking with a clerk. The scorched corner is from the brazier in the Long Cold and has taken two words clean off the last verse; I have not written them back, because a song with two words gone is a song said slowly and everything in this port is said slowly enough already. The green bead in the lower corner is cliff-glass and has left a sweet smear that will not dry. The struck figure is mine. I wrote nine hundred in the clean hand and six hundred and ten beneath it, and both stand, because the roll holds what was entered and what came down, and the difference has no signature.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The pot stood cold and the paper held,

and the Council wrote what the paper told.

Nine hundred went up and six hundred came down,

and the hands went to the oars for a word with no sound.

Ask the pot what it held. Ask the hand.

I asked a furnace-master, a clerk come down from the Council room, and a woman who sells eels on the Quay, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written. The roll goes back into the tin under the table, which smells of tar and wet rope and has smelled so through five Councils. Tomorrow a contract will be written on paper the weather is invited to settle, and the Council will call it a virtue, and by evening it will be a debt no man can prove. The furnaces will take another child at twelve with a name she will not use at the pot, and I shall enter the sheets, because that is what the roll is for.
