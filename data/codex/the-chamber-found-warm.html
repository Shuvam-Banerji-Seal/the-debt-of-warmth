I keep the low chronicle in the Weighing Hall, in the drawer beneath the fire-tongs, where the paper takes the warm off the grating and goes soft at the fold. It is not the book the steward’s clerk carries under his arm, nor the clean roll the factor reads from at the Weighing. Those books have had somebody taken out of them. This one has not, which is why it is kept where a man must kneel to reach it.

I sign with my name, Senn, and not with the office, because a name can be called to account and an office cannot. The roll-room would prefer the office. I have written the name at the head of every copy since I took the drawer, and at the foot of this extract, and in neither place has anybody corrected me, which I take to mean that nobody has read the foot.

This extract is the first hundred and twenty years. The temple calls them the mercy. The roll-room calls them the lighting. The low chronicle, which is older than either name, calls them the years in which the chamber was found. Found. I have kept the word. A man who writes lit in place of found has put a hand into the story that was never there, and in this country a hand is never harmless.

The line, and the four times it was written

The four copies stand on one leaf in the order they were made. I have not cleaned the earlier ones off. A line that has been copied four times is a line that somebody wanted changed.

Copy — Hand — What the leaf says

First — a hard hand, not Court, every verb standing — The chamber was found already warm.

Second — an early roll hand, the word pressed small and close — The chamber was found warm.

Third — a tidy College hand, the finder gone out of it — The chamber was warm when we came to it.

Fourth — the clean hand now read at the Weighing — The Hearth was lit in the first year by the Nine Who Kept.

The first has a man in it. The second has lost a word. The third has lost the finder. The fourth has lost the finding. The temple tells the children that the Nine Who Kept lit the Hearth with a coal brought out of the first fire, and the children grow up and repeat it, and the clean hand reads it at the Weighing, and the chamber keeps its warmth and says nothing about how it came by it.

In the narrow upright hand of a clerk of the College, against the first copy: An account of a tradition, not an entry. Enter as the lighting. Strike already.

Underneath, in mine: I have left already standing. It is the whole of the first copy. A chamber found warm is a report; a chamber found already warm is a report made by a man who had expected it cold, and had gone in with a tool to see.

I asked a keeper of the grating why the copies had grown smoother. He said a thing copied often enough becomes true, which is not an answer and is the belief of the building. I have set it above the table and not beneath it, so that a reader knows which of us said it.

The years as the low roll has them

Copied out of the drawer leaf by leaf. The Court hand stands above; mine stands beneath. Where they agree I have said nothing.

Y.H. 1. The chamber was found already warm. No ember entered, no figure, no name of a finder. A coal was taken up at the grating, carried out of the hall, and brought back before the tend was out; the roll does not say why, and I have not asked the grating.

Court hand: The Hearth kindled by the Nine. Strike found.

Y.H. 3. The first Returns recorded: six measures drawn whole and pressed into six newborn vessels, with the names entered before any face was looked at. This is the year the roll begins to have something in it worth counting.

Court hand: Six whole returns recorded. Strike before any face was looked at.

Y.H. 9. Three offices set in the Hearthseat: assessors to value a life, collectors to draw the measure, bankers to press it into a vessel. Entered in the low chronicle as three clerks and a table.

Court hand: The offices of the Keeping established. Strike table.

Y.H. 40. The deduction fixed at one part in twelve and printed upon the Weighing notices. The figure has not moved in seven hundred and seventy-two years.

Court hand: The deduction fixed at one in twelve throughout the bowl. Entered and confirmed.

Y.H. 62. Four of the Nine Wells sealed in the Keel and the keys hung in the steward’s room. Entered as four sealed, the keys kept. No reason given on either side. A fifth was sealed in Y.H. 618 and is written in another book; the low chronicle does not follow it, being a record of the beginning and not of the middle.

Court hand: Four springs reserved to the Hearth. Strike sealed.

Y.H. 88. The first bank opened in the Hearthseat, the second at Greybank. Greybank keeps a Court’s hours and speaks a Court’s voice, and the clay towns have never quite forgiven it for either.

Y.H. 120. Here the chronicle begins. The hand changes. What stands before was copied out of a book with no cover and no first leaf; what follows was entered in the year it happened, by men who were there. I have kept the change of hand. It is the only honest thing in these years.

The cost of those first whole returns was paid by a province. The roll has a line of figures for it and then a line of squares, and I have copied the squares as squares, which is a thing no clerk of this building does twice. The province has a name in the temple and no name in the roll. What I know of it I have set in another extract and will not repeat here; a chronicler who says the same thing in two books has written a sermon, and a sermon in this country is always copied more often than an account.

The three offices, as the keepers speak of them

The assessor asks what a life was worth. The collector draws it. The banker presses it into the next vessel that opens its mouth. The order matters: a man must be valued before he is drawn, and drawn before he is spent, or the account will not balance and somebody will be made to stand at the Weighing until it does.

There is a saying among the keepers at the grating, said into the iron and not at a person: The assessor counts, the collector takes, the banker gives, and the Hearth is thanked for all three. I asked whether the Hearth gave anything back. The keeper said that was a question for the temple, and went on polishing the bars. He had the answer and had put it where I could not reach it, which is the usual way of answers in this building.

The figure on the notice

One in twelve. It is printed large on the Weighing notices and read aloud at the Weighing in a voice that does not hurry, and a household goes home believing it has paid one in twelve because it heard one in twelve and the slate was in front of it. The tube does not read the notice. A printed figure is a promise and a drawn figure is a fact, and the two have never been introduced to one another.

I have heard another figure spoken in the yards of the clay country when the slate is put away. It is not on any notice and has never been entered in this hall. I have left it out of the years and put it here in the margin, where a figure can be kept without being made a promise. If it is true, it has been true a long time and has managed very well without me.

The four sealed Wells

Four springs in the Keel give back more than they take, and the Court sealed four and kept the keys, which is a strange thing to do with a source and a reasonable thing to do with a key. The keepers say the Wells were sealed to keep the Warm in. A surveyor come up from Keld and drunk enough to speak said they were sealed to keep the amount of it a secret, and then turned his slate face down. I have set both sayings here. One of them has a key in it and one does not, and I have no way of weighing a key.

The kindling verse

Sung by the keepers at the grating on Kindling, before the year-fire is carried out. It is not a prayer; the litany belongs to the Weighing, and the keepers do not sing it. I took it down from a junior who sang the first two lines and would not sing the third while I had a slate out.

We found it warm and left it so,

and counted what the flame would keep;

the first fire came from no first fire,

and the coal came back to sleep.

Four wells are shut and four keys hung,

the ninth runs where no key is kept;

the assessor writes, the collector draws,

and the Hearth is thanked as if it slept.

The line grows smooth the more ‘tis said;

I keep the rough one where it lay.

Ask the chamber, not the coal,

how warmth came in and stayed.

He said the third line was not a verse but a thing a keeper once wrote in the margin of a book, and had been sung ever since because men will sing what they are afraid to say. I have left it where I found it, in the middle, where a clerk correcting the leaves is least likely to put his hand.

What the borrowed hands did

Two hands have been at this extract besides mine.

  • The College hand, narrow and upright, takes the verbs out. Where I wrote the chamber was found already warm, it set the lighting is recorded in the first year. Where I wrote a keeper pressed a coal into the wax, it set a coal was pressed into the binding. A verb with nobody in it is a verb that cannot be argued with, which is why they take them out. I have put mine back underneath and left theirs standing above, as the first copy stands under the fourth.
  • A temple hand, softer and rounder, wrote were gathered into the Hearth across the line about the first Returns. It did not strike anything. It wrote across, which is a gentler way of taking a sentence away and a harder one to argue with, because the old words can still be read if a man is willing to lean over them and put a thumb on the leaf. I have left the thumb-print where it fell.

At the head and in the gutter

The slate dust in the gutter has been there since the drawer was a drawer. The coal pressed into the wax of the sewing was put there by a keeper who had come off the grating with a coal in his fingers and had nowhere clean to set it; it is not part of the binding and never was. The scorch along the third leaf is where the leaf lay against the grating before I took the drawer, and it has not grown and will not go. The tallow at the head is mine; I turn the leaves by the light of a tallow end and have never managed to hold the book and the light with two hands. The flake of cold-iron scale in the lower margin came off the bars when a keeper struck one to show me where the fire comes up, and I kept it rather than ask him to strike another. The grey in the crease of the fourth leaf is Ash, and no keeper sweeps it during the Five, and no keeper has ever said why. The word struck through in the year-entries is mine: I wrote taken where the Court hand had drawn, and set drawn above, because the book holds what was entered. What the tube did is in the verb underneath, where the College hand does not reach.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The line grew smooth and the smooth line stands;

the rough one stays where the coal was pressed.

Four keys in a room and a fifth elsewhere,

and a figure on paper the tube never reads.

Ask the chamber. It has not forgotten.

It was warm before we came.

I asked a keeper of the grating, a clerk of the College, and a surveyor up from Keld who would not give his name, and had three answers and no hand, as I have had before. I have left the line where it was written. The leaves go back into the drawer under the fire-tongs. At the Opening of the Slates I copy them forward and put the four copies in again with my own hand, in the order they were made, so that the smooth one never sits alone on the leaf. The chronicle goes on. Next tend I copy the Weighing figures from the clean roll, and I shall put the verbs back there too, and set them under the Court’s hand, where a man must kneel to read them.
