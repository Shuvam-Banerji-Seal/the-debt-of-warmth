I throw at a wheel two doors down the Clay Yard from the Vail kiln-house, and I have kneeled at it six-and-twenty years. A man come up from Greybank with a soft cuff and a slate under his arm stood in the shed door on an Account-day and asked a girl at the bench what the wheels said while they turned. She told him. He wrote it while she was still saying it, and what he wrote had the turns taken out of it and a figure set where the wetting stood. He thanked her and went back over the north bridge with his boots clean. I have set the words down as we say them and put the verbs back where he took them out.

The clay comes up off the treads wet and heavy and full of its own opinion. You set it on the wheel’s own centre, wet your hands in the slip bowl, and begin. The first twelve settle it. The second twelve open the well with the thumb. The third twelve bring the wall up between the thumb inside and the fingers outside, and the lip is taken at the top of the last of them. That is the whole of the throwing, and a cup is a well that has been persuaded to stand up.

We say the count down into the lump, not up into the shed. Said up into the air it grows a tune in it, and a tune is a thing a man in a clean coat can carry away and set by his own fire, with the lump left behind him. The wetting is not a turn. It is a hand in a bowl of slip, and it is kept beside the count where no clerk can put a figure on it.

The slip at the head is mine; I held the tally down in the draught under the door and did not know I had marked it.

How it is thrown

  • Wet both hands in the slip bowl before the first turn, and again where the wall goes thin. A dry hand leaves a ring the wall will keep, and the knock at the rack will tell you which hand it was.
  • Set the lump on the wheel’s own centre, the worn one in the seat. A lump set proud of the centre spends all its turns arguing with the thumb.
  • Twelve to settle, twelve to open, twelve to lift. The wetting stands beside them and is not one of them. A girl who counts the wetting aloud will drown the cup.
  • Keep the thumb in the middle of the well. A thumb set to one side makes a wall thin on one side, and the thin side goes first in the firing.
  • Knuckle the cup before it leaves the shed. A whole one answers full; a hollow one gives back the small dead flat. If it answers flat, take it off the wheel and begin again, however late it is.
  • Say the count to the clay and not to the girl beside you. Said to her it becomes instruction, and instruction learned at this wheel is unlearned at another.
  • Do not sing it on an Account-day. The bell at the Cross goes four, four and a gap, and a wheel count set against that gap comes home wrong in the hand.
  • The last part is kept back. It is for when the kiln-master has gone up the terrace and the man from Greybank has gone back over the bridge with his satchel under his arm and the shed can hear itself again.

The song

Said kneeling, low, into the lump. Refrain after every verse, the last line held until the hands are off the clay. One line of the refrain to one twelve.

Twelve to settle, twelve to sink,

twelve to bring the wall to the brink;

*the hand in the bowl is not a turn ---

the wheel keeps its own and the slate keeps its own.

The lump comes up off the treads with the water still in it,

set it where the seat has worn its hollow;

a lump set proud of the centre spends the whole of its turns

arguing with the thumb that must open it.

Twelve to settle, twelve to sink,

twelve to bring the wall to the brink;

*the hand in the bowl is not a turn ---

the wheel keeps its own and the slate keeps its own.

Wet the hands in the bowl before the first turn,

and wet them again where the wall goes thin;

a dry hand leaves a ring the wall will keep,

and the knock at the rack knows which hand it was.

Twelve to settle, twelve to sink,

twelve to bring the wall to the brink;

*the hand in the bowl is not a turn ---

the wheel keeps its own and the slate keeps its own.

Twelve to settle and the lump stops fighting,

the thumb goes in at the middle and the well opens;

open it slow, for the well is the first shape the cup knows

and a well opened wrong is a cup begun wrong.

Twelve to settle, twelve to sink,

twelve to bring the wall to the brink;

*the hand in the bowl is not a turn ---

the wheel keeps its own and the slate keeps its own.

Twelve to sink and the wall comes up between

the thumb on the inside and the fingers outside;

pinch it and the wall goes thin in the pinch,

spare the hand and the wall falls in on itself.

Twelve to settle, twelve to sink,

twelve to bring the wall to the brink;

*the hand in the bowl is not a turn ---

the wheel keeps its own and the slate keeps its own.

Twelve to bring the wall to the brink and stop,

take the lip at the top where the thumb left it;

a lip left thick is a cup that will crack in the throat,

a lip pinched thin is a cup that will crack at the table.

Twelve to settle, twelve to sink,

twelve to bring the wall to the brink;

*the hand in the bowl is not a turn ---

the wheel keeps its own and the slate keeps its own.

Knuckle it before it goes to the rack; a whole one answers full,

a hollow one gives back the small dead flat;

twelve and twelve and twelve, with the wetting beside them,

and the number read at the Cross is another twelve, not this one.

Twelve to settle, twelve to sink,

twelve to bring the wall to the brink;

*the hand in the bowl is not a turn ---

the wheel keeps its own and the slate keeps its own.

There is a space before the last refrain in the singing, the length of a hand dipped to the wrist in the slip bowl. I have left it on the tally. A clerk put a round nought in it once, which is a thing you can hold up and look at; a wetting is a hand in a bowl and will not stay still to be looked at.

The part they keep back

Not sung while a kiln-master stands in the shed or a man with a satchel stands in the door. Said low, to the seat and not across it.

One in twelve, says the notice, read twice at the Cross,

but the box goes up the clay road with a different figure shut in it;

the wheel gives a cup its twelve and the slate takes one away,

and the two twelves are not the same twelve and nobody says so.

There’s a hollow in the seat worn by six-and-twenty years of kneeling

and a girl kneels in it now who has never asked what the figure was;

we go around the place where a hand went into the strips

the way we go around the well while the water is still in it.

Twelve. Twelve. Twelve. The wetting stands beside them.

The shed keeps the turn. The clerk keeps the nought.

What the kiln-master did

He came up from the terrace on the wrong wind with the sweet edge of the glaze at one corner of his mouth and read the leaf with one eye on the throat of the kiln. Against a lip pinched thin is a cup that will crack at the table he wrote in broad charcoal: A lip is set by the firing and not by the table. What cracks in the throat never reaches one. He underlined it twice.

Underneath, in mine: He is right about the lip and wrong about the song, which was counting turns and not lips. He has read every line with one eye on the kiln mouth, which is how a man reads who has never had to kneel in a worn seat and bring a wall up by the feel of it.

What the clerk did

The factor’s clerk borrowed the leaf on an Account-day, ink sitting in the first joint of two fingers of his soft cuff and not coming out. He took the verbs out and set his figures above mine, and I have left his hand standing and put ours underneath, as a shed keeps the old hollow in the seat under the girl who kneels in it.

  • At twelve to settle, twelve to sink, twelve to bring the wall to the brink, he wrote thirty-six rotations; vessel formed; no charge arises. True in the figure and false in the shed, where a rotation is a turn and the turns belong to whoever knelt for them.
  • At the hand in the bowl is not a turn, he wrote wetting: incidental; nil. He has made a bowl of slip into nothing at all. A wetting is a wrist in a bowl, and somebody mixed the slip.
  • At the number read at the Cross is another twelve, not this one, he wrote deduction one in twelve. Printed. Current. He has joined two twelves that stand apart, which is the whole art of that hand. Both stand in my leaf and neither stands with the other.
  • At the space before the last refrain he put a round nought and wrote vacancy. A nought is a figure and can be carried over the bridge. A hand in a bowl cannot.
  • At a hand went into the strips, he wrote one absence, nil, and set a figure against the worn seat. I have left his figure standing beside our going-around. Both stand.

What the rack-woman put in the margin

The thick charcoal belongs to a woman who keeps the drying racks two doors up and has lifted more wet cups than she will count. I have copied it as it stands, without making the hands agree.

At a dry hand leaves a ring the wall will keep: I set a wet cup down where the wall was thin and took it up in the morning with the ring standing in it. It is not the wet that does it.

At the well is the first shape the cup knows: I have taken cups off the rack with the well almost rubbed away and no thumb left to put it back. They hold water all the same.

At the two twelves are not the same twelve: I have asked what the wheel is worth and been given the weather, the river and the price of barley. I have stopped asking.

At the kept-back part: The hollow in the seat is the only honest figure in this shed and it is not a figure. It is a place where somebody knelt.

At the head and in the gutter

The dry line across the second leaf is where a sleeve passed at the third turn; the wall took the mark off my cuff and gave it to the leaf. The barley-water ring stood through the fifth verse while a girl stood over me telling me to keep the thumb in the middle; she took her cup up when I had done, and the ring has gone the colour of the pot it stood on. The sherd between the third and fourth leaf came off a cup with the well almost rubbed away, and is lighter in the hand than a sound sherd, which is the whole of the lesson and no use at all to a clerk. The green bead came off a girl who leaned to see the writing with sweetness still at her tongue; she touched the tally, said nothing, and went back up the terrace. I have not washed it. The hair the colour of wet wool has caught in the seam and will stay there. The grey along the gutter came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying on a wheel-shed bench. Nobody will own it. There is no Last Day at a wheel.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The wheel keeps the turn. The bowl keeps the wrist.

The slate keeps neither and counts all the same.

I asked the kiln-master, a girl who has only this year come to the wheel, and a man come up from the Fishstairs with eels to sell, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The tally goes back under the slip bowl. Tomorrow the clay comes up off the treads and I shall kneel in the worn seat, wet my hands, settle it, open the well, bring up the wall, and leave the wetting where it stands, outside the count, and the clerk may have his copy of the nought.
