I keep the pipes and the anneal at the third furnace. A house brings a child and goes away; the child stays with the fire. A clerk comes with a cuff soft from washing, writes what the pot was worth, and the pot is on a boat before he has finished. This leaf hangs on its wire beside the anneal shelf, where twenty years of heat have taken the lower edge into a clear bead of glass. The last two rules read best held up to the light. So does everything in this trade: a thing held to the light shows the bubble and the man who blew it, and the Council has never once asked to see the man.

I set the rules down as they stand. Where a hand has corrected me I have left both readings.

They were gathered in Y.H. 627, the year after the rising of Y.H. 626, when the masters found that men who could melt sand could also stop melting it, and put the stopping into articles so that a refusal would have a place to be entered. A rule with no heat on it is a rule nobody has had to stand. These have been stood.

The articles as they stand at the third furnace

Of the taking

  1. The furnace takes a child, not a house. A house may bring him and feed him, but from the day he lifts a filled pipe he answers to the master. The Council takes the profit and leaves the child alone. This is the one thing in Vhal nobody has thought to tax.
  2. Taken at twelve, set to the gather at thirteen, and not before he can lift a pipe and keep the end of it up. A child taken later is not made up the year he missed; the furnace keeps no count of the heats it did not make.
  3. Kept, not hired. He eats from the furnace dish, sleeps in the loft, and what he makes goes to the furnace and comes back as bread and a place by the anneal, neither entered. A master who tells a girl she is paid tells a true thing about a figure and a false thing about a living.
  4. His birth-name is said once at the taking and not used at the furnace. What he is called afterward is the heat, given at his first gather and kept in the mouth. A name in the mouth cannot be entered, and what cannot be entered is the last thing in this port the Council has not found a way to sell.
  5. The gather comes before the name. A child gives what came up on his pipe before he gives who he is; a pipe that has gathered tells more about a boy than a boy at a gate does. A child who gives his name first is sent back to the pot, and the pot is not interested either.

Of the sand and the lead

  1. Wash the sand in seawater and the sea takes the salt out of it and leaves the sand. The same sea takes the figures off a contract left in the damp, which the Council calls a virtue and the furnace calls a day’s work lost.
  2. The lead comes out of the blue cliffs, blue-green and sweet in the heat. No child is sent to the cliffs. A girl who has once tasted the cliff will ask to go again, and that is the whole of the answer.
  3. No child tastes the pot. The master tastes, with the side of the tongue, and spits it out. The pale line at the gum is lead and not a mark of skill; a master who calls it skill is selling the girl’s fingers one at a time and calling it a living.
  4. A girl who rubs her gum with cliff-glass and says sweetness answers sweetness is not to be argued with before the young. Take the glass away, give her seawater, and let the sea be the one paid. We do not say kindly at a furnace; a kindness there is a debt with no date.

Of the pipe and the anneal

  1. Lift the end of the pipe and never point it at a man. A pipe pointed at a man has put out an eye in every generation, and the Council has never written a rule against it, which is how a man knows the rule is ours.
  2. Roll on the marver and do not keep the glass on the pipe while you decide what it ought to be. Glass asked what it should be cools into what it was, and a thing only nearly what it should have been still goes to Sere with a figure on it.
  3. The gather is taken from the pot and the pot is not left. A girl who leaves the pot to answer a question has given her heat to the question and taken it out of the glass. There is no figure for the heat that went into an answer, and no column where it would go.
  4. The anneal is where a finished thing goes to stop being finished. Nothing is taken warm out of it to show a buyer. A thing cooled too soon keeps its heat in the wrong place and breaks a year later in a stranger’s hand, with no entry; the thing has crossed the sea and the hand belongs to somebody the Council never counted.

Struck hard in charcoal against rule 13, in a furnace-master’s hand, and written again underneath: Do not take it warm. Written again above, the second time heavier: Nobody takes it warm. In mine, below both: The master has struck anybody into nobody, which is the same rule said to a person and said to a room. A girl does better with the person.

The table of heats

Kept on the anneal shelf. What is written is the heat that came up, in a hand that does not change between one line and the next.

Year — At the furnace — What is taken

Twelve — Carry sand, wash it, pile lead, sweep, hold the pipe-ends up — The birth-name, said once and not used again

Thirteen — The gather and the marver; the pipe kept level — The habit of asking what the glass ought to be

Fourteen — The first blow; the first heat named at the pot — The heat-name, given by what came up

Fifteen — The anneal; nothing taken out before its hour — The belief that a thing finished is done

Sixteen — Take a pot alone through a firing and read the green at the gum — The last of a girl’s account with any house

  1. Sixteen is the common end of a heat and not a short one. A girl who has been through it is not paid out and sent away; she stays where the furnace needs her, at the third furnace or at the Quay or on a boat whose share goes through her mother. The master does not choose which. The pot does, and the Council after it.

The tally of hands

Kept by the under-master. The figure is the Council’s and is set first. What stands beneath it is mine and is not a figure.

Condition — The Council’s figure — What stands beneath

Girl taken at twelve — one apprentice, current, no wage arising — A child who eats from the dish

First heat named — one decorative appellation, not a vessel — A name said to the pot and kept in the mouth

Green at the gum — occupational mark; no deduction arises — Lead. Paid out of the hand.

Hand that will not hold a pipe steady — one hand, current; measure not affected — The pipe taken from that hand; the hand kept

A thing broken in a year — breakage not entered — A stranger’s hand, in a house we have not seen

The end of the heat — discharged at sixteen — Paid out of nothing and sent where the pot needs her

  1. The green at the gum is entered as an occupational mark because an entry that says lead would want a payer, and no payer in Vhal will put his name to a girl’s hand. A physician came up from Greybank in Y.H. 798 and left a page saying the sweetness came from the lead; the word went out of it before the year was over, as though a word could be scraped off paper the way slip is scraped off a bench. I have put it back here, where a clerk can see it and choose not to read it.

In the narrow upright hand of a Council clerk, against rule 15: Occupational mark; no deduction arises. In mine, underneath: It is lead. I did not learn it from a page, which is the only reason I can still say it. Both stand.

Skill said of the green — The word struck in charcoal and lead set above it, by a hand that will not sign

A warm thing taken from the anneal — Set back; the thing broken at the door and not sold

A heat-name written on salt-paper — The paper left out in the rain; the name said again to the pot

The pipe pointed at a man — The pipe taken away for a tend; the eye not put back

The Hearth beneath the salt asked about — Answered once: the Sallow has a Hearth under the salt; asked twice, send it to the dippers

A heat sold to the Council and the other kept — Not entered. There is no law against it, which is why the furnace does not speak of it

  1. A fault copied twice in a firing is a fault the girl has not understood, and the master sits with her at the anneal until she has. The pot is not a rod.

The gathering-song, pasted inside the cover

Sung at the first gather, standing, with the pipe across the knee and the end up. Nobody sings it at the anneal and nobody sings it at the Council table. The third line of the second verse is the girls’ line and the masters sing it wrong on purpose.

Lead from the blue and sand from the bay,

the sea takes the ink and the lead takes the day;

hold the end up, never point it at a man,

and take what comes up, not what you began.

The green comes up where the pale line was;

the master calls skill what the girl calls hers;

a name in the mouth is a name without sum ---

the clerk puts a nought where the heat should come.

Lead from the blue and sand from the bay,

one heat for the tally and one for the sea;

the pot keeps the flaw and the gum keeps the lead,

and the girl keeps the heat that was named for her.

The wet cloth across the song is from a girl singing in the rain at the outer furnace.

What the borrowed hands did

A clerk of the Council borrowed the leaf on the morning after the salt boats came down, ink in two fingers of a soft cuff, and took the verbs out as though a verb were a thing a man could smuggle past a table.

  • Against he is kept, not hired he set retained at the furnace, no wage arising, true about a figure and false about a girl who eats from the dish. I have put mine back underneath.
  • Against the row about a hand that will not hold a pipe steady he wrote one hand, current; measure not affected. I have set the hand kept beneath it where a girl can read it.

The other hand is charcoal, a girl come down from High Kiln with the Glaze Room in her mouth. Against rule 8 she struck a mark of skill and wrote It is lead. It is paid out of the hand, and would not sign it. She has put the price where the proportion should have been, which is the only column in this trade that tells the truth. I have not asked her name and she has not given it.

At the head and in the gutter

The white at the head is salt and not Ash; there is no Ash in Vhal that has not been bought and sold. A factor from Greybank asked once if the grey thumb was northern Ash. I told him it was a thumb and had not been paid for. The tar at the fold is mine, off the pipe-rest I coil every dusk. The nick is from a glass-cutter’s point; the vinegar ring, set down by a girl who would not eat until a question about the green was answered, has taken two letters off the last line. The bead in the second fold is cliff-glass, pressed in by a girl who watched me write and said nothing; it has left a sweet smear two months dry. The lower edge has gone over into clear glass and will take no more ink; the words nearest it are the ones that matter most and the ones the light must be asked about. There is no Last Day on an anneal shelf; the heat does its own correcting and keeps no roll of it.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The pot keeps the lead and the gum keeps the green.

The heat keeps the name that the tally left out.

I asked a furnace-master, a clerk come down from the Council room, and a woman who sells eels on the Quay, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written. The leaf goes back on its wire; the heat will take another word by the next firing and I shall not write it back. Tomorrow a child will be brought at twelve with a name she will not use at the furnace, and I shall lift the pipe-end, keep it level, and say what came up before I say who came.
