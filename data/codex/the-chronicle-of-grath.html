I keep the Chronicle of Grath. There are two chests in the thane’s house at Sill and I keep the one that is not the road-book; the road-book holds distances and tolls, and a man cannot get a year out of it, only a price for clay. The moot-chest below the Cross holds the town’s year-book, which begins at the Blue Cliff and holds what the town was told. This book holds what happened, when a man can be got to stand still long enough to ask. Where the two disagree I set them side by side and do not join them. A clerk who joins two accounts has begun a sermon, and a sermon in this country travels faster than a cart and lasts longer than a pot.

The office is smaller than it sounds. A Thane of Clay is chosen by the kiln-masters of Sill, who know a man by the way he sets a pot in the yard, and confirmed by the factor at Greybank, which is the only way a thane is confirmed and the reason every thane in this book is the factor’s thane before he is ours. The choosing takes an afternoon. The confirming takes a line on a slate eighteen leagues down the Clay Road, in a hand the thane cannot read without asking, which he does not, because a man newly chosen does not want to find out what he has been chosen for.

The years as the chest has them

Copied in the broad clay-hand. I have put the verbs back where they were taken out and left the narrow hand above mine where it stands.

Y.H. 418, the Blue Cliff. Commissioners sat at the cliff with salt-paper, a cold-iron clamp and a piece of Sill clay on one table. The Clay Road was made a Court road; an assessor could stop a cart without asking the thane, and did. The treaty slate was cut and laid in the moot-chest under the road-book, and read at the Cross on market morning. The thane was not at the cutting. Wyn Clay held the office, confirmed in 411; he asked who had signed for the road and was told that four powers had set their marks, which is true and not a name. Entered in the road-book as a conference at the cliff; entered here as no thane at the cutting. Both sentences are in the chest.

Y.H. 560. The thirty-one years the Court calls the War of Deductions ended; we have always called them the taking. Hal Clay held the office, confirmed in 552, and was not called to hear the ending. One figure was printed on the notice and read at the Weighing; another was paid in the clay households and entered nowhere. The two stand together in the table below without a signature between them.

Y.H. 668, the Rising. Ninety children’s warmth taken in Low Kiln for one whole return no household could name; eleven weeks of disorder; Assessor-Marshal Pell Varr came up with twelve collectors and a list, and forty-one persons were dead and entered as spent in the keeping of the peace. Hob Clay was thane, confirmed in 661. He called the levy in those weeks and called the disorder a disturbance when the factor’s letter came down. The eleven weeks are counted in the Hearthseat and not swept in Low Kiln; I have put the heap under the table where a clerk of the College will not think to look.

Y.H. 747, the Cold Pass. Karric VII shut the pass in 741 and seven years were spent arguing about which mountain the Hearth lies under. The agreement was cut on a slate in Slate Row, a Court street and not a Grathish one; Keld kept the pass and shaft, Grath kept the road, and the Hearthseat was left standing. Rade Clay was thane, confirmed in 739. He was not asked. The Grathish say this is how all important treaties are made; the Court says the thane’s absence is not an entry. A man who states his measure before his name is not thereby asked what he thinks of a border.

Y.H. 811, the Long Cold. Ice came on the Ilm in early Stone and did not break for ninety-four days. The barley was thin, the peat in Mud End would not dry, and fuel prices doubled and then stopped being quoted because there was none to sell. Bel Clay was thane, confirmed in 803. The levy was raised to one in three across every Low Kiln household and announced as being for the Hearth; the notice beside the Court’s room still said one in twelve. Nobody in Low Kiln reads it. Households called themselves walking thin, which is a name for people who keep moving because stopping is the same as cooling.

Y.H. 812. In the first weeks of Stone forty children of Low Kiln were banked for a private account. The Greybank slate gives no purchaser and no destination for the warmth. It gives a name for the return, written before the collector had seen the infant’s face. I have copied the figures and left the name where it was written; a name from Greybank gets repeated at the well and comes back as a cause, and there is no cause in this year that I can enter.

The choosing and the confirming

Between the names below there are other thanes whose years the chest has and which I have not copied. A list of men is not a list of what they did, and the factor’s clerk turns first to the names.

The present thane — the kiln-masters of Sill — — — Reads the notice at the Cross and calls the levy in the name of your house, never I.

In the narrow upright hand of a factor’s clerk, written above the second column: Confirmed by the Court’s factor. In mine, underneath: confirmed at Greybank. I have left both. A man is chosen in an afternoon in the yard at Sill and confirmed in a line at Greybank, and the line outlasts the afternoon and the yard. He writes the Court’s factor as though a factor were a door that had always stood there and nobody had cut it out of the wall.

The years in which no thane was asked

Year — What was cut or fixed — Who set marks — What the thane was told

418 — Clay Road made a Court road; border fixed at Greybank — Four powers, one mark set by a man of Sill who was not the thane — That the matter was closed

747 — Pass and shaft to Keld; road to Grath; Hearthseat neutral — Keld, the Court, Grath, and a fourth mark the chest does not name — That the road was open

749 — Neutrality of the Hearthseat guaranteed — Grath, Keld, Vhalen and Sere; Grath’s mark set at Greybank — That the fire was left standing

In the narrow hand, against the last row: The thane’s attendance was not required. In mine: not asked. A thing not required and a thing not asked are the same thing said from two rooms, and the rooms do not agree about who was standing in them. The first can be entered at a Weighing; the second said at a well. I have kept both.

A thane hears of a treaty when it is read at the Cross, after the pigs are over the boards and before the bell goes four, four and a gap. Nobody crosses himself at the gap. The gap is not a figure and has never been entered, which is a mercy of a kind.

The levies called in a thane’s name

Year — Printed on the notice — Called in the households — What was entered

811 — one in twelve — one in three in every Low Kiln household — additional warmth for the Hearth

812 — one in twelve — forty children, privately — purchaser not recorded

this year — one in twelve, as the notice says — read twice over the basket at the Cross — the levy is called

The last column is the only one the College reads, and it has nobody in it. A levy called is a levy with the caller taken out. The figure that goes up the clay road in a locked box is not the figure that came out of the house. Paper is not a payer. I have drawn no line across.

In the narrow hand, against the 811 row: The deduction is uniform. In mine, beneath: one in twelve on the notice. A uniform deduction can be read aloud in a clean voice in a hall with the fire behind the grating; it cannot be paid in Low Kiln in a winter when the Ilm lies frozen for ninety-four days. I have not struck the clerk’s sentence out. A clerk who never walks the clay road should not be made to carry what he wrote.

The moot-song

Taken down at the Cross from a mason who set the sill stones and would not give his day’s wage, sung after the reading and never during it. The crowd says the last line.

They chose him in the yard where the pots stand rim-down,

and confirmed him a line down the Clay Road;

a line is a lighter thing to carry than a pot,

and it goes where the pot does not.

Four marks at the cliff and none for the thane,

four, four and a gap at the bell;

the road is the road and the toll is the toll,

and the thane wipes his thumb on his coat.

One in twelve on the notice at the Court’s room,

one in three on the house with the thin floor;

the figure that goes up the road in a box

is not the figure that came out of the door.

A pot mended twice is the pot that was broken,

and the pot that was broken is not there;

ask the sill what it kept of the river,

ask the thane what he kept of the year.

In charcoal, pressed in the lower margin by a hearth-maid of Low Kiln who would not give her name: Four, four and a gap is the bell that tells the figure and not the cost. In mine: I have left it where she put it. She writes small, the way a woman writes where somebody is asleep.

The hands that are not mine

The factor’s clerk borrows the chronicle when the salt boats come up. He writes upright and narrow and takes the men out of a sentence as though a man were a thing a clerk could carry off in a cuff. He set three readings above mine and, against the charcoal line, wrote nothing at all, passing over it without a stroke, which is the readiest correction I know.

I have left every reading where it was written. A book with two hands in it is the only kind a man in this country will believe; a book with one hand in it is a Court notice, and a Court notice has never told a man what his house would pay.

At the head and in the gutter

The brick dust at the head came off the High Kiln terrace on the sleeve I wipe with, and no damp takes it out. The clay in the gutter comes off the floor of this room and has been there since the first copy; a clerk who keeps a chronicle walks clay into it every day and does not sweep it out. The slip-shaving in the second fold was left by a man come to be confirmed who went away without it; it has gone the colour of old bone. The silt along the foot is off the sill where the Ilm drops four feet over hard stone, dried pale and coming and going with the weather. The tallow thumb at the head is mine. The green bead in the corner is not ink: a glaze-mixer come up from High Kiln leaned over to read the moot-song and pressed it there with her thumb, and went off with the sweetness still at her mouth. The ring is where a cup of small ale stood from the first entry to the last. The square of bare paper is where I scraped a word away with a knife-point and put no other in; it is the only square in this quire I made myself. The grey along the outer fold is not mine and not the factor’s. I have not swept it. There is a Last Day in this house and we do not sweep the corners on it, which the factor’s clerk has entered as a custom and we keep as a debt.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The kiln chooses and the quay confirms;

the road is measured and the man is not;

four marks at the cliff and one gap at the bell,

and the figure goes up and the house stays cold.

I asked the kiln-master, a factor’s clerk, and a man who has read at the Cross for thirty years, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The book goes back into its chest. Tomorrow the thane will read one in twelve, as the notice says twice over the basket, call the levy, and go up the clay road with the year’s arithmetic where no man can read it; this book will keep the other figure underneath, where a clerk of the College will not think to look.
