I write on the back of a survey sheet that spoiled in a sweating in the Ninth Gallery. The surveyor had set it down near a seam giving off the smell of hot lead, and the heat took the figures off before anybody could read them, which is a mercy of a kind: a figure that cannot be read cannot be entered. I have cut the counts into the board cover with a file, because a notch is still there when the paper has gone soft, which is more than can be said for the Court’s figures. I am a leech of the deep. I tend the clamp-hands and the stone-cough and men who come up from the lower galleries older than they went down. I have set down what I do and what the men before me did, and where the two disagree I have set them side by side, as the survey office keeps two readings of one seam.

Nothing in this book is free. That is the only thing I will stand behind.

The coal at the head is mine. I sign in coal because it goes into the grain and will not come out, and a leech who signs his cures in a hand a clerk can read is a leech who will be asked to answer for them.

The number before the name

In Keld a man gives his number before he gives his name. At the lamp-room door I ask it too. A man with no number is come down from the low countries, and I tend him last, for I do not know what is owed against him and will not ask. This is not cruelty. It is the order of the room: the man with the least number goes first, for he has the least held between him and the cold.

If a man cannot say his number, look at the forearm. If there is a smooth place where a number was cut out, do not ask how it went. I have asked twice in nineteen years and had the same answer both times, a shrug and a look toward the shaft. A place the size of a thumb is the whole of what a man will tell you about it.

A survey-clerk from Greybank asked why I number the dying. I told him so the iron would know who to take from. He wrote it down and went away pleased, which is how I know he did not hear me.

The order of the lamp-room

Cut into the first leaf, in the coal hand, with one word gone over in lamp-oil.

  • Set the lamp in the niche, never on the bench. A lamp on the bench will be knocked into a man’s wound, and then the wound has two troubles where it had one.
  • Lay the warm-stone in wool and try it on your own wrist before it goes to a hand or a belly. The stone takes from the lamp’s account, and somebody down the shaft paid for the lamp.
  • Ask the number before the name, and write it first, so that if the man goes while you are writing, the figure is the part that stays.
  • Look at the hand before the iron. A hand you cannot save is still a hand you must not make worse.
  • When the sweat comes sweetish with the smell of hot lead, get the men up. A man who stays to argue with such a gallery will not argue with you again.
  • Do not tap a man to hear him. A body is not a cup. I have seen a man do it and have not forgotten the sound he said he heard.

Remedies, as they are used

Trouble — What is set to it — The manner of it — Who burns

The Sweating — Get the men up the shaft; cold iron to the wrists, never the chest — Heat comes up through the stone and takes a man by the head. Iron at the wrist gives it somewhere to go. Whether out of him or only lower down him, I have never been able to say. — The gallery, which gives back nothing.

Stone-cough — Steam of lamp-oil and hot water under a cloth; honey if the house has it — To the mouth, not the face. The dust of the Ninth gets into a man and he coughs it up in the morning in a colour he will not discuss. — The lamp and the man’s own morning.

A hand gone rope-coloured below a clamp — Take the clamp off — Leave it to the sixth day, which is what we have always done. If the colour has not come back by then, the hand is gone; do not set the clamp again higher up and call it a second chance. — The hand. This is the honest row.

The cold that stays — Walk it off; rub with warm-stone; set the man to work — See what I have written below. I have done these three things and I have not stopped doing them. — Nobody knows.

A cut from the Duelling Floor, or a first way gone bad — Tallow first, then a clean fold — The clamp-master says cold iron to draw the heat out, and that the iron takes as well as draws. I have set his words above mine and both stand. — The man who opened the way.

A greyed hand, from years at the iron — Nothing I know. Keep him from cold iron for a tend — It does not come back with rest and it does not come back with warm-stone. A collector’s hand is paid partly out of other people’s heat, and the debt shows at the fingertips. — The collector who paid him.

A fever that cooks a man from within — Cold iron to the temple; bleed at the elbow to let the head fall — A physician come up from Greybank says bleeding lets the measure out and not the head, and the man is weaker after. I have watched four such men and two were quieter by evening; quiet is not the same as better, and I have set that down. — The man, in the arm.

A boy at the Descent — A warm-stone in the pocket and a number across the forearm — Thirteen is early to have a number cut in a bone that is still growing, but the Warden’s roll wants it and the roll gets what it wants. Give the stone after the knife, not before. — The boy, at thirteen.

A man come up from beyond the pass — A stone, a place by the lamp, and no questions — He will give you a number if he still has one and a name if he still has that. Take whichever he offers and do not ask for the other. — Nobody. He has paid already, and not here.

On the full note, and why a man is not a cup

Tap a sound warm-stone with the knuckle and it answers full. Tap a cracked one and it answers flat, a small dead sound that stops at the surface. The surveyors of the First Seam use it on the stone of the shaft, and it is the one thing in this country that costs nothing and is still allowed to be true.

I knew a man who tapped the chest of a clamp-hand gone thin and said the note came back flat, and said it as though the man had refused him. The man had not refused him. There was simply nothing there for the knuckle to find. I did not say so at the time, because there was a slate in the room and a man who was going to be entered on it. I have written it here, which is a different matter.

The ways run down the arm the way a river runs, with smaller ways coming off it, and this is what every leech in the Keep believes and what I was taught at the Descent. I have put no drawing of them in this book, because a drawing is a thing that can be carried into a Court room and read aloud there. Whatever runs in a man was put there before the slate was, and the slate has never had the better of it yet.

The cold that stays

Below the sill, and in the lower galleries where a man stands in water to the calf through a shift, a foot or a hand will come up cold and stay cold after the water has gone and the wool has gone on. In the low countries they call it the cold and go home warm on the word. We know it by the feel under the thumb: the extremity goes first and the heart holds out, and there is an hour on the far side of the warming when a man gets worse before he gets better, or does not.

I have no name for that hour. I have left the place where the name would stand, which is the right length for a name of that kind.

We walk it off. We rub it with warm-stone. We set the man to work, because a moving man does not notice how cold he is going and can finish his shift. I have done all three and seen a man lose two toes on the fourth day, and I have gone on doing them. There is no honest remedy in this book for the cold that stays, and this is the row where I have been least honest, so I have set it in the middle where nobody can miss it.

A song for the Descent

Sung going down, not coming up, by a boy of thirteen with the number newly cut in the forearm. The last line is said twice, the second time for the one who has had his number taken out.

One for the lamp and one for the rope,

one for the stone that has kept the fire;

cut me a number across the arm

and let the name wait till the shift is higher.

Two for the lead and two for the sweat,

two for the hand that holds the light;

the Ninth Gallery takes and does not forget,

and the Warden counts us at night.

Three for the going-down, three for the up,

and one for the place where the number was;

ask me my measure, I’ll give you my name ---

the measure goes down and the name stays above.

There is a space before the last line in the singing, the length of the smooth place on the arm. No clerk has yet put a figure in it, which is the only reason it is still a space.

What the Court clerk did

A survey-clerk of the Court borrowed the leaves at Greybank, being a man who cannot let paper lie. The ink sits in two fingers of his cuff and will not come out. He struck out my column headed Who burns and wrote No cost entered above it, which is a true thing in a dishonest place. Where I set that the gallery gives back nothing, he wrote The measure is retained in the stone. Against the smooth place on a man’s arm he put a round nought and wrote vacancy, as though a place where a number had been were the same as zero.

I have filed the notches deeper in the cover. He can take a word off paper because paper is soft. He cannot file a notch out of a board without the board being the thinner for it, and then he has entered something of his own, which is the only kind of entering I have ever seen him avoid.

From the clamp-master

At the foot of the remedies, in a blocky hand pressed in coal, the letters wide apart as though cut with a chisel.

On the fifth take the iron off. I have held it on the sixth and carried the hand away in a pail. What you have written is what we did. It is not what I do. --- H.

Hald Iron of the Duelling Floor borrowed the leaf before the clerk did and returned it with the fold changed. I have set his line above my row and mine under it, as the floor keeps the clamp and the hand that was in it. A man who has lost a hand to his own trade is allowed one sentence in a book of physic.

At the head and in the gutter

The coal at the head came off my own thumb setting down the sheet in the lamp-room and went into the grain before I knew I had touched it. The lamp-oil down the fold came from a lamp knocked over on the third night of a sweating, and has taken the ink clean off two words, which I have not written in again: a remedy with two words gone is a remedy said slowly, and everything down here is said slowly. The filings in the gutter are off the clamps on the Duelling Floor. The scorch on the second leaf is a spark that caught while I was writing and went out under my hand; the hand was mine and I have not entered the burn. The splinter sewn into the cover is warm-stone from the Ninth, and is why the book is a little warm in the morning, a kindness bought by somebody whose name is not in here. The frost on the third leaf came up out of the paper on the climb over the Cold Pass, and is the only thing in this book that entered itself. There is no Last Day under the Keep and nothing gets swept.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The stone keeps the heat and the iron keeps the number;

neither of them asks who paid.

I asked a clamp-master, a survey-clerk, and a man with a smooth place on his arm, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The book goes back into the coat pocket against the splinter of warm-stone. Tomorrow a man will give his number before his name at the door, and I shall write the number down and cut another notch in the cover, and the notch outlasts the figure and the figure the man, which is the whole trade here.
