I keep the racks at House Silt, three doors up the Clay Yard from the Vail kiln-house, and I have set this down because a man come down from Greybank with a cuff gone soft from washing asked a girl at the tubs what the women sang when they carried the wet pots up from the wheel, and wrote what she said before she had finished saying it. A carrying-song is not a song for a fire. It is said to the feet, and the answer is said to the pot at the set-down. Sung at a fire it grows a tune in it, and a man in a clean coat can carry that away.

The raw-slip thumb at the head is mine. I held the tally down with the hand I tread the slip with and did not know I had marked it.

How it is carried

  • Two pots on the forearms and one against the apron. Never a fourth, because a fourth wants a hand you have not got, and a pot wants both hands at the set-down.
  • The call goes to the pot and the answer goes to the rack. Said looking at a woman it becomes an argument, and an argument at the racks ends with somebody setting a pot down wrong on purpose.
  • Four paces to a verse and a rest at the set-down. The rest is not a pace and is not counted. It is where the pot goes down, and where a fifth pace would be if there were one.
  • Knuckle every pot before you set it. A woman who sets down a pot she has not heard has set down a stranger’s pot, and nobody in the yard will answer for it.
  • If a slate lies open on the rack bench, turn it face down or take the long way round the yard. A pot said over an open slate arrives somewhere the carrier did not mean to send it.
  • A girl come up from the Glaze Room with green at her mouth and the pale line of the sweet-sickness at her gum does not carry that day. Not a punishment. Sweetness makes a hand careless, and a careless hand sets down a pot that will not sing.
  • Do not sing it at the well on a flood morning. The water has its own note, and two notes over one another make two counts that will not agree, and the yard goes around both all day.
  • The last verse is kept back. It is for when the man from Greybank has gone back over the north bridge with his satchel under his arm and the yard can hear itself again.

The song

The woman at the wheel calls the first line of each pair, the women on the row answer the second. The refrain is said low, into the rack, at the set-down. The last line of the refrain is held until the hand is off the pot.

The wheel gives it up and it’s heavy as a debt,

bone-coloured under the slip where the thumb went;

carry it level. Carry it the way you’d carry a question

you don’t want slopped on the man that asked it.

Set it down. Leave the crack showing.

Four paces to the rack and a rest where the fifth should be;

the pot gives its note and the note goes through,

and the slate has no ears and is entered regardless.

Knuckle it once before you set it down.

A whole one answers full and the note goes through the clay;

a cracked one stops at the skin and gives back flat,

and you set it by the ruts and sweep around it too.

Refrain, said low into the rack at the set-down.

Don’t close the crack. The neighbours must see it was handled.

A hidden crack is a lie with a pot built round it;

a showing crack is a thing a woman can carry

into another house, set down, and be believed in.

Refrain, said low into the rack at the set-down.

The girl who tastes the glaze has green at one corner

and a pale line where the lead has kissed her gum;

don’t let her carry. Sweetness makes a hand careless,

and a careless hand sets down a pot that won’t sing.

Refrain, said low into the rack at the set-down.

There’s grey in the corner where the promise went;

the broom goes round it and the bucket goes round,

and the rack goes round it at the turning of the row;

it’s left for the Last Day and burnt next morning, and called dealt with.

Refrain, said low into the rack at the set-down.

Under the lowest rack is a space nobody enters.

Whole slate should stand there, they tell the new girls;

we go around it as wide as a pail of hot water

set where a child might knock it, and we do not look in.

Refrain, said low into the rack at the set-down.

At the end of the returning row there’s a place we go around.

A hand went into the strips and did not come back with the rest;

we go around it with a look and no figure,

and the loaf-shelves say two gaps stay two gaps

till a finger lays them together and wants a name.

Refrain, said low into the rack at the set-down.

One in twelve, says the notice, read twice at the Cross,

once for the moot and once for them that came late;

the bell goes four, four and a gap and nobody crosses,

and the figure you heard is not the figure they paid.

Refrain, said low into the rack at the set-down.

There is a space before the last refrain in the singing, the length of a set-down. I have left it on the tally. The clerk put a round nought in it once, which is a thing you can hold up and look at, and a rest is not.

The verse they keep back

Not sung while the factor’s man is in the yard. Taken up after he has gone back over the bridge. The women say it to the rack and not across the square.

The number goes from a fist to a mouth to a shoulder

and arrives at nobody, and nobody asks where;

the grey cord keeps what the slate cannot spell —

tie once for owed and untie for paid, and don’t say whose.

They took warm children in the year of the flower and the count was forty,

and forty-one knots were tied and not one of them entered;

the Court put a nought in the empty place and called it vacancy,

and the river went on under the bridge and carried no figure.

Four paces and a rest. The rack keeps the place.

The clerk has no word for a place he cannot enter.

The rain has eaten one line of the song. I know what it said. Knowing a thing is not the same as having it, and I have not written it in again.

What the clerk did

The factor’s clerk borrowed the leaf on an Account-day, ink sitting in two fingers that would not come out. He took the verbs out and set his words above mine. I have left his hand standing and mine underneath, as a rack keeps the pot’s weight and the water’s mark.

  • At the pot gives its note and the note goes through, he wrote vessel sound, entered; quality not a figure. True in the figure, false in the yard, where a note that goes through is the whole of the quality.
  • At a cracked one stops at the skin and gives back flat, he wrote vessel failed, having reached its term, and underlined it twice. A pot cracked through at the racks on Stone was called a pot gone wrong and swept around. It had not reached anything. It was knocked.
  • At leave the crack showing, he wrote defect disclosed; no deduction. He thinks a crack shown is a debt confessed. In this yard a crack shown is the only honesty a pot has left.
  • At a hand went into the strips and did not come back, he wrote one absence, nil, and at the place in the row vacancy. He has put a number where a place should stand, which is the whole art of that hand.
  • At forty-one knots, he wrote 40 plus one unentered. It is a true thing in a language nobody at these racks speaks. We do not say the extra knot and we do not untie it.
  • At the rest in the refrain, he put a round nought and wrote fifth nil. A nought is a number. The rest is a place where you set the pot down. Both stand.

What the woman at the tubs put in the margin

The thick charcoal belongs to a woman at the tubs below the racks who has carried more wet pots than she will count. I have copied it as it stands, without making the two hands agree.

At carry it the way you’d carry a question: She carried a question about her man’s debt level for seven years and never slopped it. Set down on the Last Day, it had gone cold. Do not ask whose.

At the girl who tastes the glaze: I have held the pale line up to the light for three of them. Two went south. The third still carries, and I have said nothing, which is a kindness and a fault in the same breath.

At a hand went into the strips: It was my sister’s. I said it to the board and the pot, never to the woman who asked, and I will not put a figure to it with a clerk’s pen on the leaf.

At two gaps stay two gaps: My mother said it of the shelf and I of the rack. I have never laid a finger across either, though I have stood with my hand over the board a moment longer than the reaching wanted.

At the kept-back verse: The river took what the Court did not. You can shout at either one. I have tried.

At the head and along the gutter

The raw-slip thumb at the head is mine and will not come out of the grain, which is why I use it to sign. The green bead in the lower corner came off a girl who leaned to see the writing with sweetness still at her tongue; she touched the paper, said nothing, and went back to the rack. I have not washed it. The barley-water ring went through the second verse while a woman stood over me telling me to carry level; she set her cup down and took it up when I had done, and the ring has gone the colour of the pot it stood on. The long grey along the gutter came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying on a rack. Nobody will own it, and there is no Last Day behind the racks. The scratch is a rack-hook catching the fold. The grey wool through the hole is for the word I could not write down. I will not untie it.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The clay keeps the foot. The rack keeps the place.

The slate keeps neither and is read twice over.

I asked the woman at the tubs, a girl from the Glaze Room and a man with a barrow at the yard gate, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written.

The leaf goes back under the broken pot on the lowest rack. Tomorrow the wet pots come up from the wheel and we carry them four to the verse and set them down with the crack showing. If the clerk comes again he may have the tally. He cannot have the rest, because the rest is a place, and he has no word for a place he cannot put a figure in.
