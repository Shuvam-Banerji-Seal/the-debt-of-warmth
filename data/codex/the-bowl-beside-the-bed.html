I give suck in a house that is not mine, to a child whose name I am not permitted to say until the household has chosen it. I have done this for four years. I know the weight of the child on my arm the way a potter knows the weight of a pot before the glaze goes on, and I know the sound the house makes when the bowl goes round at the third hour, which is a sound with a debt in it.

A clerk come up from the factor’s house at Greybank stood in the portico and asked the women what we sang over the cradles. He wrote down the names, because names are what a clerk can carry away. I have set the song down as we say it, with the verbs left where we put them and the space left where the space is.

The ring at the head is wine. I held the leaf down in the draught under the portico and did not know I had marked it.

How it is said

  • In the third watch, after the household bowl has gone round and the fires in the open court are banked for the night. Not at the Bowl feast and not on a name-day; on those days every name in the house is already in the air, and a song said then is a song with a name in it, which is a different thing.
  • Hold the child with its ear to your collar and not to the wall. Said toward a wall, the song becomes an instruction to the house, and there is no room in a great house that has not already been told what it is to do.
  • Set a cup down at the cradle’s head and do not drink from it. It is not the child’s cup and it is not yours. It stands for the one that was poured out in the court and cannot be carried back up the steps.
  • Say the refrain to the bowl and not to the child. Said looking into a face, the second line becomes a question, and there is nobody in this house, including me, who may answer it in the child’s hearing.
  • If a phial of quiet ash stands on the shelf, leave it corked. A voice that cannot arrive is not the same as a voice that has stopped wanting to.
  • Say the last line once. The space before the last refrain is the length of a name and is kept empty. I do not fill it and neither does the child.

The song

Said low, with the ends of the words let fall the way a drop falls from a dipped cup. The refrain is said to the bowl.

The bowl came down from the open court warm,

the colour of skimmed milk gone blue at the rim;

the household drank and the act was announced,

and nobody asked who had carried it in.

Sleep, little bowl. The wine keeps warm.

I bore a child and it is not this one.

Seven names stand ready above your bed

and the house will keep the one it likes;

I have one name of my own and it is not mine ---

I say it to the bowl and not to you.

Sleep, little bowl. The wine keeps warm.

I bore a child and it is not this one.

They teach in the court that a vessel was made

to be emptied in public and praised for the emptying;

I have held a cup through the whole of the third hour

and learned which hand pours and which hand pays.

Sleep, little bowl. The wine keeps warm.

I bore a child and it is not this one.

I had a child with a brown edge to his crust

and a way of turning his head at a cup;

he went to the mills when the term was called,

and the voice that called it was clean.

Sleep, little bowl. The wine keeps warm.

I bore a child and it is not this one.

The mistress sets a phial of quiet ash by the bed

so no voice from a former life will arrive;

I have uncorked one and smelled it and set it down ---

a voice is a thing a woman can bear to hear.

Sleep, little bowl. The wine keeps warm.

I bore a child and it is not this one.

So sleep. I will not say the seventh name,

which is not chosen and has no weight in it yet;

the cup stands full where the hand set it down

and the wine keeps warm for the one who will pay.

There is a space before the last refrain. I have left it on the leaf. It is the right length for a name and for nothing else, and the child who cannot yet ask what it is will one day ask and will be told that names are chosen above stairs.

What the tally-clerk did

A bowl-house tally-clerk came into the women’s quarter with a cuff gone soft from washing and ink sitting in two fingers of it that would not come out. He writes in the leaning hand that keeps the glaze-tallies, and he took the verbs out as though a verb were a thing a woman could hide in her sleeve and carry past a door.

  • At the wine keeps warm he wrote the measure is in circulation; temperature not entered, and underlined the figure twice.
  • At I bore a child and it is not this one he wrote issue: not entered; relation: contractual, which leaves the room standing and takes both women out of it.
  • At the house will keep the one it likes he set name assigned by household; no deduction arises, which is true in the figure and false in the room where I said it.
  • At a voice is a thing a woman can bear to hear he wrote no efficacy recorded and put a small round nought beside the phial.
  • Where the space stands before the last refrain, he put a nought and wrote vacancy beside it. He has put a number where a name should stand, which is the whole art of that hand.

I have put my words back underneath and left his standing above. He takes the verbs out because a thing always true has no need of them. I put them back because a child at the third watch does not know it is hearing something always true, and wants to know which hand will hold it and which hand will be paid.

What the northern woman wrote underneath

The thick charcoal belongs to a woman bought out of the clay country who keeps her words on the backs of linen tallies and will not let a clerk see them. I have copied them underneath as she set them.

At I bore a child and it is not this one: She has not seen that child in six years. She asked me whether I had, and I said no, and she put the cup down and did not take it up again.

At seven names stand ready: Where I come from a child has one name and a pot has a crack you can see. Here they give seven and glaze over the crack. I have set both down and joined neither.

At the voice that called it was clean: A clean voice is a voice with nobody in it. I have heard one. I was on my knees at the time, and I have never been able to say what the words were.

At a voice from a former life: She says this of the ash, but she has never asked what is in the phial. I have asked, and was told that the poor here dream in other people’s voices and the rich buy not to.

At the space before the last refrain: The saying is not for the child. I asked whose it was and she gave me a date, which is an answer with nothing in it to argue with.

At the head and in the gutter

The wine at the head came up out of the paper itself and is the only thing here that entered without a hand. The yellow dust is off the burning winds and has worked into the fold, a southern thing in a book that has been north. The myrrh across the second leaf came from a phial set down while I was telling the child to sleep and it would not; I left the mark where it fell. The milky thumb in the gutter is mine and nobody will own it, which is right, since nobody in this house owns the milk either. The green bead came off a bowl chipped at the third hour when a woman leaned over to see the writing and put her cup down too hard on the rim; it rolled as far as the fold and I have not picked it out. The grey wool in the sewing is off the sleeve of the northern woman, caught when she reached to turn the leaf; she did not see it go and I have not told her. There is no ash left in the corners of a bowl-house to sweep up on a Last Day. They burn the slates instead, and then write new ones before the wine is cold.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The bowl keeps the wine. The house keeps the name.

The mills keep the child. Nobody keeps the account.

I asked the tally-clerk, the northern woman and the woman who carries the bowl down from the court, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written. The leaf goes back under the cradle mat. Tomorrow the bowl will go round at the third hour and the act will be announced and the women will carry it in and nobody will ask who paid, and at the third watch I shall say the same words into the same space, and a child who cannot yet say them will hear them for the fourth time. If an officer finds the leaf, let him take the verbs out. The next hand will put them back, and will hold the child with its ear to its collar, and will not look into its face while it says the second line.
