To Milla at the shrine in Low Kiln, who has begun to put her hand to the slate before she puts it to the child. From Wenna at Cold Acre, where the well has not frozen in thirty-one winters and nobody on the ridge has ever thought to say why.

I write on the back of a rubbing I took off a slate before the writing had set, so that you have the true hand and not a fair one. A fair copy is a thing a clerk makes. Do not rub the fold. The birth-water has eaten into it and it will come apart between your fingers if you rub it, and then you will have a clean piece of paper and nothing on it, which is what the Court would like every birth to end as.

You wrote to ask about the child who went worse on the third day. You wrote it down as a question about a child and I have read it three times and it is a question about a stone. I will answer the stone. The child will hear the answer over her mother’s shoulder and will not know she has heard it, which is the whole of our trade.

The stone

Put it at the middle and not at the ends. A warmed stone goes to the belly, under the wool and against the skin, and stays there while you count three breaths and then three more. The hands and feet are wrapped and left. They are not rubbed and they are not set to the fire, and if a neighbour tells you to hold the foot to the hearth you say that the house has spoken and carry the stone back to the middle, because a woman who spends her heat on the extremity has none left for the part that can keep it. The chill comes up from the ends and settles in the middle on the second or third day, and it is the second chill and not a sickness and not a thing to be entered. Call it that where the mother can hear you and call it nothing where the father can. A name spoken in a room becomes a figure by supper in this country.

I have had thirty-one winters at this well and I have never once seen a stone that was too warm for a child and four that were too cold. A stone taken fresh from the fire will burn a belly as easily as it will burn a hand, so hold it to your own wrist before you put it to the child, the way you would taste a spoon of broth before you give it. The wrist knows. The eye does not.

The three days, as I keep them

What is done to the child — What goes on the slate — What must not go on it

The first day — The stone to the belly; the child to the breast; the birth-flower covered — The house, the day, the hour before first grey — A name you would keep

The second day — Wrap and leave the cold ends; count the breaths; do not rub — The day again, in the same hand — What the child said in the night

The third day — Keep the stone if the chill came; give the child over — The name if the mother has given it; else the date and no more — The chill, the stone, the neighbour’s advice

A birth is entered within three days, which is a rule the College wrote and the hearth-maids keep because it was ours before it was theirs. A hand goes to the slate before it goes to the child, and a hand that goes the other way has already decided what the slate will say and will spend the rest of the evening making the child agree with it. Write the day first. The face will tell you the rest in its own time and will keep telling you for a year.

Alda Reed crossed two fingers over a birth-flower on the seventh day of Stone and would not say the word for it. I have crossed two fingers over nine children in this room and never once told a person what I saw under them, and I am not telling you now, though you are my maid and have a right to the practical part. Cross the fingers. Say nothing. The day you tell somebody what you saw is the day it gets written down, and a thing written down in this country does not stay a thing you saw. It becomes a figure in a column, and the column goes up the road.

The man with the tube

He will come before the three days are out if the house has owed, which in Low Kiln is most houses. He takes the mother’s attention first, and that is a kindness the Court has never entered, because a woman who is being asked for her warmth will look at the man asking and not at the child, and the child is left to the stone and to you. Give him the mother and keep the child behind the slate. If he asks what the stone is for, tell him it is for the warmth. It is for the warmth. That is a true answer and has no figure in it and he cannot put it on a slate without putting a warmth on a slate, which no man does while he is standing in the room where the warmth is.

Do not say no to him. We do not say no. We put the pot between the child and the door and we talk to the pot about the weather until he has gone back over the bridge, and the pot has never once been asked what it held. If he leaves a pale ring on the mother’s wrist, do not rub it and do not cover it before he has gone. Cover it after. A thing covered too soon looks like a thing hidden, and a thing hidden is a thing that will be asked about twice.

The washing verse

Sung low to the stone, with the child at the belly and the wool drawn up. Never across the room. The second verse is the one my mother sang and I have set it under yours so you can see where the words change.

Stone from the hearth and wool from the ewe,

the second chill comes through and through;

warm the middle and wrap the end,

and the house will keep what the house can mend.

Four at the well and one gone dry,

a hand on the slate before the eye;

the name goes down and the face comes up,

and the stone keeps warm in the old tin’s cup.

Wash the pot and leave the ring,

the slate takes one and the child takes nothing;

cross two fingers and hold your tongue,

and the count comes back to the one it came from.

I keep a stone from every birth in a tin under the bench, thirty-one of them and not one marked. A clerk would look at that tin and call it a count and ask me for the figures, and when I gave him none he would write unentered residue above it and go away satisfied, because a figure with nobody in it satisfies that kind of man more than a figure with a person in it. It is not a count. It is a tin of stones. If you keep such a thing, keep it where the light does not fall on it and never put a mark on any stone, for a marked stone is a slate and a slate can be read by anybody who can hold it.

In the margin, in a younger hand, set close to the lines

It was the second day, not the third. I have struck your three through and put two underneath in your own iron-gall so that you cannot say a stranger wrote it. The child was cold in one foot and I wrapped it and did not rub it and did not set it to the fire, and the foot stayed dark until the morning and then came back of itself while I was counting breaths at the belly. I set the stone to my own wrist first, as you say, and it was too hot the first time and I waited. I did not cross two fingers over the mark on her skin because I did not know the sign was mine to make, and nobody at the shrine has told me whose it is. Tell me what to do when the man with the tube comes, because I do not know whether to give him the mother or the child and I cannot ask the mother in front of him and I will not ask him. I have kept the stone. It is under the bench and it is not marked.

What the Court hand did

A clerk of the Court’s table borrowed the leaf on a market morning, being a man who cannot leave paper lie. He has a cuff gone soft from washing and the ink sits in two fingers of it and will not come out. Against the second chill he set a common event; no entry. Against do not rub the foot he wrote no remedy applied, which is true and cold and leaves out the woman who did the not rubbing. Against give him the mother and keep the child behind the slate he set the measure is drawn as provided. Against the tin of stones he wrote residue unentered. At the foot of the count he put a round nought and wrote no vacancy, where I had written that the child came back of herself while I counted.

He takes the verbs out of a thing and leaves the figures standing, as though a thing always true had no need of anybody to do it. He can enter the chill as common and the stone as residue and the ring as a thing provided, because all of those have a place in his book. He cannot enter the wrist that tested the heat or the fingers that stayed crossed, and he has not tried. I have put my words back underneath and left his standing above, where they belong, the way the Ash is left in the corner until the morning after the Last Day and called dealt with.

At the head and in the gutter

The wax at the head is off the shrine candle I held over the rubbing while the ink was still wet; it went into the grain in a drop and no knife takes it out clean. The slip came off the hand that held the slate down, and it is the fine grey slip from the upper pit and not the coarse, which is why it stayed in the fold instead of falling through. The brown in the fold is birth-water and has eaten two words off the back; I have not written them in again, because a verse with two words gone is a verse said slowly and all of ours are said slowly. The grey in the gutter came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying; I have not swept it and there is no Last Day in a slate-box. The curl of grey wool through the tie-hole is mine and I tied it there for the word I could not put down, and I will not untie it. The tallow is off the lamp I kept low so that the mother would not know I was writing and change the account for me. The tideline is well-water, from the pail set down on the lower leaf while I was telling Milla about the stone, and it has taken the corners off two lines without taking the sense.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The stone keeps the belly and not the name; the slate keeps the name and not the cold. Between the two a woman carries the child, and sets it down, and goes to the next room.

I asked the woman who brings the well-water, a potter’s wife come up to have her mark confirmed, and a man who reads the notices at the Cross, and had three answers and no hand, as I have had before about the grey print and the two words gone. I have left the line where it was written. The leaf goes back into the slate-box with the slates that have been answered. Tomorrow there will be another child up the ridge and I will put a stone in the tin and not mark it, and Milla will put her hand to the slate before she puts it to the child, and neither of us will call the tin an account.
