I am a hearth-maid of the Clay Yard and I have caught children in it for twenty-six years. Nobody asked me for this book. I write it because a girl came to me in the lean week with a foot gone dark inside three turns of wool and asked me what I did for the second chill, and I heard myself answer her the way my mother answered me, and did not know whether I believed it. A remedy you have to say in your mother’s voice is a remedy that has not been looked at in a long while. I have looked at some of mine here. I have left the others standing, because a woman at three in the morning wants a hand more than she wants a correction.

I write on the back of a glaze-house tally that a fall of slip spoiled. The figures went before anyone could price them, which is a mercy of a kind: a figure that will not read cannot be entered. The tallow at the head is mine. The grey in the gutter came off my cuff when I measured a corner in a house two doors down and came back to write. I have not swept it off. The district burns its grey on the morning after the Last Day and calls it dealt with. I have kept mine, which is the only part of this book I have not copied forward.

The order of a birth-room

Written small, with one word gone over in slip.

  • Set the slate face down before you go to the child. I have no slate-name and never shall; a midwife who writes the child before she looks at it has written the wrong one already, and a wrong one is hard to get off a slate.
  • Keep the door latched but not held. A woman who is watched through the door will hold her breath, and a held breath is a poor thing to bring a child on.
  • Put the stone in wool at the small of the back, never at the feet. Try it on your own wrist first. The stone takes from somebody’s fire, and somebody in the terraces paid for that fire.
  • Do not wash the red branch. Cross two fingers over it and say the word the mother gives you, and no other.
  • When the second chill comes, count with her. Do not count for her.

I asked a woman of the Weir what the red branch meant and she crossed two fingers and gave me a date. A date is an answer that does not spoil in the mouth. I have put the date in the margin and left the question standing, which is where it was when I asked it.

The remedies, as they are used

Trouble — What is set to it — The manner of it — Who goes thin

The second chill — Warmth, barley water, a watch kept to the fourth day — It comes after the warming, not before. We walk her, believing a moving woman cannot go cold. A physician come down from Greybank says a body made warm spends faster and that walking spends it quicker still; he has seen a woman go stiller by evening and be gone by morning. I have seen the same and I have walked her. Both stand. — The household, one night’s sleep and, sometimes, the woman.

The sweet sickness (the line at the gum) — A spoon of sour barley-water; the gum rubbed with a pinch of wood-ash — The glaze girls taste the mix to get the colour, and the sweetness answers sweetness, we say. The physician says it is lead in the glaze and that sour water does nothing but trouble the teeth. I have seen the pale line go off a girl who left the glaze room and come back on one who stayed. I have not seen the lead. — The glaze room, which cannot stop and does not ask.

A kiln-burn — Tallow first, then a clean fold; a damper-key laid across it to draw the heat — We say the iron draws. The physician says cold iron takes the measure as well as the heat, and that a burn can lose a hand by being made cold. I have set the key on a burn and watched the skin below it go the colour of old rope. I have not done it a second time. — The burn, and the hand if the iron stays.

Milk that will not come — Another woman to hold the child while the mother sleeps; clay-water to cool the breast — The favour is paid out of the other woman’s own house, and I say so before I ask it. The physician says clay-water dries the milk and feeds nobody. I have given it and watched a child cry over a dry breast. I have not given it since the third such child. — The other woman’s child, first.

The birth-flower — Nothing. Two fingers crossed, and the word the mother gives — I have rubbed one with salt and one with slip and one with nothing, and the branch kept its shape in all three. A girl of the terraces told me her mother washed one and the child went flat by evening, and I have set that beside the three and not joined it, because a flower staying and a child going flat are two things in one room. — Nobody knows. The child’s skin, perhaps.

The cold that stays (a foot out of the Slip Brook) — Wool, a stone at the belly, and work to keep the woman moving — The extremity goes first and the heart holds out, and there is an hour on the far side of the warming when she gets worse. I have no name for that hour and have left the place where the name would stand the length a name of that kind wants. We walk her and set her to work. I have seen a foot go dark inside three turns of wool through a whole lean week and have gone on winding the wool. — The woman. This is the row where I have been least honest.

A cord that will not fall — Wait. A hand on the belly and a hand kept still — A midwife of the Weir says never pull, and I have pulled once and had the cord come away with more than the cord. I have not written what came with it, because a thing with no name in Grath is a thing a clerk will put a figure to if you leave it lying. — The woman, in the lying-in.

A child that comes back with another house’s want — The name nine times at the door; a bowl of water under the bed; threshold clay rubbed at the wrist — She calls her mother by a name not given in this house and reaches for a door that is not in the room. We give the name back nine times and set water where the old water stood. The physician says a spoken name puts no shape back, and he is right as a man is right who has never held one of these children. One went off it by the ninth speaking and one kept it a year. I have entered neither in the birth-slate. — Nobody. The house, in a year it cannot spend.

A cut from a pot-shard — Tallow first, then a clean fold; never the grey — The women of the yard sprinkle Ash into a cut to dry it, believing the grey takes the wet. I have dressed three such cuts and found the grey under the skin in all three when they came back to me, and have not been able to get it out. That is as much as I know and as much as I shall write. — The cut.

Nothing in this book is free. That is the only thing I will stand behind.

The second chill

The second chill comes on the third night, after the warm has come back into the room. The district calls it the child’s own work and says it goes off by the fourth morning. I have said that for twenty-six years. A body that has been warmed gives heat away faster than one never warmed, and the hour on the far side of the warming is the hour to be afraid of. I know it and I still walk her.

The physician told me to hold her instead and count with her, and not set her on her feet till the fourth day. I have held two women and walked twenty. Both were up on the fourth morning. Of the twenty, three went thin and one did not get up. Three and one are not a number a district will let you use: two is a coincidence, twenty is a habit, and a habit is what the district calls an answer. I have left the place where the count should stand, the length a count of that kind wants.

The note under the thumb

Tap a sound cup with the knuckle and it answers full. Tap a cracked one and it answers flat, a small dead sound that stops at the surface and will not go through. The kiln-masters keep this to themselves, which is their trade, and I have borrowed it from the rack where the cups dry.

I do not tap a woman to hear her. I did once, on a girl gone thin after a return, and the flat I thought I heard was not in her. A body is not a cup. But a house is: I can tell by the note in the pot on the bench whether the room held its warm through the night, and set the stone by that and not by a clock, for there is no clock in Low Kiln and no Court figure that will tell you what a pot knows.

The watch-song

Sung low at the third night, to the woman and not to the child. The room says the last line with her.

The coals are banked, the water thin,

the child is come, the door shut fast;

hold the wrist and count the breath

and leave the place where two should be.

The red branch keeps what the water takes,

the name comes late, the figure soon;

a slate is a stone with a neat hand on it

and a hand is a thing that can go cold.

The chill comes after the warm is back,

it is not the water, it is not the night;

walk her soft, or hold her still ---

the fourth morning knows which one was right.

Sing to the coals, sing to the child,

sing to the woman counting under wool;

what comes back is not what went,

and comes back as it can.

I have changed one line in thirty years. I sang walk her soft and now sing hold her still, and the women singing with me have not noticed, which is how I know it was needed. A line said over a hundred births belongs to the room, not to me.

What the borrowed hands did

The physician came down from Greybank in Ember of Y.H. 811 with a book that had the word lead in it and a hand that wrote in the margin without asking. He set a common settling over my second chill and struck the nine speakings of the name and wrote no efficacy above them, then went off with a purse. I have put the nine back underneath. He was right about the lead and wrong about the name, and I have kept both sentences so the next reader can tell which is which.

A clerk of the College borrowed the leaf when it went to Greybank in Ilmen. He struck out my column headed Who goes thin and wrote No cost entered above it, which is a true thing in a dishonest place. Against the space where I had left the name of what a returned child wants, he put a round nought and wrote vacancy beneath it. A nought is a figure and can be entered; a want cannot. He took the verbs out of the child comes when the kiln does and wrote the child is come when the kiln is above, saying a thing always true has no need of a verb. I have put comes back underneath. A clerk can speak without verbs because he is never in the room; a woman at three in the morning wants to know whether the child is coming now.

At the head and in the gutter

The tallow at the head came off the lamp I held low while a woman was saying something I wanted the end of. The grey is off my cuff. The brick dust in the fold is off the rack where the cups dry and is the only thing in this book put there by the trade and not by a person. The wet slip took two words clean off the second leaf, and I have not written them back: a remedy with two words gone is a remedy said slowly, and everything here is said slowly. The ring of barley-water came from a cup set down while I was telling a girl to drink and she would not. The scorch is a spark off the kiln that went out under my hand, and I have not entered the burn. The hair in the seam is the colour of wet wool and is not mine, and I have left it where it caught.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The slate keeps the name. The clay keeps the print.

The warm that came between them is kept in no house.

I asked a physician, a clerk of the College, and a woman of the Weir, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The cloth goes back over the book in my apron. Tomorrow a girl will come to the door with a foot gone dark or a child that will not come, and I shall put the verbs back where the clerk took them out, and set the slate face down before I look at the child, because a child is not an account and a woman at three in the morning has a hand that wants holding.
