I work the windlass at the mouth. Two and thirty years I have let men down and drawn them up, and never once asked a man his name before I had him by the rope. The rope comes first. After that there is time enough for the slate.

Below the floor they work the stone with men bought for a term, condemned men and bought breath, whom the Grathic buyers call under-vessed. Their lives are reckoned at eleven months, a year with the month of the buying taken off. I asked the mine-reeve once what became of a man at the end of the eleven and he said the term was expiring, a phrase that lets a man go on writing. The men say other things. A saying said at the weigh goes up with the figure and is not entered, and the figure goes up alone.

Keldish is cut in bone and does not travel, and the men below speak four tongues besides. I have put everything in Grathic letters and left a southern word standing where ours would not come. Say these to the rope, the iron, the lamp, the floor, never to a man who is standing in front of a slate. Said looking at a buyer, a sentence becomes a petition, and there is no petition in any of them.

Of the eleven months

  1. “They buy eleven months and call it a year. The month of the buying is the one you keep.”

Gloss — A life bought for the deep is entered at eleven, and a man who lasts fourteen is entered at eleven still, because the figure is for the life bought and not the life lived. The month taken off at the buying is the only one he keeps, and no slate has found where he put it.

  1. “A month paid for before you warm it is a month already gone.”

Gloss — Said of advances, of grain, and of a man given his term in the morning and sent down before he has eaten. The buyers below do not cheat by false figures. They cheat by early ones.

  1. “A term is a rope with a knot at the far end. You cannot see the knot from this side.”

Gloss — I have watched a man count his months on one hand and keep the other shut. When the mine-reeve says a term is expiring, he is not lying; it is the only word the roll has for a thing that ends.

  1. “Eleven in the slate and one in the mouth. The one in the mouth is not entered.”

Gloss — The twelfth is a man’s own because nobody thought to ask for it. It is spent saying the sayings, which is perhaps why the sayings matter to them.

In the narrow upright hand, written above this one: Term eleven. Value retained.

Underneath, in mine: He has taken the buying and the keeping out and put a value where a month stood. The value travels well. The month stays where the man said it.

Of the rope and the count

  1. “The rope asks your weight. It does not ask your name.”

Gloss — At the weigh the name goes down on a slate and the body goes down on the rope, and the two arrive in different places. A man who steps off the board is counted by whatever the board says.

  1. “Give your number before your name if you have one. If you have none, give the month.”

Gloss — A free Keldishman states what he holds before he says who he is, and is sent back to the foot of the post if he forgets. A bought man has no number to give, his measure being held in law by the man who paid for him, so he gives the term. The same courtesy, at a higher price.

In the narrow upright hand: No measure arises.

In mine: True in his hand. In this one the man is standing here, which is a thing his sentence has no room for.

  1. “A clay-name is a pot with another man’s mark fired into it.”

Gloss — A bought hand is named by the house that holds him and the old name is forbidden. I have heard a man answer to a name the way a pot answers flat: the sound comes, and nothing is behind it. A southern man said the forbidden name is written inside a bowl that the slave is never allowed to look into. I have left his word standing.

  1. “Ask the arm that carried it. The arm knows the weight and not the buyer.”

Gloss — This one came up from the reed country and has been said in the deep ever since. An arm that has carried a shift knows what went into it and nothing of the man who sold the season. An honest answer, useless at a weigh.

Of the iron and the stone

  1. “Cold iron takes from a bought man and takes from a free man. It does not ask which account to enter.”

Gloss — The tube, the clamp, the blade across a fever: one iron, one hunger. A free man’s loss goes into a slate. A bought man’s goes nowhere, there being no account of his to carry it, which is the cheapest way of taking a thing there is.

  1. “A clamp on a free hand is a trade. A clamp on a bought hand is holding.”

Gloss — The same iron, the same bite, the same fortnight in which the arm is of no use. The free man has chosen it and the bought man has not, and the floor calls both by one name. Nobody on the floor has ever corrected that sentence.

  1. “Nine clamps go to the floor. The tenth goes to the stone. We are the tenth.”

Gloss — The tenth hangs behind the board and is not taken down, and no man below will say what it does at the stone. The bought hands say this of themselves. The masters do not agree and do not forbid it, which is how a floor keeps two readings of one thing.

In a clamp-master’s coal, the letters wide apart as though cut with a chisel: A bought man is not a floor man. Keep him off my board. I have had one die on it and will not have another.

Underneath, in mine: He is right about the board and wrong about the men. They were on it before he was. I have left the line where he put it.

  1. “The lamp is paid for out of somebody’s month. Do not warm your hands at it.”

Gloss — A warm-stone takes from the lamp’s account and somebody down the shaft paid for the lamp. A bought man has no account to pay with, so he warms at another man’s cost and knows it. Said to the lamp, not to the man holding it.

  1. “When the lead smells sweet, the mountain has made up its mind already.”

Gloss — In the lower galleries the Warm comes up through the stone in a lethal flood and the only warning is the smell of hot lead. A man who has smelt it does not say the saying again.

  1. “The stone coughs and the man coughs. The stone is not asked to stop.”

Gloss — The dust of the Ninth gets into a man and he brings it up in the morning in a colour he will not discuss. The leech steams it under a cloth and sends him back to the same gallery.

Of who pays and who is named

  1. “Sere buys a man whole. Keld buys him by the month. Both pay somebody else.”

Gloss — Crowns come north with the mercenaries and go south again with them, and a crown will not buy a month in this mountain. The southern buyers want the shape of a life; the northern buyers want eleven pieces of one. Neither asks the man which he would rather be.

  1. “A Fen man sells a season. A Keldishman buys one. Neither asks the man what a season is.”

Gloss — Bought breath is common in the Fenmarch, a term of a season or a year to repay a peat-debt, and the hook in it is in the mouth so a man cannot say the word he would need to complain. I have had Fenny men down here counting by the drying of peat and northern men counting by the swell of a hand.

  1. “The clay man says one in twelve. The stone man says the number first. The bought man says yes.”

Gloss — This is the dry joke of the shaft mouth and nobody laughs at it twice. The printed figure belongs to the low countries, the number cut in the arm belongs to Keld, and the bought man has neither. He has the yes, which is the only thing of his that is never entered.

In the narrow upright hand: Consent is recorded.

In mine: He has entered the yes and left out the man who had no other word. That is the whole talent of that hand.

Of the dead who stay down

  1. “They wall the dead in facing the deep seam and add a number to a slate nobody reads aloud.”

Gloss — There is no drawing and no pouring below the Keep. The mountain keeps its own, the surveyors say, and the figure goes into a book turned face down. A bought man says the mountain keeps its own and does not keep him, which is the same sentence turned over.

  1. “No return for a man who was never banked. The stone has him and the slate has a figure.”

Gloss — The dead of the deep are not brought up for the Weighing. Somewhere above a household may be paid for a life it never saw; I have never asked who. The stone has the better claim of the two.

  1. “A man goes down by the rope and comes up by the count. There is a third way and nobody speaks of it.”

Gloss — I know the two ways of this windlass. The men below know a third and have a sentence for it, but they say it only when the lamp is out, and I have not set it here. A saying that needs the dark to be said is not a saying for a book.

The month-song

Said low at the weigh, going down and not coming up, by men whose term has less than a tend in it. The place after the last line is the length of a name, and no singer has filled it.

One for the month they paid and one for the month they kept,

the rope goes down and the rope comes up and neither knows a name;

nine on the board and one at the stone,

and the tenth is the one that stays below.

Eleven in the slate and one in the teeth,

the cold comes up and the warm goes down;

ask the lamp who paid --- it will not say,

and the hand that held it knows.

We give no number, we give no name,

we give the month we have left to stand;

the deep seam keeps what the deep seam takes,

and the figure goes up where the man does not.

[a place the length of a name]

Gloss — The place is left because a name said below travels up the rope and arrives at a slate, which is a journey no bought name is permitted to make. A survey-clerk of the Court put a round nought in it and wrote vacancy underneath. A nought is a number. He has made a month into a figure and a name into a hole, and both travel well and arrive empty.

What the borrowed hands did

A survey-clerk borrowed the leaves at Greybank with ink settled in two fingers of his soft cuff. He strikes the doer out as though a doer were a thing a man could spill. At the month of the buying is the one you keep he set term: eleven; value retained. At we give no number he wrote no measure arises. At the bought man says yes, consent is recorded, the truest and coldest sentence on the leaf.

The mine-reeve set a red cross against the eleven, meaning the figure has been checked and is to stand. It does not mean the man stood it. The clamp-master’s coal is the only hand down here that argues with the floor instead of the paper. I have set it above the saying and mine under it, as a board keeps two weights and chooses neither.

At the head and in the gutter

The smut at the head is coal off the sleeve I wipe the rope with, and no wet sleeve takes it out. The lead bead in the fold rolled out of the Ninth Gallery and was laid here by a man who went away without it. The filings in the gutter are off the clamps. The shaving kept its heat through one night on the stone floor and is cold now, which is the order of things here. The frost was a feather on the well lip and was gone by the time I found the leaf. The tallow thumb is mine, from holding the tally down in the draught up the shaft. The grey in the gutter came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying in a room where men are counted; I have not swept it, and there is no Last Day under the Keep. The knife-cut is where a word would not come, and a notch is still there when paper has gone soft.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

They bought the month. They did not buy the sentence.

The rope carries the weight. The saying carries the name.

I asked a man with a month left, a surveyor come down from the stones, and the master of the floor, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written. The leaves go back into the rope-glove and the glove on its nail beside the windlass. Tomorrow the carts come up with the winter’s lead, and a man will give me a month where another man would give a number, and I shall write down what he gives and not ask him which he means.
