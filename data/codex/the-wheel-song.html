I throw at the wheel in the shed beside the Slip Brook, and I have done it eleven seasons. A lump goes down on the board, the foot takes the fly, and the wheel has a note before the clay has one. There is a round on the board left by the last pot, wet when the pot came off and dry by morning. We do not wipe it. We set the next lump down where the last one stood, and the board remembers what the hand has forgotten.

A man came up from Greybank with a soft cuff and ink settled in two fingers of it and stood in the shed door asking what the women sang at the wheel. I told him. He wrote it down before I had finished, with the doing taken out and a figure put where a place should stand, and went off over the north bridge with his boots clean. So I have set the words down as we say them, to the wheel and not to the women beside it, and put the verbs back where he took them out.

The slip at the head is mine. I held the tally down with the hand I throw with and did not know I had marked it. Slip goes into the grain and no washing takes it out, which is why we sign with it here and not with ink.

How it is thrown

  • Set the lump on the round the last pot left, not beside it. A board wiped clean is a board with no memory, and the first pot off a clean board goes flat under the knuckle, which the pot pays for and the thrower carries.
  • Let the wheel take the lump true before the hands go in. A lump that runs crooked will make a crooked wall, and a crooked wall makes a rim that opens in the firing; the fire finds what the hand let go, and it does not ask who let it.
  • Draw with two hands and let the clay come up between them. You do not pull the wall; you hold the space where the wheel puts it. A hand that pulls makes a thin side, and a thin side is a lie the clay tells the fire.
  • Keep the thumb on the wall as it rises, and keep the wall even to the thumb. The thumb is the only measure in this shed that nobody can carry up the road.
  • At the third draw bring the lip round and quiet. A lip left proud is a lip that will crack, and a crack in the rim is a room the pot did not ask anybody into.
  • Knuckle the rim before the thread goes under the foot. A whole one answers full and the note goes through; a hollow stops at the skin and gives back flat. The rack takes both and does not say which was which, so the knuckle has to say it first.
  • Cut the pot off with the thread drawn under the foot, and leave the round standing on the board. The round is not a pot and has never been entered. Count the draws going up, never the pots coming down off the rack; a count coming down is a count of empty racks, which a clerk could enter and nobody else wants.
  • Do not sing it at the Cross, and do not sing it on an Account-day. The bell goes four, four and a gap, and a throwing-song set against that gap comes home wrong in the wrist.
  • The last part is kept back. It is for when the kiln-master has gone up the terrace and the man with the soft cuff has gone back over the north bridge and the shed can hear itself again.

The song

Said low into the wheel, with the foot steady on the fly. One verse to one draw of the wall. The fourth is not a draw; it is the thumb coming away, and it is not said aloud. The refrain goes into the board and not across the shed; said to a person it becomes an account, and there is enough account at the Cross without bringing it into the shed.

The lump goes down where the last one stood,

on the wet round the board kept from the pot;

don’t wipe the round, center on it,

and the wheel takes up what the hand put there.

Draw it and lift it, three to the rim,

and the fourth is the thumb coming clean away;

the pot goes up to the rack and the round stays here,

and the note in the rim is the note the clay came with.

At the third draw the lip comes round and quiet

and the collar sits where the thumb left it;

a lip stood proud is a lip cracked in the firing,

and the fire finds it though nobody set it.

Draw it and lift it, three to the rim,

and the fourth is the thumb coming clean away;

the pot goes up to the rack and the round stays here,

and the note in the rim is the note the clay came with.

Knuckle the rim before the thread goes under;

a whole one answers and the note goes through;

a hollow stops at the skin and gives back flat,

and the rack takes both and doesn’t tell which.

Draw it and lift it, three to the rim,

and the fourth is the thumb coming clean away;

the pot goes up to the rack and the round stays here,

and the note in the rim is the note the clay came with.

A thread under the foot and the pot comes away,

and the round sits down where the pot stood;

the next lump goes on the very same round,

and the board keeps count of every one.

Draw it and lift it, three to the rim,

and the fourth is the thumb coming clean away;

the pot goes up to the rack and the round stays here,

and the note in the rim is the note the clay came with.

One in twelve is printed and read at the Cross,

but the round on the board isn’t entered;

the pot goes up and the figure goes up,

and the clay the pot left on the board stays here.

Draw it and lift it, three to the rim,

and the fourth is the thumb coming clean away;

the pot goes up to the rack and the round stays here,

and the note in the rim is the note the clay came with.

There is a space before the last refrain, the length of a thumb laid flat on the round where you feel for the seam before you lift the pot. I have left it on the tally. A clerk put a round nought in it once, a thing you can hold up and look at; a thumb laid flat is not.

The part they keep back

Not said while the kiln-master stands in the shed door, and not while a man with a satchel is on the boards. Said to the board and not across the wheel.

Three to the rim and the fourth on the board.

The pot goes up and the ring stays down.

We don’t show the round to a man with a slate;

he puts a nought in it and carries it off.

There is no verse after it. The next thing is the lump going down on the round and the foot taking the fly, which is not a verse and has never wanted one.

What the kiln-master did

He came up from the terrace on the wrong wind with the sweet edge of the glaze at one corner of his mouth and read the tally with one eye on the drying racks. Against the rack takes both and doesn’t tell which he wrote in broad charcoal: Unsound ware rejected before firing. No loss arises. He underlined it twice. Against the round on the board isn’t entered he set: Board residue; no entry lies. He did not look at the board while he wrote it.

Underneath, in mine: he is right about the board and wrong about the loss. What is left standing is not nothing even when it is not entered, and a board without its own round is a board that has forgotten every pot it held, which is the whole memory of this shed and the one thing he cannot carry up the terrace.

What the clerk did

The factor’s clerk borrowed the leaf at Greybank on a morning when the tile was being weighed, with his cuff gone soft from washing and the ink sitting in two fingers of it. He took the verbs out and set his words above mine, the way a face sets itself above an old repair, and I have left his hand standing and put ours underneath.

  • At a hollow stops at the skin and gives back flat, he wrote vessel unsound; not a chargeable defect, and underlined it twice. He thinks a flat note is a pot that has reached its term. In this shed a flat note is a lump set down wrong and the board told nothing about it.
  • At the note in the rim is the note the clay came with, he wrote ware conforms to the sampled body, which is true of a figure and false of a pot you have held under the knuckle while the wheel was still turning.
  • At the round sits down where the pot stood, he wrote no material of value remaining, and drew a line under it that went a finger’s breadth past the margin. The round is not material and has never had a value. It has had eleven seasons of pots on it, which is a count of another kind.
  • At one in twelve is printed and read at the Cross, he wrote deduction current, one part in twelve, and set the printed figure over it. He has entered the number that was read and not the number that was paid, which is the whole distance between a cuff and a wrist.
  • Where I had left the space before the last refrain, he put a round nought and wrote vacancy under it. A nought is a number and travels. A thumb laid flat is a place, and stays where it was put.

What the woman at the drying racks put in the margin

The thick charcoal belongs to a woman who sets the pots up rim-down and has carried them past this wheel since before the Glaze Wars. I have copied it as it stands, without making the hands agree.

At don’t wipe the round, center on it: A girl wiped the board clean one morning and threw six pots and every one went flat. She did not know she had wiped away the round, and the board did not tell her. I have not told her since, and she still throws at that wheel.

At knuckle the rim before the thread goes under: I asked the pot with the hairline, the one that comes back every firing, and it gave back full. I set it up on the rack all the same. I have not told the kiln-master and I have not set it by the wall, which is a thing I will answer for somewhere else.

At the part they keep back: Every yard keeps a fourth. The bank keeps a cut, the stairs keep a step, and here we keep the round. I have never asked what number the round would have if it had one, because it is the only thing in this yard the slate has never wanted.

At the head and in the gutter

The slip thumb at the head is mine and has gone into the grain; there is no Last Day at a wheel and nothing here gets gathered and burnt. The pale gutter-line came out of the Brook and has taken the edge off one word of the fifth verse; I have not written it back, because a word the weather has taken is said slowly and everything in this shed is said slowly. The curl of eel-sinew caught across the second leaf came off the cutting thread; I have left it lying over the word it landed on. The grey flake in the fold came off the round under the pot and is lighter in the hand than a good flake, which is the whole of the lesson and no use at all to a clerk. The damp round on the third leaf is a wet pot’s foot set down before the pot went to the rack, dried to the colour of the board. The green bead came off a girl with sweetness still at her mouth who touched the leaf and went back to the slip; I have not washed it. The sour ring is small ale set down at the first verse and taken up at the last. The grey along the outer fold came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying on a wheel. Nobody will own it. There is no sweeping here; the grey goes into the next season under the dust-cloth.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The wheel keeps the round. The rack keeps the ware.

The pot goes up the road. The ring stays here.

I asked the woman at the drying racks, the clerk, and a carter waiting with his horse at the yard gate, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The tally goes back under the throw-board. Tomorrow a lump will go down on the same round, three draws and the fourth where the board put it, and if the clerk comes again with his soft cuff he may have the nought. He cannot have the round, because it is a place, and he has no word for a place that stays where it was set down.
