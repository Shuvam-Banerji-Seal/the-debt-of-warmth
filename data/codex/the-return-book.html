I keep the return-slate in the lower room at Greybank. A collector draws in the districts and the factor enters the figure above, and what comes down to me is a measure with nowhere left in it to stay. I set it into a new vessel and write what came back. I do not carry the tube. I do not sit by the dying. My hand is the last one the measure passes through before it is called a return, and the only hand in this house that writes down what the return did when it arrived.

The rolls of the dead are carried forward here and never closed. A roll that closes is a roll a woman can ask about, and a roll a woman can ask about is a roll that has to be answered. Ours stays open. There is a kind of mercy in a thing nobody finishes.

Twelve copper bits to a silver mark; twenty silver marks to a Sere crown, which comes north with the factor and goes south when he does. The Court books an ember at five marks and no man in this room has ever traded one. One in twelve is printed on the notice at Sill and read twice at the Cross. I write the figure the notice gives and the figure the cradle gave, and I have never once made them agree, which is the whole work of this book.

In the narrow upright hand of a clerk come down from the College: Return entered. Underneath, in mine: He has taken the pouring out of the sentence. A measure may be returned by anybody and to nobody; a man pours it with his hands and the hands get cold. I have put I set it in back beneath his words and left his words standing over it.

The order of the pour at this bank

Copied from the notice that hangs over the return-slate. The words in the second column are mine and are not read at the Weighing.

Step — As the notice has it — As it stands in the lower room

The measure — Drawn by the collector, banked, account stands — Comes in on a slate with grey at the head and a name that is not the vessel’s

The deduction — One in twelve, as printed — Read twice. What the district pays is not what the district hears

Whole return — One measure into one vessel, with memory — Twelve hundred marks and a vessel named on the roll; the memory is not weighed here

Thin return — Many measures pooled, divided by lot — Thirty marks in labour, paid by the district; the lot decides which cradle

Beautiful return — A Sere physician’s word, entered as given — Calm, and wanting nothing. I have set the word down and put no gloss on it

The Kept — Held near the fire; spoken of with pity — No return due. They cannot want one, and a return is a thing wanted

The pouring — Completed when the figure is entered — Completed when the cradle takes. These are not the same hour

The Sere word is not ours and I did not learn it in Grath. A factor’s man came up from the coast with it in his mouth and said a beautiful return is one that has ceased to want, which is a physician’s praise and not a banker’s. I have entered the phrase as it came and left the wanting where it stood. A clerk who explains a word has begun to write a sermon, and sermons get copied.

The leaf, as it stood in Ash

Figures in Ledgerhand. The remarks are mine, in the hand that held the cradle, and are not part of the account. A dash is a place where no pouring was made, not a nought.

Draws on this leaf: eight. Lines with no pouring in them: two. Figures struck: one. Columns struck: one.

The likeness column

I keep a place beside the figure for what the new vessel did with the warm after it was set in. The College has never thought to strike this column out of a bank book, which I take for an oversight and not a mercy, and so I keep what I please in it.

On Ash 21 the widow’s cradle took the warm and turned toward the door of the room where the figure had been read. On Ash 22 one of the two cradles went toward a pot before a pot was shown to it. On Ash 25 the girl’s cradle kept its mouth open in the way of a person tasting something and finding no word for it. On Ash 28 the whole return lay quiet and wanted nothing, and I have set quiet down, which is a word the Sere physician would like and which I will not let him have without a figure against it.

A likeness is not a figure and cannot be added. That is why the College struck the column when the clerk came down on Ash 30. He wrote No such column above it in the narrow upright hand, and he counted the rows before he wrote it, which is how I know the column troubled him. I have drawn the column back underneath and left his words standing over it. A man does not count what he has not looked at.

What the two borrowed hands did

The College man struck the verb out of the order of the pour, as he strikes every verb he can find, and set was returned above I set it in, and above that the measure was entered, which is further from the cradle than the first. I have put my hand back underneath both. A sentence with nobody in it is a sentence no woman can ask about, and women ask about these pourings in the end, always at the door, always after the slate is shut.

The factor’s clerk did two things. Against the vessel returned twice he struck my with memory and set whole above it, because whole is the word the notice prints and the word a house upriver will pay twelve hundred marks to hear. Both stand. At the foot of the leaf, where I had written the figure is smaller than the last two, he wrote figure corrected upward and underlined it twice. I have left his line where he put it and my line where I put it. Neither is a lie. They are two accounts of the same cradle, and the cradle is not in either of them.

The small square in the lower margin is where I scraped a word away with a knife on Ash 27, after the disputed house went off without its figure. I scraped it before I knew what I had written. I have not put the word back and have not forgotten it.

The song the return-women carry up the stair

Sung low on the stair from the lower room, never while the return-slate is open, and never twice in one house.

One for the tube and one for the slate,

one for the mark that the cradle will pay;

the figure goes down and the figure goes up

and the woman goes home with the day.

One in twelve as the notice says,

four and four as the bell is told;

they read us the sum in a clean voice

and we carry the rest in the cold.

Pour it in and shut the book,

ask the cradle what it knows;

it knows the hand and it knows the breast

and it knows the shape of the house it was told.

In the factor’s narrow hand, against the last line: No figure entered against the cradle. In mine: He is right, and the women hold the last line until they are out of the lower room, which is how I know the line matters.

In the head and in the gutter

The brown thumb at the head is iron-gall and mine, made by holding the quire open through a market morning; there are clerks in the Hearthseat who can tell an assessor by his thumb before he has spoken. The pale smear down the gutter is slip from a swaddling cloth, laid on the leaf while a woman held a cradle still with her whole arm and waited for me to finish. The tallow print at the foot came off the lamp I keep low so that the women at the stair will not know I am writing down the likenesses and change their words for me. The cold-iron shaving in the fold came out of the tube’s wool wrap when the collector set it on my counter and went away for a cup; there is always a little iron in the fold, and it has never once drawn the warm out of the paper, which is the only mercy this room has that I can name.

The grey wool came off a cradle blanket and goes on coming off, though I have shaken the book three times. The tide-line along the foot is the damp of the lower room, which gets into everything left on a bench, and into some things that are not left on benches. They sweep the corners of houses on the Last Day and burn what they find in the morning and call it dealt with. There is no Last Day in the lower room of a bank. The rolls of the dead are carried forward and never closed, and the Ash stays where it fell.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The bank counts what went in. The cradle counts what came back. The two counts never meet, and the College has no word for the space between.

I asked the factor’s clerk, a woman who carried a cradle up the stair, and a collector come down from the Clay Road, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written.

The book goes back under the return-slate. Tomorrow a collector will come in off the Clay Road with a figure from Sill, and the factor will enter it above, and I shall set the warm into a cradle and write down what the cradle did, and put my verb back where the College struck it out. Somewhere in Low Kiln a child who came back this year will go toward a cup before he has seen the cup, and no figure in this book will tell his mother why. The rolls stay open. The book stays in the cover. That is the whole of it, and it goes on.
