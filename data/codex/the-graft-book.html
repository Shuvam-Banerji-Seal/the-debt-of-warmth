I keep the graft-book. It lies in the back room behind the College under a warm-stone the size of a loaf, in a room that smells of hot lead and tallow and of nothing that was ever entered. The College is a clean building and writes in a clean hand. Behind it there is a bench with a cut in it the shape of a small hand, and a stone that is warm at dawn and warm at dusk and has never once been asked where it got the heat. I copy the articles in the hand the ordinance uses, which has no person in it, and then set the person back underneath in mine. A law with nobody in it can be read at a Weighing. A law with somebody in it can be asked about, and then answered.

The ordinance condemns what this room does. That is the arrangement: the law says a blent person has the measure of the vessel its donor had, and the room goes on taking walls from beasts and setting them over the breaks of men, and both are written in the same neat hand. In nine years I have never entered a figure for a blent person that any bank would take. I enter the donor’s figure instead. It is small. A beast’s measure is small and simple, which the ordinance says elsewhere and here says nothing at all.

At the head of the first leaf, in mine, written very small: Copied in the room and for the room. The verbs are mine; their absence is not. If this leaf is read in the Weighing Hall, let it be seen that both hands were here and neither of them left.

Title the Seventh, as it stands behind the College

From the Ordinances of the Keeping, Title the Seventh: Of Blent-Work, the Grafting of Beast-Clay into Human Wall, and the Measure of the Donor. Set down in the Hearthseat after the Rising of Y.H. 668; added to in Y.H. 782 and in the Thin Year, Y.H. 812.

I. Of the thing

  1. Wall taken from a beast and set over the break of a person is blent-work. It is condemned in the Keeping. The doing is not entered; the thing done is entered.
  2. Blent-work is licensed in no district of the Keeping. It is done in the Bowl of Sere and is spoken of in the Hearthseat only behind the College.
  3. A graft takes best where the donor’s measure was shaped like the recipient’s. A graft takes anyway where it was not.

Margin: The room says the third article to men who come in with a wall failing and money under a cuff. It is true. A graft takes, and then the person wants what he did not want before. The room calls it the graft settling, which is the phrase the ordinance allows. I have entered the phrase. I have entered the wanting underneath it, in the margin, where there is no column and no figure.

Margin, in a narrow upright hand: Article 3 was added in Y.H. 782. I have set takes anyway above takes best, which leaves two articles where the College meant one. Both stand. A man who has stood at this bench knows which of the two he is paying for, and the College does not know that he knows.

II. Of the donor

  1. The blent person has the measure of the vessel its donor had. The donor is a beast. The figure is entered in embers.
  2. Where the donor is not entered, the figure is not entered. Where the figure is not entered, the account is not opened.
  3. The donor pays. Where the donor is a beast and cannot consent, consent is entered by the hand of the officer.

Margin: I have entered three such figures. Each was the donor’s, and each came to the same thing: three embers, four at the most, pressed into a man who walked out of this room with a wall that held. A man with three embers has no standing at any Weighing and no figure a bank will take. The ordinance gives him the donor’s measure. I have given him the donor’s figure. It is the same gift twice over and neither of them keeps him warm.

Margin: The officer enters consent for the beast and not for the man. The man is awake on the bench; the beast is in the yard with its throat cut; the hand that writes is the same for both. There is no word in this ordinance for the animal that did not agree, and I have not supplied one. A clerk who supplies a word has begun to write a sermon, and sermons get copied.

III. Of the figure and the reading

  1. One part in twelve of every measure banked is kept back for the Hearth, as the notice says. Where the measure is the donor’s, the deduction is entered against the donor.
  2. A blent person has no standing to complain. Where a thing is taken from a wall that cannot speak, the house in which it stands is complainant. The blent person is not the house.
  3. An account is never closed. The account of a blent person is carried forward at the donor’s figure.

Margin: I have read one in twelve in this room and one in twelve at the Cross, and the two are the same figure and not the same weight. At the Cross a woman hears it over the pigs. Here a man hears it with a hand inside his forearm and a bowl of slip on the bench beside him. I have entered neither hearing, which is how the ordinance stays true.

Margin, in a leaning hand with a little gold in it: Against article 7 a bowl-surgeon of the Ember house wrote a gift pays no deduction and underlined it twice. I have left it. In Sere they empty a bowl and call a debt forgiven; here a figure goes down and is carried forward forever. Both are ways of not paying, and neither is entered as one.

IV. Of faults and their correcting

The middle column is the figure. The last is mine and has no standing in any court in Sarn.

Fault — As entered — What the margin keeps — Not entered

Wall taken from a beast — Blent-work; the donor’s figure — The sound the donor made — Consent

A graft that takes and then wants — The graft settling — The want, in another shape — The life it wanted

A blent person brought into the Keeping — Figure of the donor; carried forward — The name it answered to — The man it had been

A figure asked for and not given — A place left standing — The hand that asked — The figure

A word cut from an article — Bare paper — The length of the word — The word

Margin: The last row is mine and has never been shown. The word I cut from the fourth article is the one this room uses for what comes back after a graft takes. It is not an allowed word, and when the College man comes he reads the bare place and does not ask how long it is. A man who has measured the place has looked at it, which is how I know he has read it. I have put the length in this table and not the word. A length can be carried; a word cannot.

V. Of the doing

  1. No article provides for a wall that takes and will not hold. In the back rooms they try again.
  2. No article provides for the man who comes back and wants a life that was not his. In the room they call it the graft settling and set a second wall over the first.

Margin: I saw a man come off this bench and turn his head toward a door he had never been taught to look at. He had the donor’s figure, which was three embers, and the donor’s want, which was a large want, and no name of his own on the slate he was entered upon. The room set another wall over the first. It took. He wanted the same thing afterward. There is no third article and no third wall that I know of.

The form of entry

Set out at the end of Title the Seventh. I have copied it with the blanks as they stand. In nine years I have had no name for the last line that this room wanted spoken aloud.

Entered this day, being ---day of -------, in the year of the Hearth ------, the grafting of beast-clay into the wall of ----------------------, the donor being ----------------------, of the measure of ------- embers, which measure is the measure of the person grafted; the want that came after entered as the graft settling; one part in twelve kept back for the Hearth; the figure entered by ----------------------, who is not the hand that cut. Entered by ----------------------, who has no house and has not asked.

The graft-song

Said low over the bench while the slip is mixed, never in the College and never while the College man is in the room.

Clay to clay and the wall takes,

the donor pays and the room gives back;

a figure goes into the empty place

and a want comes home in another face.

One in twelve, says the notice, read twice at the Cross;

here it is read once, over a forearm.

The beast has a small measure, the room a large account,

and the hand that holds the slate is not the hand that cuts.

In the narrow upright hand, against the last line of the first verse: One figure entered. In mine: Two things come home and he has numbered one. A man who numbers the other has stood at this bench longer than he admits.

What the two borrowed hands did

The College man came down from the Weighing Hall with ink in two fingers and a cuff gone soft from washing. He struck took out of the first article and set was taken above it, as though a wall could go from a beast to a man without a hand between them. I have put took back underneath and left his above. In the sixth article he struck the donor pays and set the measure is entered above, which is further from the animal and nearer to the slate. Both stand.

The other hand is a Sere hand, leaning, with a little gold in it, from the bowl that buys poor northern measures and makes them into a beautiful return for a feast. Against condemned in the Keeping it wrote condemned and bought, and against the figure of three embers it wrote three embers, and worth it. I have left both. A house of Sere can call a figure worth it, because a figure is a thing that can be emptied and refilled; a man who has held a forearm while the wall was going into it calls it something else and has no word for it that the ordinance will take.

At the head and in the gutter

The brown at the head is iron-gall and mine, made by holding the quire open over the bench while a man told me what his wall had wanted. It will not wash out. The fine grey in the gutter is warm-stone dust, ground small as meal under the weight that keeps the leaf from curling; warm at dawn and dusk and never entered. The pale crescent at the foot is grease off a pad of beast-clay laid down while the figure was read back; the smell of the room is in it still, hot lead and tallow and a third thing no name will hold. The green bead in the corner is lead glaze from a bowl out of Sere, chipped when a surgeon of the Ember house leaned to see the form of entry and set his bowl down instead of asking. The grey along the outer fold came off my own cuff going through the sealed-file room on an errand that had nothing to do with this book. The tide-line along the foot came from that same room, which is colder than the rest of Slate Row and always has been. There is no Last Day behind the College, and nothing here is swept up and burnt and called dealt with. The bare square in the article is the length of a word I cut away with a knife and have not written back. I have not forgotten it either.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The law gives him the donor’s measure. The room gives him the donor’s want. Neither of them gives back the man who lay on the bench.

I asked the College man, a surgeon come up from the quay, and a man who stood here about his forearm, and had three answers and no hand, as I have had before. I have left the line where it was written.

The book goes back under the warm-stone. Tomorrow a man will come in with a wall failing and a cuff full of money, and the room will take a wall from a beast and set it over him, and I shall enter the donor’s figure and put the wanting in the margin, where there is no column and nobody has asked to see it. The stone will be warm when I lift it and warm when I set it down. It keeps no account and gives no account of itself, which is more than can be said of anything else in this building.
