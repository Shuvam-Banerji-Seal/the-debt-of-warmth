I keep the leading rope at the Weir, and this leaf goes in the coat lining against it. A child comes down the plank walk at twelve with nothing in the hands and asks what a cutter is for, and I tell him: to carry what the arm says is there, and to come back by the same road. Then I set him to lifting a bundle and setting it down again on the flat stone. There is no lesson before that one.

These rules were gathered in the year the river came up twice and the reed stood too long, and they are carried forward in a coat and not from a book. A rule nobody has had to use is a rule nobody can carry. These have been carried. I set them down in the words we say, short words and often directions, and where a clerk has put a figure in the place of a direction I have left both standing. A man at first light wants to know which way to walk, not what the walk is worth.

The rules as they stand in the coat

Of the taking

  1. The Weir takes a child at twelve, by the rope and not by a house. A house may ask for the child and may not keep him. The child gives up a day and comes back by dusk with a bundle he did not have in the morning.
  2. A child out of the bog comes with a reed-name, and the reed-name is enough. A child out of Sill comes with a house-name and leaves it below the bridge. We do not write either. We do not sit at a table where a slate is open, and no slate has ever been set at the Weir for a taking.
  3. What is kept is the day the child first carried a bundle the length of the boards without setting it down, tied low in the cord with the teeth. A day tied in the cord is a day a man can find again by the feel of the knot, which is all a day is owed.
  4. A child is taken by the arm and not by the eye. A child who judges a bundle by the weight settling into the forearm is taken. A child who counts the stalks is set to stacking and sent home at dusk, and may come again when the reed has stood.
  5. A cutter’s child is not asked whether he wants the beds. He is asked whether he knows what is bought: silt to the knee, a rope that takes the wet, and hands that learn the weight before the price. The cutter cuts. The wanting comes after, or it does not.

In the narrow upright hand of a clerk of the factor at Greybank, against rule 3: The taking is entered; one day, one bundle. Underneath, in mine: I had written that the day is tied low with the teeth. He has entered the teeth as a figure and the bundle as a unit. A unit is a thing that can be carried up the road in a cuff. I have left both, because a man should see what was done to the day.

Of the arm

  1. A bundle is counted in the arm that carries it. The hands that cut it are counted in nothing at all. Say this to a woman at the racks who asks what the bundle holds, and go on with the next armful. Do not say it a second time; the second time becomes an answer and an answer can be entered.
  2. The arm tells what was carried and never what it cost. A cost asked as a direction is answered with a direction: downriver at first light, back over the boards at dusk, and the river in between. If the woman asks again, give her the weather. The weather has never yet been entered.
  3. A wet bundle is lighter to lift and harder to learn. If a child brings back his first bundle wet, he is not yet of the bog; send him again when the reed has stood and the water is off it. Do not tell him he failed. He will know it in the forearm, which is where the knowing belongs.
  4. A bundle is set on the stone and never in the silt. Silt gathers in a bundle laid down and becomes a weight nobody carried and everybody pays. Lift it off; leave the silt where it fell; say nothing to the pot or the slate about it.

Of the going-downriver and the coming-back

  1. A day’s work is a going-downriver and a coming-back. It is not a figure. A man who has walked it knows how far it was by where the arm begins to shake, and no slate in Sill has a column that will hold the shake.
  2. The plank walk has a board that gives under a boot. Walk across it as across the rest of the boards. A woman with a creel takes the landing sideways, one hand out to the rail, and goes on selling; nobody makes a fall of it and nobody mends the board before the thaw.
  3. The traps stand with their mouths upriver, so an eel goes in and cannot find the way out. The eels run whether anybody blesses them or not. On the fifteenth of Ilmen the hearth-maids bless the weir and the traps from the flat stone; stand where you are and do not look at the stone while the words are said. The blessing is the women’s. The run is the river’s.
  4. At the turn of the day, straighten the cord on the flat stone. The stone lies flat and has a crossed mark in it older than any cutter’s name. Do not ask the stone what it signifies. Give the date of the blessing if a man asks twice, and give the same date a third way if he asks a third.

Of the cord

  1. The tally is a length of grey wool or reed-fibre with knots set at the lengths a man’s arm can judge, and one tied low with the teeth and a pull of the thumb. It is carried inside the coat. It would burn if put to a flame, and no clerk can take a figure from it. This is not a virtue in the cord; it is a use a man has found for a piece of wool.
  2. Turn it once so the knots stand. Look at it yourself. Tie what must be tied, bite the end off, and put it back inside the coat with the flat of the hand. Do not count it aloud. A cord counted aloud is a cord with a figure in it, and a figure goes up the road.
  3. To a woman who says she cannot read a cord, say You can ask the cord and No more can the Court. Say nothing after. The cord knows what the mouth will not say; the mouth knows what the cord will not count. Keep the two apart. Laying them together is a trade no honest house makes.
  4. Do not lay the cord beside the knots a woman keeps at the lower boards or the eel-seller’s two twists at the Fishstairs. Three keepings laid together make a tally, and a tally wants a reader, and no reader stood at the Weir.

The walkings

Kept in the corner of the coat lining where the days leave off. A day is tied, not written; an entered cutter could be called back to a road he has walked.

Condition — What is kept — What is done — Discharged

Child taken at twelve — the day first carried, tied low — bundle lifted and set on the stone — when the arm judges without the eye

First bundle brought back wet — nothing tied — child sent again when the reed has stood — when the bundle comes back dry

Woman with a creel at the landing — nothing — she takes the boards sideways — not entered by us

One who does not come back at dusk — a knot low in the cord — his place gone around with a look — never

Knots at the lower boards — by the woman who tied them — looked at by her and nobody else — not ours to discharge

The leading rope — the rope itself — straightened at the turn of the day — kept by whoever holds it

  1. There is no term in years. A cutter stays while the rope is in his hand and goes with nothing written down. The knot for one who does not come back at dusk is not taken out to make the cord come even; a cord kept even has forgot who tied it.

Of faults and their correcting

Fault — As corrected at the Weir

Stalks counted instead of carried — Bundle set down; child set to stacking until the arm learns

Bundle laid in the silt — Lifted; silt left where it fell; nothing entered

Wet bundle taken for a dry — Child sent again after the reed has stood

Cord counted aloud — The count stopped; the knot tied low with teeth, in silence

A figure set where a going-downriver stood — Figure struck; downriver and back set underneath

Cord laid beside another keeping — Put back inside the coat; a hand’s breadth kept between

A verb struck out of a rule — Put back underneath, where the clerk can see it

A charcoal line swept from the stone — Not swept; the line written again below

The bundle-song, pasted inside the cover

Sung low going downriver at first light and again coming back over the boards, with the bundle on the shoulder and the cord inside the coat. Never at the Fish Blessing, and never twice in one house. The refrain is said with the whole crew; the last line belongs to whoever carried the most that day, and is not claimed.

Down at first light and back by the stone,

a bundle on the shoulder and a cord in the coat;

the arm knows the weight and the arm knows the bend,

and the slate knows nothing the river won’t take.

Ask the arm. Ask the cord.

What it carried, not what it cost.

Four to the bundle and one gone green,

the eels go in where the mouths have been;

the stone lies flat and the traps lie still,

and the run comes up whether we bless or not.

Ask the arm. Ask the cord.

What it carried, not what it cost.

A knot for the going and a knot for the back,

and one tied low where the thumb can track;

no figure on the boards, no name in the tin,

and the Court may ask the cord if it can.

The press of the wet bundle across the middle verse came from a morning the song was sung under a load that had not stood long enough. I have flattened the leaf as I could. The brine-gull took the last word of the last line; I have not written it back in. A verse with a word gone is a verse said slowly, and all of ours are said slowly.

What the borrowed hands did

The Greybank clerk wrote one day, one bundle above rule 3 and set transport over carried wherever I had put the word. Both stand. His transport is true of a cart and a barge and a man with a load on his shoulder, which is why it is true of nobody. I have put carried back underneath.

A clerk come down from Slate Row wrote the cost is not entered above give her the weather and put a round nought where the low knot is, with vacancy beside it. The place was a knot before he numbered it. I have left his nought above and my knot underneath, because a figure and a piece of wool have never agreed at the Weir and are not going to begin here.

A woman at the lower boards wrote in charcoal, struck it, and wrote it again below:

A knot is not a figure. Ask the wool.

She would not put her name to it. I have not swept the charcoal or put the struck line back.

At the head and in the gutter

The grey at the head is reed-ash off the flat stone, where a fire was made of the stalks that would not stand; it goes into the grain and no wet sleeve takes it out. The silt in the gutter came up the river on the day the tally was spoiled and is still in the second leaf. The press across the song is wet reed, and goes soft when the weather goes soft. The tar is off the leading rope, from the hand that holds it while the other writes. The eel slime came off a trap set down while its owner counted the boats going up; he did not wipe it and I have not. The white at the wrist-print is salt from a woman who asked what the bundle held and went away with the answer she was given. The ale ring is a boatman’s; he would not drink with cutters and set his cup on the corner all the same. The grey along the outer fold is not mine and not the clerk’s, and I have not swept it. There is no Last Day at a Weir.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The cord keeps the going. The arm keeps the weight. The river keeps the man who goes down and does not come back.

I asked a woman at the lower boards, the eel-seller below the bridge, and a man come up from Slate Row, and had three answers and no hand, as I have had before. I have left the lines where they were written. The leaf goes back into the lining and the rope goes back into the hand. Tomorrow a child will come down at twelve with nothing in the hands, and I shall set him to lifting a bundle before I tell him what a cutter is for, and by dusk the cord will have one more knot tied low with teeth, and the Court may ask the cord if it can.
