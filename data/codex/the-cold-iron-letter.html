To Nessa at the quay, who keeps the iron tally and will not call a figure a figure before company. From Harn at the smithy east of the Clay Yard, Low Kiln, Sill.

I write on the back of a packing-sheet because there is no slate in this shop that I would put a sentence on. A slate is a thing a man reads to you. I have set my count in notches on the bench with a chisel-point for eleven years and no officer has ever asked me what the notches were, which is how I know they are not a figure. The charcoal takes badly on the oil and I have gone over every other word twice. Don’t rub the fold. It comes apart in the hand if you rub it.

The barge goes on the ebb the day after Account-day and the carrier says a thing said in Silt does not travel well once the mud takes it, so I am putting down what matters first. It is this. I mended nine tubes in the last tend and eleven in the one before, and I did not mend them faster or slower than they came in. They come in with the grip polished grey to the thumb, the grey worked into the creases of the handle where a man has held the same iron every day since he was taken on. I can tell a collector by his cuff before he comes through the door, turned back twice from washing, and by the fingers he keeps shut until he is at the bench. He never says what he took. He says “the account stands” to the iron as if the iron had read it.

I do not set a tube to a wrist. That is the officer’s work and I have no word for it and do not want one. I mend the mouth and the man who carries it goes away with it, and the district calls the whole of that a kindness because the law has no other name for a room where a man is held until he agrees. There is a pale ring left at the wrist where the clip held. It shows through the winter. I have seen a man rub his cuff down over it while he was being told a figure and go out saying it would go in the thaw. My mother said that about everything. It is not in any ordinance and I have never once heard it read aloud.

The tubes I mended in the last tend, set here and not on any slate

When — What was brought — What was on it — What I did — What I entered

Firstday — A collector’s tube, the grip grey to the shoulder — Six grey prints; cuff turned back twice — Drawn clean, the seam set cold — Nothing

Thirdday — A barrow iron from the north gate — Ash in the notch, thicker than a year ago — Re-set and sent back — One mark, to the house

Fifthday — A kiln iron out of the glaze-room above — Green at the edge, sweetish at the smell — Filed it down and said nothing — Nothing

Seventhday — A damper iron with no house-name on it — Soot, and no warmth that belonged to it — Re-set; asked no question — Nothing

Ninthday — A second tube, the grip smooth as old bone — More prints than I counted, and I did not finish the count — Drawn clean — Nothing

I tap iron the way the women at the racks tap a pot, because a rule that holds for clay holds for anything that has been worked and may fail. A pot answers full and a cracked one answers flat, and a man answers if you know where to put your ear. A cold-iron tube answers neither. It has no note of its own. It takes and it does not give, and I have never in eleven years heard one ring full. I have set that down here because it is the strangest thing I know and there is nobody in this shop to tell it to. The kiln behind my bench takes its breath in fours and misses one, and I have worked beside it eleven years and never once heard the fifth. I do not call the missing breath anything. The yard calls it the kiln and goes on.

I was apprenticed in the year of the Rising, Y.H. 668, when ninety children’s warmth went into a private account and Pell Varr came down with a sword and put the district back the way a man sets a table. I was old enough to hold the bellows and not old enough to be told what the tubes were for, and I learned what they were for by watching what came back. Last year the hearth-maids tied a count in wool and the Weir tied one too, and no man has given me a figure for either, and I have not asked twice. A count a woman ties in wool is a count that stays where she put it. I have not entered it and Nessa must not enter it either.

Berr Clay came down from his house with the red cord folded under his coat and wiped his thumb twice before he touched nothing, and read one in twelve over the basket at the Cross, twice, once for the moot and once for the ones who came late. The bell went four, four and a gap and nobody crossed himself. Here is the thing I cannot put on a bench and cannot say to a man who has just had a measure taken from his sister: the figure that goes off a household in this yard is not the figure read over the basket. I have never seen the other written. That is how I know it is not written. I have held my tongue on it for nine years and it has grown a weight in the mouth like a stone a child carries without being told why.

The counting song

Sung low over the bench, to the iron, never to a person. The second verse is the one Nessa’s mother taught us and Nessa has written it back under the line I rubbed out.

Iron comes down and the warm goes up,

the tube takes six and the slate takes none;

nine on the bench and two in the house,

and the count keeps what the clerk left out.

Four for the kiln and a fifth not heard,

grey in the corner and grey at the cuff;

a widow’s clay at the opening touched,

and nothing entered and nothing enough.

Write it in charcoal, write it on oil,

the barge takes it down when the river will;

what came from the mountain goes back to the cold,

and the hand that mends it is paid in a loaf.

I have mended the Vail kiln irons for four generations of that house. A woman there touches a thumb-length of glazed clay at her throat before the opening and at no other hour, and I have never asked her what it costs to touch. The factor at your end confirms the mark and calls the confirmation a kindness, and a kindness is entered. There is no column for the touch itself. I put no figure against it. On the Last Day the grey in my own corner is left where it lies and burned next morning and called dealt with, the same as everywhere else, and I have never once seen a thing dealt with stay dealt with.

Now the asking, which is what I wrote for. The Keldish iron came down the Rim Road again this month, the bars with the blue bloom on them the potters won’t take a glaze over. A man off that road at the well said a Keldish person states his measure before his name and has the number put on his forearm at thirteen, and that when a man comes back thin they cut the old numbers out and put new ones in. Is that true, or is it a thing travellers say to make a room go quiet? I have no number on my arm. If a man asked me for my measure before my name I would give him his own looking face and be none the worse. Tell me what the quay paid for the last lot of Keld iron and whether the Vhaleni factor is still writing his contracts on paper that rots, because a contract that rots in the damp is a virtue in your town and a folly in mine and I have never been able to keep the two things in one hand at once.

There is a story in this district of a counting room shut up in the last war with the damper closed and the men inside dead of the breath, eighteen leagues east, told twice now at the well as a curiosity with a date. Nobody joins it to any kiln in Low Kiln. I have mended damper irons and never asked what room they came out of, and I am not asking you now. Only: if the old man who keeps the quay’s chains was the one who made the iron of that room, ask him whether a damper shuts from the inside. Ask it of the iron and not of him, the way you ask a pot. He will tell you a thing about a room and not know he has told it.

You kept the tally after our mother and you keep it in a hand I can read at a glance even when the salt has got at it. We have never once put the other’s name into an account, and I would sooner burn this sheet than have a clerk at the factor’s table enter the two of us as current. What is said downriver does not come back upriver in the same words. If it does, I will know it was carried by a man who had been paid to carry it, and I will not send another.

Harn, at the smithy, the last tend of Silt, Y.H. 813.

In the margin, in a second hand, small and set close to the writing

The tend before was nine too, not eleven. You counted the two you mended for the thane’s house a tend earlier and carried them into the last one because the notches ran together on the bench. A notch that runs into another notch is a notch a man has counted wrong, which is the whole of our trade. I have struck the eleven through and put nine under it in your own iron-gall, so that you cannot say a stranger wrote it.

A man off the Rim Road was at the quay on Tenthday with the numbers on his forearm and gave his measure before his name to the factor’s clerk, who wrote it down and did not look at his face. It is true, then. I have not asked him what the numbers were and he has not asked me what I keep in the tin. Do not ask the old man about the damper. I asked him about a chain instead and he told me a room shuts from whichever side has the more to lose by it. That is the whole answer and it is not an answer. Put it where you put the missing fifth breath and leave both alone.

The clerk takes the verb, the quay takes the mark,

the tube takes the warm and the slate takes none;

a notch is a place where the count stood still,

and a count that stands still is not yet done.

I have not written a verse since our mother died and I would not begin on a wet sheet. You have written one in charcoal, which does not keep, and I have set mine under it in iron-gall, which does. That is the difference between the two sides of this river and I have never known how to say it in a room.

What the clerk did

A clerk of the factor’s house borrowed the sheet on the morning after it came up the quay, being a man who cannot leave paper alone. He writes in the neat upright hand they teach at the College, with the verbs taken out and everything in the passive. Against the barrow iron he set one mark, current. Where Harn wrote the tube took six measures from a neighbour he set six measures banked above it, and the neighbour went out of the sentence as though a neighbour were a thing a man could carry off in a cuff. Against the count in wool he wrote vacancy, and against the closing of the room he wrote not entered, which is the Court’s own art in a small space. He put a round figure where Harn had put none, and the figure has outlasted the notches it was meant to explain.

I have put the verbs back underneath where I could still read them. He takes them out because a thing always true has no need of verbs. We put them in because a man at a bench at three in the morning wants to know which hand to hold the iron with. I have left his figure standing on the back, where it belongs, and the nine notches on the front, where nobody has asked for them.

At the foot, in a hand nobody has claimed

The smith mends the mouth that eats the house. He counts the mouths and does not name them. The tube has no note of its own; a man has one, and this district is full of notes nobody taps.

I asked the old chain-man, a dipper come up from Vhal with salt at his wrists, and a man who keeps the factor’s tally, and had three answers and no hand, as we have had before. I have left the line where it was written. The sheet goes back in the tin among the iron tallies. When the barge goes upriver in Ember I will send it back with the verbs still in it, and you will take them out again if you have a mind to, and neither of us will call it an account.
