I cart sand and lead to the third furnace and have done it eleven winters. The sand comes up off the shore in barrows and the lead after it in pigs, and the two go into the pot together the way bread and water go into a batch. I know a good barrow by the grain before it is tipped and a bad one by the smell, which I learned with my nose and not from any written word. The yard counts the glass going out. Nobody counts the sand going in, and nobody counts what the quench takes off the hands at it, which is not the same thing as saying nobody knows.

I set this down because a clerk of the tide-tally house stood at the shed with a leaf and a soft cuff, asked what we sang at the quench, and wrote it while I was still saying it, with the doing taken out and a term put where a place should stand. His hand is above mine and the damp is taking it, which the Council calls a virtue in its contracts and would not call a virtue in its clerk. I have left his words where he set them.

The white at the head is salt off the shore sand and will not come out of the grain, which is why we sign in charcoal here and not in ink.

The manner of it

  • At the trough, after the last pot is drawn and before the fire is banked. Not while a man from the Council room stands in the yard; said with such a man in hearing, the song comes out as a statement and is entered as one.
  • Standing, with one hand on the edge of the trough and the other at the jar. Said with a hand on a woman, it becomes an argument, and there are arguments enough in a furnace yard without carrying one up the Salt Road.
  • To the water and to the jar, not to the company. The last word of each verse is let fall into the trough and not finished. The jar keeps the refrain: one voice begins and the rest come in on the second line.
  • One verse to a hand at the quench, going round with the jar. The sixth hand holds the jar and does not drink, and the verse said there is said low, into the water, because a verse said loudly is a verse with a price in it.
  • Tap the trough with the knuckle during the refrain. The tap is not a number; a clerk can carry off a figure, not a tap on wet boards.
  • The kept verse is not sung while strangers are in the yard. It is said to the trough when the last cart has gone up the road, with the jar going round once more and nobody drinking.

The song

Said low to the trough, standing. The jar keeps the refrain. The hand goes to the edge of the trough before the first line.

The refrain, as the jar keeps it after every verse:

Quench, quench, the pot comes out.

Pass the jar and say what it cost.

The furnace keeps the heat. The trough keeps the steam.

The sand goes in and the glass goes north.

I. Of the sand

The sand comes up from the shore in a barrow,

grey as the road after rain and no more than that;

we know it by the grain and not by its name,

and the lead comes after it, sweet at the lip.

Quench, quench, the pot comes out.

Pass the jar and say what it cost.

The furnace keeps the heat. The trough keeps the steam.

The sand goes in and the glass goes north.

II. Of the sweetness

There’s a sweetness at the corner of the mouth

that comes from the lead and not from the wine;

a child at the Cliffs is told not to lick,

and we are paid to do what the child is told not.

Quench, quench, the pot comes out.

Pass the jar and say what it cost.

The furnace keeps the heat. The trough keeps the steam.

The sand goes in and the glass goes north.

III. Of the drawing

The pot comes out of the mouth on a rod

and the light that was in it goes into the glass;

you cannot look at it and you look all the same,

and the eyes go on seeing it after the shift.

Quench, quench, the pot comes out.

Pass the jar and say what it cost.

The furnace keeps the heat. The trough keeps the steam.

The sand goes in and the glass goes north.

IV. Of the quench

The trough takes the pot and gives back a cloud;

the cloud takes the hair off the forehead and goes.

One hand on the rod and one on the jar;

the jar goes round and the hand does not.

Quench, quench, the pot comes out.

Pass the jar and say what it cost.

The furnace keeps the heat. The trough keeps the steam.

The sand goes in and the glass goes north.

V. Of the north

The glass goes north in a consignment of oil

and the wine comes back in the same kind of jar;

a crown buys a bowl in the court of the south,

and none of our pots was ever asked to one.

Quench, quench, the pot comes out.

Pass the jar and say what it cost.

The furnace keeps the heat. The trough keeps the steam.

The sand goes in and the glass goes north.

VI. Of the hands

There are six hands at the trough when the last pot’s out;

the jar goes to five and comes back to the sixth.

The sixth holds it level and does not drink,

and the ledger gets that one and we get the rest.

Quench, quench, the pot comes out.

Pass the jar and say what it cost.

The furnace keeps the heat. The trough keeps the steam.

The sand goes in and the glass goes north.

There is a space before the seventh verse, the width of a hand laid flat on the trough before the pot goes in, felt for with the palm and not the eye. I have left it. A clerk put a round nought in it, and the damp has eaten the word beside it down to the figure, so that his nought has come through the wet and his verb has not, which could only happen to a sentence written on salt-paper.

The verse kept back

Not sung while a man from the Council room is in the yard. Said to the trough after the last cart has gone up the road, with the jar going round once more and nobody drinking.

In the year the men stood at the mouth of the third,

the sand went in and the men went out;

the Council wrote resolved in a hand gone soft,

and lit the furnace again in the very same week.

We do not say what they wanted. We say what they were given,

which was the same fire and the same jar going round.

Pass it to the water. The water has no clerk.

There is no verse after it; the next thing is the damp taking the leaf, which has never wanted one.

The jar, as it goes round

The hands as the yard keeps them, set above what the tide-clerk entered on the morning the winter glass was counted. The two readings stand.

Hand — What the yard says — What the clerk entered

The lead hand — Sweet at the lip, and paid for it — Exposure, occupational

The rod hand — The light goes into the glass — Product, sound

The quench hand — The cloud takes the hair and goes — vapour, unentered

The sixth hand — Holds it level and does not drink — One retained; no charge arises

The kept verse — Said to the trough, after the yard — Historical disturbance; no account lies

The clerk wrote no charge arises against the sixth hand, which is true in his office and leaves the hand standing there with the jar in it. A hand holding a jar is not nothing even when it has not drunk, which is the whole of this song and no use to a counting room.

What the furnace-master ruled

He came down on the wrong wind with the sweet edge of the glaze at one corner of his mouth and read the tally with one eye on the furnace. Against we are paid to do what the child is told not he wrote in broad white slip: Protective instruction current. No loss arises. He underlined it twice, and the slip has flaked at the second word, which is a thing white slip does and a thing he did not provide for.

Underneath, in mine: He is right about the instruction and wrong about the loss. The sweetness is part of the wage and is not paid in coin, and a mouth gone pale at the corner is a thing a man carries home and cannot set down at the shed. He reads with one eye on the furnace, as a man does who has never stood at the quench.

What the tide-clerk did

He borrowed the leaf while the consignment was counted. He writes in the leaning hand on salt-paper, and the shore has taken half of it, so that of four corrections two are figures, one the ghost of a verb and one gone clean away. He took the doing out and set his words above mine.

  • At the furnace keeps the heat, the trough keeps the steam he wrote heat retained; vapor unentered, which takes both keepers out and leaves the furnace standing with nobody in it.
  • At we are paid to do what the child is told not he wrote occupational exposure, current, which is a true thing in a counting room and leaves the mouth out whole.
  • At the jar goes to five and comes back to the sixth he set five parts consumed; one retained, a true figure with the not-drinking taken out, which is the part the yard wanted to keep.
  • At the kept verse he wrote historical disturbance, Y.H. 626; no account arises, and the damp has since eaten the year, so that the disturbance stands with no date on it, which is how the yard has always kept it.

I have put my verbs back underneath and left his standing above. He takes the doing out because a thing always true has no need of anybody doing it. I put it back because a woman at the quench wants to know which hand held the jar.

What the glazer put underneath

The charcoal belongs to a woman who glazes the small bowls and has had the pale line at the gum these six years. She writes on the backs of spoiled tallies, pressing hard at the ends of words. I have copied it underneath as she set it.

  • At sweet at the lip: The sweetness is not the wine. I have tasted both and they are different, and I have not told the clerk which is which. He would write it down and carry it away.
  • At the kept verse: My mother was at the third in the year the men stood. She never said what they wanted, only what they were given, which was the same fire and the same jar going round. I have kept her one sentence and not made a second out of it.
  • At the space before the seventh: I put my hand there when you wrote it. It was the width of my hand then and it is the width of my hand now, which is more than can be said for the clerk’s word.

At the head and in the gutter

The salt at the head came off the barrow sand and has gone into the grain and will not come out, which is why the yard signs in charcoal and not in ink. The pale streak beside it is lead, worked in by the hand I held the leaf down with. The tar is off the barrow rope; the strand in the second leaf goes slack in wet weather, and I shut the pot when it does. The blue-green chip came off Cliffs glass a girl set on my tally while she licked her thumb, which children are told not to do. The scorch is where the leaf lay too near the mouth of the third. The grain of sand has cut a line through one word of the fourth verse and I have not written it back, because a song with a word gone is a song said slowly. The ring is sour wine, set down at the first verse and taken up at the last, by which time it had gone the colour of the trough. The grey along the outer fold came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying in a sand-shed. I have not swept it. There is no Last Day at a furnace and nothing here is burnt in the morning and called dealt with; the fire goes on and the damp goes on eating the clerk’s words and leaving his figures.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The sand keeps its grain. The furnace keeps its heat.

The trough keeps the steam and gives none back.

The Council keeps a word that rots and a figure that does not.

The sixth hand holds the jar and is not entered.

I asked the glazer, a pot-drawer and a tide-clerk, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written.

The leaf goes back into the sailcloth and the sailcloth into the pot. Tomorrow the jar goes round to five and the sixth holds it level, and if a man from the Council room stands in the yard we shall sing the kept verse to the trough instead of to him, and the damp will go on eating his verbs and leaving his figures, which is the only counting here anybody has ever wanted to keep.
