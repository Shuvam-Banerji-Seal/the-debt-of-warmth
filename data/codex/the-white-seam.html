I take in washing for the quarry at Stonemouth. The lime comes out of the coats and will not come out of my hands, and I have long since given up trying; a woman who scrubs her knuckles raw for a living learns which stains are hers to keep. I set this down because the weigh-clerk at the landing asked one morning what the children counted when they hopped the flagstones behind the kiln. He wrote the answer while I was still saying it, with the hopping taken out and a figure standing in the middle of it. What follows is what they said before he came up the bank and what they said again after he had gone back over the ford with his cuff laid flat against the leaf, as though a hand laid flat were a hand that had done nothing.

The white at the head is lime and not the Ash. There is Ash enough in this country without borrowing it for a children’s game, and I have swept neither of them.

How it is played

Played behind the lime kiln on the flat flagstones of the dressing yard, in the dry weeks when the dust lies thick enough to hold a line. Not in rain: a line drawn in wet dust is a smear, and a child who hops a smear learns the wrong count. Not in the Five, when the children are made to stand and listen and there is no yard for it. In Bone they hop the trodden floor of the ale-room, which is the same game with the cold taken off it.

  1. One child draws the line with a wet thumb and is called the hand. The seam is thumb-wide and runs across the stones from the lower side to the upper, after the seam a dresser cuts along in the rock. A hand does not hop in the round she drew; she stands with the dust on her thumb and watches the others cross it, which the children take as a punishment and the yard takes as practice.
  2. The seam must be crossed and never touched. A child who sets a heel in the dust has paid, and sits on the stone with her hands in her lap. She is brought back by no name and no forgiveness; the next child to draw calls her across, and she goes over on the first hop. A line that has been rubbed out can be drawn again, and a line that has been drawn again has no grudge in it.
  3. One line of the rhyme, one hop, going out from the lower stones to the upper. On the ninth the hopper holds the back foot above the seam and does not let it fall, and the last word is said to the dust and not to a face. Said to a child it becomes an argument, and there is always a child who will argue.
  4. Then the seam is rubbed away with the flat of a hand and drawn again, by the one who paid or by the hand if nobody did. There is no last round and nothing is won. A count a child must make again each time is a count nobody can owe her, which is the whole reason they keep it in dust.

The counting-out, said over the fists

Right fists held out, knuckles up. The child who says it touches each fist in turn and does not look at the faces. The fist it stops on draws the seam and stays out of the hopping. Say it to the fists and not to the owners of them, who will argue about anything.

One for the bit and two for the block,

three for the lime on the cuff and the smock;

four for the tooth that the buyer has paid,

and the fist it stops on lays the line and stays.

The rhyme, as it is hopped

One line, one hop. The heel goes down beyond the dust; on the ninth it stays up and the word goes down into the stone. The children will not be told why the last line is said low. Ask them and they ask you why a person wants to know what dust is for.

One for the bank where the block was cut,

two for the lime that will not wash out;

three for the kiln with its mouth gone white,

four for the Ferr that takes what it can;

five for the bit and the mark beside it,

six for the tooth and the two marks paid;

seven for hands gone white to the wrist,

eight for the name the ale-room keeps;

nine for the seam, and the heel held still.

What is said when a child whitens her heel

Said low, to the stone, while the child sits down with her hands in her lap. The next line-drawer calls her over with the first hop and there is no more said about it.

The dust is up. The seam’s gone thin.

Draw it again and the child comes in.

Say it to the stone and not to me;

the dust takes the foot and gives it back.

The verse they do not hop while the weigh-clerk is on the bank

I have this from the girl who drew the seam the morning he leaned over the wall with his cuff laid flat. She said it to the stones with her back turned and made me set it down with none of my words in it, because a verse hopped to a stone wants nothing added and a verse said to a man at a bench wants everything taken away.

They take the name at the door for the cup

and give it back when the tend is done;

they weigh the block and they write the mark,

and the block stays here when the mark is gone.

We keep the dust on the back of the hand.

The hand goes home. The dust stays here.

What the weigh-clerk did

He laid his cuff on the leaf before his eye went to the words, the way they do at that bench, and set the flagstones down as common ground of no tenure, used for gaming. Against the nine lines he wrote nine passages, each charged at a half-bit, payable by the house of the child hopping. Against the heel held still he set one party in arrears; the line is a boundary of the Court. At the foot, where the seam is rubbed out and drawn again, he put a figure and wrote perpetual, which is a word for a thing that never needs a thumb to hold it down.

I have put the children’s words back underneath and left his standing above. He takes the hands out because a thing always true has no need of them, and because a hand in a sentence is a hand that might be asked whose it is. I put them back because a girl with lime to the wrist does not know she is hearing something always true; she wants to know whose turn it is to draw. He has set a charge where a child sat down. A figure travels down the river road and a seam in dust does not, and that is the whole trouble between this bench and this yard.

In the margin, in Ivo’s charcoal

The stone-dresser writes small, the way a man writes who has one hand out checking a bank and the other holding a child by the collar.

At two for the lime that will not wash out: She says lime. It is quicklime when it is fresh and will take the skin off a child as readily as it will take a block off the bank. I had rather a child stepped over dust than stepped off the edge.

At eight for the name the ale-room keeps: The room gives the names back at the end of the tend. It is the man who has forgotten his own that does not come back with them. I have set more of those than I have set bones.

I have left both where his thumb put them. He has caught children in this yard for three-and-twenty years and has never once entered a fall.

At the head and in the gutter

The lime at the head is off the sleeve I wipe the coats with and has worked into the grain; no scrubbing takes it out, which is why I sign in charcoal and not in ink. The blue-grey in the gutter came off a sleeve wet from the Ferr when a man leaned over to see the writing and would not sit down; the river dries the colour of old rope on a man and stays in him after he has gone. The round mark is where a buyer set a two-mark piece down to hold the leaf and took it up again; he paid for a tooth and did not stay to see it written. The ring at the fold is small ale from a cup set down by nobody in particular and claimed by three children and denied by one. The flat white print where the leaf was held in the draught is mine, and is the only word on this leaf I wrote with my thumb. The grey along the outer fold came off a cuff that had no business lying in a dressing yard, and I have not swept it. There is a Last Day in Sill and the corners are swept there in the morning and burnt and called dealt with. There is no Last Day behind this kiln. The seam goes under the cart tomorrow with the cuffs.

At the foot, in the print of a child’s white thumb

I drew the line. I am the one who cannot cross it.

I asked the girl who drew, the woman who keeps the ale-room, the dresser of stone and the weigh-clerk himself, and had four answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was made. The bundle goes back on the cart and the dust goes back on the stones, and if the clerk comes up the bank again with his hand laid flat I shall let him charge what can be charged, and keep the seam where a child can draw it again.
