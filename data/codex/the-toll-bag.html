I keep the meal-book at the mill and the toll-bag hangs on a nail behind the door, where a man must turn his back on the wheel to reach it. A carter comes in with a sack and asks what his barley will fetch and what the road took out of him on the way. I can answer the second better than the first. The road writes on the sack; the Cross writes on a board, and the board is read twice.

Twelve copper bits to the silver mark; twenty marks to a Sere crown, which comes north because it must and cannot always be spent here. The Court books in embers and will tell a man an ember is worth five marks. I have ground nine winters and never found one in the hopper, so I keep no column for it. A figure that will not move with the weather is one somebody has taught to stand still.

In the narrow upright hand of a factor’s clerk come up from Greybank: Coin and reckoning entered. Ember: five marks. Underneath, in mine: He wrote is worth where I should have written the Court calls it worth. I put the verb back. A thing called five marks and a thing changing hands at five marks are two things, and only one comes through this door.

Say the figures to the hopper and not to the man asking. Said looking at a man, a price becomes an argument, and an argument gets carried out with the meal and comes back with a second figure on it.

What the mill gives and what the Cross cries

The left column is what was called under the moot-post on the Account-days of Stone; the right, what passed over this bench between the last market of Bone and the frost on the hopper board. The wheel does not stop for a figure.

Meal, dressed, a sack — not cried here — 8 bits; the brown in the fold to whoever is waiting

Grist, a peck — not cried — 2 bits

Bran — not cried — to the pig at the door, and not entered

Brown-edged barley loaf — 1 bit — 1 bit, and the crust to the child at the door

Salt, a sack off the pans — 3 marks — not sold here; the man carries it on past the mill

A sack left overnight under the wheel — no figure — 1 bit, and the mice pay the rest

What the stone takes

These are not cried anywhere. A man learns them when the meal comes out lighter than the barley went in.

Charge

The handful for grinding — lifted off the top before sacking; never weighed

A cup on the nail beside the hopper — left for the woman who carries; she does not ask

Frost on the hopper board — weather; no figure

A foundling left at the mill in a fold of grey wool — a stone warmed under him, no name until a house claims him; not a sale

In the clerk’s narrow hand, against the last row: No charge arising. Underneath, in mine: True of the mill and not of the house that takes him.

The dues of the roads that bring a sack

Four ways carry grain to this door and five carry it away. What is below is what I am told at this end, before the sack is set down. The tally-men at the far ends keep their own figures, and the two do not agree.

The ridge road, from Cold Acre

Two leagues north on the ridge, where six houses have one well that has not frozen in any winter anyone can name. The barley comes down this way, and the turnips with it.

Due

Cart with barley, at the gate — 1 bit

A man going up for the never-freezing well — 1 bit; he brings back a common stone, which is not a warm-stone and is not called one

Pig driven north — 2 bits

A sack on a man’s back — nothing at the gate; 1 bit here when it is set on the bench

Barrow of grey going out — not weighed, not entered

Margin: A woman off the ridge asked why her barrow of grey went out unpaid when her barley paid. I told her the barley had been bought and the grey had not. She said that was a distinction a mill could afford and a ridge could not.

The river road, to the Weir and Stonemouth

One league upriver to the traps and four more to the quarry village at the meeting of the Ferr.

Due

Beast on the bank way — 1 bit

Plank walk at the Weir — 1 bit; the boards are counted and nobody says which will bear a man

Foot passenger in Silt — nothing; the water has already charged him

Basket of eels at the bridge shade — nothing; the Fishstairs folk argued it and won

The Clay Road, east to Greybank

Eighteen leagues of packed clay over corduroy, a Court road since the treaty of the Blue Cliff in Y.H. 418. Salt and iron come back on it; meal and grain go out. The slate is read twice in the square and the cart pays at both ends.

Due

Cart with a load, at this end — 2 bits

The same at the quay, by the factor’s man — 2 bits more

Foot passenger — 1 bit; nothing on Account-day, when a man comes to hear the figure

Barrow of grey going out through a gate — not weighed, no figure reaches it

The bridge and the water under the mill

Due

Loaded cart over the bridge — 1 bit

The wheel’s own water — no toll; it is the same water that gives the note and sets the town’s hour

The mill threshold — no due; a sack pays inside, not on the step

The ford in the deep cold — nothing when the river bears ice; in the Long Cold no collector crossed for ninety-four days

In a carter’s thick charcoal, added at the bridge: Paid going over and paid coming back, and the sacks lighter every time. In mine: Both stand. A road has two ends and both have a hand; a river has one hand and does not write.

The roads that do not bring a sack here

Three roads go out of this country which have nothing to do with the mill. The Salt Road takes a mark at the coast pass and calls it a freely entered contribution; the Rim takes a mark or iron and promises no man back; the Hearth takes nothing at any gate, having taken it at home. A man asked which was dearest and I showed him the empty column. He said the Hearth, because a man pays it without seeing the hand.

The song said into the hopper

Said low while the sacks go over, to the hopper and not to the company, and never while a man from Slate Row stands in the door.

Twelve to the mark and twenty to the crown,

the Court books five and never sends them down;

a sack comes in at the bridge with dust on the seam

and goes out as meal with a handful gone.

The stone takes first and the road takes twice,

and the Cross reads twice and calls it once;

say it to the hopper, say it down to the wheel,

and nobody enters what the river took.

What stands outside the figures

The dust of this mill gets into a man and he coughs up a colour he will not discuss; no due at this door pays for that. A wrong wind off High Kiln brings the sweet edge of lead glaze and takes it out on the wind that brought it. In Silt the flood line goes up the wall to the second course of stone and the mill grinds on with what the river sends. In the Long Cold the note came under the ice and the town kept its hours another way. A cup was set down on this leaf while I counted and taken up before I saw who set it.

The hands that are not mine

The factor’s clerk takes the doer out, as though a sack could set itself down and a stone take its own handful. Where I wrote I lifted, he wrote was charged; where I wrote the road took, he wrote was taken. I put my words back beneath his, as old grinding shows under new on a dressed stone. He set the printed figure in the margin beside the one spoken in the yards, and did not ask why.

The carter wrote at the bridge. A girl of the Cross set her sums in the corner, two bits going into a mark where four should have stood. I have not rubbed them out. They are the only figures here nobody will be asked to pay.

At the head and in the gutter

The white at the head is mill-dust, not salt; my father’s hands were white to the wrist and never noticed he had marked a leaf. The brown tideline came up from the Fishstairs in an apron, and I carried the leaf dry though there was no figure in it. The barley awn in the second fold is stiff where the crease kept it from the damp. The ring is not ale and I did not see the cup go down. The nick came from the dressing-pick I use when my hands are wet. The grey along the fold came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying, and I have not swept it. There is no Last Day at a mill. The dust goes out with the meal and nobody calls it dealt with.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The Cross reads twice and the road takes twice;

the stone takes first and the bag keeps none;

a sack comes in with a house’s name on it

and goes out as meal with the name ground off,

and no figure stands in the space between.

I asked the miller, a carter off the ridge, and a clerk up from the Greybank beam, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The leaf goes back into the toll-bag and the barleycorn into the crease. Tomorrow the sacks come down frozen hard, and I shall give the figure I gave today; by the time the wheel has turned it will be a thing that was true.
