I keep the room where they stand overnight. The collector draws in the district and brings his slate to the table; I set a pallet, a dish of banked coals under the bench, and a pot of water by the door, and the vessel lies here until first light, when the figure goes up the Clay Road and the warmth goes into a cradle. The College calls this the holding. The district calls it the night. I have written both names into one book and neither of them is mine.

The room is the coldest in Slate Row and the dampest, and the draught under the door goes along the floor to the corner where the grey gathers. I do not sweep it. There is no Last Day in this room, and nobody burns the corner in the morning.

What follows is one leaf, from the first Account-day of Tallow to the second, in the firing month of the year after the Thin Year. Twelve copper bits to a silver mark; twenty marks to a gold crown, a Sere coin that does not stay in Grath. An ember is booked at five marks and no man has exchanged one for the other. A newborn vessel holds twelve to fifteen, a labourer in good case forty to sixty, one of the Kept three hundred, which is a figure no household can spend. One in twelve is printed on the notice and read twice at the Cross. I have heard another figure spoken in the yards and have not entered it, not being a figure I can weigh.

Vessels held on this leaf: ten. Nights with no vessel: one. Figures struck: one. Columns struck: one.

The leaf, as I set it down

Figures in Ledgerhand, copied from the day-slates. The remarks are mine, in the hand that fed the dish and turned the pallets, and are not part of the account. The column at the end has no heading; a heading would make it an account.

Tallow 11 — A widow of the lower town. Loaf — 41 — full — 2 — Greybank — Four loaves baked, three to the levy. Asked whether it went into a child. Not entered.

Tallow 11 — A kiln hand of High Kiln. Bent — 46 — flat — 5 — Greybank — Sweetness at the mouth. Note flat before the pot was set down; the sweetness came and went with the speaking.

Tallow 15 — A man of Mud End. Peat — 47 — full — 4 — Greybank — Taken quiet in his sleep. Neighbours called it merciful. Room gave full and nobody crossed.

The forty of Low Kiln were held here last year, one line for the batch and no names taken; a barrow of grey went out through the north gate the morning after and I have not swept this corner since. They are carried as forty, not as forty persons, and the room remembers the difference in the damp.

The note in the dark

Before the dish I set a knuckle on the pallet rail or the rim of the pot. A whole vessel answers full. One cracked through answers flat, a small dead sound that stops at the surface. A vessel with air under the note answers nearly full, and the yards have no word for it, so I have set down the phrase as I heard it, which is not Grathic and will not be read at a Weighing. A vessel turned toward in expectation may give nothing at all; I have entered that as none and not as a nought, because a nought is a figure and a silence is not.

A note taken at the drawing and a note taken at first light are not the same note. The room takes a little off everything it holds, the way any pot set in a draught loses heat without being asked, and the loss column is the only place in this book where the cost of keeping is set down. The College struck it on a market morning and wrote No such column above in the narrow upright hand. I have drawn the column back underneath and left the words standing over it. He drew his thumb down every row before he could write that there was nothing in any of them worth entering, and I watched the thumb go.

I feed the dish at four and four, and it misses the fifth, and I put nothing into the gap. The bell at the Cross does the same thing over the market and nobody crosses himself. Two counts in two rooms, and I have kept them on separate places rather than make one out of them. A clerk who joins two counts has begun to write a doctrine, and doctrines get copied.

What the borrowed hands did

The College clerk struck I held against the widow and set the vessel was held above, and underneath that the measure was kept, which is further from the pallet than the first. I have put I held back beneath both. Take the man out of a sentence and there is nobody left for a woman to put her question to, and the questions come in the end, always at the door, always after the figure has gone up the road.

Against the man of Mud End I had written the neighbours called it merciful. A tall leaning hand from Greybank set the draw was lawful above and underlined it twice. Both stand. Lawful is true and does not mention a man who was gone before the tube came through the door; merciful is what the room said after I had put the dish out, and there is no column for what a room says in the dark.

He struck a third thing also. Where I wrote asked whether it went into a child, he wrote no entry required, and he was right by the rule and wrong by the door. I have left both.

The verse the collectors say coming down the road

Said low going down Slate Row before the light, never while the slate is open on the table, and never twice in one house.

The room keeps four and the room keeps four

and a place where the fifth should stand;

the figure goes up in a clean dry hand

and the warmth goes down in the clay.

I do not ask what the morning does

with a thing that lay warm on the bench;

the slate is read twice and the cradle once

and nothing is read of the night.

The man who said this to me would not say the last line while the factor’s man stood at the foot of the Row, which is how I know which line matters. I have copied it as it came and put no gloss on it. A man who sets a meaning beside a song is writing a notice, and notices are read twice at the Cross.

In the margins

The grey in the outer fold came off a district bundle laid on the bench while a man was being carried in from Mud End; it took the shape of a thumb and I have not brushed it away. The tallow thumb at the head is mine, from holding the lamp low over a row when somebody behind me was saying something I wanted the end of. The filings in the gutter come out of the wool sleeve the tube rides in; there is always a little iron in the fold and it has never once drawn the warmth out of the paper, which is the one small kindness this trade allows the written word. The bone-pale streak is slip from the thrower’s forearms, left when she set her slate beside mine to wait for a figure. The green bead is lead glaze and not slip; she leaned over to see the writing with the sweetness still at her mouth, touched the leaf, said nothing, and went back up the terrace. The tide-line along the foot came up from the flagstones the night the barrow stood here, and comes back every wet month. The small square in the lower margin is where I scraped a word away with a knife and put no other in its place. I have not written the word back and I have not forgotten its length.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The room keeps them warm. The morning keeps the figure. Nobody keeps the hour between.

I asked the College clerk, a man come up from Greybank, and a woman who stood at the table about her house, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written.

The leaf goes back under the bench. At first light Hessa Rale will come with her strap settled and her slate held edge-on to her sleeve, and carry the figure up the Clay Road without reading it, and I shall feed the dish and turn the last pallet and set the note down before the College man can strike it. The bell will go four, four and a gap over the Cross. The corner will keep its grey. There is no Last Day in this room and nothing here is burned in the morning.
