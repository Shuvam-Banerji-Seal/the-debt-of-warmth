I keep the moot book of Sill in the chest under the post at the Cross. It is not the Court’s book and not the thane’s. The factor at Greybank writes my office down as the chest, which is as much name as a man who keeps other people’s years is given. What follows is the year the Court came to stay: Y.H. 776, in which a house was raised on the east side of the Cross and a street got its name from what was done in it. I was not alive in that year. My hand is the fourth or fifth that has copied it forward, and I have put the verbs back where I found them taken out.

This is not a history of Sarn. The Hearthseat keeps that, in a clean hand, and it begins at the lighting and leaves out the clay. Our book begins with a treaty and ends each year at the last market. It keeps what happened where a man can stand and be asked. A chronicle that leaves out the pot-board has left out the greater part of what a town ate, and I have kept the board in.

The years before, as the chest has them

Y.H. 418. The treaty of the Blue Cliff, copied into this chest by the road-clerk who kept it before me and read at the Cross on market morning. The Clay Road was made a Court road; an assessor could stop a cart on it without asking the thane, and did. He came with the market and slept at Greybank. The thane of Clay was not asked to the slate. This is how all important treaties are made here, the Grathish say; the Court says the thane’s absence is not an entry. Both sentences are in the chest.

Y.H. 726. The granary built at Greybank and the grain levy doubled in Grath. We felt it at this end as a second figure on the same load. Entered as the levy was answered, which is the phrase with no men in it that the Court uses when men are cold.

Y.H. 774. A clerk came up from Greybank and counted the doorways of Sill, standing at the Cross with his slate to his forearm and never asking to come in. To the woman who asked what for he said, the book. To the woman who asked which book he said, the book. I have entered two answers and no book, because a book that a man will not name is a thing a town has a right to go on not having.

Y.H. 775. Ground measured east of the Cross, where the pot-board stood in the wet. The pot-board was moved into a shed and no board put in its place. A woman who had sold there for nineteen years set her own board down on the new stones and said nothing at all about them, which in this town is a judgment a man can hear. There had been a well in that ground. The masons filled it, and the Court paid for the filling, which is the first figure in these years I can put a payer under.

Y.H. 776, the year of the raising

Entered first in Smoke of that year and copied forward four times. The hand changes at the fourth entry; I have kept the change, being the only honest thing in a copy.

Stone. Stakes set on the east side of the Cross. The thane of Clay was not asked where the house should stand, and has never said whether he would have put it elsewhere. A man from the Court’s room at Greybank laid a cord along the ground and tied it to a stake, and the cord stayed there through the frost, and the children of Low Kiln tied nothing to it and went around it.

Ilmen. Foundation stone laid. A slate was bought at Greybank and brought up on a cart, eighteen leagues, packed clay over corduroy in the wet places. It cost eleven marks and the cartage was not entered, which is the custom of that house: the figure travels and the road does not. That slate is still on the assessor’s counter. Three assessors have stood at it since and none of them bought it.

Silt. Walls to the lintel. Lime on every course. The kiln hands came up from the terrace at the going of the light to watch, and one of them sang while the lintel went on, and was told to stop by a man who had come down from Greybank. He stopped, and went on singing under his breath, which is where most things in this town are kept.

Ember. Roof on, and the street named Slate Row, because there was no other slate in Sill that a man could point to and be believed. A street named after what is done in it and not after a family is the Court’s way and not ours. Our streets are named after water, stone, and the trade that ruins a man. The older women went on calling it the east side for twenty years and some do still.

Smoke. The Court’s room opened next door. Behind it a room was dug out and floored, to keep the collected overnight before the banking. The town had not known it wanted such a room until it had one. A hearth-maid from Low Kiln came up on the first morning and stood in the doorway with a birth-slate under her shawl and did not go in. She was paid by the Court, as we all were after that year, and she went back down the terrace with the slate still under the shawl.

Ash. The figure read twice at the Cross, once for the moot and once for those who came late. One in twelve, as the notice says, and nobody crossed himself. The bell went four, four and a gap over the reading, and the count of the yard stood beside it and was not folded into it. Another figure was spoken in the yards that winter. It is not on any notice and has never been entered in this book, and I have put it in the margin where a figure can be kept without being made a promise.

Margin, in the broad round hand: The difference between the two figures is three parts in twelve, which any child at the pot-board can work out with eel-bones and does. I asked a man who copies the College’s hand why a figure might be read in one place and paid in another. He said a figure read was a kindness and a figure paid was an account, and that a man who laid the two together had begun to write a sermon. I have set his sentence down. It is the only true thing he gave me, and he did not know he had said it.

The Row as it stood at the year’s end

What the Court’s man entered on the delivery slate, set beside what the town called each room. He wrote the rooms in a column and I have put their doings back.

Room — As entered — What it is called here — What was left at the Cross

The assessor’s house — Valuation office, established — The house with the counter — The pot-board, in a shed

The Court’s room — Public room for the reading — The room where the figure is said — The moot-post and the block

The Waiting Room — Accommodation before banking — The room nobody goes in — A woman with a slate under her shawl

The collector’s table — Draw to be performed — The iron and the tube — Two answers and no book

The filled well — Site prepared — The well that was — Nineteen years of standing there

A table is a thing a clerk reads and a margin is the only place in a book where a silence is allowed to stand. The woman with the slate under her shawl has no column and never will. The room nobody goes in is the name the younger women gave it, and the older ones called it the hollow, and neither name is on the slate that came down from Greybank.

The raising-song

Taken down at the lintel from a mason who would not give his day’s wage and gave the song instead. Sung while a course was laid, never on the Clay Road and never at the Cross while the slate is out. The last course is left out of the singing and set in its place in silence.

We brought the slate up from the quay,

eighteen leagues and a wet ford;

we set it level on the east side

and the pot-board went into the shed.

Lime on the course and lime on the back,

a course for the thane and a course for the Court;

the thane was not asked and the Court was not told,

and the wall stands because somebody paid.

One in twelve is a clean figure;

the yard keeps another and does not sing it.

Leave the fifth course out of the verse —

the wall has a stone where the song has a place.

He said he had learned it from his father, who carried lime up for the granary at Greybank in Y.H. 726, and that his father had never sung the fourth line facing a man from the factor’s house. I have left that where he put it.

What the three borrowed hands did

A clerk of the College at the Hearthseat borrowed the leaf on a market morning. He writes upright and narrow and takes the verbs out as though a verb were a thing a man could smuggle past the block in his boot.

  • Against the Court came to stay he wrote an assessor’s office was established, and underlined it twice, as though a man coming to stay and a room being opened were the same event seen from the same distance.
  • Against the room nobody goes in he wrote accommodation provided for persons awaiting banking, which is true and has no person in it.
  • Against the yard’s other figure in the margin he wrote nothing at all. His hand passed over it without a stroke, which is the readiest way of correcting a figure I have yet seen.
  • Against leave the fifth course out he set omission in the verse, and underneath, in mine, I have put a course stood there and was paid for, which is the sort of difference that gets a leaf copied again.

A factor’s man soft-cuffed from Greybank wrote against the naming: the street was named for the material of its roofing. This is true and is not the reason any woman in Low Kiln would give. I have left his hand above mine everywhere, as a newer repair lies over an older one and does not pretend the first hand never touched the stone.

The charcoal is a hearth-maid’s, thick and leaning, and stops before it finishes:

A slate in a room is a slate anybody can read. We kept ours in the fold. They asked and we said the children were warm and that —

She did not finish it and I have not finished it for her. A sentence left in the mouth is a thing this book has room for and the College’s rooms have not.

At the head and in the gutter

The lime at the head came off a mason’s sleeve when he leaned over to see which line I had set down, and it goes into the grain and no damp takes it out. The slate shaving in the second fold came off the delivery slate when it was new; it has kept a edge that would take a finger, and I have folded the leaf over it so that it lies where a man’s thumb goes and not where his eye goes. The silt dried pale in the gutter came up from the sill on the morning the figure was first read twice; the river was low and the note under it had not yet come on. The green bead is lead glaze out of High Kiln; a girl leaned over to see the rooms listed with the sweetness still at one corner of her mouth, touched the corner, and went back up the steep street without saying what she had seen. The corner tastes sweet in wet weather and I have not washed it. The nail came out of the lintel frame and was shut into the sewing by a man who had no business sewing the book and did it anyway. The square of bare paper is where a word was scraped away with a knife and no other put in its place; I have not written it back and have not forgotten it. The tallow thumb is mine. The ale ring stood from the first entry to the last and went dark in the time it takes to read a figure twice. The grey along the outer fold is not mine and not the College’s. I have had this leaf six years and have not swept it. There is no Last Day in a chest.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The house came up and the pot-board went down,

the well was filled and the well had a name;

the figure is read where the pigs are weighed,

and the other one walks the yard all day.

Ask the Row what it keeps.

I asked the thane’s clerk, a woman who carries birth-slates in Low Kiln, and a mason who came up in 776 and has been dead these thirty years, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The book goes back into the chest under the post. Next tend I copy the figures of the Long Cold, and the notice will still say one in twelve and the yard will still keep the other, and both will stand where they stood, with a finger’s width between them and no signature on the space.
