I mend what the kiln spoils. In Bone there is no drying to do and the shared shed is the only warm room in Nine Pots, so in Bone I keep the guess-shed, which is what we call the riddle bench, though a man from Sill would call it an alehouse and charge the road for it.

Nine Pots and Sill are in feud over the road. It is eight leagues of packed clay and belongs to both of us, which is why neither of us mends it. In the thaw the feud is carried in stones thrown and in loaves withheld. In the frost the road shuts under its skin and a man cannot throw a stone eight leagues, so we throw questions instead. A riddle is a stone the other man has to carry home.

The bench is a drying board laid on two clay cones and blackened by ten thousand sleeves. I keep the wagers in a jar of sherds at the end away from the fire, where a sherd shows white against the soot, and I empty the jar in the morning back into the pit. Nothing is carried away from this shed in a slate. A man who wants a thing entered can go east to Greybank and sit with people warmer than himself, and he will not get a riddle there.

The rules, as the shed keeps them

  1. A riddle is put once. Ask for it a second time and you owe a sherd, because the second asking is for your own pleasure and not for the shed.
  2. Answer in one breath, and say it to the pot, the bench or the fire, never to the man who put it. Said looking at a Sill man, an answer becomes an argument, and the argument is the feud, and the feud has a date a clerk would want.
  3. Four questions are scored. A wager is not a question and is kept on a place of its own, because a loaf laid down is a thing that can be taken up again.
  4. The judge is the shed. I say whether an answer serves and I do not say why. A reason given is a riddle given away for nothing, and the giver can go home and sell it.
  5. Sherds are counted by the hand and not by the eye. A sherd for a round, a cup of small ale for a night, and neither leaves in a figure. This is a skill we share with the eel-women downriver, who keep their accounts in cords and will not say where they learned it.
  6. No slate lies on the bench. If a slate comes in, the guessing stops for that night, because a thing entered is not a riddle any more; it is a debt with the warmth taken out of it.
  7. A pot is set down before the first question and nobody lifts it until the fourth. If it answers flat when it is set down, the night’s answering will go badly. I have never known this to fail and I would not say so before a thrower.

The four that were scored

The first went to a girl who sweeps the Glaze Room up the terrace in Sill, come down with the frost to see our kilns and stay longer than she meant to. She had her answer before the verse was out, which is why she never wins anything and is always first.

Sweet before it is trusted, green where no grass grew,

a pale line at the gum and a price on the shelf;

the mixer knows it by the tongue, the potter by the thumb.

What is it?

“The lead in the glaze,” she said, and said it to a pot. “My mother has the line,” she said, “and she will not say what she tastes.” Nobody asked her to say more. A girl of twelve in this country knows what is in the mix and what the mix does to the women who taste it, and knows that the knowing is not to be said across a room where men have slates.

The second I put to a thrower of Nine Pots, with slip dried bone-coloured on both forearms and the little finger bent where a splinter went in at eleven.

A tube of cold iron opens a way no knife made;

what it takes leaves a round place that shows through winter;

the figure goes up the road and the warmth goes

nowhere a body can follow.

What is it?

“A measure,” he said, and said it to the floor. There was a man at the bench with a pale round place above his wrist who set his cup down and did not lift it again. The measure is not a thing for a feast, and a riddle answered right is still an answer that has cost somebody. I scored it and passed the jar; he did not pass his cup back, and nobody made him.

The third went to a carter out of Sill, who had come up the road that morning and would go back before dark.

Eight leagues long, owned by two houses

that will not share a name;

both mend me, and each says the other

laid the first stone;

I carry their loaves and their stones

and neither crosses me in frost.

What am I?

“The road,” he said, and said it to the door. He would not say which house laid the first stone, because a first stone is a thing a Court clerk would want a date for, and a date given in a shed is a date carried out of it. The old man who mends said something low about the frost holding the road together better than either village did, and the carter said nothing to that, which is the proper way to answer a man who has said something true.

The fourth I put myself, because the room had got noisy and wanted quieting, and a riddle put by the shed is not scored against anybody.

Set me down and I tell you whether I am honest;

tap me and the whole shed hears it;

I can crack and come back, and I never say

the crack went away.

What am I?

“A pot,” said the old man who mends. “A pot and the seam in it.” He said it to the bench. Then he set his own cup down and it answered full, which is the only answer a man can get from a pot without paying for it. He left his sherd on the board and did not take it back, and I have put that sherd in the gutter of this account where a figure should stand. If he wanted it again he could ask, and he has not asked.

The tally, in sherds

Round — Put by — Answer given — Served — Sherds

The sweet — the shed — the lead in the glaze — yes, said to a pot — one

The tube — the shed — a measure — yes, said to the floor — one

The road — the shed — the road — yes, said to the door — one

The pot — the shed — a pot and the seam — yes, said to the bench — one, left lying

Four answered and none drunk. The cup stood from the first question to the last and went back on the shelf with the ale still in it, which is how small ale is drunk in Nine Pots when there is a thing to say and no proper word for it.

The loaf that was not paid

The wager is not in the table, and I keep it where it belongs, on a place of its own. The carter laid a browned loaf on the bench against the girl from High Kiln: she could not name the sweet in the glaze, he said, without tasting it, and there was lead enough in the room to have done the naming for her. She named it and did not taste it. He took the loaf up again and said she had named a thing and not the name of it, which is the sort of distinction a man makes when the loaf is his.

He said it to the bench and not to her. She went back up the terrace with the frost on her shawl and did not ask for the loaf, and I have not put a figure against the place where it stood, because a sherd is not a loaf and a figure is neither. When a man takes up a loaf that has been laid down, what is left is a place on a board, and there is no word in Grathic for the holding of it.

What the factor’s clerk did

A clerk of the factor’s office came up from Greybank in the second tend of Ilmen, with a cuff gone soft at the edge from washing and ink sitting in two fingers of it that would not come out. He laid his hand on the leaf before his eyes went to the room, which is the manner of that table. He set the four questions down as queries entered and the answers as responses recorded, put two marks against each sherd (a sherd, he said, is a half-bit), struck the loaf as consideration not tendered, and wrote levy on small ale, one in twelve across the head of the tally, so that a night of guessing came out as a night of trading. Against the round place on the man’s arm he wrote measure drawn; cost to district, nil. Where the old man had said the seam keeps the crack, he wrote repair retained; material not entered and left mine underneath, which is more than he usually leaves.

In the shed’s hand — In the Court hand

Sherds in the jar, counted by hand — Four responses, two marks each

The loaf not paid — Consideration not tendered

The road, owned by both and mended by neither — Way, public; upkeep not entered

A pale round place above a man’s wrist — Measure drawn; cost to district, nil

A pot that cracks and comes back with the crack showing — Repair retained; material not entered

The guessing stops when a slate comes in — No entry required

He asked me what a riddle was worth and I told him nothing a clerk could carry, which is the first true thing I have said to that table in two years. I have put my words under his and left his standing above, the way a drying board keeps the wet mark under the sherd. He has entered four answers and struck the loaf, and the loaf was not struck: it went back to the man who laid it down.

What the carter put in the margin, in thick charcoal

He borrowed the leaf on a market morning and returned it with the fold changed. I have copied his hand as it stands.

At neither crosses me in frost: We cross it. We cross it before the frost sets and come back with the clay on our boots. Saying we do not is how we keep from asking who laid the first stone.

At the figure goes up the road: It goes to Greybank. The man with the round place walks the other way and is not paid for the walk.

At the loaf: I am the man who took the loaf back. She named it and I said she had not. I have said it to my own daughter since, and she has asked me twice which of us I was.

I have not answered him in the margin. A man writes that in a book and it follows him home.

At the head and in the gutter

The slip at the head is mine; I held the tally down with my whole hand because the draught under the shed door takes paper before it takes a man, and there is slip in my knuckles whatever I am doing. The green at the fold came off a girl who leaned over to see the writing with sweetness still at one corner of her mouth. The tallow in the gutter is mine. The press of wet wool across the first leaf came off the carter, who held the door while the factor’s clerk read and did not let go when it was done. The crust of frost along the outer edge came up out of the paper on the night the shed froze, when I carried the leaves inside my coat and the coat froze to my back; I have not scraped it off, because a night of riddles kept in a warm room would be a different night. The sherd folded into the gutter is the one the old man left on the board. I have set it where the figure should stand, because it is what the night left, and a figure is not.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

Four answers went home in men’s mouths. The road kept the stones.

The loaf went back to the man who had laid it down.

I asked the carter, the girl and the old man, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The jar goes back under the bench and the sherds go back into the pit. When the clay sets in Seed the feud will go back to stones and loaves, and the shed will be a drying room again. I shall keep the guess-shed for the next frost, count what is left in sherds, and set the loaf’s place where the figure was, and not ask who wrote the line at the foot.
