I have turned the fire at a kiln two doors above the Vail through nineteen firings and have never seen a man write down what we say at the mouth. A factor’s man came up off the north bridge with a cuff gone soft from washing and asked a girl at the tubs what the women sang when the fire was kept through the night; she began to tell him and he wrote before she had finished. I have set it down in her words and in mine, with the rests left in.

A firing song is not a tune. A tune wants a voice with nothing in its hands, and at the mouth every hand is full. It is said to the iron and to the coals, to keep the rake moving and the count honest. Sung at a table it grows a melody in it, and a man in a clean coat can carry that away and enter it as a custom. I have put the verbs back where the clerk took them out.

The brick-dust at the head is mine. I held the tally down on the flagstone with the hand I rake with and did not know I had marked it.

How the fire is kept

  • Turn the coals with the back of a broken pot, never with iron. Iron remembers the heat and gives it back in a man’s face.
  • The call goes to the damper and the answer goes into the mouth. Said looking at a woman it becomes an argument, and an argument at the mouth ends with somebody setting the tongue wrong.
  • Four pulls to the rake and a rest at the turn of the tongue. The rest is not a pull and is not counted. It is where the iron is given its other bite.
  • The damper has two bites and no Grathic word that holds both. Hold one and the pot comes whole out of the firing; hold the other and a hand comes off a man. Call the iron by what it is doing that hour and do not ask it what it means.
  • A girl come up from the Glaze Room with green at one corner of her mouth and the pale line at her gum does not rake. Not a punishment. Sweetness makes a hand careless, and a careless hand turns the tongue too far.
  • If a slate lies on the bench, turn it face down before the first pull. A fire said over an open slate arrives somewhere the speaker did not send it, and gets entered there.
  • Do not sing when the Slip Brook is up brown. The water has its own note, and two notes over one mouth make two counts that will not agree, and the yard goes around both all day.
  • The last part is kept back. It is for when the man from Greybank has gone back over the bridge with his satchel under his arm and the yard can hear itself again.

The song

The woman at the mouth calls the first line of each pair; the hands along the bank answer the second. The refrain is said low into the mouth at the turn of the tongue, and the last line is held until the rake is back on the iron.

The coal came down from the Last Day under ash,

we broke it out with a broken pot and gave it a name of our own;

it is the same fire in every house in the yard,

and every house says it kept it, and none says who lit it.

Keep it low. The mouth takes what the hand will give.

Four at the rake and a rest where the fifth should be;

the pot keeps the note and the hand keeps the burn,

and the slate keeps neither, and is read in the morning.

Green clay will stand up straight all day

and slump in the night with nobody to see it;

so will a promise, and so will a man

who has been told the thaw is only weather.

Keep it low. The mouth takes what the hand will give.

Four at the rake and a rest where the fifth should be.

One iron holds the heat in and takes it out;

say which mercy you mean before you touch the tongue.

A man with his finger in it holds his tongue instead,

which is the answer most of us give.

Keep it low. The mouth takes what the hand will give.

Four at the rake and a rest where the fifth should be.

The glaze goes on green and comes off with a sweetness;

the girl who tastes it has a pale line at her gum.

Do not let her rake. The lead does not care whose hand it takes,

and takes the steadiest first.

Keep it low. The mouth takes what the hand will give.

Four at the rake and a rest where the fifth should be.

The breath kills in sleep and leaves no mark worth speaking of;

they call it merciful who have not gone in after breakfast.

We set a pot at the door of the house where it happened,

and say nothing to the pot, and the pot says nothing back.

Keep it low. The mouth takes what the hand will give.

Four at the rake and a rest where the fifth should be;

the pot keeps the note and the hand keeps the burn,

and the slate keeps neither, and is read in the morning.

One in twelve, says the notice, read twice at the Cross,

once for the moot and once for them that came late;

the bell goes four, four and a gap and nobody crosses himself,

and the figure you heard is not the figure they paid.

Keep it low. The mouth takes what the hand will give.

Four at the rake and a rest where the fifth should be.

Take nothing out while the hot is still in it.

A cup knocked at the draw answers full or answers flat;

the flat ones go by the road for the cart to crush,

which is kinder than letting a buyer find them.

Keep it low. The mouth takes what the hand will give.

Four at the rake and a rest where the fifth should be;

the pot keeps the note and the hand keeps the burn,

and the slate keeps neither, and is read in the morning.

There is a space before the last refrain in the keeping, the length of a turn of the tongue. I have left it on the tally. The clerk put a round nought in it once, which is a thing you can hold up and look at, and a rest is not.

The part they keep back

Not said while the factor’s man is in the yard. Taken up after he has gone back over the bridge. The women say it to the damper and not across the square.

Four at the mouth and a gap where the fifth should be;

we do not name whose gap it is, and the house does not ask.

The heat we keep was paid for somewhere before it came here,

and a quiet man down at Mud End went in his sleep last week

and nobody made a lesson of him.

The number goes from a fist to a mouth to a shoulder

and arrives at nobody, and nobody asks where;

a brine-gull went off upriver with a coal from a banked fire

that was not yet cold, and carried it out of the account.

Four and a rest. The iron keeps the other bite.

The clerk has no word for a thing a woman does with her hand.

One line of this part is gone where the leaf lay too near the mouth. I know what it said. Knowing a thing is not the same as having it, and I have not written it in again.

What the kiln-master did

He borrowed the leaf at the firing and wrote in broad charcoal over my small hand. He has read every line of it with one eye on the damper.

At the glaze goes on green and comes off with a sweetness: Green ware, current. The sweetness is not entered.

I have left it standing. He is right about the ware and wrong about the sweetness, which is entered somewhere, and not by him.

What the clerk did

The factor’s clerk took the leaf on the morning we drew the second firing, ink sitting in two fingers of it that would not come out. He took the heat out and set his figures above mine. I have left his hand standing and mine underneath, as the mouth keeps the pot’s heat and the flagstone keeps the scorch.

  • At the pot keeps the note and the hand keeps the burn, he wrote vessel sound, entered; quality not a figure. Labour, one mark; no deduction for injury. True in the figure, false at the mouth, where the note is the whole of the quality and the burn is the whole of the price.
  • At four at the rake and a rest, he wrote four pulls, fifth nil. A nought is a number. The rest is where the iron changes hands.
  • At we do not name whose gap it is, he wrote cost to district, nil. It is true in the figure and false in the room that breathes through its mouth at the end of the yard.
  • At a quiet man down at Mud End went in his sleep, he wrote one death, cause undetermined, and drew a line under it as if a line could close it.
  • At carried it out of the account, he wrote loss, nil; gain, nil, and put the leaf face down, which is the only thing he has done all morning that the yard could agree with.

What the woman at the tubs put in the margin

The thick charcoal belongs to a woman below the racks who has raked more firings than she will count. I have copied it as it stands, without making the two hands agree.

At the same fire in every house: We say it kept it because there is no other sentence. In my first winter I asked who lit the first one and was told to ask the fire, which is an answer given by people who asked the same thing at their own first firing.

At say which mercy you mean: A man held the cold side of that iron for twenty years and never once said which bite he meant. He is dead now and the pot he kept is still on the shelf.

At the girl who tastes it: I have held the pale line up to the light for three of them. Two went south. The third still rakes, and I have said nothing, which is a kindness and a fault in the same breath.

At the flat ones go by the road: My father set a flat cup by the road and a buyer took it up anyway and carried it three leagues before it broke. Somebody paid for those three leagues and it was not my father.

At the kept-back part: The river takes what the Court does not. You can shout at either one. I have tried.

At the head and along the gutter

The brick-dust thumb at the head is mine and will not come out of the grain, which is why I use it to sign. The scorch along the lower edge is where I laid the fold on the flagstone while the mouth was open; it took the corner off two lines, and I have not written them in again, because a song with its corner gone is a song the yard has half forgotten, which is how most of them reach us. The green bead came off a girl who leaned to see the writing with sweetness still at her tongue; she touched the paper, said nothing, and went back to the Glaze Room. I have not washed it. The small-ale ring went through the third verse while a woman stood over me telling me to keep it low; she set her cup down and took it up when I had done, and the ring has gone the colour of the pot it stood on. The long grey along the gutter came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying on a kiln bench. Nobody will own it, and there is no Last Day behind a fire. The sherd between the fourth and fifth leaves answers flat when I knock it; I keep it there to remind me what flat is. The grey wool through the hole ties the leaf to the damper handle, and there is a knot in it for the one line I could not bring up and have not looked to see whether it has come back.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The clay keeps the thumb. The fire keeps the cost.

The slate keeps neither and is turned face up at the Weighing.

I asked the woman at the tubs, the kiln-master and a man who came up from Greybank with a satchel, and had a proverb, a figure and a date, and no hand to set over any of them. I have left the line where it was written.

The leaf goes back on the damper hook. There is a firing in Ember and another in Smoke, and the fire will be turned with the back of a broken pot, four pulls and a gap, and the gap will be left where it was found. If the factor’s clerk borrows the leaf again he may strike out the verbs. The fire does not care. The hands do.
