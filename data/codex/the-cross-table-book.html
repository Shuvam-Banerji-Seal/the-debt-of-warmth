I keep the Court’s table at the fringe of the Cross. It is set out on market mornings in the open air, between the direction that is Slate Row and the square where two hundred lean over a block to hear a figure. There is no room and no door. A woman comes and stands before me with her house roll in her hand, and I touch the top slate before I look at her, and say it’s entered as it stands. That is the whole of the office. The assessor in his house sets the worth upon a figure; the man at the block reads it twice; I enter what is brought and say it stands. Between the three of us a life is carried the length of this square and nobody in it has carried the life.

There is an order to the reaching, and it is the one thing this table teaches. Hand to the slate, then eye to the woman, then mouth. If a figure is in her mouth before my hand is on the slate I will have to answer for the figure and for her together, and no table in Grath is furnished to answer for a person. I have watched the thane pass a finger over a number before he looked at the crowd, and the collector wipe his thumb twice before he touched nothing, and the eel-women set a hand to a cord before they lifted a creel. I do not say what this order means. A clerk who explains his own hand has begun to write a sermon, and sermons get copied.

Twelve copper bits to a silver mark; twenty marks to a gold crown, a Sere coin that comes north because it must and does not stay. The Court books in embers and calls five marks an ember. No man has exchanged one for the other under this table, and I have left no ember column on the leaf I hand to the drawer.

In the narrow upright hand of a clerk come down from the College: Ember: five marks. Reckoning entered. Underneath, in mine: He has written it as though a mark could be poured into an ember by a man standing in the rain. I have put called back under his line. It is the Court that calls it five marks; the coin does not agree.

The leaf, as it stood in Ember

Figures in Ledgerhand. The remarks are mine, in the hand that held the slate, and are not part of the account. The last column has no heading.

Ember 10 — Eight pigs over the weighing board; the levy called for the kiln month — — — 1 in 12 — read twice — One in twelve, as the notice says, once for the moot and once for those who came late. A man brought a number in his fist and asked what the firing would cost now that hands had been in it. Sent to a slate at the watch.

Ember 10 — A woman of Low Kiln with a house roll; asked what her house was worth — 33 — 1 in 12 — as it stands — Read her the figure the notice gives, which is the only figure she can carry away. Green at one corner of her mouth; a pale line across the back of one hand. A colour entered, no name.

Ember 10 — A narrow slate taken up the clay road before noon, writing turned to the sleeve — — — — — not read — The runner said she carries and does not read, and that the road does not care which. No figure came off the slate at this table.

Ember 20 — Second Account-day. Eight pigs; the bell gone four, four and a gap — — — 1 in 12 — read twice — Nobody crossed himself at either bell. The heard bell kept beside the told; a man who wanted them to agree would have had to put a hand to both.

Ember 20 — A man carried out of the clay yard on the ninth, going up the road breathing — — — — — — — No collector called, no figure entered, no cause named in this square. Asked three ways and had no slate. I have left the row empty and not set a nought in it.

Ember 20 — A woman at the pot board turned a cup a quarter and set it back where it stood — — — — — — — Green in the crease beside the mouth, clay under the nails, a pale line where something had been worn tight and long. A colour joined to no name, held on a place of its own.

Ember 30 — Third and last Account-day of the kiln month. Eight pigs over wet boards — — — 1 in 12 — read twice — The district’s arithmetic changed a third time and cannot be changed back. The printed figure stood unchallenged.

Ember 30 — A basket set in the moot-post’s finger’s-width of shade and carried home at the going of the light — — — — — — — No slate opened near it. I set down the shade and not what was in it, which is the only thing this table is fit to set down.

Ember 30 — Below the north bridge, a clerk set at the out-of-line landing for a measure nobody called — — — — — — — Cuff grey from washing; hand to slate before eyes to stalls; writing turned inward. Not one of ours. The market sold over the top of him.

Ember 30 — At the market’s edge, a shape in cast-off clothes with a green sliver on a string at the throat — — — — — — — No loaf passed, no sale, no slate came near. It caught the light on the sliver and went dull. I have not called it a seventh of anything.

Figures entered on this leaf: none that were not read. Lines with no figure: five. Rows struck: none. Columns struck: one, drawn back. Noughts set by another hand where I left a place: four.

The two readings

I keep a place beside the figure the notice gives for the figure the household actually gave. I have set no heading over it. A heading would make it an account, and an account is a thing a man may be called to answer for, and this table has answered for enough this year.

On Ember 10 the number came up out of a fist and went to my mouth and then to the shoulder of a man going up toward High Kiln, and arrived nowhere. I entered the figure the notice prints, the only one a woman can carry away, and in the place beside it set the fist and the mouth and the shoulder, which are not figures and cannot be added. On Ember 30 a woman asked what the kiln month would take and was told a number goes into a slate and not a bundle, and that there would be a slate at the watch. No number came off the slate. I have written the sentence down and not the number, because there was no number to write.

The bell is kept the same way. I write four, four and a gap as I heard it, above the four, four and a gap that was told to me later by a man who had not been in the square. Both are true. A clerk who makes one out of the two has begun to keep an account of a rumour, and the Court pays for those by the page.

What the College hand did

The man from the College borrowed the leaf on Ember 20, with his cuff gone soft from washing and ink settled in two fingers. He did not sit. He laid his hand on the top sheet before he looked at the woman standing at my elbow, which is the order of that office and not the order of this table.

Where I wrote I entered he set was entered above, and above that the figure stands recorded, which is further from the woman than the first. I have put I entered back underneath and left his words standing over it. A sentence with nobody in it is a sentence no woman can ask about, and women ask about these entries in the end, always after the slate is shut.

Against the man carried out of the yard he wrote no charge arising, which is true of this table and cost him one glance at the empty row. Against the pale line at the back of the woman’s hand he wrote no entry, and I have left it. He struck the column with no heading from the first row to the last and wrote No such column above it in the same upright hand he uses for the deduction. He had to count the rows before he wrote that there were none worth entering. A man does not count what he has not looked at. I have drawn the column back underneath and left his words where he put them.

The numbering hand is not the College’s. It is narrow and leaning and came down the clay road. Against four places where I had left the Entered as column bare it set a round nought apiece. I have not struck the noughts out and I have not set them above my blanks either; they stand beside them. A nought is a figure and a blank is a place, and this leaf now holds both. A man who wanted those noughts to be true had to write them himself, and he did it in the hour before the last pigs went over.

The verse the table-keepers say

Said low while the block is scraping, never while a slate lies open and never facing the direction of Slate Row. The last line is said to the table.

Hand to the slate and then to the eye,

and the mouth comes last, as the mouth should do;

one in twelve as the notice says,

read twice over a basket and once over you.

Four and four and a place at the end

where the driver sets down what the clerk won’t take;

the thane wipes his thumb, the runner turns her sleeve,

and the table keeps the shape of what they gave.

A woman who stood here about her house said the first lines while she waited for me to finish, and would not say the last while a man from the factor’s office stood at the square’s edge, which is how I know the line matters. I have copied it as it came and put no gloss on it.

In the head and in the gutter

The brown at the head is iron-gall and mine, made by holding a quire open through a market morning; there are clerks in Slate Row who can tell a table-clerk by his thumb before he has spoken. The Ash in the outer fold came off my own cuff going through the square after the pigs, where I put my hand into a corner I had no business putting it into. They sweep the corners of houses on the Last Day and burn what they find in the morning and call it dealt with. There is no Last Day for a table set out in the rain.

The tallow print is mine, from holding the lamp low under the table’s edge while a woman at my elbow was saying something I wanted the end of. She did not tell me the end. The streak of slip came off the forearms of a thrower who set her slate beside mine to wait and left a pale mark along the gutter. The green bead is not slip; it is lead glaze from a woman who leaned to see the writing with the sweetness still at one corner of her mouth, touched the paper, said nothing, and went back across the square to her cup. I have left it where she put it. The brown crust gone stiff in the fold was laid down by a woman whose child would not eat it; a crust put down and not eaten is a thing this square knows how to read. The pale ring was made by setting a cold iron tube on the leaf while I reached for a slate. The tube was not used at this table and the ring is not a wound, but it is the thinnest paper on the page. The tide-line along the foot came from a board left standing overnight against the table leg. The small bare square is where I scraped a word away with a knife and put no other in its place. I have not written it back and have not forgotten it.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The table keeps the figure. The yard keeps the place where the figure stood. The two never meet, and there is no column for the road between.

I asked the man at the block, a woman who came to ask after her house, and a runner come up from Greybank, and had three answers and no hand, as I have had before. I have left the line where it was written.

In the first tend of Grain a word went over the boards that a slate had burned in the night, and went again, and came a third time from the direction of Slate Row on the breath of barley price and weather. I have set it in the remarks as a telling and not as a fire. No slate burned under any table I have kept, and a word about a slate is not a word about a pot. The leaf goes back into the drawer under the loose slate. Tomorrow the tenth of Grain will bring the boards up the clay road and the pigs over the wet boards, and a man will bring a number in his fist and ask what the month will take, and I shall touch the top slate before I look at him and enter the figure as it stands, and set the fist and the mouth and the shoulder in the column with no heading, where the noughts stand beside the blanks. Nothing has been joined or closed. The table stays in the open air, and the figure stays where it was entered.
