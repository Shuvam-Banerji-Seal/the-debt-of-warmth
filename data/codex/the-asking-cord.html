I keep the little fire on the back boards at the Post and the cord of answers that hangs beside it. It is one work with two hands: the fire must be coaxed before anybody is looked at, and the cord must be taken up before a question is heard, and neither asks who laid the peat or tied the wool. In Stone the bog bears and the winter way goes east over it with carts that pay nothing and no reeve will stand on it, so there comes a fortnight in which the only trade on these boards is questions.

Put a question to a man and you have a difference with him before you have an answer. Put it to the board and it stays a question. A question whose answer you already know is a riddle, which is a thing the Fen can afford; a question whose answer nobody knows is a wager, and there is wagering enough here without a fire in it.

We answer in knots. Fen Cant is not written and a knot keeps when paper has gone soft at Greybank, which is not an argument for knots so much as a fact about paper. Where no Grathic word will come I have tied a knot in the margin, and I have put no word into it. Say these low, through the nose, and let the last word fall into the mud instead of finishing it. Do not look at the person you say them to.

The manner of the cord

  • A riddle is put once to the board. Ask for it again and you lay a peat-block by the fire, because the second asking is for your own pleasure and not for the hut.
  • Answer in one knot. A second knot is a different answer and is not read. A man who ties two has not understood the first.
  • The judge is the board. I say whether a knot serves and I do not say why. A reason given is a riddle given away for nothing, and the giver can take it down the mud road and sell it at the Cross.
  • The wager is a peat-block laid against the wall where the fire can see it. We count them by touch and not by eye, because the eye listens to the face and the fingers do not.
  • No slate lies on the back boards. A slate turns a question into a figure and the figure goes up to Greybank in a clean hand and comes back as something a household owes.
  • The asking-cord is the only cord in the Fen that is never untied. Ours are tied when a thing is owed and loosed when it is paid, but a riddle paid is a riddle spent, and the answer goes home in the asker’s mouth and is not entered anywhere.

There had been eleven nights of it before the Long Cold shut the road, and there would be no more until the ice went. This is the one the reed-cutter told over, in the words he used, which are short and have their ends dropped.

The four that were tied

The first went to the reed-cutter himself, come up over the winter way with a bundle on his back and the snow still standing on his shoulders. He had his answer before the verse was out, which is why he never lays a peat-block and is always first. He tied it with his teeth because both his hands were full of reed.

Ten go over and none come back wet,

no reeve will stand where the cart went;

it is paid by nobody and kept by none ---

what bears the crossing?

The winter way. He tied one loop and drew it low, the way a man ties what he cannot see. Nobody asked him which cart had gone over, which is the proper way to leave a crossing alone.

The second went to a girl who dips eels at the Line and answers a board before she answers a person.

I keep the warm behind the head;

cut me there and you learn nothing.

Count me going and count me coming,

my basket has no column for me ---

what am I?

An eel. She tied two turns with a space between them and set the cord back in my hand without letting me see her thumb do it. Two counts, going and coming, and no column for either. When the factor’s man asked which count she meant, she told him to ask the eel, which is the whole of the answer and the manner of it.

The third went to the man from Greybank, who had road-mud on his boots that had dried the colour of the clay dish and would not come off on the edge of his hand.

It holds a whole country under a skin;

we do not dig it out, we ask.

It has no keys, no slate, no clean coat,

and a priest come up from the south called it a sister ---

what is it?

The peat, by the old name. He tied a square knot, neat enough to lie flat on a clerk’s table, and I made him tie it again, because a knot that lies flat is a knot somebody can read and this one was not meant for reading. He tied the second the way we do and put his hand over it a moment before he let go, which is not the manner of it either, though I have never known a man from the factor’s house do it any other way. The peat keeps what it keeps. It has no door for him to put his hand against.

The fourth I put to the fire, because the room had got noisy and wanted quieting, and a question put by the boards is not scored against anybody.

A thing is lent and called a house;

it stands on legs that are not its own.

The water calls it in when it pleases

and the hut does not go with it ---

what is it?

Dry ground. An old woman who had come in to warm her hands put her palm flat on the cord and took it away again without tying anything. That is an answer in this country, and the oldest one here. I have seen a man from the College stand on the Post and ask where the dry ground is, and he was standing on it when he asked, and nobody told him, because a thing you point at is a thing you can put a figure to.

The fifth, which was tied low

After the fourth the hut went quiet in the way a hut goes quiet when there is nothing left to say and nobody will leave. A child of the Post, nine or ten, who had been sitting with her back to the fire and her hands in her sleeves all evening, came and put her fist into the cord. She tied one knot below the others, low down where the fingers go by teeth and thumb and the eye cannot follow, and she set the cord back exactly where it hung. She did not say what the riddle was and nobody asked her. A knot tied low is a thing the tie-keeper knows and nobody else does, and asking a child to say it aloud is a way of making her lose it.

She went out on the winter way before the going of the light with the cord still warm from her hand. In the morning there was a round dry place on the wall by the fire where no peat-block had stood, and I have not found whose it was. I have not asked. The knot is still on the cord. It does not lie flat with the others and no thumb of mine has ever read it, which is how I know it was tied by somebody who did not want it carried away.

The tally, nicked in the lintel

Question — Put by — Answer tied — Peat-block

The crossing — the reed-cutter — the winter way — laid, not counted

The two counts — a girl from the Line — an eel — laid, not counted

The sister — a man from Greybank — the peat — laid twice, once for the wrong knot

The house — the fire — dry ground — none

The low knot — a child of the Post — not read — not scored

What the factor’s clerk did

A clerk of the factor’s house borrowed the leaf on a market morning when the square was full; there was ink settled in two fingers of a soft cuff and he laid his hand flat before his eye went to the words, which is the manner of that house. He set the questions down as queries entered and the answers as responses recorded, valued each peat-block at a half-mark of warm-stone and wrote gaming, not commercial across the head of the tally, so that a night of asking came out as a night of trading with the profit left off. Against the low knot he wrote no answer tendered; struck. He asked who had tied it. I told him a child of the Post, and that she had given no name to the riddle, which is a true thing and not the thing he wanted, and he wrote party unidentified.

He also wrote one part in twelve reserved to the fire above the peat-blocks, because one in twelve is printed and a printed thing travels. I have left his line standing. The fire has never asked for a share and has never kept a slate, but a figure is a thing that goes where the fire does not, and it is easier to write the figure than to sit and watch a peat-block burn.

I have set the cord over his hand and left his hand standing on the leaf, the way a board keeps a man’s weight and the water’s mark and does not choose between them. He takes out whoever did the thing, as though a sentence stood truer with nobody in it. I tie the doer back in, because a child of the Post wants to know which hand went into the cord, and there is always a hand.

In the margin, in a slate-runner’s sloping hand

I carry the slate edge-on and never look at what is on it. The figure that goes up to Greybank does not know what came over the winter way or who tied what by the fire. I have walked this road nine years and never opened one.

Below, in mine: she said it to the board and would not say it twice. I have not asked. A person who carries a slate is not a person who reads one, and the road cares about neither.

At the head and in the gutter

The peat thumb at the head is mine; I hold the leaf down in the draught that comes up between the boards and do not know I have marked it, and peat goes into the grain and will not come out, which is why we sign in clay here and not in ink. The tideline is bog-water off a pail set down while I was telling the girl to eat and she would not. The pressed reed came from the reed-cutter’s bundle and has gone flat and pale where he laid it across the second leaf while he decided whether to pay for his second asking, and did not. The hook nick is from a basket set down by a man paying for six eels and charged for five, or the other way, which he has not settled. The slime in the fold came off that basket and goes soft when the weather goes soft, which tells me when to shut the oiled reed. The glass-eel thread in the lower margin is fine as a hair and came from nobody I can name. The grey along the outer fold came off a cuff that had no business lying on the back boards, and I have not swept it. There is a Last Day in Sill and the corners are swept there in the morning and burnt and called dealt with. There is no Last Day at the Post, and nothing here is swept while the bog is bearing. The knot of grey wool through the hole in the first leaf is mine, tied for the word that would not come into Grathic, and I will not undo it to see what is inside.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

Four answers went home in men’s mouths. One went down the winter way still tied.

The board was answered. The slate was not.

I asked the reed-cutter, the girl from the Line and the man who came up from Greybank, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written. The leaf goes back under the loose board with the market stick and the sayings, and the cord goes back on its nail beside the fire. In Ilmen the weather will take the ice off the bog and the carts will pay to cross and there will be better things to do of an evening than ask one another what we already know. The child will be a year older and may come back and may not, and I shall not ask her about the low knot when she does. If the factor’s clerk comes again with his pen I shall let him count what can be counted, and I shall keep the cord where the fire can see it, because a knot is not a figure and a figure cannot sit here and warm itself.
