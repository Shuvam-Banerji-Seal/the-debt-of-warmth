I keep the night-store of a salt boat out of the Quay at Vhal, and that is the whole of my post. The boat belongs to a house that holds its share through its women, and a man who marries into it is come aboard and has his say counted at half, which is an arrangement I have never had cause to test. I am not aboard by marriage. I am set there by the Council and paid by the watch, and my work is to see that what the boat keeps warm at night is still warm in the morning.

The tallies I wrote on first went soft in a week. The sea took the figures off clean, which was a mercy of a kind: a figure the sea has eaten is a figure no clerk can enter. I write in charcoal now, on the backs of spoiled leaves and on folds of salt-paper I have stiffened with tar. Charcoal goes into the grain and salt-paper rots, and a contract that rots is held a virtue here, being a contract that was never quite made. This book is the one thing aboard that the damp has not been asked to settle.

The bloom at the head is salt. I have not brushed it off. A book that has never been out in a Sallow winter was never taken on watch.

What it comes for

It does not fight. This is the thing a new man gets wrong and the thing that costs him his breakfast. A man looks for teeth and a way of holding on, and meanwhile the loaf goes.

It comes for the coal that has not gone out, the warmed stone, the crust still holding heat from the oven, the fish a man has set by to thaw. It takes the thing a body is keeping against the cold and leaves the body colder than it was. I have watched it come down out of the grey light with its feet white and go off with a coal in its beak, and the hand that had been holding the coal stayed shut for a moment after, the way a hand does when there is nothing in it and it has not yet understood that.

It is a gull. It has a gull’s beak with a small hook at the end and a gull’s way of turning its head to look at a thing sideways. It is the colour of wet stone with a darker grey across the back, and the salt stands on it in a fine white crust at the feet and along the leading edge of the wing, so that a bird come in off the pans looks as though it has been walking in flour. It is no bigger than the gulls that follow the ferry at Greybank, and a man who has seen one at the fishstairs would know this one for a cousin of it. The difference is what it wants.

It has no fear of a man on his feet. I have stood with my spear across my knee and had one come within two paces of the store and go on with its own work. But let a man turn his back on the store, or let him sit down with the crust in his hand, and it is gone in the time it takes to say the tide-word for the turn. There is no courage in testing this twice, and I have not tested it twice.

The turn it comes on

The Sallow runs against the wind. Every child on the Quay knows it and every man from the clay country has to be told it twice, because their rivers go the way the air goes and they expect the same here. Our water follows the Warm and not the moon, and the dippers read the draw off it before they bless the nets, and tell the Council only what is profitable, which is a Vhaleni way of telling the truth. The gull comes on the turn of the draw, in the hour when the water is deciding which way it will go and has not yet chosen.

Our store is a clay pot of coals under blind ash, set in a box in the lee of the mast, and a warm-stone out of the Keel that came south on the Hearth Road and is worth more than my year’s pay. The stone goes in the pot at the second watch and comes out at the first. Between those two hours the pot is the warmest thing on the boat, and there is always something in the dark that knows it.

In a hard winter they come inland. They have been seen as far as Greybank, over the pass where the clay country gives way to the salt, and a man will tell you they come for the uncollected that lie along that road. I have seen them come for a baker’s basket outside the tally house. I have set down both things and joined neither.

The song

Sung going out on the first watch, not coming in. The fourth line is the old line and every man on the Quay sings it wrong on purpose, because a song that gives the bird a reason is a song a man can carry home and set by his own fire. I have set it down as it is sung, with the wrong line standing and the gap where the men leave it.

One for the ebb and one for the draw,

one for the turn that brings it in;

it takes the coal before it takes the crust,

and takes the stone the boat kept warm.

Salt on the foot and salt on the wing,

salt from the pan and not from the dead;

a thing that can lift is a thing that can go,

and the teller’s the same on the either shore.

Count them going and count them back,

a bit for the bundle, a feather for the sore;

we sing the old line wrong and leave the gap ---

the place where the warm thing stood before.

The gap is the space where the coal was. The men know it and sing it and have never once said what it is a gap in, which is the nearest thing we have to a grace here. There is a rower on the pulled benches who sings the last line twice and will not say why, and I have let him keep it.

The tally

These are the watches I kept a true account of. There were others I did not write because my hands were full and one I did not write because I had drunk. I have set down both sorts as they stand. The last column is the clerk’s, not mine. I counted what went; he entered what could be paid.

Watch — Place — Draw — What went — What came back — Entered

Stone 9 — Quay, against the salt pans — turn — a crust — none — 0

Stone 14 — Off the Glaze Cliffs — slack — two coals — one, dropped in the water — 0

Stone 21 — The coast pass, wind off the clay — ebb — a warmed stone — none — 0

Stone 27 — Quay, under the tally house — turn — a fish set out to thaw — the bones — 0

Ilmen 3 — Greybank landing — draw — a crust, a bit of rope — the rope — 0

Ilmen 12 — Quay, after the thaw — slack — one coal — none — nil

In the narrow upright hand of the clerk of the tide-tally house, struck through the “What went” column: Fowl not a vessel. Petty taking by a bird: no deduction arises. Warm in a boat’s store is not entered. Account stands.

I have left his hand standing. A clerk’s hand has its use: it keeps a man from arguing with what he cannot change. But the stone that went on Stone 21 came up the Hearth Road and somebody paid for the keeping of it, and the clerk has put nil against it with a neat instrument and no name on it. I have written the stone in the margin below, where his column cannot reach, and I have put my own mark beside it in charcoal.

Below, in my hand: One warm-stone, gone. Paid for on the Keel. Not entered anywhere, and missed by nobody but me.

The feathers and the meat

The feathers hold salt. A bundle is a bit in Vhal and is used to salt a wound, which is a brute method and does sometimes work, though a physician will tell you it is not a physic and a Fen trader will tell you it is the only physic the poor can afford. I have seen a man with a gull-feather laid in a cut that would not close, and the cut went on being a cut. I have seen another go quiet under it for a tend. I have set down both and have no rule out of them.

The meat is not eaten. They say it makes a man thin, and a Vhaleni will say this with one shoulder raised, which is a way of saying that the matter is closed and the meat is not worth the opening. The dippers bless their nets against the bird at dawn in the pans and will not do it while a man from the tally house is standing on the board. I have asked one why. He said a blessing said in front of a clerk is a blessing with a figure on it, and a blessing with a figure on it has already been half bought. I have written his words and not his name.

What they say at the pans

They say the gull carries the dead out to the Sallow, that a drowned man cannot pay the ferry and goes instead in the beak of a bird. There are songs for it with a woman’s name in them and a wrong done at a landing. I have heard them and I have no quarrel with a song. I have watched one carry a coal, a crust, a fish, a bit of rope, and a child’s wooden boat from a bollard on the Quay, and what it carried was what it could lift. It did not choose between the boat and the coal. One was heavier and it took the coal.

The salt on its feet is the colour of the pans. That much I have seen. I have not written where the salt comes from beyond that, because a man who writes a cause into a book he keeps in a tarred wrap is a man who will be asked for it by somebody with a slate. The pans are a place a man can stand and look at. What the bird wants with the warmth it carries I have left where I found it.

Written in the pale ink of a dipper on a corner of salt-paper gone soft, the words showing through where the damp has taken them: It takes the warm and it gives nothing. We say it takes the dead and we are kinder to it than it is to us. Bless the net and do not ask the bird.

I have put the dipper’s line above my own. He wrote it at the pans and I wrote mine on a boat, and neither of us has asked the bird anything.

At the head and in the margins

The bloom at the head came up out of the paper itself and is the only thing in this book that entered without a hand. The tar at the fold is off the wrap and has taken two lines of the first watch and made them slow to read, which is no great loss. The tideline along the gutter came from a morning when the Sallow came up over the rail and into the box where I kept the leaves; the figures ran together and I did not write them again. The nick at the fore-edge is a fish-hook, from the watch when we lost the fish and found the hook in the cover instead. The brown spot on the second leaf fell there from a boy’s finger when he sat down beside me with a cut in it, and I have not scraped it off, because it is the only entry on this leaf that cost anybody anything. The charcoal is rubbed thin where I carried the wrap against my thigh. The clerk’s two rulings I have not touched. The song is as the men sang it, with the old line wrong and the gap standing.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

It carries what can be carried. It is the boat that pays.

I asked a clerk, a dipper, a rower on the benches, and a man who had lost a stone, and had four answerings and no hand. I have left the line where it was written.

There is a reach beyond the Glaze Cliffs where the lead comes down into the sand and the gulls stand in the shallows with their feet white to the knee, and they are not the white of the pans. I have not walked that reach. Next Stone I will take the boat in on the turn with a fresh pot of coals and a coil of cord, and I will count them going and count them back, and if they carry something out of that water that no hand on the boat put there I will set that down too, in charcoal, with the verb left in it and no figure at the end of the row.
