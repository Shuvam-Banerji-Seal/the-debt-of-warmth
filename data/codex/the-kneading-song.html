I work the lump at the beating-bench two doors below the slip pits, twelve seasons at it. A lump comes off the bank with a face as smooth as a loaf and something shut up inside it that has no name in the yard. We call it the little room. You drive it out with the heel before it goes to the wheel, because the wheel will not find it and the fire will. A pot with a room left in it goes into the kiln whole and comes out with a seam you can lay your thumb in, and nobody asks whose hand let the room stay.

A man came up from Greybank with a soft cuff and ink settled in two fingers of it and stood at the bench end asking what we said at the work. I told him. He wrote it down before I had finished, with the doing taken out and a figure put where a place should stand, and went off up the Clay Road with his boots clean. So I have set the words down as we say them, to the lump and not to the women beside the bench, and put the verbs back where he took them out.

The clay at the head is mine. I held the docket down with the hand I work with and did not know I had marked it. Clay goes into the grain and no washing takes it out, which is why we sign with it here and not with ink.

How it is worked

  • Set the lump on the round the last one left. A bench wiped clean is a bench with no memory, and the first lump off a clean bench hides its room where the hand does not look.
  • Beat with the heel, not the fist. A fist leaves a hard place that throws the wheel crooked; the heel shuts the room without marking the face.
  • Listen as you go. A lump that answers solid has no room in it; one that answers hollow gives back a sound like a cup with a hair in it, and the hand goes back to where the sound came from.
  • Turn after three thumps and look at the cut face. The room shows as a dark pinprick no bigger than a flea, and a pinprick left in the clay is a pot with a seam two months hence.
  • Press it shut with the thumb. Do not cut it out. Clay taken out will not marry to the clay about it, and the fire finds the place you made as surely as the room you left.
  • Knuckle a worked lump before it goes up. A whole one answers full; one with a room still in it gives back flat. The wheel takes both and does not say which was which, so the knuckle has to say it first.
  • Count the thumps going in, never the pots coming down off the rack. A count coming down is a count of pots, which a clerk can enter and nobody wants. The fourth is the turning, and a turning is not a thump.
  • Do not sing it on an Account-day. The bell goes four, four and a gap, and the gap is the turning; a working-song set against it comes home wrong in the wrist.
  • The last part is kept back, for when the kiln-master has gone up the terrace and the man with the soft cuff has gone back over the north bridge and the bench can hear itself again.

The song

Said low into the lump, with the heel flat. One verse to three thumps going in. The fourth is the turning of the lump and is not said aloud. The refrain goes into the bench and not across the yard; said to a person it becomes an account, and there is enough account at the Cross without bringing it to the slip pits.

The lump comes down from the bank with a face like bread,

smooth as a loaf and as quiet under the hand;

but there’s a little room shut up in the middle of it

that the wheel won’t find and the fire will.

Thump it and turn it, three to the lump,

and the fourth is the turning no heel let fall;

the room lies where the knuckle can’t come at it,

and the fire finds out what the hand let go.

Beat with the heel and not with the fist;

a fist leaves a hard place the wheel will remember,

and a crooked wall comes up round a hard place

and opens at the rim when the kiln takes it.

Thump it and turn it, three to the lump,

and the fourth is the turning no heel let fall;

the room lies where the knuckle can’t come at it,

and the fire finds out what the hand let go.

A solid lump answers back and the note goes through;

one with a room in it gives back small and flat,

like a cup with a hair in it, like a pot set down wrong ---

and the bench keeps both sounds and tells neither.

Thump it and turn it, three to the lump,

and the fourth is the turning no heel let fall;

the room lies where the knuckle can’t come at it,

and the fire finds out what the hand let go.

Turn it after three and look at the cut face;

the room shows dark where the wire went through,

a speck the width of a flea, and a flea’s worth of air

is a seam you can lay a thumb in come Smoke.

Thump it and turn it, three to the lump,

and the fourth is the turning no heel let fall;

the room lies where the knuckle can’t come at it,

and the fire finds out what the hand let go.

One in twelve is printed and read twice at the Cross,

once for the moot and once for them that came late;

the bell goes four, four and a gap, and the gap is the turn,

and the figure you heard is not the figure they paid.

Thump it and turn it, three to the lump,

and the fourth is the turning no heel let fall;

the room lies where the knuckle can’t come at it,

and the fire finds out what the hand let go.

There is a space before the last refrain in the singing, the length of a hand laid flat on the lump where you feel for the room before the heel comes down. I have left it on the docket. A clerk put a round nought in it once, which is a thing you can hold up and look at; a hand laid flat is not.

The part they keep back

Not said while the kiln-master stands at the bench end, and not while a man with a satchel is on the boards. Said to the bench and not across the yard.

There was a lump one Ember went up with its room

and came back a pot with a seam down the side;

the rack set it by the wall with the crack showing

and nobody said whose heel had let it stand.

We go round the fourth with the lump the way a house goes round

what it will not sweep. The clerk put a nought where it turned;

a nought is a figure and travels up the road,

and a turning is a place and stays where the clay put it.

There is no verse after it; the next thing is the heel going back into the lump, which has never wanted one.

What the kiln-master ruled

He came down off the terrace on the wrong wind with the sweet edge of the glaze at one corner of his mouth and read the docket with one eye on the wheel-shed. Against the room lies where the knuckle can’t come at it he wrote in broad charcoal: Air inclusion to be proved at the wheel. Sound bodies only to the firing. No loss arises. He underlined it twice. Against the fourth is the turning he set: Turning is not a count. Nothing enters. He did not look at the bench while he wrote.

Underneath, in mine: he is right about the count and wrong about the loss. A turning is not a thump, but it is where the hand finds what would otherwise go up inside a pot, and a room shut in the clay is not nothing because it cannot be entered. He reads with one eye on the kiln, as a man does who has never stood at a bench and listened to a lump answer back.

What the factor’s clerk did

The factor’s clerk borrowed the leaf at Greybank on the morning the tile was weighed, with his cuff gone soft from washing and ink sitting in two fingers of it. He took the verbs out and set his words above mine, the way a newer repair lies over an older one on a pot, and I have left his hand standing and put ours underneath.

  • At one with a room in it gives back small and flat he wrote vessel unsound; not a chargeable defect, and underlined it twice. He thinks a flat note is a pot that has reached its term. At this bench a flat note is a room the heel missed and the bench was not told about.
  • At the fire finds out what the hand let go he wrote loss at firing, unascertained, which takes the hand out and leaves the fire standing with nothing to find.
  • At the room shows dark where the wire went through he wrote inclusion, sampled, and set a figure against it. A speck in a lump is not a sample and has never had a value, which is why the yard asks the pot and not the clerk what a room cost.
  • At one in twelve is printed and read twice he wrote deduction current, one part in twelve; twice read, and set the printed figure above it. He has entered the number that was read and not the number that was paid, which is the whole distance between a cuff and a wrist.
  • At the three thumps he wrote three struck, fourth nil. The fourth is not nil. It is the turning of the lump, which a man can carry away in a figure and a woman cannot.
  • Where I had left the space he put a round nought and wrote vacancy beneath it. A nought is a number and travels. A hand laid flat on a lump is a place, and stays where it was put.

What a woman of the firing work put in the margin

The thick charcoal belongs to a woman who has carried lumps from this bench to the wheel since before the Glaze Wars and has had the pale line at the gum these six years. I have copied it as it stands, without making the hands agree.

At the rack set it by the wall with the crack showing: There has been a seam down every firing since I came to this yard. I have asked the pot what it cost and the pot has told me, which is more than the slate has ever done. I have not told whose heel let the room stand, and I have not set the seam on a slate, which is a thing I will answer for somewhere else.

At turn it after three and look at the cut face: A girl turned hers only at the end and sent up six lumps in one morning. Three went flat and the other three went whole, and nobody could say which hand made the difference, least of all hers. She still works at that bench. I have not taught her to turn, and the bench has not either.

At the head and in the gutter

The clay thumb at the head is mine and has gone into the grain; nothing here is gathered and burnt on a Last Day, so the grey goes into the next season under the dust-cloth. The gutter-line came out of the Slip Brook and has taken the edge off one word of the fifth verse; I have not written it back. The curl of eel-sinew across the second leaf came off the cutting-thread and I have left it over the word it landed on. The grey flake in the fold came off the round under the lump and is lighter in the hand than a good flake, which is the lesson and no use to a clerk. The hair came off a carter’s horse going up the Clay Road; it is the only part of this docket that has walked the road on its own. The pinprick through the fourth verse is where a wire went through the leaf and showed a dark speck where the verse speaks of one; I have not widened it, because a wire leaves the hole and a clerk leaves the nought, and neither is the room in the clay. The green bead came off a girl with sweetness at her mouth who touched the leaf and went back up the terrace; I have not washed it. The sour ring is small ale set down at the first verse and taken up at the last, gone the colour of the bench it stood on. The grey along the outer fold came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying on a bench. Nobody will own it.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The bench keeps the round. The lump keeps the room.

The pot goes up the road with the seam in its side.

The slate keeps neither, and goes up the road.

I asked the woman of the firing work, the kiln-master, and a carter waiting with his horse at the yard gate, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written.

The docket goes back under the beating-bench. Tomorrow a lump will come down with a face like bread, three thumps will go into it and the fourth be the turning where no heel fell, and if the clerk comes again he may have the nought. He cannot have the hand laid flat, because it is a place, and he has no word for a place that stays where the clay put it.
