To Gessa at the Glaze Room, High Kiln, Sill, who taught me to taste with the back of the tongue and never to say what I had tasted. From Wenna at the furnaces, Vhal, in Ilmen of Y.H. 813.

I write on the back of a Council contract because there is no paper in this town that was made to keep a sentence. Salt-paper is made to rot. A clerk of the Council will tell you the rotting is a virtue, and he will tell it with a straight face and a cuff soft from washing, because a contract that goes soft in the damp is a contract nobody can read against a man, which is the whole of their justice and the whole of their trade. I have written my wages three times on three such sheets and had two of them go. This is the third and the ink is already coming up in little pale islands where the salt has worked. If it reaches you with the verbs gone, know that they were mine when I set them down.

Do not rub the second fold. There is a bead of cliff-glass pressed into it and the edge will take your thumb.

I have been at the furnaces eleven months. You will want to know first whether the Vhaleni taste their work. They do. That is the answer I came eighty leagues to find and did not want to bring back. The glass women rub their gums with cliff-glass and say the sweetness answers sweetness; a salt-wife of the Quay gives them seawater to drink and says the sea has been paid for already. A physician come up from Greybank in Y.H. 798 left a page in the glass-house saying the sweetness comes from the lead, and the page came back to me with the word taken clean away, as though a word could be scraped off paper the way slip is scraped off a bench. I have written it here in my own hand.

It is lead.

I knew that in High Kiln at nine and I have crossed a country to learn it again under a Vhaleni name. They call it the glass-sickness here. It is the green with a Vhaleni name written on it. The pale line comes up at the gum before a girl has thought to look at her own mouth in a pot’s glaze, and after that the hand goes, slowly, so slowly that a woman can go on holding a cup for years and blame the cup. I have watched it happen to a girl who could taste a firing by the smell of the room. She tells it now by the colour of her own fingertips, which is an instrument, of a kind, and no kinder than the last one.

When I left you I thought the green was a thing of our room: the sweetish lead of Ember, the mix tasted on the back of the tongue, the wheel turning under the window, the pot tapped with a knuckle before anybody said it was finished. I wrote you from the road that the glass men of Vhal used a different flux and did not taste their work at all. I wrote it in a confident hand. I have set that hand beside this one and let them stand. A confident hand is what a girl of sixteen uses when she wants a sentence to be true more than she wants it to be accurate, which is the same defect I have spent my life covering with lead.

The furnaces take sand from the beach and lead from the Glaze Cliffs, blue-green and sweet-smelling in the heat, and melt them together into glass that goes as far as Sere. The women who work it have the green at the mouth and a hand that will not hold a cup steady, and one of them told me her mother used to tap the ribs of a sick man and say he had gone flat. I did not ask what he had been returned as. There is no column for it in the Council’s figures, and I have learned not to put my questions where a clerk can reach them.

The proportion, as I set it down in charcoal on the back of the third sheet

What goes into the pot — What the mixer is told — What I have seen

Lead out of the blue cliffs — A flux, and a flux is nothing to speak of — Lead. It goes in by the handful and comes out at the gum.

Beach sand, washed in seawater — The sea takes the sting out of it — The sea takes the figure off the contract. It does not take the sting out of the sand.

The tasting, twice in a firing — A glaze-mixer tastes. That is the trade. — A glaze-mixer tastes. That is the price of the trade. These are the same sentence said in two rooms.

The tap with the knuckle — A full note means the glaze has filled the flaw — A full note means the flaw is under the glaze, which is not the same as gone.

The green at the mouth — The mark of a girl who does her work — The beginning of the sweet-sickness, which you told me at nine and which I would not hear at sixteen.

You will say I have put the price in the column where the proportion should be. It is the only column in this trade that tells the truth.

The mixing song

Sung low at the wheel, to the pot and not to a person. The second verse is the one you taught me; I have set your correction underneath where you wrote it, and left my own words standing above, as you left mine.

Mix it with the back of the tongue,

lead in the morning and slip at noon;

a cup that sings has a flaw beneath,

and the flaw keeps the note of its own.

Two for the wheel and two for the fire,

two for the girl who knows her place;

the green comes up where the gum was pale,

and nobody counts it, and nobody says.

Three for the crack and one for the glaze,

a whole return in a beautiful dish;

what it covers is what it keeps,

and the mixer pays for the dish.

The Council clerk at Greybank borrowed the sheet on the morning after the salt boats came up, being a man who cannot leave paper alone. He writes in the neat upright hand they teach, with the verbs taken out and everything in the passive. Against it is lead he set the flux is retained above it, and the lead went out of the sentence as though a lead could be carried off in a cuff. Against the place where I had left the payer unnamed he put a round nought and wrote vacancy under it. Where I wrote the mixer pays for the dish he set the measure is banked, which is true in the way a closed room is true. I have put the verbs back underneath and left his hand standing on the back, where it belongs. He takes them out because a thing always true has no need of verbs. I put them in because a girl at a bench at three in the morning wants to know which hand to hold the cup with.

I have two questions and I am putting them where you can answer them without a slate in the room. The first: is the green at your own mouth wider than it was when I went? Do not tell me it is the mark of skill. I know the district’s saying and I know what it is for. The second: a woman at the Quay says a child upriver turns his head toward a pot a breath after the knuckle falls, and that the yard calls it quickness. Is that a thing people say, or a thing you have seen? I am not asking for a name. A name is the one thing a girl in Vhal can send home in a letter and have entered against her before she has taken her coat off.

In the daughter’s hand on the back, in charcoal, written at High Kiln in Seed of Y.H. 813

Your grandmother’s line for the second verse was different and you have left it out. It went:

The green comes up where the gum was pale;

the house calls it skill and the girl calls it hers.

I have written it above yours and yours stays where you set it. A song with two second verses is a song a woman can sing either way, which is the only kind we have ever had the sense to keep.

I have not answered the question about your mouth because you did not ask it. You asked about mine. You have got thinner in the wrist and I have got greener, and those are two facts in two rooms, and if you put them in one sentence you will have made a cause and a cause wants a name. Keep them apart.

There is no such thing as a flux that is nothing to speak of. A man who says that to you is either selling you the mix or has already sold the recipe to the factor at Greybank, and in this house those are the same trade with two different cuffs. Vinegar takes the edge off a tongue for an hour and off nothing else. I have held the price where it stood this year and Sella has not asked why, which is a kindness from a woman who cannot afford one and is therefore worse than a bill. Do not write it down. A kindness entered is a kindness gone.

The yard says a figure read aloud is not the figure paid. I do not know that as a fact and I have not set it down as one, so do not set it down in a letter with your name at the foot of it. Berr Clay came to the block with the red cord folded under his coat and wiped his thumb twice before he touched nothing and read one in twelve over the basket, twice, once for the moot and once for them that came late. The bell went four, four and a gap and nobody crossed himself. That much a girl can carry away without a clerk taking it off her. The rest is a place, and a place is not a debt, whatever the College calls it.

I have tied two knots in a strip of grey wool and sent it with the man. One is for the eleven months you have been gone and one is for the firings I have mixed since you left. The wool stays where I put it and no clerk can spell it, which is more than I can say for the sheet you sent me. Your father’s tap is on the bench and nobody has used it. The cup you chipped is where you left it, with the chip turned to the wall.

Come back in Ember if the green goes darker. Do not come back for a figure.

At the head and in the gutter

The green at the head is mine, off my thumb setting the sheet down on the bench in the Glaze Room with the mix still on it, and it has gone into the grain so that the whole sheet smells faintly of a firing. The bead in the second fold was pressed in by a girl of the furnaces who watched me write and said nothing, and has left a sweet smear that has not dried in two months. The salt along the gutter came up out of the paper and not out of the sea, which is a thing only a person who has kept salt-paper knows the difference of. The ring on the third leaf is vinegar from the tasting-cup; the cup was set down while I was telling a woman to eat and she would not. The lead at the foot is not from the mix but from the rim of the dish, and I have left it where it fell. Two words have gone out of the second verse where the damp took them clean, and I have not written them in again. A song with two words gone is a song said slowly, and everything in this trade is said slowly.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The pot keeps the flaw and the glaze keeps the name;

the green at the mouth keeps what the mixer paid.

I asked a dipper, a salt-wife of the Quay and a factor come up from Greybank, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The sheet goes back into the sailcloth and the sailcloth into the tin among the tasting-spoons. On the next salt boat a girl will press another bead into a contract and write home that the Vhaleni do not taste their work, and I shall not correct her, because a girl has to cross a country to learn what her mother knew at nine, and a correction sent too early is only another kind of glaze.
