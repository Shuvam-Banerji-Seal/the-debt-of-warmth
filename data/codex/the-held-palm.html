I keep the mothers’ share-lines on this Quay and mend the rope that holds the salt galleys when they come in, which are two works that would each keep a man busy and neither of them pays a woman for the trouble of writing. The children clap on the dry boards above the water-line, and I have set their words down as they came, with the clerk’s hand above them where he put it and theirs underneath where they stand, as the Quay keeps the wet mark under the dry one.

The salt at the head is my own thumb. I set the sheet on a bollard before I had done with the rope and did not know I had marked it. The grey came off a cuff that had no business lying on a coil, and I have not swept it. There is no Last Day on a Quay; the salt eats what a broom would take and the tide takes the rest.

The boards and the hands, as the children keep them

Played on the dry boards of the Quay in Ember and Grain, when the salt has been raked into heaps and the boards will bear a foot and a clap. Not in the Five, when the children are made to stand and listen; not in rain, because a salt-paper sheet left in the wet is a debt made nothing and a clap on a wet board brings nobody home. In the hard months they play it in the rope-walk, where the tar pots stand and the floor is dry under the racks.

Two children play. One is the mother, because shares pass through mothers on this Quay and nobody has thought to ask why. One is the aboard, the one married into the boat, whose word on the boat is weighed at half. They are called these names and never their own, for a name said on a Quay travels up the Salt Road in a cuff and comes back entered.

Each holds both palms up. A clap of palm to palm, mother to aboard, is a whole. A clap where the aboard lifts one hand and keeps it in the air is the half, and the hand in the air is called the place, which is not a fraction but a child standing with her hand up. One line of the verse is one clap. The verse is said to the rope and the salt, never to a face; said to a face it becomes a bargaining, and there is bargaining enough on this Quay without teaching it to children.

The clap must sound, palm to palm. A clap that goes edgewise, or slaps a wet board, sends the player to the coil at the end where the wet ones sit with their hands folded, and she comes back only when three voices say together, The rope calls. Nobody counts how often this happens. There is no winning in this game and no last clap. When the tenth has been held the hands come down together and the count goes back the way it came.

The counting-out, said over the bead

A glass bead out of the furnaces, which the children call a tide-bead, is passed from fist to fist along the row, right fists held out and knuckles up. The child who says the words touches each fist in turn and does not look at the faces. The fist the bead lies in at the last word is the mother; the next fist is the aboard, and the aboard does not choose it, which the children accept as a punishment and the share-women accept as practice.

Bead for the glass and tar for the boat,

salt on the board and a rot in the note;

pass it low and pass it dumb ---

the fist it stops in is the one that’s come.

The bead going round the row is called the boat. An old woman here will tell you a boat goes round and comes back with the same hands, which is a thing no factor at Greybank has ever entered.

The clapping count, as it is clapped

Nine whole claps going out, then the palm held, then nine claps coming back. The tenth is not a clap. At it the aboard raises one palm and holds it up while the mother’s hand stays where it is; both stand still under the held hand, feet on the board and chins down. A player who claps under it has gone into the water and sits on the coil until the rope calls. The coming back is clapped to the same nine lines read backward, and the last clap is the first line said again.

One for the keel and one for the oar,

one for the rope on the galley floor;

one for the salt in the raker’s shoe,

one for the gull and the coal it flew;

one for the lead in the furnace sand,

one for the contract that will not stand;

one for the tide that runs ‘gainst the wind,

one for the share that the mother holds in;

nine for the board and the boat’s own name ---

the tenth is a palm and it isn’t a claim.

The brine-gull line came up the Salt Road, they say, and the children will not give it up. They clap it to a gull that went off upriver with a coal out of a banked fire that was not yet cold, which is a thing a child can see and a clerk cannot enter, and so it stays.

What is said at the held palm

Said low, to the salt, while the hand is up. All three say the last two lines together.

The hand’s up. The boat’s still.

The wind’s against the water and the water has its will.

Say it to the salt and not to me ---

a half’s a body standing, not a fee.

The verse they do not clap while a tally-man is on the boards

I have this from the child who was aboard, with the space left before it, which is the space the children leave in the clapping. She said it to the coil with her back turned and made me set it down with none of my verbs in it. A verse clapped to a board wants nothing added, and a verse clapped to a man wants everything taken away.

One for the warrant and two for the tide,

three for the oar and the hands it tried;

four for the bench and the seven-year term,

five for the cuff and the figure it’ll confirm;

the half stands up and the half stands still,

and what goes up the Salt Road comes back as a figure and not as a girl.

The pulled benches take a man for seven years, or until his hands give out; a Vhaleni hull is never long from the oars. The child who said this had an uncle on a term and would not say whether his hands had given out, which is a child’s way of keeping two things apart without putting a word between them. I have put no word between them either.

What the tally-man did

A clerk come down from the tide-tally house borrowed the leaf on the morning after, with his cuff soft at the edge from washing and the ink sitting in two fingers of it and will not come out. He laid his hand on the sheet before his eyes went to the room, the way they do at that house. He set the board down as a transaction in shares and the mother as the holder of record. He wrote the aboard as a party at half interest. Against the held palm he struck the children’s words through and set above them, one half-share entered; value, one half; the residue nil. At the coming back he wrote nine operations returned; no balance outstanding. At the head of it he wrote the notice’s one in twelve, as the figure the districts are read, and set the Quay’s half beneath it, and the two figures agreed, which is a thing that has never once happened on a board. At the foot, where the hands come down and nothing is clapped, he put a round nought and wrote no remainder.

I have put the children’s words back underneath and left his standing above. He writes of a thing always true and so has no need of a hand in it; I write for a child with her palm up in the salt wind who wants to know when to bring it down. He has set a fraction where a person stood, and a fraction travels up the Salt Road in a cuff and a child does not. A man married into a boat has his word weighed at half on this Quay, and I have never argued with the share-lines; but the half in this game is a place, and a place is not the same thing as a figure however neatly it is written. A child stands in it with her chin down until the rope calls her back. A figure has no chin.

He asked what the children learned by it. I told him the count and the holding and the calling back. He said that was an account of the half-share taught before a child could read a slate, and he entered it. The children stop when a man in a soft cuff comes onto the boards. They take it up again when he is past the Council room with his satchel under his arm, and they never once clap under the held palm.

The gloss in the margin, in charcoal

The woman who blesses the nets at dawn and reads the Sallow draw pressed one line into the margin beside the ninth clap. She does not write often, and when she does she writes small, the way a person writes in a room where somebody is sleeping.

Not nine. Ten, and the tenth is a palm held up out of the count. There’s a difference, and the tally house won’t learn it.

Beside the tide that runs ‘gainst the wind she wrote: The dippers say the Sallow follows the Warm and not the moon. A child on this Quay knows the water comes against the wind and will not say what pulls it. She has been asked, and has asked the water.

I have left both where her thumb put them.

At the head and in the gutter

The salt bloom at the head is mine and has worked into the grain, which is why a Quay sheet takes a mark and never gives one back. The tar thumb is the man who holds the galleys’ rope; he does not wash before he comes to the bench and says a sheet that has never been tarred was never used on a Quay. The press of wet rope across the second leaf is from the coil the leaf lay in through a tide of rain. The glass in the fold came off a furnace barrow that went past while I was writing and gritted the sheet, and I have not picked it out. The fish-scale in the gutter is not mine and I have left it where it fell. The tideline along the foot took two words off the back of the share-sheet, and I have not written them in again, because a rhyme with two words gone is a rhyme said slowly, and all of ours are said slowly. A scrap of tarred rope-end is tied through a hole in the second leaf for the word I could not put down. I will not untie it.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The board keeps the clap. The coil keeps the child.

The tally keeps the half and has never kept the hand.

I asked the mother, the aboard and the dipper, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written. The sheet goes back into the coil under the bench, and tomorrow the salt will be raked into heaps and the tide will come up against the wind, and the children will clap nine and hold the tenth where it has always stood, with their feet on the board and their chins down. If the clerk comes again with his pen I shall put the verbs back. He may keep the half; the hand comes back at the end of every count, and no house on this Quay has ever entered it.
