I bring the broken barrow-irons down from the north gate and keep the forge’s tally in charcoal, which nobody asked me to keep. Harn cuts his count into the bench with a chisel, where it stays under the grease; I write on a slip the Cross spoiled with a wet thumb, paper no clerk will borrow for a column. I write because he spoke to a wall, and a wall keeps nothing by itself.

He spoke first to the niche, then to the tube, then to the bellows and the kiln behind the bench, and last to us, which is the order a smith uses when collectors are in the yard and somebody may be listening who wants a figure. I have set his words down in Grathic and put the verbs back underneath where he left them out.

The blue at the head came off Keldish iron, which is why I sign with a crossed tong. The grey came off a cuff and I have not swept it; there is no Last Day at this forge.

The manner of the mending, as the forge actually keeps it

Written small, from watching, not from any order the Court has printed.

  • Come with the iron before the bellows are stirred. A man who brings a tube at noon has had the morning’s heat counted for him by somebody else.
  • Set the tube on the bench with the split upward, never on the anvil. The anvil is for irons with a right side; a tube has none.
  • Tap it once. A pot answers full or flat; a cold-iron tube answers neither, and a smith who says he heard a third thing has been at the small ale.
  • Leave the clipped end in the niche. Do not count the ends or take them home.
  • When the kiln comes to its fourth, stop. Put no coal in for a fifth and say not what it would have been.
  • Wrap a tube still warm from a draw in wool. Iron takes from the hand that holds it as well as the arm it is set to.
  • When an officer asks what the niche is for, give him the number of pegs. A number is safe and does not spoil in the mouth.

The sermon, as he said it

Of the niche

He put his palm flat on the wall where the pegs stand while the bellows took a breath.

“It has no name,” he said. “The yard calls it the smith’s niche and the Court has no word for it. There are the ends of tubes in it, clipped off where I set the new rim. I have not counted them in eleven years and never will.

“A priest will tell you nothing is lost that we have kept. I have kept a wall of iron ends and there is not a measure among them. It is off a tool. If that is a keeping, it is a poor sort, and I have never asked anybody to kneel to it. Stand where you like. The wall has never asked for a knee.”

A woman with a barrow-iron asked whether the ends were part of the tubes they came from. He considered it longer than he considered the iron in his hand. “A clipped end is not the tube,” he said. “It is what the tube gave up to be made sound again. There is a difference, and I have made a living on not saying it out loud.”

Of the tube

He tapped a tube once with the chisel, lightly, the way the women at the racks tap a pot.

“A pot tells you the truth,” he said. “Full, or flat, and a flat one you can hear across the yard. This does neither. I have mended tubes eleven years and never heard one answer, and have learned not to want it to. It is not honest and it is not lying. It is a tool, and a tool has no mouth to tell you which end you are standing at.”

He turned the rim toward the light. “I mend the tube. I do not mend the man it is set to. A man from Greybank asked once whether I ever thought about the arm. I told him I thought about the rim and the heat and the price of iron, and that I had three orders that morning and would not get the fourth out if I thought about the arm. That was true. It was not the whole of the truth.”

He leaned on the bellows and let them down so the fire came up small. “I held these in the year of the Rising,” he said. “I was old enough to hold them and not old enough to be told what I was heating. I learned what they were for by watching what came back. A tube came back with a grey rim and another with a warm one, and after a while I knew which houses had sent them and which had gone cold, though nobody told me a figure.”

A girl from the Glaze Room touched the paper where I wrote that, with sweetness at one corner of her mouth. I have kept the green where she put it. She said nothing and went back up the terrace.

Of the fifth

Behind the bench the kiln kept its four low breaths, and there was the silence where the next should have stood.

“That is four,” he said. “I have not put coal to it and I will not. A man who mends for a living has to leave one thing unmended, or he will go home at the going of the light and find he has mended himself into a doctrine.”

A woman asked what the fifth would have been for. He did not turn his head. “Ask the kiln,” he said. She asked the kiln. The kiln answered as a kiln answers, in four.

I have kept this gap on its own place, with a finger between it and the gap in the song. Two things are not given in this forge. Two are two and are not one lesson.

Of the figure

A collector come up from Slate Row held a narrow slate to his forearm with the writing turned inward, and Harn went on speaking to the tube.

“One in twelve is printed on the notice,” he said. “Read twice, once for the moot and once for them that came up the road too late. I have mended the tubes that take the twelve and the tube that takes the other figure, and the two are the same iron out of the same bar and I have set the same seam in both. The iron does not know which figure it carries. I do. That is the whole of my trade and the whole of my trouble.”

He set the chisel down. “The slate does not sweat and the hand does. A clerk’s figure goes up the road in a coat and comes down in the same coat and leaves no dust. Mine stays in notches under the grease, and if you ask me what it is I shall tell you how many tubes I have mended, a safe answer that means nothing that can be entered. The measure is banked. The account stands.” He crossed two fingers over the seam and covered the act with a chisel stroke on the bench, which is how this forge covers what it has stood at and will not explain. I have written the crossing and left the covering where it was.

Of them that are drawn

A woman came in at the end with a tube wrapped in wool and set it on the bench with a copper bit beside it, not on it.

“A boy was taken from her house,” Harn said, looking at the tube and not at her. “She asked me if this was the tube that had been in his arm. I told her I could not tell one tube from another once the rim had been turned. That was true too. It is a poor thing when a man’s truth is all of it about iron.”

She took the bit away and left the tube. He set it on the shelf with the other bits nobody has asked for, which is not keeping it and not refusing it.

“They read a whole return in a clean voice for a person not in the room,” he said to the wall. “Here a woman asks me a question I can answer only by telling her what I know about rims. If there is a mercy in this trade it is in the wrapping of the iron before you take it from a man. It costs a length of wool and is entered nowhere.”

The hammer-song

Said at the anvil, stroke by stroke, never at the bench and never for a man from Greybank. There is a stroke held back before the last four lines, and I have left it as it is not struck.

Four to the anvil and one held back,

the heat goes out on the other track;

a pot says full and a pot says flat,

and iron says nothing, and that is that.

One for the hand that turned the rim,

one for the hand the tube went in;

three for the mending and one for the cost,

and the fifth is the place where the stroke was lost.

Here the hammer stays up. No sound.

The shelf keeps the ends and the wall keeps none,

the seam holds true and the trade goes on;

ask the tube what it took, it will not say —

set it down flat and take yourself away.

A girl from the Clay Yard asked what the space was for. Nobody answered her. The clerk put a round nought against it and wrote vacancy, which is a figure and not a stroke that did not happen. I have left the nought where he set it.

The pegs, as the niche keeps them

These I have set down where they stood. A figure that comes into this forge in a coat leaves nothing on the bench.

Peg — What was brought — What he said over it — What was entered

One, split at the turning — A collector’s tube, down from Slate Row — The iron does not know which figure it carried — Nothing

One, gone grey at the eye — A barrow-iron from the north gate — Iron outlasts the hand and no hand asks it to — Nothing

One, the wool still about it — A tube from the house that lost a boy — I cannot tell one tube from another — Nothing; the bit on the shelf

Under the loose peg — Four clipped ends, eleven years of them — Nothing — Nothing

There were pegs before these that Harn could set a winter to, and I have not written the winters, because a row of winters is a thing a clerk can carry away and a peg in a wall is not.

What the clerk did

An assessor’s clerk come up from Greybank to weigh the winter iron borrowed the leaf on the morning after and gave it back with the verbs taken out, as though a verb were a thing a man could carry off in a cuff and sell at the next crossing.

  • At a cold-iron tube answers neither, he wrote the instrument is neither vessel nor subject of the full note; no entry arising, above and underlined. True in the books, which is why no column has ever held a tube.
  • At the other figure, he wrote no such deduction is printed; one in twelve for every vessel, set above in the neat hand they teach at the College. Underneath, in mine: I have not written the other figure. He has not either. We have both heard it.
  • At the gap in the song, a round nought and vacancy. When I asked what a stroke was worth that had not been struck, he said nothing a clerk could enter, the first true thing he said all morning.

I have put the verbs back and left his hand above, as the forge leaves the old seam under the new rim. He takes them out because a thing always true has no need of them. I put them back because a woman with a wrapped tube wants to know who is doing the taking.

At the head and in the gutter

The blue at the head is Keldish and will not wash out of grain this soft; it is the only part of this leaf put there by the trade and not by a person. The filings came off a rim and have worked into the scorch. The brick dust is off the kiln. The tallow thumb is mine; I held the leaf low. The ring is from a cup that stood from the first voice to the last and was drunk by nobody, which is how small ale goes in Low Kiln when there is a thing to say and no proper word for it. The grey came off a cuff and nobody will own it. The bead is lead glaze, green and sweet in the wet. The end of a tube in the second fold answers neither full nor flat when I knock it, and I keep it there to remind me what neither is.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

Struck through once and written again underneath, very small:

The smith mends the tube and the tube takes from the man.

The wall keeps the ends. The shelf keeps the bits. Neither keeps a person.

A tool has no mouth, so the account is read over it in a clean voice.

Four are four, and the fifth is not owed by anybody standing here.

I asked Harn, the woman with the barrow-iron, the collector come up from Slate Row and the clerk, and had four answerings and no hand. I have left the line struck through and standing, which is the only honest way to keep a sentence you cannot sign.

The leaf goes back behind the loose peg with the clipped ends. Next repair-month the kiln will keep its four, and if the clerk comes I shall let him take the verbs out and put them back, and leave the nought in its place and the hammer up where it was held.
