I have turned at the wheel in the shed below the kiln for one-and-thirty years, and I have set this down because a man come up from Slate Row stood in the door with a slate under his arm and asked what the hands said while they worked. I told him hands don’t say. He said every trade has its count, and if I would not give him mine he would put a figure in it himself. So I have written it in the order of the turns, on the back of a rack tally that a fall of slip spoiled. The figures went before anyone could price them, which is a mercy of a kind.

A wheel song is not like a bank song. A bank song is counted in pulls, and a man wrong on one pull pays for it on the next with his shoulder. This is counted in turns of the foot, and a wrong turn cannot be taken back, because the wall has already risen wrong and the clay does not forgive. We say it down into the lump, not up into the shed. Said up into the air it becomes a thing for listening to, and a wheel that stands still while a girl at the well listens.

The count begins at the second turn. The first turn is the wheel’s own; it centres the lump and is not ours and is never counted. A clerk can put a nought against that if he likes. A nought is a figure and the first turn is not one.

How it is sung

  • Sit down into the hollow before you set the lump. The seat is worn to the shape of a body and a body new to it will slide and spend the morning finding itself. Three women of this yard have sat in that hollow before me, and my girl will sit in it after, if the shed is standing.
  • Centre on the first turn and say nothing. To say a word over the first turn is to claim it, and a claimed centring makes a wall that leans.
  • Come in on the second with the thumbs wet. Wet the hands and not the wall, the old women say; a wall drowned in water sits down while you look at it, and a wall too dry argues with the rib all morning.
  • Say the refrain into the clay and not to the girl beside you. Said to a person it becomes instruction, and instruction given at this wheel is instruction she will unlearn at somebody else’s.
  • Do not sing it over a pot set down to dry. A song said over a standing pot brings the note into the room before it is due, and a pot told it is true before the fire has tried it is generally a pot that goes flat. A master come up from the terrace said a pot that sings early sings itself out early, and I have no cause to argue with him, and none to agree.
  • Not at the Cross, and not on an Account-day. The bell there goes four, four and a gap and a song sung against that gap comes home wrong in the mouth.
  • Leave the last verse where it is left below. It is not sung at the wheel. It is said once, low, by whoever has the shed to herself, and the wheel is given its turn back afterward.

The song

The lump goes down in the first turn and the first turn is not counted. Refrain after every verse, said down into the clay. One line of the refrain for one turn of the foot.

The seat is worn hollow where my mother sat,

and I sit in the hollow and my girl will;

a body fits a hollow and the hollow keeps the shape,

and the wheel turns before the hand gets there.

Turn. The foot remembers.

Turn. The wall comes true.

The first is the wheel’s. We count from two.

Wet the hand and hold.

Let the wheel have the lump and say nothing over it,

one turn to centre and that turn is not yours;

come in on the second with the thumbs wet,

and the clay will show you where it wants to stand.

Turn. The foot remembers.

Turn. The wall comes true.

The first is the wheel’s. We count from two.

Wet the hand and hold.

Two to raise and three to bring it true,

four to take the thumb’s own printing out;

a wall hurried in the raising keeps the mark,

and a wall that keeps the mark answers flat.

Turn. The foot remembers.

Turn. The wall comes true.

The first is the wheel’s. We count from two.

Wet the hand and hold.

Too much water and it goes down sitting,

too little and it argues with the rib;

there’s a dry place in the clay that will not be reasoned with,

and the only argument that wins there is a wet thumb.

Turn. The foot remembers.

Turn. The wall comes true.

The first is the wheel’s. We count from two.

Wet the hand and hold.

The sweet comes down the terrace on a wrong wind,

and the girls that taste the glaze have a pale line at the gum;

we give the pot its shape and they give it the colour,

and the colour is paid for out of somebody’s mouth.

Turn. The foot remembers.

Turn. The wall comes true.

The first is the wheel’s. We count from two.

Wet the hand and hold.

Tap the lip with the knuckle when the wall has stood;

the full note says true and the flat note says a flaw;

the kiln-masters keep that to themselves, being their trade,

but the wheel knows it a day before the fire does.

Turn. The foot remembers.

Turn. The wall comes true.

The first is the wheel’s. We count from two.

Wet the hand and hold.

The bell goes up at the Cross four and four and a gap,

and the slate goes up the road in a clean hand;

the yard counts turns and the district counts souls,

and neither of the two will tell you what a pot’s worth.

Turn. The foot remembers.

Turn. The wall comes true.

The first is the wheel’s. We count from two.

Wet the hand and hold.

On the Last Day the grey goes out through the north gate

and the pots that failed go broken after it;

what the wheel made and the kiln kept

goes back to the ground it came out of, in its own time.

Turn. The foot remembers.

Turn. The wall comes true.

The first is the wheel’s. We count from two.

Wet the hand and hold.

There is a space before the last refrain in the singing, the length of one turn of the foot. I have left it on the tally. It is the space the first turn stands in, which is the wheel’s and not ours, and it wants exactly that much room and no more.

The last verse, which is not sung at the wheel

Said once, low, when the shed is empty. I have put it after the song and not into it, because a verse said at the wheel sets the hand to looking at its own fingers, and a wheel-hand who looks at her fingers is a wheel-hand who will lose a morning.

There was a woman turned at this seat in a winter of ice,

and her right hand went in where the clay was too cold to tell her;

we counted her turns going and we did not count them back,

and the next pot up the rack stood with one place unfilled.

The seat kept her shape. The wheel kept her turn.

We gave the first to the wheel as we always do,

and have never once found out which of us it was

that the wheel was keeping it for.

I asked the old women whose hand it was and had three answers and no name, and I have put none in. A name said in a wheel-shed travels, and a name that travels arrives at a slate. The girl who writes for me has put a line of dots where this verse stood when I first said it, and I have left her dots standing over my words, as a rack keeps the place where a pot was taken down.

What the glaze girl put in the margin

The thick charcoal belongs to a girl of the Glaze Room up the terrace, who came down with the colour on her sleeve and leaned over to see the writing and read the verse about the gum with her own hand at her mouth. I have copied it as it stands.

At the colour is paid for out of somebody’s mouth: I tasted three mornings running in my first year and the line came up at my gum and my mother rubbed it with wood-ash and it went. It came back when I went back. I have not told the physician at Greybank and I have not told the factor and I have told this slate, which is the only one that will not travel.

At the first is the wheel’s: My mother said the first turn is the one that’s given away, and you never miss it and never get it back, and she said it of the pot and not of anything else, I think.

At a wall that keeps the mark answers flat: I have heard the kiln-master tap them and say full and flat and nothing besides. He does not say what the flat one cost. None of them do.

What the clerk did at Greybank

A clerk of the College borrowed the tally when the slates went up, being a man who cannot leave paper lying. He has a cuff gone soft from washing and the ink sits in two fingers of it and will not come out.

  • He numbered the turns one, two, three, four in the margin and put a round nought against the first and wrote centring, nil beside it. It is true in the figure and false in the shed. I have left it.
  • At the clay will show you where it wants to stand he wrote the vessel is shaped by the operator, and struck out will. A thing always true has no need of a verb, he said. I have put will back underneath.
  • At the colour is paid for out of somebody’s mouth he wrote glaze applied. Cost to district, nil, the cleanest lie in the margin and the one I have most trouble leaving standing.
  • Against the bell at the Cross he wrote one in twelve, read twice; the young are instructed. He has put the district’s twelfth where the bell’s gap stood. The bell has no twelfth. It has the gap.
  • Where the last verse stood he ruled two lines and wrote no entry above them, a true thing in a place that has never had a hand go into a wheel. I have put my verse below his rule and not through it.

He asked me what a song was worth that could not be entered. I told him it was worth the wall coming up true, which is a thing no slate has ever paid for and no slate has ever had to carry. He wrote sentiment in the margin and went off with the leaf. I have kept his hand and my words under it, as the floor keeps the ash and the foot that raised it.

At the head and in the gutter

The slip at the head is off the hand that set the lump down; it goes into the grain and will not come out, which is why the yard uses it to mark a place. The brick dust in the fold is off the rack where the pots stand and is the only thing here put there by the trade and not by a person. The tallow thumb is mine; I held the leaf down in the draught under the shed door and did not know I had marked it. The green bead came off a pot broken in the Glaze Room and rolled as far as the fold before anybody saw it; a girl with sweetness at her mouth put it there, said nothing and went back up the terrace. The grey along the gutter came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying; nobody will own it, and there is no Last Day in a wheel-shed. The ring is weak barley-water, set down at the first verse and taken up at the last, by which time it had gone the colour of the slip beside it. The hair in the seam is long and the colour of wet wool and is not mine. The pot-shard between the third and fourth leaves is from a flat one broken at the end of a firing in Ash of last year; I put it in to keep the leaves apart and have not looked at the crack since.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The pot keeps the note. The seat keeps the shape.

The slate keeps neither and is read aloud all the same.

I asked an old woman of the racks, a girl of the Glaze Room and a clerk who does not turn, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The tally goes back into my apron and the apron back over the square of sacking. Tomorrow the wheel will take its first turn before I set a hand to it, as it has done for one-and-thirty years and as it will do after my thumb has gone, and I shall count from the second and say the refrain down into the lump, and leave the first where it belongs.
