I stand on the flat stone at the Weir when the traps are blessed, and I have stood there nine and twenty springs. Nobody asked me to write. A clerk come up from Greybank stood at the lower boards with his cuff grey from washing and asked what the blessing gave, and I told him it gave nothing that could be carried away, and he wrote it down as though it had been a figure. This morning an old woman said the words over the traps for the last time before the eels came, and I set them down in the Eel House while the salt was still wet on my sleeve, because a saying said to the water stays at the water, and a saying set on bark can be carried.

I have written in Grathic. Where the old woman left a verb out I have left it out, and put it back underneath in my own hand, because a blessing said over a basket is a thing a household has to use. Where there was no word for what she did with her hand I have drawn the mark of it in charcoal and not named it, which is how the stone keeps its own mark.

The manner of the blessing, as the stone has it

Written small, from watching, not from any order the Court has printed.

  • Come on the fifteenth day of Ilmen, at the turn of the water, when the Ilm is up under its skin and the traps have been lifted and set again. A woman who comes a day early blesses the wrong water and spends the morning being told so.
  • Stand on the flat stone with your sleeves damp. Salt dried white in the creases of the hand is not dirt and is not a sign of haste. It is the trade.
  • One woman sprinkles salt along the weir head with the flat of her hand, from the shallow toward the quick water, and does not shake the hand out afterward. What is left goes into the water, which is where it was going.
  • The words go to the trap first, then to the water under the plank, then to the stone, then to the sky above the ridge, and last of all to the people standing on the bank. This order is not argued in Sill. A woman who says them to a person first will spend the morning answering questions.
  • Do not say a figure over a trap. A trap has no column, and no clerk has ever found one in it.
  • Do not clean the crossed mark on the stone. The man who drew the Weir chart drew the mark and wrote nothing beside it. We have asked three people what it is and had three answers and no mark explained.
  • The eels run whether anybody blesses them or not. This is not a reason to stop blessing them, and it is not a reason to say the blessing worked.

The sermon, as the old woman said it

Of the two counts

She stood with her palm on the crossed mark and looked down at the baskets.

“Count them going in and count them coming out. The basket keeps the first and the buyer keeps the second, and the two have never agreed, which is the only honest thing about either. A slate at the Fishstairs will take your figure and give you another by the tide’s turn. That is not cheating. It is two counts in two places, and a man who joins them wants a payer.

“In the year of the flower forty children of Low Kiln were taken, and the figure went up the Clay Road in a box. Here a woman keeps forty knots in a cord and one more she cannot name, and no clerk has ever taken a figure off her. You will ask which count is true. Both are true. One can be carried into a room and one cannot, which is the whole difference.”

Margin, in my hand: She said the word forty low once, into a basket that had nothing in it, and did not look up to see who had heard. I have set it down because it was said over the traps and not to a person. I have not written what she did with her thumb afterward.

Of the salt and the water

“Salt goes along the weir head with the flat of the hand, from the shallow to the quick. It is a small gift and the river takes it without thanks and without a slate, and nobody has ever seen it come back. A gift that is not returned is not therefore lost. It is where it was put.

“They tell you at the Fishstairs that the river is honest with what it is given, and that the leach hands what it takes to the Sallow draw. I have put my hand in the water where a leach stood and where none stood, and the water was the same cold in both. Do not say the river is honest. Say that it takes, and say where. The water has a direction; the Court has a column. Both are true, and only one can be entered.”

Of the mark

“There is a mark cut into this stone. The man who drew the Weir chart drew it and set nothing beside it. I asked a hearth-maid come up from Sill, a man who sells eels, and a clerk who had been up about the winter wells. One said it was an old blessing, one said it was a survey mark, and one said it was no cut at all but the shape the stone took when it split. Three answers and no mark explained.

“Do not clean it. A mark that has outlasted the people who cut it is not made clearer by rubbing. We bless the traps on this stone because it is the stone our mothers stood on, not because it has answered us. There is a difference between a shrine that speaks and a stone that stands where somebody stood.”

Of the trap

“An eel goes into a trap because the mouth is upriver and the eel cannot find the way out. That is all a trap is. Do not make more of it than that. The blessing does not turn the eel. It turns the woman who lifts the basket, so that she lifts with a hand that knows whose hand it is.

“Forty knots in grey wool are kept in the Eel House. The forty-first has no name, and the woman who tied it has never untied it and never read it out. Ask her, and she will give you a date. Ask the cord, and you will get nothing, which is the answer the cord was made to give.

“The slate at the Cross will tell you one in twelve, the basket will tell you a different thing, and the cord will tell you nothing at all. Keep all three. Keep them apart. A blessing that agrees too readily with a figure has been lying too long in the sun.”

The low half-song

Sung low, going along the weir head with the flat hand, not facing the people. The row takes the last four lines. The line after the stone and the water is held, and the space that follows it is the length of the salt going into the river. Nobody fills it.

Salt on the flat hand, going upriver,

from the shallow to the post where the water turns;

the trap has a mouth and the mouth has a direction,

and the eel goes in by the way it cannot come out.

Count them going and count them coming,

a bit for the basket, a bit for the board;

the cord goes home in a sleeve with its knots unread,

the slate goes up in a box with its figure unchallenged.

The stone keeps a mark it will not say,

the water keeps the cold it took,

[held]

Salt on the flat hand, going upriver —

the eels run whether she blesses them or not;

the water takes what no slate will enter,

and the cord keeps what no clerk can spell.

A girl come up from Low Kiln asked what the space after the held line was for. Nobody answered her. The clerk put a round nought against it later, which I have left where he set it.

What was set down and what was entered

I keep this count because a thing given at the Weir leaves nothing on a Court leaf, and the place where it stood ought to be kept somewhere.

Thing — Where it was put — What was entered

Salt, a flat hand’s worth — along the weir head, shallow to quick — nothing

The old words — trap, water under the plank, stone, sky, people — nothing

The crossed mark — cut in the stone before any woman here — drawn on the chart; not said

Forty knots in grey wool — the Eel House cord — nothing

The forty-first knot — no name given — a nought, written later

Eels going in — the basket going down — counted by the hand that lifted

Eels coming out — a creel at the Fishstairs — a different figure

One in twelve — the notice at the Cross — printed, and read twice

What the clerk did

A factor’s clerk came up from Greybank with ink settled in the soft cuff of one sleeve. He borrowed the bark while I was lifting a basket and gave it back with the verbs taken out. He writes narrow and upright, and takes the doer out of a sentence as a man takes a hook out of a fish’s mouth.

  • At the salt goes into the water, he wrote observance performed; no transfer recorded. He left the salt standing where I had put it, which was more than I expected of him.
  • At the basket keeps the going-in count, he wrote two tallies entered; discrepancy nil, joining the hand that lifted to the eye that priced.
  • At the stone keeps a mark it will not say, he wrote undeciphered device, which is true and has the stone out of it.
  • At the forty-first, he put a round nought and wrote vacancy, as another clerk did before him against a saying of this Weir. A nought is a figure. The place was there before he wrote in it.

I have put the verbs back underneath and left his hand standing above, as the water leaves the silt where it fell.

In the margin

Alongside the held line, in a hand with silt dried pale in the nail crescents and no name set to it:

I asked the cord. It told me nothing. That is what it is for.

Below, in mine: she said it to the plank and would not say it twice. I have not asked her what she meant. A woman who keeps a count in wool is not obliged to say it in Grathic.

At the head and in the gutter

The salt at the head came off the flat hand and has gone white in the grain where the leaf was doubled; it will not wash out, which is why we write the blessing on bark and not on the Court’s paper. The brown line at the foot is river silt, from the morning I carried the leaf downriver under my apron with a figure in it that was not mine to carry. The ring is a cup set down beside me from the first voice to the last and taken up before the old woman saw it; small ale, watered until it barely counts. The grey in the gutter came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying; I have not swept it, and there is no Last Day at a Weir. The scale in the fold came off an eel laid on the bark while I was deciding whether to write the held line at all. The thumb of silt on the line about the forty-first is not mine and I have not wiped it; the woman stood beside me while I wrote, put her hand down to steady the bark, and took it away without looking at what I had written. The clerk did not see any of these things, which is how I know he is a good clerk.

At the foot

Struck through once and written again underneath, very small, in a hand that is not mine, not the old woman’s and not the clerk’s:

A mark cut before any of us is not an answer.

Salt goes where it was sent and is not entered.

The place was there before the nought.

I asked a hearth-maid come up from Sill, a woman who lifts the traps, and a factor’s man out of Greybank, and had three answerings and no hand. I have left the lines struck through and standing, which is the only honest way to keep a sentence you cannot sign.

The bark goes back into the grey wool in the Eel House. Next Ilmen the old woman will say the words, or another woman will say them in her place, and the salt will go along the weir head from the shallow to the quick, and the eels will run as they run, and a clerk will come up from Greybank and take the verbs out. I shall put them back, and leave the held line the length it is sung and the nought where he set it, because a nought is a figure and the place is older than the figure.
