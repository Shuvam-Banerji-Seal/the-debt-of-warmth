I am a reed-midwife of the east marsh and I have never said this litany standing up. It is said face down, with the cheek in the peat and the mouth shut against the smell of it, because a thing said into the air goes up and gets entered somewhere, and a thing said into the ground stays where the child is. Fen Cant is not written. It is carried in the mouth, cut on sticks and tied in knots; a notch is still there when the stick is wet, which is more than can be said for the paper they use at Greybank. I learned my letters off an eel-trader and write them with the ends bitten off, the way we speak them and the way the south says we cannot read.

What cannot be written is tied. I have tied the answer in grey wool at every station, and the cord goes back into the thatch with the leaf, and a clerk from Greybank who came to count the winter thin returns has set his hand above mine in narrow upright letters. I have left his hand there and my knots underneath it, the way water leaves the print under the boot. There is no name on this leaf. A name written is a name entered; I have signed with a knot, which he held in his fingers and did not ask me about.

The manner of it, written small at the head

  • Say it at first grey, face down, before the collector’s boat is seen on the cut. A child named after the boat has two names and only one of them ours.
  • Say it to the peat, then to the water in the prints of your knees, then to the ground beside the hut. Do not say it looking at the child; said looking at a person, it becomes a bargain, and the child cannot keep one.
  • The answer is not spoken. It is tied. Every station has its knot, and a knot tied in the dark is still tied in the morning, which is more than can be said for a word.
  • Say the reed-name three times: once for the mother, once for the child that cannot say it, once for the mud. Written once, it belongs to the clerk.
  • If they come before the third saying, stop. Give them the number and keep the word. Begin again when the boat is gone.
  • If an officer takes the cord, give him the leaf and keep the cord. He can enter a leaf. He cannot enter a knot without taking it apart, and a knot taken apart is no longer a knot.

The litany

I say it into the peat. There is nobody present to answer. The answer is made with the hands after every station: grey wool, two turns, a slip. I have set down what each knot is called, which is not what the clerk entered.

I. Of the ground that is lent

Dry ground is borrowed.

The house stands on posts so the loan can be called

without taking the house with it.

Say it to the post. It has stood longer than any of us

and never counted a man.

The answer is tied: the loan-knot, two turns and a slip. It is untied when the water gives the ground back, which it never does.

II. Of the Marsh Mother

She keeps it warm down there and hands it back

to whoever can reach it.

She has no keys, no slate, no figure.

A priest come up from Greybank told me she was the Hearth

under a Fen name. The Hearth has a grate.

Ours has a skin of water and moves when it is walked on.

The answer is tied: the warm-knot. The warm place where a cow stood moves by the next tend, and the cow with it. I have kept the knot and not the place.

III. Of what goes in

What goes into the mud is kept.

A body goes out on a raft into the reedbed

and nobody fetches it back.

The mud keeps what it wants, which is not always

the whole of a person. We have never asked for the rest.

Say it to the water under the water.

The answer is tied: the keeping-knot. It is the knot we tie when a thing is owed and untie when it is paid. This one is not untied. There is nobody to pay.

IV. Of the iron

Cold iron takes. The mud takes.

One of them writes it down.

The tube does not keep. It opens a way and leaves it open.

A pale round place shows on the arm all winter.

The mud leaves nothing an officer can enter.

Do not ask which is kinder. A man who asks that

wants a kind way to be emptied.

The answer is tied: the taking-knot. It has the same two turns as the loan-knot and is tied on the other end of the cord. I have stood at both and cannot tell them apart with my eyes shut, which is the whole difficulty.

V. Of the figure

One in twelve, as the notice says. Printed. Read twice.

Twelve bits to the mark, and a crown will not change here.

A thin return is a return. Ask for more

and you get a colder one.

We do not say what a whole return costs.

A man who buys one has paid somebody,

and here we know who, because he paid here.

Here I tied two knots, one for the figure on the notice and one for the other figure, and set them side by side on the cord. The clerk has put his small stop above the first. Both knots stand.

VI. Of the name

Say the name three times and the mud will know it.

Once for the mother, once for the child that cannot say it,

once for the ground beside the hut.

If they come before the name is given,

give them the number and keep the word.

A name said at home is a door.

A number is a hole in the wall.

The answer is tied: the word-knot. There is ground between here and Grath with doorways in it and no smoke. A doorway is built for coming back through. We do not say who went, or what hung in the bare clay above the lintel; a thing you can name is a thing the Court can figure.

VII. Of the coming back

Come back as you can. The mud does not ask who you were.

It keeps the shape of where you stood

and cannot tell you whose it was.

If you have no name, leave the place where the name would stand

and let the water stand in it.

Say it to the ground. Said looking at a person,

it becomes a bargain.

The answer is tied: the coming-back knot. I tie it with the ends left long so the child can find it later. I have never seen a child find one, and I tie them long all the same.

Here the cord has a loop with no knot in it. It is not a knot forgotten and not a vacancy. It is the loop for them that came back into a house that did not name them in time, and nobody has tied it. I have left the loop the length it is.

The cord, as this dry keeps it

A figure that comes into the marsh in a coat goes out again in a thin return and leaves nothing in the thatch. These are the names said into the ground before the boat was seen. The slate column is not mine.

Reed 6 — a girl of the east cut — said three times, into the door-hole — word-knot — number only; the word kept

Reed 9 — a boy of the upper board — not said; his mother was down with the fever — loop left untied — thin, from the district pot

Reed 11 — a bought breath’s child — said twice; the third said for her by the woman who held her — loan-knot — nothing entered; the boat went back empty

Reed 14 — a child out of Reedholt — said three times and written nowhere — keeping-knot — whole, by a house upriver; the name not entered here

The fifth row is a space the length of a name and I have not filled it. A stick has no ear and a cord has no clerk. What is tied here goes into the thatch.

What the clerk did

He came out from Greybank in Ilmen with ink in two fingers and cuffs soft from washing, to count the winter thin returns. He writes in the narrow upright hand and took the verbs out as a man takes bones out of a fish, and set figures where the knots stood.

  • At she has no keys, no slate, no figure, he wrote: the measure is retained in the peat. Underneath, in mine: I have put keeps and hands back under, because a woman standing in water to her calf wants to know who is doing the handing, and whether she will get it back.
  • At the mud keeps what it wants, which is not always the whole of a person, he wrote: recovery not possible; account closed. Under it: it keeps. Whether it keeps the whole of him is not a question the mud answers.
  • At cold iron takes. The mud takes. One of them writes it down, he wrote: draw recorded; lawful extraction. Under it: he takes and does not write; they write and do not take. I have stood at both.
  • At the figure he put a small stop in one in twelve and wrote it again above, larger. It is the figure they like to see. The other figure is tied on the same cord and cannot be carried out in a cuff.
  • At the loop he put a round nought and wrote vacancy. A nought is a figure and a figure travels. The loop stays where it is tied.
  • At the answer he wrote: no verbal response recorded, the only true thing he wrote all morning and cost him nothing, because a knot has no tongue to enter.

I have put the verbs back underneath and left his hand standing above, as the water leaves the print under the boot. He takes them out because a thing always true has no need of them. I put them back because a woman face down wants to know whose hands are holding her child.

What the eel-trader put in the margin

The leaning hand is Orra’s, out of Reedholt, who taught me my letters and smells of eels even when her sleeves are dry. I have copied her as she wrote it.

At the iron: “I have seen a man come up out of the black stand with his fingers the colour of old rope. He had been drawn the week before. I did not ask which took more, and have not stopped asking.”

At the name: “My mother said mine into the water and the collector came up the board an hour later. He wrote a number. She kept the word. I answer to neither at the Post.”

At the loop: “Leave it. There is a girl on the upper board who came back last winter and goes toward the water when somebody calls a name that is not hers. I have not told her which.”

At the close: “You tie more knots than any midwife. You said a knot keeps better than a clerk. Good answer, and not the whole one.”

At the head and in the gutter

The brown at the head is peat worked into the grain by my own thumb; the marsh signs with a hand and not a name, which is why the clerk could not tell who wrote this even while I stood beside him. The press of sedge has made the first two words soft and I have not gone over them, because a word written soft is a word said into the ground. The tideline is bog-water that stood on the leaf two nights; the writing there has gone thin and stayed. The knot through the hole is the loan-knot and I have never untied it. The horn-shaving came off my count-stick where I used the point to open a word I could not spell. The hook nick is not mine. The grey is Ash off the cuff of a man who sells it, and there is Ash enough in this marsh without a stranger’s. The ring is small ale that stood from the first station to the seventh and was drunk by nobody, which is how ale goes at a dry when there is a thing to say and no proper word for it. There is a Last Day in the low houses and none at the peat-dry, and even here the morning comes.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The mud keeps what it is given. It does not say whose.

The slate enters what it is given. It does not say who gave.

Struck through once and written again underneath, very small, in a hand that is not mine, not the clerk’s and not Orra’s:

Both keep. Only one of them is asked to answer.

I asked the clerk, the eel-trader, a woman of the east cut, and the man who sells the Ash, and had four answerings and no hand. I have left the line struck through and standing, the only honest way to keep a sentence you cannot sign.

The leaf and the cord go back into the thatch. Next reed-cutting a woman will lie face down before the boat comes up the cut, and I shall say the seven stations to the ground and tie the answers and leave the loop the length it is. The clerk will take the verbs out and I shall put them back. The collector will write a number he will not bring into the dry. The word will stay where it was said, in the ground beside the hut, where a doorway is built for coming back through and nobody says who went.
