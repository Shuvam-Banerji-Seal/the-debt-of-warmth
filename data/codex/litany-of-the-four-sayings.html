I keep the order at the collector’s table in Slate Row. That is a fine name for holding a leaf while an officer sets a cold tube to a man’s arm and I write down what the house gives him. The leaf came down from the Weighing Hall in a clean hand with no thumb on it. I have kept it eleven years, and the clean has gone off the corners, which is the only part of it the Hearthseat would not recognise.

What follows is the Keeping’s own litany. It is said over the dying, at the draw, and again at the pressing when the measure goes into the new vessel. I have set the rubrics underneath as the room actually does them, because a rubric written in the Hearthseat is a rubric for a room with no draught under the door. The house has a hand in this leaf as well. I have not washed it out, and the clerk who borrowed it on an Account-day has not been able to wash his out either.

The filings at the head came off the tube-case when the officer set it down hard. The grey along the gutter came off his cuff when he knelt to look at the ridge in a corner and said it was old ash from the firing. He went on with his counting. The tallow print belongs to a woman who held the leaf in a draught while her sister was being lifted out of water, and I have left it where she put it.

How it is said

  • The leaf lies face down until the officer is ready. It has heard enough from the waiting.
  • The saying is not sung. It is read in the clean voice, the one that takes the reader neatly out of his own sentence.
  • The answer is said by whoever is in the room, including the child, who cannot say it and says it anyway.
  • When the bell goes at the Cross, stop. It is counting a different thing. Begin again after.
  • At the pressing the leaf is turned face up. The dead do not mind. The new vessel cannot yet read.

The litany

The officer says. The house answers. The rubrics are mine.

I. Of the walls

The pot came up from the rack and answered full.

Set down on the bench again, it answered flat.

The wall was not malicious. It was old.

The Warm went out of it the way water goes

out of a cracked cup into trodden ash,

and the ash is the floor of the world,

which holds what nobody found a place to hold.

Here the officer waits with the tube laid by. Nobody touches the dead before the saying is done.

The house answers: The measure is banked.

II. Of the iron

The iron is cold before the hand that takes it up.

It does not take. It opens the way.

The slate takes. The slate is a piece of stone

with a clean hand on it; the hand does not shake

and the figure does not sweat.

Take what is owed and leave the shape behind.

Here the tube goes to the arm and I write while the officer draws. The house looks at the floor. There is no ceremony in it, only iron, a small hiss, and the time it takes.

The house answers: The account stands.

III. Of the figure

Count embers into the cup, not years.

A labourer books forty. A child books twenty.

The dead book what the living can pay to have back.

Write it small. The slate does not care

how large a thing looks when it is written down.

Read it twice: once for the moot, once for those who came late.

Here I read the figure twice, as the notice requires. The second reading is for the people at the door, who have not yet been told they are late.

The house answers: Your return is recorded.

IV. Of the share

One part in twelve is kept for the fire, as the notice says,

and the notice is not a lie. The fire wants nothing.

What a thing wants nothing, that is kept.

A man who gives to a thing that wants nothing

has paid the only debt that cannot be owed back.

One in twelve. Printed. Unchallenged.

Here I touch the printed figure. In this district I do not read the other one aloud. Nobody has ever asked me to.

The house answers: Nothing is lost that we have kept.

V. Of the pooling

One measure into one vessel: he comes back as himself,

with the price of bread and the name of the pot.

Many measures into many: they come back as they can,

with a taste for a country they cannot name

and a hand that goes out to a cup already broken.

Both are poured. Both are booked the same.

Here the leaf leaves a space the length of a name. I do not say whose name. The officer does not ask.

The house answers: The measure is banked.

VI. Of the new vessel

The measure is pressed into a vessel that has not held a cup.

It takes the shape of the walls it is given,

the way slip takes the mould and keeps the thumbprint.

The name is written into it before it can refuse one.

On the third day it will grow worse before it grows better.

Keep it. Keep it anyway.

Here the hearth-maid warms a stone in her hand and tries it on her own wrist. This is not part of the litany. It is the part before.

The house answers: Your return is recorded.

VII. Of the Kept

Blessed are they that are Kept.

No want can reach them. They no longer feel the cold.

There is nothing left in them for the Hearth to take,

and so they are past the need of any return.

There are fewer than twenty in the world.

Speak of them with pity. Do not ask one whether he is warm;

he will say no, and you will have no other question.

Here I close the leaf. The dead are already in Slate Row and the new vessel is under a roof I shall not enter.

The house answers: Nothing is lost that we have kept. The account stands.

The four sayings

  • The measure is banked — written at the draw, in the officer’s hand, before I set the figure down.
  • The account stands — read at the Cross, twice, over the pigs.
  • Nothing is lost that we have kept — printed on the Weighing notices and at the foot of every slate.
  • Your return is recorded — said in the Remembering, in a clean voice, to the room and not to the person.

There is no fifth saying. The house keeps a place where a fifth would stand, and I have left the place the length of it.

What the clerk did

A clerk of the College came down on an Account-day with a cuff gone soft from washing, and ink in two fingers that would not come out. He borrowed the leaf and gave it back with my verbs taken out.

  • At the wall was not malicious, it was old, he wrote, The vessel failed, having reached its term. Mine stands beneath.
  • At take what is owed and leave the shape, he set the measure is drawn and the residue entered, and underlined it twice.
  • At one in twelve, printed, unchallenged, he put a small stop in the figure and wrote the figure again above it, larger. It is the figure they like to see.
  • At they come back as they can, he wrote a thin return is entered, which is a true thing in a language nobody in this room speaks.
  • In the space where the fifth saying would stand he put a round nought and wrote vacancy beside it.

I have left all of it standing. He takes the verbs out because a thing always true has no need of them. I put them back because a man at the draw does not know he is hearing something always true, and wants to know which hand to hold out.

What the other hand put back

The house has written in the margins at the bedsides. The charcoal is thicker than mine and it leans. I have copied it as it stands.

At I: It was a good pot. It split at the lip. We set it by the door and went around it.

At II: Take it and go. It is not ours to watch.

At VI: She was warm when I wrote it. She was cold by the fourth day. Both are in the house roll and only one is read.

At the four sayings, against Nothing is lost that we have kept, the same hand has written: The grey in the corner is not dirt. Then, struck through and written again underneath, very small: We leave it. We burn it in the morning. The clean line where it stood is not the same as gone.

At the foot, where the answer should be, a line is struck through twice. Under it:

Come back warm. Come back as you were.

And under that, in letters pressed hard into the bark:

Come back warm. Come back as you can.

I have left the struck line standing. A thing said over a child is a thing a house has to use.

In Ilmen

In Ilmen a woman was taken by the brook below the nineteen steps and no officer was called. There is a space on the leaf where her measure should stand. The clerk has not put a nought in it. I have not filled it, and nobody from Slate Row has asked why it is empty. The house says she was gone before anybody could reach her. That is true, and it is not the whole of it, and I have written both sentences down because the space is the right length for both.

At the head and in the gutter

The lamp-black thumb is mine; I held the leaf with the same hand I write with and did not know I had marked it. The barley is from a crust laid down on the leaf and not eaten. The ring is small ale. A hair is caught in the fold and I have left it, because the fold is where the leaf opens and somebody was always leaning over to see what was written. The tallow is the woman’s, and the grey is the officer’s, and neither of them will own either mark.

At the foot

The measure is banked. The account stands. Nothing is lost that we have kept. Your return is recorded.

That is the clean voice, which is the Hearthseat’s own. Under it, in a hand that is nobody’s, struck once and written again:

Four sayings and a place where the fifth would stand. We say the four. We keep the place.

The leaf goes back under the slate on the collector’s table. A slate is a thing that gets carried into the square and read in a clean voice; this is a thing that gets read by the light of one lamp after the house is in bed. If an officer ever finds it, let him take the verbs out. The next hand will put them back, as I did, and will warm the stone first.
