To Hedda of the mills, who sets her charcoal under the physician’s gold and will not say whose hand the charcoal is. From Neva at the Ember house, who keeps the list outside the room and puts the endings back.

I write on the back of the sheet that was spoiled at the third hour. A cup went over it while the announcement was being made, and the house clerk would have sent it to the fire with the rest, and I asked for it, which was a rudeness, and nobody in Sere was rude to me about it, which is how I knew I had been rude. There is no rudeness in this country. There is only a person who has not yet been told. I was told by being given the sheet.

The stick is the one they use at the presses. Charcoal will not take on prepared paper, and the face of this leaf is gold and finished, so I have written on the spoiled side, where nothing is meant to last. That is the only side of a wine-list a bond-woman may write on, and the only side on which a thing said can still be said by somebody. You will know the hand. It is the one that presses hard at the ends of words.

Three days ago a name-day was kept for a girl of the house. Solene was brought out and shown, whole, as she was in 799 and 804 and 809, and the fourth bowl was set down full at the third hour and carried away full at the going of the light, and nobody poured it, and nobody said whose debt it stood for. The crowd was kind about the not knowing. It is a mercy of the Spending, the house not knowing. I have set it down as it was said, said to the bowl and not to a person.

At the burning, one household’s slates went into the fire by lot and new ones were made before the wine was cold. A woman of the presses wept and was comforted and then applauded the new slate, and I have written both things, because both things happened, and the second is the one that gets copied. A man beside me asked whether a debt had been forgiven or a province ruined and was told yes, and asked which, and was told measure for measure, and went back to his wine. I have copied that too, though it is not a true account of anything, which is why it is said at the third hour and never written in gold.

The thing I write for

The wine-list keeps what was poured at each feast and the names of those the house chose to remember. A girl come down from the north is given seven names here and keeps whichever the household prefers, and the names she does not keep have endings that a Sere clerk’s hand finds no use for: a vowel, a small syllable, the sound a mother made at a door. The house clerk writes the name that is kept and throws the rest away. I put them back on the spoiled side in charcoal.

Two tend ago a woman of the presses asked me to put back an ending for her daughter, who was entered under a house-name and answers to it now and has forgotten the other. I put it back. She asked me whether a name kept in charcoal kept anything. I told her I did not know, which was true and was not what she asked. She went back to the presses. At the next feast her daughter was poured a cup and thanked the house in the southern manner, and the thanks was entered, and the ending was not. I have kept both rows below and let them stand together, the way you and I have stood things together before with worse between them.

The list as I keep it and as the house entered it

Feast — What was poured — What the house entered — What I put back

Name-day, Grain 6 — Warm wine; the fourth bowl not poured — Seven names, one kept — The mother’s ending, in charcoal

The third hour, Grain 9 — A public bowl; an act announced — Measure for measure — The name of the province, struck through

The burning, Grain 9 — One household’s slates, by lot — Debt discharged; new slate issued — The weeping, on the back of the leaf

The presses, the tend before — A cup for a girl gone thin — No entry — Two syllables and a vowel

The clerk writes wine issued where I write the house poured, and takes the house out of the sentence as a man takes his hand out of a field before he lays the dressing on. I have put the house back underneath, where it stood. He has not struck a word of mine. He has only written above it, which is the manner of that hand and the whole of its art.

The press-song, with the last line put back

Sung at the presses after the third hour, going down the rows and not up. The women sing four lines and leave the fifth for the press to answer. I have put the fifth back in charcoal on the spoil; the clerk has set *no verse entered above it.*

The press takes the grape and gives back the wine,

the house takes the name and gives back a sound;

said it to the bowl and said it to the fire,

and the end of the word went down with the wine.

Put it back, put it back, in charcoal on the spoil,

where no clean hand comes and the bowl will not ask;

a cup goes round and a name goes round

and the one that is kept is not the one that was heard.

The last line was put back by a woman who would not give her name, and I have not found out which of the presses sang it. A verse sung while a cup goes round is a verse about work, and work is a thing a woman may remember without being told to, which is more than can be said of an entry.

The physician’s book

You wrote under the gold hand and would not give your name. I have seen the book. The physician writes in gold on prepared paper, and what he writes is clean and goes into the room; what you write in charcoal goes under it and stays where a woman’s hand can read it. I have copied the two rows as they stand, with the gold above.

The physician’s gold — Your charcoal underneath

The pale line at the gum; the sweet sickness — She carried green ware from High Kiln before the road took her south

A settling after a whole return; the third-day tremor — She was calm when she came back and would not say where she had been

No remedy required — She stopped tasting the rims; the line went off in a tend

I write because of the first row. There is a girl at the presses here who has the pale line at her gum and has never been near a glaze room or a cart from High Kiln. She washes the small bowls and puts her tongue to the green edge to see whether they are clean, the way her mother taught her and the way women here have taught their daughters since the glaze came down the Ferr. I have told her to stop. She says a sweet answers a sweet, which is a true thing in this house and a false one in a body. Tell me what the mill woman carried, and whether the line goes off when the tasting stops, and whether there is anything else a woman does with her mouth that leaves the same colour. I have put the question on the back of a spoiled sheet because I have no paper of my own that the house has not counted.

What the clerk did

A tally-clerk of the house borrowed the leaf on a feast morning and wrote in the leaning hand he keeps for the wine-list. A factor’s clerk come down from Greybank wrote above him in the narrow upright hand they teach at the College, with the verbs taken out as though a verb were a thing a man might hide in the fold of a cuff.

  • Against the house poured he set wine was issued. The house went out of the sentence and nobody went with it.
  • Against I put the ending back he set no entry. An ending is not a figure, which is true in his room and is not what the woman at the presses asked.
  • Against the mother’s ending, in charcoal he set vacancy, and underlined it, which is the readiest way of correcting a mother I have seen.
  • Against the question about the girl’s gum he wrote nothing. The hand passed over it without a stroke. He has never once changed a figure in this house, and has taken the girl out of the account without touching the figure at all.

I have put the verbs back underneath where I could still read them, and left his three lines standing above mine. A slate with two hands on it is the only kind anybody here believes. I have not swept the charcoal off the fold where his cuff passed.

In the margin, in a second hand, small and set close to the writing

The end of a word is not kept by writing it. I have written endings on the backs of spoiled tallies for nine years and have kept none of them, and the women whose endings they were went on answering to the house-name and were none the worse or better for my charcoal. A name kept in charcoal keeps the hand that wrote it, not the woman it names. That is the whole of it, and it is not a comfort.

The mill woman carried green ware. She had worked the glaze room at High Kiln before she came south, and the colour went on her hands before it went to her gum, and she tasted the rims because the women there did. She stopped when the physician told her, and the line went off in a tend, and she has the tremor still on the third morning after a feast, which the physician calls a settling and I call nothing, because I have no word for it that would survive a room with a door in it. I have set both under his gold where you will find them.

Tell the girl at the presses to stop putting her tongue to a rim that came green out of a kiln. If she will not stop for you, let her see the mill woman’s hands, which are the colour of old rope at the thumb and have been so for two years. A thing shown to a woman stays with her longer than a thing said to her, which is how we learn in the north and how we have forgotten here.

Do not ask me for my name in a letter that goes through the house. A name written on the back of a spoiled sheet has a way of reaching the room before the sheet does.

At the head and in the gutter

The yellow at the head is dust off the burning winds and has worked into the grain and will not come out. The ring at the head is the cup that spoiled the face of the leaf; it went over at the third hour and nobody saw who did it, and I have not asked. The olive at the outer crease came off the tray that carried a contract past the lower table while I wrote the third paragraph, and a fleck of gold leaf from that contract is shut in the fold and catches the light when I open the leaf, which is the only bright thing in it. The husk of grain in the gutter came down with the cart that carried this letter to you and has no business on a wine-list, which is why I have kept it. The scorch is where I laid the burnt stick down while the bell was rung for the act, and the stick went on burning under the leaf for the length of the ringing. The myrrh at the foot came off the fire-shrine on the back of a woman’s hand that rested here while she told me about her daughter; it has gone hard and smells of the feast still. The grey along the outer fold is not mine and not the clerk’s and not the physician’s. I have had this leaf since Grain and have not swept it. There is no Last Day for a letter, and nothing here is burnt in the morning and called dealt with.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

She asked if a name kept in charcoal kept anything.

The clerk wrote vacancy above the answer and the figure stood.

The fourth bowl went out full and came back full.

Ask the press what it kept. Ask the gum.

I asked the woman of the presses, a bowl-surgeon who sells resin for the graft, and a clerk of the factor’s house who came down with the gold book, and had three answers and no hand, as we have had before. I have left the line where it was written.

The cart goes south on the morning after the next third hour. Send the answer by the same hand that brought this, and put it on the spoiled side, and press hard at the ends of words where I shall know it. I will put the verbs back under whatever he writes above them, and neither of us will call it an account.
