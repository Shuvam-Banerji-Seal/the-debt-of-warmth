I sing above the shaft, at the cradles in the row behind the count’s room. Our women do not go below: a woman’s measure lies too near the surface and would draw the sweat out of the stone, and the stone is not ours to trouble. I have sung to three children of this row and borne two. My youngest turned twelve in Bone and goes down at thirteen, a year off. The cradle has stood empty three years, and I sing to the nail in its board now, because a cradle with a child in it will look at you, and a woman singing about a knife does not want to be looked at.

I set the words down the night before his birthday, on the back of a tally a fall of slate dust spoiled, and cut a notch in the edge where the Grathic would not come. A survey-clerk from Greybank borrowed the leaf at the weigh in Ilmen and wrote his hand above mine, with the doing taken out and a figure standing where the nail stood. I have left his words where he put them and put mine back underneath, as the board of a cradle keeps the shape of a child after the child has gone out of it.

The bone at the head of this leaf is a shaving off a tally-stick. Keldish is cut in bone, not written, and a notch stays when paper has gone soft. I sign with charcoal because charcoal goes into the grain and no wet sleeve takes it out. The smut in the second fold came off the sleeve I wipe the keys with. I did not know I had marked it.

The manner of it

  • Said to the arm and to the nail, never to the child. Said looking at a child it becomes a promise, and there is no promise here that a woman can keep.
  • The hand goes to the nail before the eyes go to the cradle: the record first, then the person, then the mouth opening after both. A lowlander does the opposite and thinks nothing of it.
  • One verse to one finger. Count down the fingers of the left hand and stop at the thumb. The thumb is not a number and does not go down.
  • The stone is not put into the hand at the cradle. It is given after the knife, by a woman at the mouth who has counted the numbers. A stone in a cradle is warmth paid for and spent on nothing, and no warmth here comes unbought.
  • The name is not sung. Where it would stand in the last part I have cut a notch in the edge of the leaf. A notch keeps better than a word and cannot be carried up the road in a cuff.
  • Sing the first parts while the child still has a face you can look at. Keep the last part until the boots have gone up the Rim Road and the room is quiet. A song with a name in it travels; this one stays where the cradle is.
  • Do not sing it at the weigh or while a number is being checked in red. A lullaby said during a checking is entered as a disturbance, and the mine-reeve does not ask twice.

The song

Said low, to the nail. The hand goes to the nail before the first line. One verse to one finger, counting down and stopping at the thumb. The last word of each part is said to the iron and not to the room.

The refrain, as the cradle keeps it after every part:

Hush. Count. The arm has spoken before the face.

One for the cradle, one for the knife, one for the stone.

The number goes down. The name stays here.

Say it once. The mountain keeps its own.

I. Of the arm

Before you had a face you had an arm.

Before you had a name the number was kept in it.

The arm speaks first in this house; the face comes after.

We say what we hold before we say who we are.

Hush. The arm is listening.

Hush. Count. The arm has spoken before the face.

One for the cradle, one for the knife, one for the stone.

The number goes down. The name stays here.

Say it once. The mountain keeps its own.

II. Of the knife

Thirteen is a short road. I have walked to it twice.

The knife goes across the surface way and stays there.

A way once cut is a door and does not close again.

It aches before the thaw. It will ache after.

You will answer to the number the room gives you.

Hush. Count. The arm has spoken before the face.

One for the cradle, one for the knife, one for the stone.

The number goes down. The name stays here.

Say it once. The mountain keeps its own.

III. Of the stone

They will put a stone in your hand after the knife.

I cannot put it there. My hands do not go below.

A stone keeps its night longer than a mother keeps a child.

It is paid for at the mouth; no man here has called it ours.

Hold it. It is the only warmth with a price I can pay.

Hush. Count. The arm has spoken before the face.

One for the cradle, one for the knife, one for the stone.

The number goes down. The name stays here.

Say it once. The mountain keeps its own.

IV. Of the iron

Hold the rail going down. It is iron and it takes.

Hold the rail coming up. It is the same iron.

The clamp that closes a crack will close a fist;

the iron has never been asked which one it meant.

Hush. I have asked it. It does not answer.

Hush. Count. The arm has spoken before the face.

One for the cradle, one for the knife, one for the stone.

The number goes down. The name stays here.

Say it once. The mountain keeps its own.

V. Of the number

State what you hold. Then say who you are.

The number stands. We do not say it twice.

Two go down and the count comes two.

Whether two come up is a thing the mountain keeps.

Do not ask the mountain. It will tell you the number.

Hush. Count. The arm has spoken before the face.

One for the cradle, one for the knife, one for the stone.

The number goes down. The name stays here.

Say it once. The mountain keeps its own.

VI. The part kept back

Not sung while boots are on the road or a clerk is at the weigh. Said to the nail with the thumb laid over the notch.

I gave you a name at the cradle and the road will not carry it.

Where the name would stand in this song I have cut a notch.

A notch is not a figure and cannot be entered.

I did not carry you down. I cannot carry you up.

I can hold the board until it remembers your shape.

Hush. Count. The arm has spoken before the face.

One for the cradle, one for the knife, one for the stone.

The number goes down. The name stays here.

Say it once. The mountain keeps its own.

There is a space before the last line of the last refrain, the length of the notch in the edge of the leaf. I have left it on the tally. It is the right length for a name and for nothing else, and the women leave it when they sing, though none of them has ever said what stands in it. My thumb is in it now and has been since I cut it.

What the survey-clerk did

He came up from Greybank with a cuff soft from washing and ink in two fingers of it, and borrowed the leaf while the pair before ours was weighed. He writes narrow and upright and takes the verbs out as though a verb were a thing a man could smuggle past a weigh in the seam of a cuff. He set his hand above mine and did not look at the notch.

  • At before you had a name the number was kept in it he wrote capacity declared at birth; designation pending, underlined twice. True in a counting room and false at a cradle, where the number was still waiting in the arm for the knife.
  • At the number stands he wrote the vessel was re-entered --- the same sentence with nobody in it, and a man’s thirteenth year made to sound like a correction.
  • At I cannot put it there. My hands do not go below he set stone issued after procedure; no gift arises. Right about the stone; he has left out the hands, which is the whole art of that hand.
  • At whether two come up is a thing the mountain keeps he wrote no return due; remains retained on premises. The cruellest line on the leaf and the truest, and I have not crossed it out.
  • Where the notch stands he put a round nought and wrote vacancy. A nought is a figure and travels in a cuff. A notch is a place and stays where it was cut.

I have put my words back underneath everywhere and left his standing above. He takes the doer out because a thing always true has no need of anybody doing it. I put the doer back because a woman singing to a board wants to know which hand held the child, and the answer is in the sentence if there is somebody in it.

What the grandmother put underneath

The thick charcoal is my mother’s. She counted at this mouth thirty-one years and never set her name to anything. She pressed these lines in below the verses while I was finding the end of the fourth, and would not read them over. I have copied them where she set them.

At a stone keeps its night longer than a mother keeps a child: He came up with two sets in one arm. The later cut was deeper. I have looked at that arm and I have not asked him which set he answers to, and he has not told me.

At you will answer to the number the room gives you: Your father answered to two numbers in one year and held the cup with the other hand. The room entered the later one. I have kept both, which is a thing a room cannot do.

At the notch: I put my thumb in it when you wrote it and I will not tell you why. You have cut it the width of your own thumb, which was the width of his.

I have set all three where she wrote them. She would not sign them and I have not made her.

At the head and in the gutter

The bone shaving at the head came off a tally-stick my mother kept and is the only part of this leaf written before it was said. The coal in the second fold is off the sleeve I wipe the keys with; it goes into the grain and no wet sleeve takes it out, which is why I sign in charcoal. The tallow is off the lamp I kept low so the women in the next room would not know I was setting down what we sing and change their words. The curl of hair is his, cut at two years with the knife that cuts the tally notches; I shut it in the sewing and have not opened it. The crust is Last Day bread; there is no Last Day at a shaft mouth and nothing here is gathered and burnt in the morning and called dealt with. The dent is where the warm-stone stood while I found the sixth verse: it held through the writing and was cold before the charcoal set, which is the order of things. The red at the second corner came off the mine-reeve’s point while he checked a man whose figure would not hold; he did not look at me and I did not look at the man. The frost-feather was on the mouth stone after a warm night and was gone before I came to put it in. The small-ale ring is where a cup stood from the first verse to the last and was not lifted; it is not the cup on the nail and belongs to no Order. The grey along the outer fold came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying above ground; nobody will own it and I have not swept it.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The cradle keeps the shape. The arm keeps the number.

The road keeps the name and will not give it back.

The stone keeps one night. The mountain keeps the rest.

I asked a woman who has counted here longer than I, a man off the Duelling Floor, and a surveyor who carries the slate up, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written.

The leaf goes back into the tin with the keys, and the nail into the board. Tomorrow a pair will go down and the count’s woman will put the stone into whichever hand the knife leaves free; I shall sing to the board and stop at the notch. He will have the number and I the name, the road will have neither, and the mountain will keep its own and will not be asked to give it back.
