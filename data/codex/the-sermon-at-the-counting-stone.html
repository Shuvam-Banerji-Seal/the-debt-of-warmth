I keep the sitting-register at Keld Keep, in a box beside the high-path door, and nobody asked me for this. There is no word for shrine in the Vorr tongue; they call the stones the stones, and a man below who says shrine has named what he cannot see. I write because the boy had no name. A Vorr takes his name one at a time, many years apart, and is known until then by the stone he keeps, so I have entered him as the boy of the third stone and left the place where a name would stand the length a name would be.

He spoke to the stone, the dust, the dish of meltwater, and last to the boy, the order the Vorr use when a season is waiting and somebody below may be listening who wants a figure. He said every sentence twice. I have set the second beneath where it mattered and left it out where it did not, a freedom no Vorr would take and they have never asked me for this leaf.

The pale at the head is lichen scraped off the north stone; it goes into the grain and no wet sleeve takes it out. The doubled print at the second corner is his, pressed into my lamp’s tallow while he turned the leaf; a Vorr’s last joint is split in two and leaves a mark like a cleft hoof. The rime in the fold was on the path at first grey and was water by the time I folded. The shaving kept its heat one night on the stone floor and is cold now, which is the order of things here. The copper thread came off the spoiled rubbing and has nothing to do with the sermon. The ring is the dish, set down at the first saying and taken up before the boy saw it. The grey came off a cuff and nobody will own it; there is no Last Day above this Keep.

The manner of the sitting, as the stone has it

Written small, from watching, not from any order the Court has printed.

  • Come at first grey, before the seam-tide turns. A boy who comes at noon sits in another man’s season and calls it his own, and the stone has never once corrected him.
  • Sit facing the way you came. An elder will turn a boy around without a word if he faces the road, and the road is not the direction this shrine keeps.
  • Put the dust on the palm, not on the stone. The stone marks what is kept; the hand marks who keeps it.
  • Say each sentence twice. Once for the stone and once for the boy. A third saying is the sayer’s fault; the stone has heard three before and will hear three thousand.
  • Do not say the number before the season ends. A number said early has a buyer, and a man from Greybank would pay for it and enter it wrongly.
  • The first sitting gives a number and not a name. A boy who asks for his name is given the number again, which is not anger but the arrangement of years this people keeps.
  • A season is not an hour. The impatient hear the stone scratch and bring home a number they could have had at home.
  • A number goes down the road. The dust stays. If a man from the Court writes one, let him write the number and not the sitting; the two have never agreed.

The sermon, as he said it

Of the dust

He took a pinch of grey from a fold of his coat and pressed it into the boy’s palm with the doubled joint of his last finger. The dust is marked off a blighted seam and is not sold.

“This is put down. It is not given.”

This is put down. It is not given.

“A thing given can be taken back. A thing put down stays where it was put. I have never given a boy anything in forty years and have had no cause to ask anything back.”

“The Court has a word for what is put down and a column to put it in. This has no column. If you find one on the road below, do not stand in it.”

Margin, in my hand: He used the word for put down that the factor’s clerk at Greybank translates as retained. I have left both standing, as the dust lies on the palm and not under it.

Of the first sitting

“You will sit until the number changes. It changes. That is the whole of the sitting.”

“Ask what it changes to and I will say the numbers are correct. That answers the numbers, not you. A third asking is yours.”

Ask what it changes to and I will say the numbers are correct. That answers the numbers, not you. A third asking is yours.

“You want a name. The stone gives a number. Names come one at a time and many years apart, and a man here is known by the stone he keeps. I have kept this one forty years and have not been given the other word.”

A girl from the Keep asked what the boy was to be called. He gave her the number. She asked again in another way and he gave her the same number. I have set the two askings down and not joined them.

Of the bone

“The count is in the bone. The tube finds nothing there.”

The count is in the bone. The tube finds nothing there.

“They below take what is in the blood and write it on a slate. We keep ours where no iron reaches. A thing a man cannot take is a thing he will not call a gift.”

“We were never owed. We do not ask.”

We were never owed. We do not ask.

A miner stood at the back with a pale ring at his wrist. When the elder said the last, the miner covered the ring and went down the path before the boy had heard the saying twice. I have written the hand and not the man.

Of what goes down

“A stone is kept. A number is sold.”

A stone is kept. A number is sold.

A Court survey-clerk had come up with ink in two fingers and held his slate inward so the row could not see the column.

“You will carry the number down in a clean hand and read it in a room I have never stood in,” the elder said to him. “I have given you the number and not the sitting. Do not lay one over the other.”

“The measure is declared and the person is identified,” the clerk said, in the voice that takes the speaker out of his own sentence. He wrote it down and put the boy in a column where no boy had stood before.

“If the Court’s fire is a candle lit from our mountain,” the elder said, turning toward the stone and not the clerk, “it has never been brought up here to light this one.”

In my hand: He said that to the stone. The clerk wrote claim not examined against it, true of a man who has never climbed five leagues of scree. Both stand.

Higher on the path a body sat facing the way it had come, with no fire and no word read over it. The elder said: “He came from there. He faces there. The sitting is finished. No one goes up to bring him down. There is no slate at a Vorr death and no return, and we do not ask the account below to make one.”

The sitting-song

Said low, facing the stone, the dust in the palm. The elder says each line twice and the boy says the last four twice after him. There is a season between the third part and the last, the length of a sitting, and I have left the space the length it is kept.

The stone was here before the sitting.

The stone was here before the sitting.

The dust is on the hand and the hand is on the stone,

the dust is on the hand and the hand is on the stone;

one for the way you came and one for the way you face,

one for the way you came and one for the way you face.

The number goes in where the bone is old,

the number goes in where the bone is old;

the count comes down and the stone stays up,

the count comes down and the stone stays up.

Here the boy sits for a season. No words are said. The space is the season and I have left it the length it is kept.

The number changes. The stone does not.

The number changes. The stone does not.

We were never owed. We do not ask.

We were never owed. We do not ask.

A girl from the shift asked what the space was for. Nobody answered her. The clerk put a round nought against it and wrote vacancy, which is the first figure of his that has ever fitted anything above this Keep, and still does not fit, because a nought is a number and the space is a season. I have left it where he set it.

The sittings, as the register keeps them

The number goes down the high path in a coat and leaves nothing in the box. These I have set down where they stood, without saying what the numbers count, because I do not know and the Vorr have never told me.

Who kept the stone — Count before — Count after — What was entered at Greybank

Iyn of the Listening Stone, at his third sitting — 41 — 41 — figures confirmed; no proceedings

A woman of the high path, at her second — 17 — 17 — number unchanged; no cause assigned

Ao of the Third Seam, before the Rim War — 9 — 14 — change entered; reason withheld

The boy of this morning, first sitting — not yet counted — the sitting continues — a column standing

One seated higher on the path facing the way he came — no count — no count — no entry; party not on the roll

There were sittings before these that I could set a season to, and I have not written them, because a row of seasons is a thing a clerk can carry away and a stone is not. The Vorr call no number a rise and no season a loss, and no boy has ever come down and thanked the stone for it.

What the clerk did

He borrowed the leaf on the morning after while I was entering the boy in the register, and gave it back with the verbs taken out.

  • At this is put down, not given he wrote the stone is the lawful custodian of the mark, true in a room where a mark can pass from hand to hand and meaningless on a high path.
  • At the count is in the bone he wrote the measure is not deposited; party difficult to draw, and underlined difficult twice, which is the only part he has ever understood.
  • At we were never owed he wrote the party is not entered for a return, leaving out the owing and keeping the party.
  • At the sitting continues he wrote proceedings pending the season, and put a season into a column where no season has ever stood.
  • At the gap in the song he put a round nought and wrote vacancy. When I asked what a season was worth with no number in it, he said nothing a clerk could enter, the first true thing he said all morning.

I have put the verbs back underneath and left his hand standing above, as a newer repair lies over an older one in the seam and the older is still there under it. He takes the doer out because a thing with nobody in it cannot be argued with. I put the doer back because a boy at forty with dust in his palm wants to know who put it there, and whether it was his own hand that will keep it.

At the head and in the gutter

The lichen is off the north stone and will not wash out of the grain, which is why the Vorr scrape with a thumb and not with cloth. The doubled print is Iyn’s and no other hand in this Keep could leave it. The rime was on the path at first grey and was gone before I folded; the writing there is thin and I have not gone over it, for a word written thin is a word said quietly. The shaving held one night and is cold, which the keeper of the warm-stone calls the order of things. The dish stood from the dust to the dead and was drunk by nobody. The grey came off a cuff and nobody will own it. The pinch of dust is shut in the crease and has never been sold or spent or put on a stone.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

Struck through once and written again underneath, very small, in a hand that is not mine, not Iyn’s and not the clerk’s:

The stone has kept every number that was ever brought up to it.

It has never once been asked what the numbers were for.

I asked the girl from the shift, a surveyor who carries the numbers down, a miner who came up and went down again in one morning, and the clerk, and had four answerings and no hand. I have left the line struck through and standing, which is the only honest way to keep a sentence you cannot sign.

The leaf goes back into the oiled cloth and the cloth into the register-box with the dust in its crease and the copper thread where it fell. Next Bone another boy will sit and come down with a number changed or unchanged, and the clerk will take the verbs out and put a nought in the season. I shall put them back as I did this time, and leave the nought in its place and the space the length the sitting is, and the place where the name would stand the length a name would be.
