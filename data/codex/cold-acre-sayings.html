I keep the curb at Cold Acre and set the dish on it for six houses, and have done it four-and-twenty winters. A child of ours was sent down the ridge road in the Thin Year to be entered and came back with a figure in her head and no word for what it counted. She asks what the ridge said before the slate came up. I have set the sayings on the back of a mill tally because a figure spoiled a figure and left paper enough. The well-clay came up under my nails and no washing takes it out of the grain.

Say these low, to the pail, the curb, the dish, the rope and the bare ground, and to a person last. Said looking at a person, a saying becomes an argument, and there is argument enough down the ridge road on a market-day without carrying it home. Our speech is short and the ends of the words fall into the well; a long sentence up here sounds like a man from the town explaining a figure. There is no Last Day on this ridge and nothing gets swept, so the sayings stay where they were said.

Of the well and the eleven links

  1. “Eleven go down. The twelfth hangs in your hand and has never wet.”

Gloss — The chain has lowered eleven links every morning of my life and never twelve. A man of the College stood here with a cord and said the twelfth is kept back for the Hearth. I told him the water comes up on the eleventh and if the Hearth wants its share let it send a man with a longer arm. One in twelve is printed on the notice and is not a lie. A printed thing travels; a chain does not. Both stood in the air between us and neither went into the other.

  1. “The well gives. It does not ask.”

Gloss — Three assessors have measured the lip and set a figure in a column that has never held anything. They leave it empty not out of kindness but because there is nowhere in the house of the world where a figure like that would stand. I told them I never sold the well. I fear the figure, not the carrying of it; a well cannot be carried. Once a figure stands at a thing the thing is a gift, and a gift can be refused, and a man who refuses water on the ninety-fourth day of ice has paid for it somewhere else where nobody stood to see.

  1. “It is not warm. It has not given up, which is a different thing.”

Gloss — A priest come up from Greybank called it a little sister of the Nine Wells and a mercy of the Hearth drawn through the ridge. The well has given no sign that it heard him. They tell us the Kept feel no cold; the well does not feel it either, and I have met it every morning for four-and-twenty winters. Carry that likeness one step further and you come back with a doctrine, and a doctrine is an account that has found somebody to pay it.

Of the hand at the pail

  1. “A hand gone white at the pail wants a stone and somebody to hold it.”

Gloss — The well has not frozen in any winter anyone can name and still draws from a hand faster than the Ilm draws from a stone. A physician from Greybank says water is only water and draws faster than air. I held one hand in the pail and one in the north wind for one count, and the pail hand was colder. Both stand. We say the well has somewhere to put what it takes, as the collectors say it in a cleaner room, only they write it down.

  1. “Warm the stone on your own wrist. The wrist knows; the eye does not.”

Gloss — It came off somebody’s hearth and somebody in these six houses paid for the coals. Put it at the middle and not at the ends, under the wool and against the skin, and count three breaths and three more. The hands and feet are wrapped and left; they are not rubbed and are not set to the fire. A stone taken fresh from the fire will burn a child as easily as a hand.

  1. “Count with her. Do not count for her.”

Gloss — The second chill comes after the warming and not before, on the second or the third day, and it is ordinary. I have kept the counts on birch bark and not added them, for a count added is a count a clerk can carry down the ridge. A woman at three in the morning wants a hand to count with and not a figure to carry away.

  1. “Never put grey in a cut.”

Gloss — A turnip knife goes in clean and the women put grey from the corner in the cut to dry it, believing the grey is clean. I dressed three such and found grey under the skin in all three and could not get it out. The cut keeps a white line. There is no Last Day here to burn it off.

  1. “The end goes first. The middle holds out.”

Gloss — Of the member gone dark. There is an hour on the far side of the warming when a body spends what it was given faster than it takes in. I have no name for that hour and have left the place where the name would stand the length such a name wants. I wind the wool and set them to work, and have seen a foot go dark through a lean week, and gone on winding. This is the row I have been least honest in.

Of the six houses and the road

  1. “Six houses draw and six have buried their dead down at the river.”

Gloss — Feet to the water and a pot broken at the door, and the well draws for the house that is left behind. It has never asked which house. I have looked into the bucket every morning of my life and never once seen a name come up in it.

  1. “Stand and let the barrow go by.”

Gloss — On a market-day the barrow of grey Ash goes past on the ridge road toward the north gate, heaped a finger above the sides where the load has shifted. The children stand and let it pass and say nothing, and take up the stone when the road is empty. No rule was ever taught them.

  1. “Judge the hare by the carrying arm. Ask the weather what it cost.”

Gloss — A man come down from here with a hare across his shoulders will not weigh it; he shifts it on his arm and says the weather, and goes toward the clay road. Asked what the ice would cost he said the weather laid it on and will take it off. These are directions, not figures, and no slate has been opened for one.

  1. “Give the man with the tube the mother and keep the child behind the pail.”

Gloss — He comes before the three days are out if the house has owed, which here is most houses. He takes the mother’s attention first, and a woman being asked for her warmth will look at the man asking and not at the child. This is not refusal. It is the order of the room. We put the pot between and talk to it about the weather until he has gone back down the road.

  1. “A gift on this ridge is not a thing that gets entered.”

Gloss — In the well-game the stone stops at the eighth and the child shuts her fist on it and sets it on the curb, and is brought back only when another takes the stone from her hand, breathes on it twice and puts it back. Nobody says who gave the warmth and the child says nothing when she comes in. A clerk wrote *one restored, nil against it. A nought can be carried away in a cuff; a breath cannot.*

Of the birth-flower and the name

  1. “Cross two fingers and give the mother a date.”

Gloss — The slate-woman up the ridge crossed two fingers over nine children and never told anybody what she saw. A woman down in Sill washed a birth-flower with salt and her child went flat by evening; I have set that beside the six I did not wash and not joined them. When a girl asks what the sign is for, give her the date. A date does not spoil in the mouth and has no figure in it.

  1. “Keep the stone. Do not mark it.”

Gloss — I have a tin under the bench with a stone from every birth, four-and-twenty of them and not one marked. A marked stone is a slate, and a slate can be read by anybody who can hold it. A clerk called the tin residual matter. It is a tin of stones.

The barrow verse

Said standing, with the dish on the lip and a hand on the rope, while the Ash barrow goes past on the ridge road. The last line is held the length of the barrow going by with nobody stepping into the road. Nobody fills it.

Six for the houses and one for the well,

eleven to water and one hanging dry;

barley goes up and a figure goes down,

and the well gives still and asks no price.

Four, four and a gap from the bell at the Cross,

the barrow goes by and the children stand off;

grey in the gutter and none in the ring,

and the twelfth hangs where no water goes.

A hand goes white and a stone goes warm,

a name goes down and a face comes up;

count with her, count with her, one and again ---

[held the length of the barrow passing]

Gloss — The gap after the bell is not ours to fill; it is counting something at the Cross that is not our well. An assessor’s clerk put a round nought in the held place and wrote vacancy beneath it. A nought is a figure and travels. The passing of a barrow is a place and stays where it stood.

What the clerk did

An assessor’s clerk borrowed the leaves on the morning after, with ink in two fingers of a soft cuff. He takes the doer out as though a thing with nobody in it were truer than a thing a woman had done with her hands.

  • At eleven go down he wrote one part in twelve reserved to the Hearth above. He did not ask to see the chain.
  • At the well gives. It does not ask he set the measure is retained in the spring and lawfully drawn, which leaves the well with no hand and the water with no giver.
  • At six houses draw and six have buried their dead he put six returns entered; discrepancy nil, joining the houses that draw to the houses that bury, which is the whole art of that hand.
  • At the held place he put the round nought. I have left it where he set it and put the barrow back underneath.

I have put the sayings back under his hand. He takes the verbs out because a thing always true has no need of anybody doing it. I put them back because a child who came back with a figure in her head wants to know which hand lowered the bucket, and there is always a hand.

In the margin, in the slate-woman’s hand

I crossed two fingers over nine children and told nobody what I saw. Give her the date. I have kept the dates and never set them beside the children, which is a keeping and not an account. --- W.

She wrote it while I was at the curb and set the leaf back with the fold changed. A woman who has kept the slates of this ridge one-and-thirty years is allowed one sentence.

At the head and in the gutter

The well-clay at the head is mine and comes up under the nails in this hamlet, which is why I sign in clay, not ink. The frost-ring took two words off the second leaf when I left the tally outside one night in Stone; I have not written them back, because a saying with two words gone is a saying said slowly and all of ours are said slowly. The barley awn came off a sack by the mill door and has gone stiff as a splinter. The nick at the fore-edge is a turnip knife’s, from a woman who paid me with the knife still in her hand. The tideline is well-water off a pail set down while I was telling a girl to drink and she would not. The tallow is off the lamp I held low so nobody at the table would know I was writing. The grey hair came off a hare carried past by a man who answered the price with the weather; it has lain in the third leaf since. The grey along the outer fold came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying on a ridge. I have not swept it. There is a Last Day in Sill and the corners are burnt there in the morning and called dealt with; there is none here. The twist of rope fibre through the hole is mine and holds the fold, and I will not pull it out to see whether the twelfth has wetted.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The well gives and has never asked who paid.

The barrow passes and the children stand off.

A figure goes down the ridge and the water comes up, and neither carries a signature.

I asked the woman who winds the rope, a man come down from Greybank with a cord for the lip, and a hunter who answered the price with the weather, and had three answers and no hand. The leaves go back into the sacking under the curb-stone, in the nick where the rope is coiled, which the children do not lift. Tomorrow I shall let eleven links go down and the twelfth will hang in my hand as it has always hung, and the man with the cord will go back down the ridge with his column standing empty, and nobody here will say what the figure would be if it had one.
