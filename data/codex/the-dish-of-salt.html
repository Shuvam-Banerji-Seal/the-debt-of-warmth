I keep the brazier room at the head of the Quay. It is a narrow house with the water going under it, and in Stone the frost takes the iron ring at the landing, so that a man puts his boot against it before he will believe it is iron. The boats lie high. The pass is shut and no cart has come down from Greybank in nine days, which means no clay ware, no wool, and nothing to argue about over a fire. So we guess.

A guess here is a question said to the fire. Said to a person, an answer becomes an argument, and an argument in Vhal is a debt with no date on it, which is the one thing this house will not carry through the winter. We put four questions. The wagers are pinches of salt laid in a dish. Salt is the only thing in the room a man can taste and cannot spend, so it is the right stake for a night when there is nothing to buy. The dish goes from hand to hand and the answer is said to the dish. No slate lies where the dish lies. A man who wants a thing entered can go up to the tide-tally house and sit with people colder than himself.

The coal is paid for by the boat-house at the step, which enters it against the share in the spring. Thus a man warms himself before he owes for the warming, which is the ordinary order here and the reason the fire stays lit. I have no word in Vhaleni for a person who sits by a fire without an account. I have a word for a person whose account is current, and I used it of everyone who came that night.

The night as it went

It was the ninth night of the shut pass. The Sallow came in against the wind and went out against the wind, as it does, and the frost stood on the Quay boards in feathers where the Warm had lately gone out of the stone. There were six of us and a boy who swept the landing. A question is put once; ask for it again and you owe a pinch, because the second asking is for your own pleasure and not for the dish. Four are scored. A fifth may be said from the door and is not counted, being a thing said by a man who has not sat down.

The first went to a salt-wife of the third pan, with the salt worked white into the creases of her hands.

Written to be lost, and lost is how I keep;

a wet morning is my ending

and the Council calls it a virtue.

What am I?

“A contract on salt-paper,” she said, and said it to the dish. She did not look at the man from the tide-tally house, who had not yet put his slate down. He put a pinch in all the same, which is the sort of thing a clerk does when he wants it known that he can lose a wager and still be paid.

The second went to a man come aboard at the step, whose opinion on the boat is counted at half and who knows it.

I am held by a mother and not by me;

my word on the boat goes in at half;

I am married to the account

and may never own it.

What am I?

“A man come aboard,” he said to the dish, which is how a man says a thing about himself in Vhal without claiming it. His wife’s sister laughed, and he said nothing, and the dish went on. That is the whole of a marriage at the head of the Quay, and it is not a sad thing unless you have watched it.

The third I put myself, because the room had got loud and wanted quieting, and a question put by the fire is not scored against anybody.

I steal a coal and leave no track but a warm crop;

I am a bird with a borrowed name;

the pan-master counts me as weather.

What am I?

“A brine-gull,” said the glass-woman from the furnaces. She had the sweetness of the cliff at one corner of her mouth. After she had said it she wiped that corner with the back of her hand, though there was nothing there to wipe, and held the dish a moment longer than the passing needed. The salt-wife put a pinch in for her.

The fourth went to the boy who swept the landing, and he answered before the verse was done, which is why he never wins anything and is always first.

I tap from below where the warm is kept;

the boat looks the same when I have done;

no hole in her, and no heat.

What am I?

“A salt-thing,” he said to the dish. He had seen one tap his mother’s boat in the summer, and nobody in the room said the boat’s name. He set his pinch on the dish and did not take it up again, though nobody had asked him to pay, and I have left it lying where he put it.

The tally, taken off the dish at dawn

Question — Put to — Answer, said to the dish — Salt laid down

The written thing — the fire — a contract on salt-paper — one pinch

The half word — the fire — a man come aboard — one pinch

The bird — the fire — a brine-gull — one, laid by another hand

The tap below — the fire — a salt-thing — one, not taken up

Four answered. Nothing was drunk, and the cup that stood by the brazier from the first question to the fourth went back on the shelf with the ale still in it, which is how small ale is drunk in this house when there is a thing to say and no proper word for it.

The salt-wife’s gloss, pressed into the margin in charcoal

She borrowed the leaf before the clerk came and put her mark against the bird:

The gull took from the Council’s brazier at the pan gate, not from ours. It has been doing it eleven years and the Council has never once entered the coal. Say which fire you mean.

Underneath, in mine: Both stand. A gull carries whatever is not watched, and a fire that is paid for by nobody is the kind a bird will always choose. She came back for the leaf at noon and read what I had written below hers and said I had put it rightly, which she would not have said if I had put it wrongly, and went away with her hands white to the wrist.

What the clerk did

A clerk of the Council came with the light, being a man who cannot leave paper lying. He laid his hand on the leaf before his eyes went to the room, which is the manner of that house. He set the four questions down as queries entered and the answers as responses recorded, valued each pinch of salt at a twentieth of a bit, and wrote gaming, not commercial across the head of the tally, so that a night of guessing came out as a night of trading with the profit left off. Against the boy he wrote no standing party, and against the coal, fire privately maintained; no public charge, which is true and is the whole of what the Council cares to know about a fire.

In this room — In the Council hand

A pinch of salt, tasted and not spent — One twentieth of a bit

An answer said to the dish — A response recorded

The boy who swept the landing — No standing party

A fire paid for by a house that is not here — No public charge

He asked me what a guess was worth. I told him nothing a clerk could carry, which is the first true thing I have said to that table in two winters. I have put the room’s words underneath and left his standing above, as salt lies under ink and shows through when the paper wets. He has entered four answers and struck the boy, and the boy was not struck: he said a thing he had seen and took nothing for it, and that is the only answer of the four that cost the speaker anything.

At the head and in the gutter

The salt bloom came up out of the paper itself and I have not brushed it off. The tar at the head is mine, off the coil I keep by the door for the boats in winter. The wine ring near the fourth question belongs to the glass-woman, who set her cup down and did not take it up until the salt-wife had paid for her; I have not washed it out. The press of wet wool across the first leaf came off the man come aboard, who held the door while the clerk read and did not let go when he was done. The crust along the outer edge came up out of the paper on the ninth night, when I carried the leaves inside my coat and the coat froze to my back; I have not scraped it off, because a guessing kept in a warm room would be a different guessing. The green bead in the second fold is cliff-glass; the girl pressed it in with her thumb and did not say what it was for, and I have not turned it to the light. The fish-hook nick came off a man paying for eels with the hook still in his hand. The grey print came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying, and I have not swept it. There is no Last Day here and nothing anywhere gets swept.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

Four answers went out on the ebb. The fire did not.

The dish came back empty and the place by it stayed warm.

I asked the salt-wife, the man come aboard and the boy who sweeps, and had three names and no hand, and none of the three was in the room when the line was written. I have left it where it stands. The leaf goes back in the tin above the brazier, which is the only dry place I have, and tomorrow the pass will still be shut and the boats will still lie high, and if a man asks what there is to do of an evening I shall tell him to ask the dish.
