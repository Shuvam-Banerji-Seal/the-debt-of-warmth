I keep the second tin. The first holds the figures that stand; the second holds the figures that were cut out of arms and are still asked for by somebody. A cutter comes to me for an old number the way a potter comes for a shard, and I give it without a word, because a figure kept in tin is not a figure entered on a slate. The mine-reeve enters the later and leaves the earlier in the skin. I keep the earlier in tin as well, which is a kindness nobody has asked me for and which I have entered nowhere.

These articles were first cut on the board at the mouth in the year the pass was shut. They have been copied forward from the patterns and not from a book, because a book can be carried over the pass and a pattern-board cannot. A pattern nobody has had to cut is a pattern nobody believes. These have been cut. Where a hand has corrected me I have left both readings, as an arm leaves the first figures under the second.

The articles as they stand at the mouth

Of the taking

  1. The cutters take an apprentice, not a house. The trade answers for her bread, her bed and the figures she is taught to cut; the house gives her up for four years and has her back at the end with two knives and no figure written against her. A house that will not give her up has given nothing.
  2. A child is taken after her own Descent, and not before. A child who has never been cut has no business cutting: she does not know how far a figure goes, and will cut the flesh instead of the skin. The arm teaches the knife, and the arm must have been cut first.
  3. She states what she holds before she says who she is, at the foot of the post and not at the table. A cutter who gives her name first is sent back to begin again. This is not pride. A name goes home at dusk; a number goes down.
  4. She is kept, not hired. She eats from the cutter’s dish and sleeps in the cutter’s room, and what she earns goes to the house that sent her and comes back as bread, not as a figure in a hand.
  5. No child is asked whether she wants the knife. She is asked whether she understands what she is bought for: the reading of an arm instead of a face, two sets of figures in one skin, a stone given after the cut and never before, and a hand that must not shake while the man is looking away. The wanting comes after, or it does not come; the trade has never claimed to teach that part.
  6. A lowlander who asks for the knife is sent to the Duelling Floor first, to learn what the iron takes before he learns what it writes. In my years at this tin nobody has asked twice. I would not call that a rule; a rule has to be read aloud.

Of the knives

  1. There are two knives and never one. The first cuts a way at thirteen and belongs to the floor; the second cuts a figure and belongs to the arm that carries it. A cutter who brings the first knife above ground has taken something that was not given, and the mine-reeve will ask her once.
  2. The figure goes in shallow. A cut that goes too deep is a clerk’s notion of permanence: a number is a thing said once, not a thing buried. Where a man has been counted twice, cut the later deeper and leave the earlier showing. Two sets on one arm is one man counted twice, which the Court has always liked; the arm has never been asked.
  3. Speak the figure to the arm and not across the table. A figure spoken to a man turns into a question by supper, and there is no entry for a question at this mouth.
  4. The stone comes after the knife, never before. A stone in the palm before the cut teaches the arm to expect mercy, and the arm will not take the figure cleanly. Give it after, and do not say whose fire warmed it. Somebody paid, and the payer is not in the room.

In the coal-pressed hand of the master of the floor, against rule 10: After. Not before. I have said this to three of you and you keep putting it into the child’s hand early because she is crying. Underneath, in mine: I have left his you standing. He says it to the room and means the whole room. The rule is for the hand that holds the stone, and that hand is mine.

  1. Cut no figure the roll has not given. A cutter who cuts a number of her own has made a man into what he was not, which is the only way this trade can take a life without blood. The mine-reeve checks the figure in red; there is no second asking.
  2. The dead are walled into the seam facing the deep, with their figures on them. No cutter goes down to correct a figure in the wall. A number follows a man where he went, and stays there.

Of the arm

  1. Turn the arm so the figures stand up and look at the arm, not the face. A face asks to be answered; an arm only states what was carried into the dark.
  2. A hand that goes the colour of old rope below a clamp is a hand that has already paid. Do not cut a new number over a dead hand to make the roll come out even. The number stands and the hand does not, and both are entered once.
  3. If a man of the Stones is at the mouth when you cut, keep the space where his answer would stand the length of a man sitting against a stone. Do not put a nought in it. A nought is a number, and he has refused numbers.

The table of counts

Kept by the woman at the tin. A figure goes in the column before a word is put beside it, which is the order of this country and not the order of a counting house.

Year — At the tin — What is taken — What is put in its place

First — Lay out the patterns, turn the arm, fetch the stone after — The child’s own haste — A hand that does not shake

Second — Follow the knife; cut the same figure on a warm-stone shaving until the stroke comes right — The child’s own number in the sentence — The figure before the face

Third — Cut a second setting under the master’s eye and leave the first showing — The want to make a count come out even — Two counts kept in one skin

  1. At the end of the third year the child cuts a second setting on an arm already counted once, with the first figures left showing and the master where he can see the point. If the new figure goes shallow or the old one is cut away, she keeps the tin for a tend and cuts nothing. There is no feast. A cutter’s trade is learned from the figures she has not changed.
  2. She is given no house and no kin and is sent where the cutters send her. She takes her own number with her, which was cut before she ever held a knife, and she does not take the figures she cut into other people, which never belonged to her.

Of faults and their correcting

Fault — As corrected at the mouth

Name before number — Sent to the foot of the post to begin again, by a cutter and not by a clerk

A figure cut too deep — Left showing; no second cut over it that tend

The stone given before the knife — The stone taken back; none given after either

A shaking hand — The knife to the tin; count patterns for a tend

A number cut that the roll did not give — The figure cut out again; the cutter’s own number set against it in the tin

A second saying of a number — The second struck; the first stands

A question asked of the Stones — Answered once, the numbers are correct; no second asking

  1. The last row is not a punishment and not a rule about knives. It is the only answer we have for the Stones, and it is not an answer to the question. A child who asks a second time is sent up the Rim Road to ask the Vorr herself, which is a long walk for a short sentence.

The knife-song, pasted inside the cover

Sung at the mouth on the morning a second setting is cut, with the stone still in the hand and not yet given. Nobody sings it at the Descent, and nobody says why.

Two knives and one arm,

the first cuts a way and the second a figure;

the figure goes down where the name will not,

and the mine-reeve takes the later.

Cut it shallow, leave it showing,

the old five under the new five like a repair left showing;

a hand that shakes goes to the tin

and counts the figures it may not cut.

Four to the first and a fifth cut out,

the old one left where the new one stands;

the stone comes after, the knife comes first,

and the dark keeps the number, not the face.

Two knives and one arm.

Say it to the skin, say it once;

keep the place where the Stones do not answer

the length of a man sitting against a stone.

The clamp-scratch across the third verse came from a man showing his brother how the iron bites, on a morning when nobody had a hand to stop him. Both were called honest afterwards and one has a dead hand. I have flattened the paper as well as I could.

What the borrowed hands did

A survey-clerk of the Court came up with the lead cart and borrowed the leaf while the rope was going down. He writes narrow and upright and takes the verbs out as though they were things a man could carry past a weigh in his boot. He laid his hand on the leaf before he looked at me, which is the manner of that office. He set his lines above mine:

  • Against state what you hold before you say who you are: the vessel is identified by measure at entry, underlined twice.
  • Against the figure goes in shallow: the marking is applied to the surface, which is true and leaves the arm out.
  • Against cut no figure the roll has not given: the instrument is not certified. He has taken the hand away and left the tool, which is a thing a tool would like.
  • Against leave the earlier showing: the prior marking is preserved but is not current. He has made a man’s first count into a page in a file, and the file is not in this room.

I have put my words back underneath his. He takes the verbs out because a thing always true has no need of them. I put them back because a child at the mouth with a knife at her arm does not know she is hearing something always true, and wants to know which hand to hold out.

The master struck writes out of rule 6 and set cuts above it, saying the iron does not write and he will not have a child thinking it does. I have left writes underneath, because a figure is a kind of writing in the only tongue this mountain uses, and a child should be able to see the word he would not let stand. He cleaned his knife while he said it, as he always does, and did not look at me.

At the head and in the gutter

The smut at the head is coal off the sleeve I wipe the tin with; it goes into the grain and no wet sleeve takes it out. The cross in iron-red is off the mine-reeve’s point, laid there while he checked a man who had given a figure that would not hold; it stays red in frost. The shaving in the fold came off a piece set down and not taken up and is still warm on the coldest morning, which is the whole argument about where warmth comes from and who paid, and I have not sold it. The bead of lead rolled out of the Ninth Gallery and a surveyor laid it on the leaf and went away without it. The frost-feather grew where the Warm had lately gone out of the stone and was gone before I had finished setting it flat. The notch at the edge is where I tried to write the Keldish word for a hand that does not shake and it would not come, so I cut the edge instead. A notch stays when paper has gone and cannot be entered by a man from the factor’s house. The grey along the outer fold came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying; I have not swept it, and there is no Last Day at a mouth. The thumb in coal dust at the foot is not mine and not the master’s.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The first knife gives a way. The second gives a figure.

The arm keeps both. The slate keeps one. The stone keeps none, and is warm.

I asked the master of the floor, the survey-clerk and a woman cut twice, and had three answers and no hand, as I have had before. I have left the line where it was written. The leaf goes back into the second tin. Tomorrow a child will come up from her own Descent with a figure already on her arm and will be asked what she holds before she says who she is, and will learn to lay the stone in the hand after the knife and never before; and the old numbers will go on being kept in tin by nobody who asked me to keep them.
