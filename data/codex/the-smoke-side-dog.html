I keep the road to the Old Kiln for the Thane. It is no post a man writes home about. The ruin stands one league south of Sill where the clay has gone grey and will not take a glaze, and the ash in its throat is deeper than a man is tall. I am set there to see that no one digs the grey in the dark and that no one lies down in it and stays there. I carry a short spear with the head gone blue on the quay stone and a fire-pot of coals under ash, and I write these lines at the end of a watch with the tallow down to a knuckle and the cold coming up through the soles.

The rolls I wrote on first were spoiled in a rain that took the figures clean off, which was a mercy of a kind: a figure the rain has eaten cannot be entered. I write in charcoal now, because charcoal goes into the grain and a clerk cannot take it off with a cuff. The soot at the head is my own thumb setting the sheet down on the watch-shed bench. I have not knocked it off.

The road and the river

The hound keeps to the smoke side. That is the first thing a new man must learn and the thing that costs him a night’s sleep. It will lie all watch on the side of the ruin where the warm comes up through the clay and the air moves, and it will not come round to the cold side though a man stand there with his coat open and his pot hanging. The folk of High Kiln say the river is a wall. I thought it was a saying until the night I watched one stand in the mud at the Slip Brook with its nose across the water and a man lying three paces beyond on the other bank. It stood. It did not go over. The man was not saved by it, but he was not eaten either, and in this trade a man takes what the night gives.

It comes off the smoke side in the second watch, when the tallow is low and the kiln-yards down at Sill have stopped their shouting. It does not bark. The sound it makes is low in the throat, like a man clearing himself in his sleep, and you hear it in your boots before you hear it in the air. I have set the watch-song down below with the hearths’ words standing where the watchmen put them, which is not where I would have put them.

What it is

It is a dog. I will set that down before anything else, because the hearths will not. It has a dog’s teeth and a dog’s neck and a dog’s way of putting its head on one side when it listens. It is bigger than the yard hounds at the Tallow Works and thinner, with a coat the colour of wet clay and no grey on the outside. The grey is in the mouth and under the nails. Behind the ears it is hot to the back of the hand, hotter than a beast that has merely been near a fire should be. It smells of smoke and hot clay, and when it has been lying in the throat a man smells of it too, for a day after.

I have opened three. Two were brought down on the road and one was found dead at the mouth of the old flue, where it had gone in after the warmth and not come out. In all three I found a dog altered by the place it lived in, the way a potter’s hands are altered by the wheel. I found no more than that and I have not put a cause beside it. A man who writes a cause into a book he keeps in his pocket is a man who will be asked for it by somebody with a slate.

The breath

The danger is the air. The dog is the thing that brings a man to where the air is.

I have stood on both sides of it. A man who stays in the throat with his head low will come out with a headache and a thirst that outlasts the watch, and his lips are a little too red, which is all the evidence there is. There was a potter of Low Kiln came up in Ash to look at the grey and went down on the second morning in his own house with the window shut, and the physician said it was a merciful death, which is a thing physicians say when they have not had to lay the body out. He had never seen a hound in his life. The potters leave a window open in a firing though the draft spoils the glaze. A spoiled glaze is a loss a man can count. A man is not.

I have written the air where the watchmen write the dog, and I have left both standing.

In the blocky hand of the kiln-master of Low Kiln, written in slip-white with a potter’s stylus, down the margin of this leaf: It is the air. I have buried two who never saw a dog. Leave the window. The glaze can bear it. --- K.

The bite

It does not bite a man on his feet. I have stood within two paces with the spear down and it went on with its own work. But let a man go down and it comes for the face, and it does not shake him or drag him. It puts its mouth to him and waits, the way a collector sets a tube and waits, and the waiting is the part I cannot write about without my hand going still.

The boy they set with me in the second watch went in at Ash 5 after a man we had heard in the grey. We found him face down with the hound at the cheek. I struck it across the back with the spear-shaft and it went three paces off and stood, which is what it does; it does not run and it does not come back at a man who has hit it. The boy’s cheek was two small holes and not much blood, and his lip was red on the side next the ground. I washed with water and bound it. At the end of the tend the holes were closed and the lip was still red and he was asking for water at every watch. He has never said what he saw down there and I have not asked him. Salt does not hold that bite and there is no physic for it that I know. Keep the man out of the close air and let him ask for water. That is the whole of what I have.

The tongue, dried, is bought by Fenfolk to test a close room, for it discolours where the breath is. Two bits a tongue and no questions. The hide is too smoky to sell, so we burn it. A man who tells you he has found a use for a kiln-hound hide is a man who wants another one, and you should keep your coals between you and him.

The watch-song

Sung going in at the first watch, not coming back. The fourth line is the hearths’ line and every watchman sings it wrong on purpose. I have set it down as it is sung.

Two for the clay and one for the smoke,

the dog keeps the side where the fire has been;

it does not bark at a man on his feet,

it is the kiln’s own breath that comes to him.

Keep the window. Let the glaze go bad.

A draft is a thing a man can pay.

The lips go red where the head lay down

and the next day’s water is never enough.

One for the watch and one for the road

and one for the man who did not come back to it;

we do not name him at the singing,

we leave his place where the gap should be.

The gap is the fifth breath the old kiln does not take. The watchmen know it and sing it and have never once said what it is a gap in. There is a man at Sill who sings the last line twice and will not say why, and I have let him keep it.

The tally

These are the nights I kept a true account of. There were others I did not write because my hands were full and one I did not write because I had drunk. I have set down both sorts as they stand. The last column is the clerk’s, not mine.

Night — Place — Hounds — Overcome — Tongues — Entered

Bone 6 — South road at the broken flue — one — one — one — 2

Bone 12 — The throat, east side — two — none — none — 0

Bone 19 — Smoke side below the ash-heap — one — one — one — 2

Bone 28 — Old damper, not gone in — three — none — none — 0

Ash 5 — The throat, where the boy went down — one — one — one — nil

Ash 14 — North bank of the Slip Brook — one — none — none — 0

Ash 26 — The road, after the rain — two — one — two — 4

In the narrow upright hand of the factor’s clerk, struck through the “Overcome” column: Persons overcome: not entered. Air not a vessel. Account stands.

I have left his hand standing. A clerk’s hand has its use: it keeps a man from arguing with what he cannot change. But the boy asked for water through the whole of Ash and the clerk has put nil against him with a neat instrument and no name on it. I have written the thirst in the margin below, where his column cannot reach, and I have put my own mark beside it in charcoal.

What the hearths say

They say it is the kiln’s breath given legs, a thing that got up and went walking because the fire was put out unkindly. There are songs in the Fenmarch with a man’s name in them and a wrong done at a flue. I have heard them and I have no quarrel with a song. I have opened three of these and found a dog, and a dog is the least of what is wrong with that place. If the breath has legs, then it is the room that does the work and the legs only carry a man into it. I have written that last sentence out once and crossed it out twice, because it is the kind of sentence that gets a man called a natural philosopher, and natural philosophers are not set to watch roads in Bone.

I do not know what the hound wants in the throat. I know the order it goes in: the ash-heap first, then the broken flue, then the old damper if the watch is long and the tallow is low. I have set the order down and no cause. The complexion of the place is not mine to explain and I have never been paid to explain it.

At the head and in the margins

The soot at the head is mine. The brown spot on the second leaf fell there from the boy’s cheek when he sat down to have the rag changed, and I have not scraped it, because it is the only entry on this leaf that cost anybody anything. The boot-print across the foot came off the morning I crossed the Slip Brook after the rain and came up to the calf in grey silt; it has dried into the grain and will not rub out. The singe on the lower corner is where the leaf caught when I held it over the fire-pot to read, and the hand that pulled it back was mine and is not entered. The charcoal is rubbed thin where I carried the roll against my thigh. The clerk’s line I have not touched. The slip-white line is the kiln-master’s and will fade before the charcoal does, which is a small kindness in the ordering of things. The song is as they sang it, with the hearths’ lie left standing in the fourth line.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

What kills in the room was there before the dog came in.

There is a place down in the throat where the ash stands deeper than my spear and the air comes up warm on the stillest day of Ash. I have not gone in. Next Bone I will take a fire-pot and a coil of cord and go down to where the old damper was, and if the breath comes up in a place where no kiln has burned in a hundred years I will set that down too, in charcoal, and let the clerk take what figure he can out of it.
