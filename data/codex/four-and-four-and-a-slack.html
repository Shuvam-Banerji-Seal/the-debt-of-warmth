I steer the salt barge that ties up below the north bridge when the water is high and at the Fishstairs when it is low. I set this down with a burnt stick on the back of a tide-tally that a Vhaleni tally-man left on our gunwale and did not come back for. There is no slate on a barge. A slate on a barge goes overboard in the first reach, and a man who writes with ink on a boat writes with water. The burnt stick is the only hand that stays.

This is not a song for a fire. It is hauled, not sung. Coming up from Greybank against the current, four of us on the rope and the bow-man calling, every line is a pull and the last line of each part is the rest. You do not say the last line loud. A man who pulls when he should hold goes into the silt to his thigh and comes up with a boot and no dignity. Coming down with the pots and the tile and the peat, we do not sing at all; the river does the work, and a man who sings going down has not yet learned which way his living lies. What follows is the up-song, which is the only one that wants words.

The call is said to the rope and not to the men. Say it to a man and it becomes an argument; say it to the rope and the rope tells your hands when to lean. The bow-man says the first line of each pair, and the four on the rope say the second. Nobody says the last line of the last part until the rope is made fast. I have set the words as they were called, with the rests left in.

The song, as it is hauled

The cast-off

Cast off where the Clay Road ends,

hand over hand and mind the hands;

the rope is stiff with yesterday’s salt

and a cart pays twice to travel one road.

Four on the near bank and four on the far,

and a slack where the fifth should be;

the rope keeps the count and the river the rest,

and the place goes down with the load.

The long reach

The silt is deep and the pole is dumb;

push where the other man put his thumb.

A loaded barge answers dull

when the mud has got into her.

The gull goes off with a coal not cold,

the eel goes in where it cannot get out;

the river takes what the potter made

and gives back salt, and a doubt.

Four and four, and a slack, and again;

the near bank holds and the far bank waits.

A man who pulls when he should hold

comes up with the silt on his hands.

Under the stone

Below the bridge the ledge gives one note,

low as a man’s throat under water;

the town sets its clock by it and says nothing,

and we say nothing, and haul.

Four and four and a place where the fifth should be,

the Sill keeps the note and the bank keeps the mark;

the figure goes up the road in a clean hand,

and the barge goes up the river in the cold.

Ninety-four days the ice held, they tell us,

and no collector crossed from the quay;

we were warmer for it, and thinner,

and paid for both in the spring.

The bank

Salt in the sack and iron in the hold,

one mark for a skin and two for a bar;

twelve bits to the mark, and the crown goes south

where a man is a bowl and is counted by the mouth.

Four and four, and a slack, and made fast;

the rope is let go and the hands let go.

The account stands where the water stood,

and the fifth is a place, and we do not pull it.

The verse they do not sing before the factor’s clerk

There is one more part. They take it up when the man from the counting house has gone back over the north bridge with his satchel under his arm, and break off if he turns. I have it from the bow-man below the bridge, with the space left before it, which is the space they leave in the singing:

One in twelve, says the notice, one in twelve,

read twice so the late ones may hear it;

but the barrow goes out of the north gate

and nothing is weighed and nobody enters it.

Orra up at the Weir keeps hers in wool —

tie once for owed and untie for paid;

we keep ours in rope-burn and river-silt,

and the Court keeps its own in a clean dark hand.

Four and four and the place where the fifth should be;

the barrow goes out and the slate stays in.

The rope keeps what the slate will not,

and hands it back in the spring.

I asked the bow-man what the last three lines were worth, and he said a song was not obliged to be worth a figure just because a clerk had written it down. Then he put his tar thumb on the corner of the tally to keep it from the wind, as the women do at the yard.

What the Court hand did

The factor’s clerk borrowed the leaf at Greybank on the morning we unloaded, being a man who cannot leave paper alone. He has a cuff gone soft from washing and the ink sits in two fingers of it and will not come out. He struck out the place goes down with the load and set above it, the vacancy is carried forward. Against the rope keeps the count and the river the rest he wrote the measure is recorded and the residue unentered. At the count he wrote eight, fifth nil, which is a true figure and a false place.

I have put the words back underneath and left his standing above. He takes the verbs out because a thing always true has no need of them; I put them back because a man up to his thigh in the silt does not know he is hearing something always true, and wants to know which hand to put on the rope. A nought is a number. The fifth is a place where a man rests. He has put a figure where a rest should be, which is the whole art of that hand.

The tally-man’s line

The Vhaleni who left the tally wrote one line in the leaning hand they use at the Council, above the part about the stone:

The tide follows the Warm, not the moon; the water goes where it is told.

He wrote it of the Sallow, which is his river, and it is true there, the dippers say. I have left it where he set it. Of the Ilm I will say only that it runs downhill, which is more than can be said for the money, and that it does not need a man in a clean coat to tell it where to go. He came back for the tally after all, but did not ask for the writing on the back of it, and I did not offer it.

At the head and in the gutter

The tar thumb at the head is the bow-man’s; he mends the ropes and does not wash before he sings. The silt is in the gutter and in the fold and worked into the rope-fibre where the leaf lay coiled; I have not knocked it out, because a tally that has never gone up the river with a barge is not the sort of tally a bargeman keeps. The eel slime at the foot came off a basket set down on the leaf while a man counted six eels and was paid five marks for them, or five eels and six marks, which is a thing he will not settle and I have not entered. The salt bloom is on the corner that lay under the sack. The boat-hook nick is mine. The green bead came off a coarse pot broken on the quay while we unloaded; it rolled under the beam and I put it in the fold. The grey print nobody will own came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying on a boat. I have left it. There is no Last Day on the river.

At the back, in a hand nobody will own

The fifth is the stroke you do not pull. It is the stroke that brings her up.

I asked the bow-man, the tally-man and a carter waiting on the quay, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The leaf goes back into the coil of rope, and tomorrow we go down again with pots and tile and peat, and sing nothing, and come back up with salt and iron, and haul the four and leave the fifth where we found it.
