I keep the last-day slate in the Court’s room in Slate Row. The order comes down every year from the Hearthseat to the factor at Greybank, and is copied here so that a house may know what is being entered against it before the bread is cut. The order is in Ledgerhand, and there is nobody in any of its sentences. I put the people back underneath, because a sweeping with nobody in it is a sweeping nobody did, and that is the kind this Court has always liked best.

It was sent down to the factor in Y.H. 418, set down after the Rising of Y.H. 668, and added to in Y.H. 782 and in the Thin Year. A rule with no broom on it is a rule nobody has had to sweep. These have been swept.

The standing order as it stands in the Court’s room

I. Of the day and the lamp

  1. The Last Day is the fifth of the Five and belongs to no month. Bread is baked in every house. No market is held, no figure is read, and the bell at the Cross is not rung.

Margin: In the Five the bell is silent, and on Account-day it goes four, four and a gap with nobody crossing himself. I have kept the two silences apart. A man who puts them together has begun to keep an account of a rumour, and the Court pays for those by the page.

  1. The grey in the corners is not swept on the Last Day. It is left where the year left it, and is gathered by lamp in the morning.

Margin: It is invisible in daylight and plain by tallow-light. I hold the lamp low so a household will not know I am writing, and the grey comes up out of the grain of the leaf where my thumb turned it. A woman at the tubs in Stone of the year gone told a girl that the grey would lie where it lay until the Last Day and be burnt the morning after and called dealt with. I have entered the telling as a telling, and not as the grey.

  1. The order is read at the Cross before the bread is cut. The reader does not ask what the grey is.

Margin: I asked once. The reader said, residue arising on the premises, which is true of a corner and of a room and of a person standing in it, and is therefore true of nobody. I have set the answer at the edge, the length of a face.

II. Of the places

The left column is the order’s own name. The second is what this district calls the same place. The third is what goes into the slate. The fourth is mine and has no standing in any court in Sarn.

The place as the order names it — What our district calls it — What is entered — What stands beneath

The corner behind the sleeping-place — a draught coming under the door — residue, no quantity — a promise that went unkept, as the yard says

The skirting of the warm room — old soot off the firing — residue, swept — the cool stone at the rack end and the wool over the grain

The boards before the shelf — weather — residue, swept — two gaps in a row of loaves and a finger that will not read them

The floor beneath the wheel — slip — residue, swept — a seat kept hollow by twenty years of sitting

The step at the door — mud brought in on boots — residue, swept — a pot broken there, with the shard left where it fell

A house from which no collector came — nothing arising — residue, not entered — a name spoken once at a bank and a whole return for one not in the room

Margin: I asked a woman at the well why the grey gathers in a corner. She said it gathers where something has gone unkept. I have put that at the edge, the length of a saying, and not into the column, because a saying with a cause in it becomes a figure by supper in this country, and a figure wants a payer, and no payer stood at that well.

III. Of the burning

  1. In the morning the grey is gathered from the places and burnt. The ash of the burning is not entered, and no deduction arises against the year.

Margin: A deduction is a figure and the grey is not a figure. Where the order says no deduction arises, I have left a place the length of a figure so that a man may see what stood there. In nine years nobody has filled it with a nought, which is the only honesty this article has ever been capable of.

  1. The fire is from the house’s own hearth. No priest attends the burning and no collector. In the Hearthseat the grey is burnt in a brazier beneath the grating, which no priest is allowed to touch.

Margin: A woman of Low Kiln carried her grey to the midden in a twice-mended pot and set the pot on the step afterward with the crack showing. I have entered the pot as a pot. I have left the crack where it was; a mended thing leaves the hand that handled it showing, and this order has no column for a hand.

  1. The house says that the grey is dealt with. The word is not entered.

Margin: I asked a woman what was dealt with. She said the grey. I asked what had left it. She said the winter, and went for her water. There is no column for the going for water, and I have not made one.

The form of the entry

Set out at the end. I have copied it with the blanks as they stand. In nine years no house has asked to have the last line read aloud.

Entered this day, being the Last Day, in the year of the Hearth ----, the bread baked in the house of ----------------------, the grey left in the corner where the year left it, gathered by lamp in the morning and burnt with the house’s own hearth, no figure arising and no deduction entered, the name spoken over the corner ----------------------, which is not entered, the word dealt with said by ----------------------, who did not say what was dealt with; written by ----------------------, whose hand went to the corner before it went to the crust.

The sweeping-song, pasted inside the cover

Said low at the door while the lamp is held, never at the Cross and never at the burning. The second line of the third verse is the women’s line; the clerks sing it wrong on purpose.

Grey in the corner by tallow shown,

the bread set down and the crust its own;

sweep it after the lamp goes round,

call it dealt with and burn it in the morn.

Four for the bell and four for the bread,

and a gap where the fifth should stand;

the Court has a figure for what was took,

and none for what the corner kept.

A crust at the corner for one not come,

a crack left showing where the wire crossed;

the grey goes up in the morning smoke,

and the house goes on where the house was.

Four for the bell and four for the bread,

and the gap goes up with the rest;

the fire keeps the year and the lamp keeps the place,

and the hand that held it keeps none of it.

The bell is not rung in the Five. I have kept the gap as a gap and have not put a twelfth into it, whatever the College writes.

What the borrowed hands did

A clerk of the College came up from Slate Row on the morning the order was copied, with ink in two fingers of a cuff gone soft from washing. He struck the grey is gathered out of article four and set the residue is collected and disposed of above it, which is true of every corner and therefore of none. Against the woman at the tubs he put a round nought and wrote no person arising under it. A nought is a figure and a woman is a person saying a thing; I have put mine back underneath and left his above, where they belong, the way the grey is left in the corner until morning and called dealt with.

The other hand is a factor’s from Greybank, brown at the edge with a little gold in it. Against no deduction arises he wrote the premises are cleared, and against the crust at the corner, an article of value, not entered. I have not struck either. A hand with gold in it can call a crust an article; a hand that has held the lamp at the corner calls it a place set for somebody. Both stand.

At the head and in the gutter

The grey at the head is Ash off my own thumb, turning the leaf toward the lamp while I found a name, and it will not come out of the creases. The white in the gutter is tallow gone hard where the lamp stood through a watching; it has taken the corner off one line without taking the sense, and I have not picked it off. The crumb in the second fold is barley from the Last Day bread, pressed in while I read the form of entry to a woman who held the crust in her apron and did not eat it. The curl of grey wool through the tie-hole came off a cradle blanket carried over a threshold on the Last Day; I have not untied it. The pale ring is a pan of washing-water set down while I read article four to a woman who kept her hands in her sleeves. The bone-coloured streak along the edge is slip off a swaddling cloth laid here while a girl from the yard waited for me to finish; it is not Ash and in this room the difference is worth knowing. The green bead is lead glaze, pressed in by a girl come up from High Kiln with the sweetness still at her mouth; she leaned to see the form of entry, did not ask what the grey was, and went back down the stair. The scorch in the third leaf is a spark off the household hearth, caught while I held the paper too near in order to read it in the draught. The tide-line along the foot is water from the washing of the step after a burning; it has taken the corners off two lines and left the lines.

There is no Last Day for a slate. The order is read, the bread is cut, the grey is burnt, and the leaf goes back into the drawer with the grey still in the grain of it.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The bread keeps the house. The lamp keeps the corner. Between the crust and the smoke a woman leaves what the order has no column for.

I asked the woman who bakes, a clerk come down from Greybank, and a man who reads at the Cross, and had three answers and no hand, as I have had before. I have left the line where it was written. The Last Day stands ahead; the bread will be baked and the grey left where the year left it, and in the morning it will be gathered by lamp and burnt, and the house will say it is dealt with, and the corner will keep whatever it kept.
