I take rats in High Kiln and the lower Ferr for the glaze house, and I have kept this book two winters. It is not a book of reasons. I have no slate-name and no learning, and I set down only what I have put my hand to. A man who writes what he has heard at a hearth is writing a hearth, not a rat. I have known both sorts of book and this is the sort a man can use.

The sweet at the head is not slip. It is lead glaze ground into the grain where I carried a gnawed rim in my pocket for three days before I thought to wrap it. It tastes sweet in wet weather and I have not washed it out. A book that has never tasted of the trade it keeps was never taken into a mix.

What it comes to

It comes to the sweet, not to the crumb. This is the first thing a new man gets wrong, and the thing that costs him a week of empty traps. Set a trap with barley and cheese and you will catch a house mouse, which is nothing to anybody. Set the same trap with a chipped rim off a glazed cup, the lip still green where the glaze ran thin, and you will catch a fistful before the tallow has burned down. I have set both sorts on the same shelf of an evening and the barley went untouched and the rim had teeth in it by morning.

It gnaws the lips of cups and the seams of bowls. It is the rim it wants and not the broth. A household that sets its good cups on the shelf above the meal will find the rims gone pale by the end of a tend, and the women will say the pots are wearing out, which they are, in the way a thing wears out when something eats it. It is no more particular than that. It will take the rim of a Cup from Sere as soon as a coarse bowl out of the Yard, and the sweeter the rim the sooner it goes.

I have set a chipped rim on a shelf in a house with no children and caught nothing for four nights. I have set a sound cup beside a bowl of barley in a house with three children and taken seven. The sweet draws them and the children give it somewhere to come to. I have set that down and joined nothing to it.

The catching-song

Said low over the bench before the first rim is set, never sung. The catchers say the third line into the cup and not up to the ceiling. I have set it down as it is said, with the last line standing where they leave it.

Two bits to the score and the score is the tail,

the tooth keeps the shape and the cup keeps the tale;

it comes to the sweet and it comes to the seam,

and a house with rats is a house gone thin.

Set not the crumb but the lip that has run,

the green at the edge where the glaze came undone;

count by the tail, never the nose, never the ear;

one bite is a nothing, the hundred is here.

Burn the tails in the slip-shed, keep the rim for the mix,

and leave the last cup where the girl’s hand will find it;

she tastes it at morning and I take it at night,

and the one that goes back is the one we don’t count.

The last line is not a figure. The catchers say it into the cup and nobody has ever told me which rat goes back. There is a man in High Kiln who sets two rims on his shelf of an evening and takes one away in the morning and will not say why. I have let him keep it.

What it is

It is a rat. I set that down before anything else, because the hearths will not. It has a rat’s teeth and a rat’s neck and a rat’s way of stopping still in the middle of a run to listen. It is bigger than the yard rats of the Tallow Works and thinner, and the coat is the colour of wet clay, so that a man sees the green at the root of the teeth before he sees the body. The green is in the teeth and under the claws. It smells of the sweet and of the warm crack behind the mixing bench.

I have opened nine. In all nine I found a rat altered by the place it lived in, the way a potter’s hands are altered by the wheel. In the third I found a green in the stomach and a sweetness in it that I could smell through the paunch, which is not what I looked for in a beast that had been at a cup rim. I have written the two things on the same leaf and drawn no line between them. A man who writes a cause into a book he keeps in his coat is a man who will be asked for it by somebody with a slate.

The physician’s year

A physician come up from Greybank in Y.H. 641 wrote that the sweetness came from the lead in the glaze and that the rat carried the same lead in its teeth, and the word went out of the copy before the year was over, as though a word could be scraped off paper the way slip is scraped off a bench. I found the year in a physician’s page that came down the Ferr with a cargo of coarse tile, and I have put it back here where a man may read it and go on reading.

In slip-white, in the blocky hand of the glaze-mixer of High Kiln, down the margin of this leaf: It is the glaze that brings them, not the rat. Keep the tasting-spoons off the bench and the cups off the shelf. Leave me the year if you must, but leave the word out of it. --- G.

I did not write his line and he did not write mine. He has taken the word out of his own house and asked me to leave it out of my book, which is a reasonable thing to ask of a man who is paid by the tail. I have left the year standing and put no name to the payer.

The bite

It does not fight. I have had one run over my boot and it went on with its own work. But let a hand lie still near a cup it is working at and it will take the hand. The bite is two holes and no blood worth naming, and a greenish edge that a man notices when he is dressing it and not before. The physician from Greybank says the lead goes in with the tooth and makes the ways sluggish over a season. I have not seen the lead. I have seen the season.

A girl of the mix went in at Grain 9 with her hand among the tasting-spoons and was bitten on the thumb. There were two holes and she bound them with tallow and went on tasting. At the end of the tend the holes were closed and the thumb was still, and she was putting the spoon down with the other hand because the first did not tell her where it was. Salt does not hold that bite and there is no physic for it that I know. A single bite is a thing a man forgets by the next tend. It is the hundredth that forgets him.

I have seen children in a house with rats in the cupboard going thin by degrees through a whole year, and the mother bringing me tails by the score and asking what the figure was. I told her the figure was two bits a score and she went away with the answer she had brought with her. It is not an answer a man is proud of. It is the answer the trade has.

In the narrow upright hand of a clerk of the factor’s house at Greybank, borrowed against the leaf at the kiln door: Rodentia not a vessel. Hand-bites: not entered. Cups gnawed: wear and tear, no deduction arises. Lead: not a figure. The year Y.H. 641: not on this roll. Account stands.

I have left his hand standing. But he has put wear against a cup that was eaten, and wear is a word for a thing that happens to a pot with nobody at it. I have written teeth in the margin below, where his column cannot reach, and put my own mark beside it in charcoal. He has left the year off his roll, which is the only thing on this leaf he and the glaze-mixer agree about.

What the hearths say

They say the rat is the sweet sickness made small, a thing that got into the cups because the glaze was mixed with a lie. There are songs in High Kiln with a girl’s name in them and a wrong done at a mixing bench in the year before the wars. I have heard them and I have no quarrel with a song. I have opened nine and found a rat, and found the green in it, and found the cup it came from with the rim gone pale. If the rat is the sickness made small, then the sickness was in the cup before the rat came to it, and the rat is only the thing that came to it. I have written that sentence out once and crossed it out twice, because it is the kind of sentence that gets a man called a natural philosopher, and natural philosophers are not set to catch rats in High Kiln.

The yard calls the rat a mercy because it takes the bad cups out of a house. I have not called it that.

At the head and in the margins

The sweet at the head is glaze off the rim I carried, and it comes back in damp weather and goes off in a dry. The brown thumb at the fold is slip. The grease along the fore-edge is tail-grease, from wiping my hand on the leaf before I could find a rag after Grain 9, and I have left it, because it is the only mark on this book that came off a catch. The greenish ring is where a tasting-cup stood while a girl told me she had tasted the mix since she was nine and had never once been asked what it cost; I did not ask her, and she went back up the steep street. The scorch is where a spark off the glaze kiln took the corner of the third leaf, and the hand that pulled it back was mine and is not entered. The charcoal is thin where I carried the wrap against my thigh. The slip-white will fade before the charcoal does, which is a small kindness in the ordering of things. The clerk’s ruling I have not touched. The physician’s year I have copied in my own hand and have not crossed out. The song is as the men said it, with the last line standing and the count left open.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The rat is not the payer. It is the witness that stays.

There is a shelf above the mixing bench where the new cups go to be tasted before they go down the terrace, and the glaze on them is sweeter than any I have found in a household cup. I have not set a trap there. Next Grain I will take a score of cord and a chipped rim from the mix and set them on that shelf among the tasting-spoons, and if the rats come to the new cups before the girls do I will set that down too, in charcoal, with the verb left in it and no figure at the end of the row.
