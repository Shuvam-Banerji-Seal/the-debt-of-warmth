To Maris Gilded at Greybank, who keeps our northern account and will not call a courtesy a courtesy before the factor’s clerk. From Ilyra, of a house of the Ten Bowls, in the open court at the Bowl of Sere.

I begin with the courtesy, which is the first thing owed between sisters of a house, as you were taught and I was not. I hope you are warm. I hope the factor’s table has a cloth on it this winter and that the new clerk has not yet learned to smile at a figure. I have put the thing itself in the fourth paragraph, where you will look for it last, as is proper. If you read only the first three you will have a letter full of hospitality and no news, which is the kind most of our correspondents send and the kind I have never managed to write to you.

The bowl was emptied on the tenth of Grain. The Sun-Emperor emptied it with both hands and the Council read the emptying as a debt forgiven in the south and a province ruined in the north, and both readings were entered before the wine had finished running down the steps. Our household’s slates were not put to the fire. I stood in the court with my sleeves wet at the cuff from pouring for two hundred people and felt relief go through me like warmth through a thin vessel, and was ashamed of the relief before it had done arriving. Relief said aloud in an open court is an invitation to the next lot. I said nothing and poured more wine.

We teach that a vessel was made to be emptied gloriously and a life hoarded is a life wasted. I have taught it to my daughter in the open court with the fire in the middle of us and the bowls going round. I am not now certain that I ever taught it to our mother. She was of the kind that holds, which in this country is called a virtue until it is called a hoarding, and the house spent a great deal to have her back three times.

The returns of our mother, as the house keeps them

Entered by the house-accountant in the careful hand. The last column is mine and has no heading; a heading would make it an account.

Year — What the house paid — How she came — What came back — What she asks for

Y.H. 799 — Twelve hundred marks, and the measures not counted here — Whole — Our mother. She remembered the name-day and the cook and the pot with the chipped lip. — The chipped pot.

Y.H. 804 — The same figure, entered as the same figure — Whole — Our mother, smaller. She remembered the pot and not whose name-day it was. — The pot, and who poured.

Y.H. 809 — The same figure, entered a third time — Thin — A stranger with a faint interest in a life it cannot name. — The pot. Always the pot.

She sits in the open court now in the warmest place that is not the fire’s own place, and asks for a pot that is not there. It is the chipped one. I keep it in my room. I have not given it to her and I have not thrown it away. A pot kept in a room is a thing with a roof over it. A pot given is an account opened, and a thing given in this house is never given twice; it is entered once and then owed back. A pot thrown away is a debt denied, and a debt denied in Sere is a debt the household carries at twice the figure. I have chosen the roof. This is the thing I put in the fourth paragraph. I have told it to no one in the court.

The northerner your factor sent south was exhibited at the feast and given small coins. He had the pale line at one gum and the fingers of an old rope, and when they set a cup in his hand he turned it a quarter and set it back where it had stood, which the court took for a trick and applauded. To the fire he said, in the clay-country speech, It’ll go in the thaw. Nobody in the court knew whether he meant the weather or the account, and it was the only thing he said all evening that was not in the singing. I asked the woman beside me what it meant and she said a northerner says it of everything, which is an answer given by people who have heard a thing often and never asked. He went off with the light and a handful of small coins and no slate of ours against him, which is the whole of our hospitality to a northerner: we clap at his answers and we do not enter him.

You remember the phial we spoke of before you went north. I bought it of the Court’s factor through your house: sixty marks, which is a great deal of wine. It is called quiet ash and it is sold to the rich who want their previous life to stop arriving in dreams. The woman who sold it said the dreams go in a tend and do not come back. She had a soft cuff and a neat hand and did not once look at my face while she said it. The phial stands on the shelf behind my bed and has never been opened. A thing bought to stop a memory and kept sealed is a memory the buyer has chosen to keep, which is a Spending done in private and entered nowhere. Every time I pass it I think I have done a clever thing with sixty marks. Every time I pass it I think of the pot.

The song as it is sung while the bowl goes round

Sung in the open court, at the emptying, never at the burning of the slates. The fourth line is the emperor’s line and the room says it with the singer. The last line is said low and held until the next bowl is brought.

Fill for the full and fill for the thin,

the vessel was made to be poured, not kept;

a life in a cup is a life let go,

and the house that hoards is the house that wept.

One for the bowl and one for the fire,

one for the hand that the bowl goes through;

the slate burns white and the slate burns black,

and the household that burned will be entered anew.

The north sends warmth and the south sends wine,

and the factor books both at a figure he knows;

the guest says a thing in a speech not ours,

and we clap at the answer and let the guest go.

Pour for the mother, pour for the pot,

pour for the phial that stands on the shelf;

the bowl goes empty and the room goes warm,

and the thing we keep is the thing we left.

I have set the song down as it is sung, with the last line held. A clerk who takes the verbs out of a song leaves the bowls going round by themselves, which is a thing bowls have never done and never will.

You used to say measure for measure when you wanted the bowl emptied and the account called even, in the tone of one who has already decided what both measures are. I have learned what the phrase means in the south, which is precisely that and nothing more. I have never heard it said in this court by anybody who had not already poured the wine.

What the factor’s clerk did

A clerk of the factor’s house borrowed the leaf on the morning after it came up from the south, being a man who cannot leave paper alone, particularly paper with gold on it. He writes in the narrow upright hand they teach at the College, with the verbs taken out and everything in the passive. He laid his hand on the sheet before his eyes went to the room, which is the manner of that table.

  • Against our household’s slates were not put to the fire he set a disposition was not recorded, which is true in the way a shut fist is true.
  • Against she asks for a pot that is not there he set a want was noted; no claim arises, and the pot went out of the sentence as though a pot were a thing a woman could want without its having stood on a shelf.
  • Against I keep it in my room he set the article is retained by the household, which makes a room a household and a sister an officer.
  • Against the phial has never been opened he set the measure remains unspent, and put a round figure in the margin where I had put none.
  • Against the song he wrote nothing at all, but he numbered the lines, which is his way of singing.

I have put the verbs back underneath where I could still read them. He takes them out because a thing always true has no need of verbs. I put them in because a woman at a table in the last tend of Grain wants to know which hand pours for the mother and which hand keeps the pot. I have left his figure standing above mine and my emptiness under it. A leaf with two hands on it is the only kind this house believes.

In the margin, in a second hand, small and set close to the writing

You have put the thing itself in the fourth paragraph and I have found it there, as you knew I would. You always did hide the thing in the longest sentence and then blame the reader for going straight to it.

Do not send the pot north. It would have to come the coast pass and the damp gets into everything on that road, and a pot that breaks on the road is a debt with no household standing to answer it. Keep the roof over it. She will not ask for it where she is now, and if she does, tell her the house has it, which is the truth and costs us nothing, being the sort of truth the factor’s clerk can enter without a verb.

The phial came. I have not opened it and will not open it for you. A sister who wants a dream stopped should say so in the first paragraph and not the fourth. I have struck nothing out. Your verbs are where you left them, which is more than I can say for your courtesy.

At the head and in the gutter

The gold is worn thin at the folds where the leaf has been carried in a coat against a wrist, and the writer’s hand shows through where the gilding has gone. The ring at the head is red wine, set down by the writer while she was pouring and not drunk until the song was done. The scorch at the second corner came off the open court fire on the night of the emptying and went out under a sleeve, which is how a great house keeps a fire and how a leaf gets a corner taken. The bead in the lower margin is cliff-glass off the Glaze Cliffs and was pressed there by the exhibited northerner, who leaned to see the gold and had not been asked to touch the leaf; he did not ask for it back, and the writer did not give it to him. The slate dust in the gutter came off a slate laid on the factor’s counter while the correction was being made. The grey along the outer fold is not the writer’s and not the factor’s. She has not swept it. There is no Last Day on a sheet of gold paper.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The bowl is emptied. The pot is kept. The phial is sealed.

Three vessels, and none of them holds what was paid for.

I asked the factor’s clerk, a woman who burns the slates by lot, and a northerner who was exhibited at the feast, and had three answers and no hand, as is our custom. I have left the line where it stands.

The leaf goes back into the tin among the account leaves, where it is the only sheet with gold on it and the only one that has ever been carried in a coat. Tomorrow the court will fill the bowls again and the fire will be lit in the middle of them, and the mother will ask for the pot in the warmest place that is not the fire’s own place, and the phial will stand on the shelf, and the pot will stand in a room with a roof over it. Nothing was opened, given, burnt, spent or ended in the writing of this letter. A thing kept under a roof is not a thing returned, and a thing returned is not a thing that has been kept.
