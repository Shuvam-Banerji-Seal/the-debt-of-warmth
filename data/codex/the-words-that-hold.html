I keep the survey-roll of the First Seam. It lies in the survey room off the Duelling Floor, in a tin with a lid that shuts, and once a year the Order of the Tenth Clamp takes a paper from a tin lined with warm-stone on the next shelf, reads it, puts it back, and does not act on it. I am not the Order. I keep the tally of the seam, which is older than the Order and will be kept after the men who read that paper have gone up.

This extract is the year 717, when a surveyor of this room set two words against the oldest repair and was taken up the shaft for it. The clerk who entered the taking used a clean hand and left nothing in it that could be argued with. I have put the two words back underneath. A correction that leaves the seam standing and takes away the man who read it belongs in the margin, where the verbs have not been taken out.

The seam as the roll has it

The rim-seam carries repairs laid one over another, the newest over the oldest, and the lowest of all lie below the Ninth Gallery where the rock sweats and the smell of hot lead is the only warning a man is given. We do not call these repairs stone and we do not call them clay. In the surveyor’s own language a repair is named for what it is made of, and the oldest are entered as that which was left unsaid made solid. The Keldish translate it as Ash. The Court’s copy, kept in the Hearthseat and shown to no one below an assessor, is said to translate it as language — said, and not seen by anyone in this room, and I have kept the saying as a saying. Both are guesses, and neither guess has ever been brought up to the floor to be looked at.

The survey of 260 has the passage that no Court history carries. It says the repairs lie in layers as a pot is mended again and again until nothing of the first pot can be seen, and it calls the oldest a repair of unkept words. I have copied that phrase nowhere but this leaf, because a surveyor who carries a phrase out of this room is apt to be met at the shaft mouth by a man with a clean slate.

The entry of Y.H. 717

The entry stands in the surveyor’s own hand, the number-first hand we use below the floor, and it has never been smoothed:

Ninth layer, below the warm-stone. Not stone. Not clay. The shape holds where nothing has been set to hold it. Entered as words that hold.

In the narrow upright hand of a clerk come up from the Hearthseat, written above: Repair of earth and mineral deposit; no linguistic quality arises. Strike the two words.

Underneath, in mine: I have not struck them. He had to read the two words before he could write that there were none worth reading, which is the manner of that hand in every room it enters. A man who takes two words off a slate has had to stand where they stood. I have set my hand under his and left his above.

The order that took him up

What was entered of the removal is shorter than what was entered of the repair, which is the order of things above ground:

The person was removed from the shaft; no entry arising. Account standing.

The surveyor is not named. He is entered as the surveyor of the ninth layer, which is the custom here: a man states what he holds before he says who he is, and what this man held was two words. The mine-reeve checked him in red at the second corner of the leaf and found no lie in his measure, and the red point stands there still, which is a check and not a correction. He was not taken up for a false figure. He was taken up for two true ones.

I asked three men what the order was for. One said the seam had been disturbed. One said the office had been disturbed. One said the person had been disturbed. All three are true and none of them is an answer, which is the Keldish way of giving a man the whole room and leaving him to find the door himself.

The two translations

The layers as this room keeps them, with the word from the Court set beside the word we use below. I have set both and chosen neither, because a surveyor who chooses between two guesses has made a fact of one of them and will have to answer for it at the weigh.

Layer — The Court’s word — This room — What the entry says

Above the Duelling Floor — dressed stone — dressed stone — stone, and a true note under the knuckle

The warm-stone workings — mineral deposit — warm-stone — sold, not lent; keeps a cup one night

Below the workings — earth and slipped clay — the Sweating — hot lead gives the only warning

The crossed entry of Y.H. 588 — irregular marking — birth-flower — a returned man with a branching red mark; the entry struck by the hand that made it

The ninth, lowest — language — Ash — words that hold — two guesses and a phrase, all standing

The entry of 588 lies in this same roll, in a hand that struck out its own finding. I have set that hand beside the hand of 717, which did not strike its finding out and was struck out of the shaft instead. Both crossings are in the same ink. I have not joined them. A surveyor who joins two crossings has begun an account of the room, and accounts of rooms are kept above ground.

The paper on the next shelf

Seven years before the year of the two words a paper came up from the Hearthseat called The Sealed Vessel. It lies in the tin lined with warm-stone in this room, and the Order reads it once a year and does not act on it. I have read it once, standing, because a keeper of the seam may stand in that place and may not sit in it. The paper asks for a world in which nothing leaks and nothing is owed. I do not know whether such a world would hold a cup. Below this floor nothing holds that has not been mended, and the oldest mending is the one we cannot name.

The song said at the mouth

Taken down from a woman who counts at the shaft mouth, said once to the boy going down and once to the ground after he has gone. The second part has a line left out. I have set the space where it stood and put nothing in it, though I know the word that would go there.

One for the arm and one for the stone,

and one for the count that goes before;

the knife goes in and the number stays,

and the name is left in the room.

Two go down and the count comes two;

the figure stands though the arm may not.

Hold the rail, it is iron and it takes;

hold the stone, it is sold and it cools.

A man wrote down what the lowest said

and the lowest said it true;

they took him up by the same iron road

that a boy goes down at thirteen.

Two go down and the count comes two;

— — — — — — — — — —

the mountain keeps what the mountain took,

and the slate keeps the place where the words stood.

The woman would not say the line that is left out and would not say who left it out. I have kept the space the length of a line. There is a cup at the end of the Duelling Floor that nobody takes, set for a man whose number was changed twice, and I have entered neither the cup nor the space as a cause.

What the borrowed hands did

A clerk of the Hearthseat came up in the year of the Long Cold, with ink in two fingers of a cuff gone soft from washing. He struck words that hold and wrote no linguistic quality arises over it, which I have set down elsewhere in this leaf. Against removed for two words he wrote the person was reassigned; no entry arising. Against the song he wrote oral tradition; not a record, and he drew a line under all my margins and wrote No such place above them, so that the edge would read as though it had never held a margin. He had to read every margin before he could write that there were none in them, and I watched him do it.

The round hand from the Warden’s hall wrote above the removal order: the person was removed for the good of the office. This is true in the manner that a clean slate is true. I have left it standing over mine. Neither of those hands has put a thumb on the ninth layer, and both have entered it.

At the head and in the gutter

The coal at the head is mine, off the sleeve I wipe the tally with, and no wet cloth takes it out. The slate dust in the gutter came off the rubbing of the 588 entry and has worked into the sewing. The red at the second corner is the mine-reeve’s point, finding no lie. The shaving in the fourth leaf is warm-stone that kept a cup warm one night on this floor and is cold now, which is the order of that trade. The frost-feather lay on the lip at the mouth and was gone by noon; a yard above ground calls that weather and this room calls it a map of the leak. The tallow thumb at the foot is my own lamp set too near while I copied the removal. The square of bare paper is where I scraped a word away with a knife-point and put no other in its place, and it is the only square in this roll that I made myself. The grey along the outer fold is not mine and not the Court’s. I have had this leaf six years and have not swept it. There is no Last Day under this mountain.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The numbers go down and the count comes back.

Two words went down and the man came up.

The seam holds what the slate would not hold,

and the slate keeps the place where the words stood.

Ask the ninth layer what it is made of.

I asked a Vorr at the stones, who said the numbers were correct; a clerk of the mine-reeve, who said no entry arose; and a woman who counts at the mouth, who said the count came two and would not say the rest. Three answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written.

The roll goes back into its tin in the survey room. Next tend I copy the walling-slate off the Duelling Floor, and after that the Ninth Gallery repairs, where the lead smells hot before it takes anyone. The ninth layer is still where it was, with two words standing in it and a clean word written above them, and where the surveyor went after they took him up the shaft the roll does not say, which is the manner this building has of shutting a door.
