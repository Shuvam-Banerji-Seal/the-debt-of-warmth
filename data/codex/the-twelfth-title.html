I keep the room-slate in the Waiting Room off Slate Row. It is the room nobody goes in, which is a saying of the Cross and not an ordinance, and it holds two benches, a cradle worn to its last bottom, a cup that stands where somebody set it, and this slate turned face down before I sit. The room smells of wet wool and cold iron and a little of tallow, and underneath those the sour mineral smell this town knows and does not say. I write what the ordinance tells me and set the verbs back underneath, because an overnight with nobody in it is an overnight nobody kept, and those are the only kind the Court has ever wanted entered.

At the head of the first leaf, in mine, written small: Copied by the room and for it. The verbs are mine. The absence of them is not. Let both stand.

It was set down in the first years after the Rising and confirmed to the factor at Greybank in Y.H. 418, when it was found that a night could pass without anybody in it. It has been added to twice since, in the same hand as the first, so that no man can tell by the writing which rule was made before the winter and which after. A rule with no night on it is a rule nobody has had to sit. These have been sat.

Title the Twelfth, as it stands in the room

From the Ordinances of the Keeping, Title the Twelfth: Of the Overnight Keeping, the Two Benches and the Cup Left Standing. Set down after the Rising; confirmed to the factor at Greybank by the treaty of Y.H. 418; added to in Y.H. 782 and in the Thin Year, Y.H. 812.

I. Of the bringing

  1. A vessel is brought to the room after the evening figure is read and before the dark. The bringing is made by the officer appointed and not by a house. A house that comes to the door is answered at the door.
  2. The slate-name is entered on the room-slate and the birth-name is not taken. Where a vessel has no slate-name, the hour and the bench are entered in its place, and the place where a name should stand is left the length of a thumb.
  3. A slate is set face down at the head of each bench. The hand goes to the slate before it goes to the vessel, and what is written is not corrected by what the vessel says afterward.
  4. Where more are brought than the benches will hold, boards are laid upon the floor and no figure is taken for the boards.

Margin: A man came to this door in the Thin Year with his coat on his arm and asked to go home for it before the dark. He was told the morning came first. I entered the hour and the bench, and not the coat. The room is cold enough that a man asks for his coat as other men ask for water. Neither has a column.

Margin: In the last tend of Stone more were brought than the benches would hold, and boards were laid. I entered boards, as the ordinance provides, and not the forty who lay on them. In the morning a barrow of grey went out through the north gate, and I set the barrow at the edge, where it has no column.

II. Of the night

  1. Nothing is entered between the bringing and the morning. What is said in the night has no column and is not carried up the road.
  2. No fire is carried into the room. Warmth is kept in a stone under the wool at the middle, and the stone is not counted and is not marked. A stone set to the ends wastes its heat on the part that cannot keep it.
  3. The cup stands where it is set and is not removed before the morning. No entry is made against the cup.
  4. No officer sits in the room. The room-clerk sits with the slate face down and reads nothing aloud after the bringing.
  5. The collected are kept until the banking and are not called by any name the room has given them.

Margin: A woman asked me whether the cup was for her. I told her it stood where it was set. She took that for yes and drank, and I did not correct her, which is the only true answer I have given here in nine years. Neither the asking nor the drinking is entered; the ring the cup left is on this leaf, where nobody can add it.

III. Of the morning and the going up

  1. In the morning the slate is turned face up and the figure is read before the vessel is looked at. This is the order of the room and not the order of a house.
  2. The vessel is taken up the road to the bank, and the room is swept after it. What is found in the corners is called grey and is carried out and burnt, and no entry is made against it.
  3. The cradle is wiped and the cup is set back where it stood. A cup set back is not a cup washed, and the ordinance asks after neither.
  4. No roll is closed in this room. What was entered overnight is carried forward with the other figures and is not called an overnight in the roll.

Margin: I put the figure is read before the vessel is looked at back under rule ten, where a College clerk set the reading precedes attendance. The same order said to a slate and said to a person. A person can be looked at; the order provides that he is not.

Margin: The grey in the corners is the colour of skimmed milk gone off, and it will not wash out. Houses sweep their corners on the Last Day and burn what they find and call it dealt with. There is no Last Day in a Waiting Room; the barrow goes out every morning and comes back the next, and nobody has called it dealt with.

  1. A fault copied twice in one tend is a fault the clerk has not understood, and the senior clerk sits with him at the bench until he has. The slate is not a rod. It is a piece of stone with a place where a person sat.

The form of the overnight

The bench-song, pasted inside the cover

Said low to the cup, sitting, with the slate face down. Never in the morning, and never within hearing of a man come down from the College. The last line is said to the boards and not to the company.

Two benches and a cup on the sill,

a cradle with the bottom worn thin;

the slate goes down before the eye

and the coat stays folded on the arm.

No fire comes in and the cold comes up,

a stone at the middle, the ends left bare;

she asks for her name and you give her a date ---

a date is safe and the cup does not care.

One in twelve as the notice says,

read to the slate and not to the face;

the barrow goes out while the boards lie still

and the grey comes back in the selfsame place.

Two benches and a place on the floor,

a ring on the leaf where the cup once stood;

what was said in the night goes up with the grey

and the room keeps the shape and never the word.

The wet reed across the last verse came in on a coat from the Weir. I have flattened it. A clerk who explains a song has begun to write a sermon, and sermons get copied.

What the borrowed hands did

A clerk of the College came down with ink in two fingers of a cuff gone soft from washing, and borrowed the leaf while I was at the benches. Against rule nine he struck the collected are kept until the banking and set the vessels remain in custody above it, true of a room and false of a woman who asked me for water. Against rule five he wrote no matter arising, and had to look at every night on the table before he could write that none had matter in them. I have put both of mine back underneath and left his standing where a man must lean to read them.

The other hand is a physician’s, come up from Greybank, taller and kinder than the first. Against the stone at the middle he set a common settling; no remedy applied, the phrase that took the second chill out of the record in Y.H. 651. I have not struck it through. A kinder phrase costs less to enter, and a clerk who has sat one night here knows which hand has been nearer the bench. Both stand.

At the head and in the gutter

The grey at the head is Ash off my own thumb, turning the leaf toward the lamp while a figure was read from the other room; it will not come out of the creases. The filings in the gutter came off a tube-wrap set down while the officer went for water; there is always iron in the fold, and it has never once drawn the warmth out of the paper, which is the only mercy this room holds. The pale ring is where the cup stood from the first watch to morning; it is the thinnest place on the leaf and no figure can be read into it. The brown at the second fold is crust-grease, left by a woman who held her crust for a child not in the room. The curl of grey wool through the tie-hole is mine and I have not untied it. The tallow is from the lamp I keep low, so a woman will not change her tale when she hears the pen. The green bead is lead glaze, pressed in by a girl come up from High Kiln with the sweetness still at her mouth. The tide-line along the foot came up out of the boards in a wet Silt; it takes the corners off figures and leaves the figures. The bare square is where I scraped a name away with a clip’s edge and put no other in its place. I have not written it back, and I have not forgotten it.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The slate keeps the hour. The cup keeps the ring.

The room keeps the shape of a person and enters neither.

I asked a collector’s man, a woman who had sat overnight on the second bench, and a clerk come down from the College, and had three answers and no hand, as I have had before. I have left the lines where they were written.

The leaf goes back under the slate and the slate face down on the shelf. Tomorrow the evening figure will be read and brought, and I shall set the verbs back where another hand took them out, and turn the slate before I look at the person. A woman will ask for her name and I shall give her a date, because a date is safe and the Court likes a date. The cup will stand where it was set, and in the morning the grey will go out through the north gate, and nobody here will call it dealt with.
