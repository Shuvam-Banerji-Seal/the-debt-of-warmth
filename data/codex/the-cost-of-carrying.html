I keep the Heaped Measure on the Clay Road in Greybank, where the clay road comes in from Sill and turns uphill before noon to the factor’s table. The sign is a bowl of barley with the grain piled above the rim. A clerk of the factor came in once and told me a heaped measure is a false measure, which is true in his trade. I told him the sign was a promise and not a measure. He wrote that down too, which is the sort of man he is.

In Stone the frost takes the road and the Ilm goes under its skin, and this town fills with men who cannot get up to the factor and men who cannot get back down to Sill. There is a granary here with a beam and a second count, and a bank with a lower room where people sit between drawing and pouring, and eight leagues of clay between us and the Cross that nobody mends. Of an evening there is nothing to do but ask one another what a thing cost, which is a riddle in this town, because the figure and the cost are two figures and never the same one.

So we play the costing. Each puts a thing he carried through the year and asks what it cost him, and the house says whether the answer serves. It is not a guessing game. The answer is in the room already, and the trick is to say it in one breath without turning it into a figure. The stake is a grain of barley in a dish. At first grey the dish goes to the fire and the grain burns, so that nothing is carried out of this room in a slate. A man who wants that may go uphill before noon and sit with people warmer than himself.

The rules as the house keeps them

  1. A costing is put once. Ask for it again and you owe a grain, because the second asking is for your own pleasure and not for the house.
  2. Answer in one breath and say it to the dish. Said to a person it becomes an argument, and an argument in Greybank is a debt with no date on it.
  3. Four costings are scored. The fifth is kept on the shelf, where a thing may stand a long time without being entered.
  4. The judge is the dish. I say whether an answer serves and I do not say why. A reason given is a costing given away for nothing, and the giver can take it up the road and sell it.
  5. No slate lies on the table. The factor’s clerk sits at the end of the bench and writes on his knee with the slate turned inward, which he says is not lying on the table. He is the only man who has argued the rule with me and won.

It was the third night of Stone, with frost on the inside of the glass and the road shut behind the door. There were five of us: old Varre who carts lime up from Stonemouth, Tess of the granary beam, a man from the lower room of the bank whom nobody called by a name, the clerk with his slate on his knee, and me behind the bar. The man from the bank held his cup with both hands and had not drunk from it. I have learned not to take that as a sign of anything.

The four that were scored

The first was put by old Varre, who had come in with lime-white in the seams of his hands and would not sit down until he had put his back to the fire.

Up on a back and down on a back,

counted at the beam, counted at the pot;

the figure goes into the house

and the back goes home.

What is carried?

“Lime,” Tess said, and that was a true answer and the dish took a grain for it. Varre said nothing until the grain had gone in. Then he said, “A man. There’s lime in every load and a man under every lime, and only one of them is ever entered.” He said that to the fire, not to the dish, so it was not scored. He has said it to the fire every winter I have known him, and the fire has never answered him, and he goes on saying it.

The second was put by Tess, who had been on the beam since before the granary had a second count and knows the difference between what a sack holds and what a sack is called.

It comes back to a house that knows the cup

and does not know the hand;

two figures go in and one figure comes out,

and the difference is fed at the table.

What came back?

There was a pause in which the man from the bank turned his cup a quarter on the bench and did not lift it. Then he said, “A person,” to the dish, which served. To the clerk he said, “A thin return,” which did not, because a man who has been poured into a cradle and cannot say which house he came out of does not want that sentence said over him at a bar. The clerk wrote it down anyway. I gave the man a grain and he set it on the bench beside his cup and did not put it in his pocket, which is one way of refusing a thing without refusing it.

The third stood to the man from the bank, though he had not asked to play and had not once looked at the dish. Put a question to a man like that and you get the answer he carries, which is often not the one he was asked.

I carry a thing I cannot set down

and cannot say the name of;

my hands go to it in a room I have not entered;

it is warm and it is not mine.

What do I carry?

The clerk wrote before anyone could stop him. A preference retained from the pooled measure, he wrote, in the hand that takes the verbs out of everything it touches. It is the name they give it in the lower room and it is a true name and it is not an answer. I told him the question was what the man carried and not what the bank called it. He said a preference was not a thing that could be carried, which is a thing a clerk can say with a straight face while his own two fingers are black with ink. The man looked at the dish at last, and said, “The warm out of a house I never lived in.” I gave him a grain. He took it this time, and held it a moment the way you hold a coal before you put it somewhere safe, and then put it in his sleeve, and I have not seen it since.

The fourth I would not have put, because a costing put by the house is not scored against anybody, but the clerk asked to put one, which was a first in eleven winters. He put his slate a little further onto his knee and did not look at any of us.

I carry a figure up the road and bring it back smaller;

I am paid for the carrying and cannot say what it cost me;

the ink is in two fingers and will not come out.

What am I?

Nobody answered for a while. Then Tess said, “A clerk,” and the clerk said that was the trade and not the answer. Old Varre, with his back still to the fire, said, “A man that carries a figure all day for a man that never carried it.” The clerk sat with that a moment. Then he said, “There’s no figure for it, which is why I asked,” and I gave him a grain, the first grain ever given out of this dish to the factor’s house. He took it up between finger and thumb as though it were a mark, and put it in his coat, and went away up the hill before morning with it. He has never said what he did with it. I have not asked.

The fifth, which was kept on the shelf

I put this one myself, standing behind the bar, so it was not scored and no grain went into the dish for it. I have put it every winter for eleven years and I will put it every winter until I can no longer stand.

I am counted and then destroyed;

I am the only measure in this town with no carrying-forward;

I lodge in a crack and the fire goes over me.

What am I?

“A grain of barley,” I said to the dish. There is a grain in the crack of this dish that has been there longer than I have kept the house. The fire goes over it every morning and does not find it. I have stopped lifting it out. A cracked dish tells you where it has been, which is more than can be said for the factor’s slate.

The tally, in charcoal on the warrant

Costing — Put by — What served — Grain

The load — old Varre — Tess: lime; the man below it said to the fire — one, paid

The cradle — Tess — the man from the bank: a person — one, left on the bench

The warm — the man from the bank — the house: the warm out of a house never lived in — one, taken in a sleeve

The figure — the factor’s clerk — old Varre: the man under the figure — one, carried up the hill

The grain in the crack — the house — the dish — not scored

The figure read at the granary that morning was one in twelve, as the notice says, and it went over the four grains with the ale and nobody looked at it twice. There is a pause in that figure the length of a held breath, which the clerk reads past and I do not, but that is not a thing for a costing night.

What the clerk did

He set the costings down as queries entered and the answers as responses entered, and put a figure of marks against each of the four, which was more than I had scored, because the dish scores in whether a man sits down again and he does not. Against the fifth he wrote house question; no entry arising. Against the burning he wrote grain destroyed by fire: waste, not a measure. Against the grain in the crack he put a round nought and wrote vacancy.

I have left his hand standing above mine, as a newer repair lies over an older one on a pot. He cannot enter an ending, so he calls a burnt grain waste, and he cannot enter a place, so he calls it vacancy. A nought is a figure. The crack is not. I have set my words underneath and let him carry his figures away, which he is paid to do and does very neatly.

Old Varre pressed a line into the margin in the charcoal he marks his buckets with, in a hand so broad it took the whole gutter.

Ask a man that carries a figure all day what his shoulder carries. Then you’ll have two answers and neither of them will agree with the slate.

I have left it where his thumb put it. He did not sign it. He never signs anything.

At the head and in the margins

The wet clay at the head is mine; I held the warrant down on the bar before I had done with the ledger and did not know I had marked it. It goes into the grain and no damp takes it out, which is why the house signs in clay. The tallow thumb at the fold is off the lamp I turn the leaf by. The barley grain pressed into the crack of the dish came out of the dish itself and not out of my hand; I set the warrant down on the rim and it was there when I lifted it, and I have not prised it loose, because a grain that chooses a crack is not a grain to move. The frozen wool across the first leaf came off the man from the bank, who held the door while the clerk came in and did not let go when he was through. The dark ring is small ale that stood from the first costing to the last and was drunk by nobody; three men have claimed it and one woman has denied it and I have given it to none of them. The browned crust in the gutter is a barley crust set down by Tess and taken away again uneaten, which is how a crust is usually taken in this town. The frost came up out of the paper on the climb from Sill and is the only thing in this house that entered without a hand. The grey along the outer fold came off a cuff that had no business lying where I keep the tin, and I have not swept it. There is no Last Day in a tavern, and nothing here is burnt in the morning and called dealt with, except the barley, which is burnt, and is the whole reason the clerk will not stand behind this bar.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The figure goes up the road. The back comes home.

The dish keeps the grain that the fire missed.

I asked the clerk, Tess, old Varre and a man come down from the bank, and had four answerings and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written.

The warrant goes back into the tin beside the wick. The grain stays in the crack, which is a place and not a number, and the clerk will come again when the road opens and ask for a costing and write his figures over mine, and I shall put the dish out and burn what is in it at first grey. If he asks me what a grain of barley is worth I shall tell him to ask the fire, which is the answer he took away in his coat and has not brought back.
