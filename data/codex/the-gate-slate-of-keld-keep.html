I cut the figures at the gate with a copper point on a slate that leans against the post. The wind comes down the shaft mouth and takes the chalk before it takes the snow, so I cut deep. At the end of every tend I wipe the face with a wet sleeve and go away, and by morning the old figures have come back through the new ones. A slate kept clean is a slate with no memory, and a road without memory is a road a man pays twice on.

My measure stands at fifty-two and my name is not cut here. At this gate a man states what he holds before he says who he is. A lowlander comes up and gives me his name and waits for me to ask the rest, and I send him back to the foot of the post to begin again. I am not shaming him. A name is a thing a man can change at the next well; a measure is what he carries into the dark, and the dark does not care what he calls himself. The mine-reeve checks a man who has lied about his number, and the checking is done in red, and there is no second asking.

What follows is the market at the shaft mouth --- what iron, lead, coal and warm-stone fetched here in Bone of the last year and in the first carts of this one --- and the dues of the roads by which a man comes up to the gate and goes on toward the pass. I set the lowland figure beside the figure taken here because a price carried up the Rim Road changes at every post, and a man who knows only one end of it will call the other a robbery.

Coin in Keld is scarce and iron is common. Twelve bits to a silver mark; twenty marks to a Sere crown, which comes north with the mercenaries and goes south again with them, a fine thing to hold and a poor thing to spend in a mountain. The Court books in embers and will tell a man an ember is five marks. I have set no ember on this slate. In eleven years at this gate I have never seen one paid, and I have seen a man try.

In the narrow upright hand of a factor come up from Greybank, written above my own: Ember: five marks. Reckoning entered. Underneath, in mine: I have left his figure where he set it and put as told beneath. He writes of an ember as though a man could carry one up the road in his boot. I have carried iron in my boot and never an ember.

The market at the shaft mouth

Cut on the face of the slate. The first column is what a man is told in Sill or at the quay; the second is what is taken here, at the weigh beside the post.

Article — As told below — As taken here

Good iron axe — 2 marks — half a mark

Iron bar, Keldish — 2 marks the bar — half a mark, by the load and not the bar

Lead, a pig — 6 marks at Greybank — 1 mark here; the seller below does not say which workings, and we do not ask

Coal, a sack — 3 bits in Sill — 1 bit; carried out in a coat and not entered

Warm-stone, a palm’s piece — not sold in Grath — 2 marks; it holds heat longer than anything else in Sarn, and is sold, never lent

Warm-stone shaving, for a brazier — --- — half a mark; swept up from the cutting floor, not given away

Cold-iron clamp, off the Duelling Floor — not sold — 3 marks; the bite that holds a vessel whole and the bite that takes a hand off a man

A mercenary, the season — hired by the Court and by Sere — 30 marks and keep; trusted by neither

Under-vessed hand, life reckoned at — eleven months — eleven months, whether he lasts them or not

Bought breath, a season — not spoken of below — paid in iron, entered in months

The Descent, at thirteen — no price — no price; the numbers cut into the forearm and a warm-stone given after the knife

In mine, against the coal row: A man from Sill paid three bits for a sack at this gate and asked why the same sack was a bit here. I told him the coal had not changed and the road had. He said that was a robbery done by a road, which is a true thing and not an entry I can make.

The mine-reeve’s red cross against the warm-stone row: He set it there with his point while I was cutting the figure and said nothing. A cross in red on this slate means the figure has been checked and is to stand. It does not mean the stone is warm.

In a sloping hand, a lowland carter’s: Paid two marks for a bar here and was charged two again at Greybank, and the factor’s man said the second was for the road. In mine: Both stand. I did not collect the second and cannot enter it. A man who carries iron down a road pays for the iron and again for having carried it.

A man who comes back from the Descent thin has the old numbers cut out of his forearm and new ones put in. Nobody asks him which count is his. I have seen a man with two sets of numbers, one over the other and the later cut deeper; the mine-reeve entered the later and left the earlier standing in the skin. The under-vessed are entered at eleven months as a matter of course, and a man who lasts fourteen is entered at eleven still, because the figure is for the life bought and not the life lived. I have kept the honest column where the honest figure is. It is a short column.

The dues of the roads

Two roads are counted at this gate and a third that is not a road but a shaft. The Rim Road comes up from Sill, a hundred and twenty leagues of improving road and worsening weather; the Cold Pass lies ten leagues beyond the keep. Karric VII shut the pass in Y.H. 741, and the agreement of Y.H. 747 fixed it as the border. There is a post there and no gate. The Warden’s surveyors keep the record of the rim-seam and state a man’s measure before his name, which they do at this gate also, as a courtesy, so that no man can say he was not told.

Due cut at this gate

Surveyor’s mark upon a load going down — 2 bits; the load is not weighed again in Grath

Cart with iron, going down the Rim Road — 1 mark, or iron to the value

Beast on the hoof — half a mark

A man going up without a warm-stone — no stone lent here; he borrows from the next man who has one, or turns back

A man who has lied about his measure — the figure corrected in red by the mine-reeve; no second asking

Traveller going farther into the Rim — no due, and no guarantee

The Cold Pass — nothing at the post; there is no gate

A load going farther than the pass — not entered; the Warden’s surveyors do not promise to bring a man back

The shaft mouth — no toll; every Keldish child goes down at thirteen

In the carter’s sloping hand: They asked me at the post what the pass cost and I said nothing, and they went through, and I never learned whether nothing was the right figure. In mine: It is the right figure. There is no gate. A post is not a gate and a border is not a wall.

In the Greybank factor’s narrow hand, beneath the row about the liar: Let the entry stand as measure misstated, corrected. In mine: I have set no second asking beneath it, because the red cross was on the slate before his hand got here and the cross is the older figure.

The roads have two ends and the figures do not agree between them. The tally-man at Greybank cuts what goes out at the quay and the clerk at the Cross cuts what is paid at Sill; I cut what is taken at the post. Between the three of us a cart is counted twice and a man is counted once, which is the proportion the Court has always liked. The Hearth Road takes nothing at any gate, having taken it at home; the Salt Road calls its toll a contribution freely entered, which is a finer name for the same figure. I have not cut either on this slate. They are not roads of this keep.

What they chant at the post

Said low by the men waiting their turn at the weigh, never within hearing of the mine-reeve. The crowd says the last line with the singer.

One for the gate and one for the name,

one for the stone you carry in;

a hundred and twenty to the keep and ten

to the post where the counting stops.

Hold the clamp and call it true ---

the hand that holds it knows.

Eleven months and a warm-stone too,

and the mountain keeps what the mountain owes.

In the Greybank hand, against the first line: One figure entered. In mine: There are two roads and a border, and that line is a place where a count stops, not a number. A man who puts a nought in it has never been to the post.

The hands that are not mine

The factor’s hand is narrow and upright and leaves the doer out of every sentence, as though a thing with nobody in it were truer than a thing a man had done. He set measure misstated, corrected above mine and went away over the pass with a warm-stone charged to the account. I have set my words under his and left both standing. The red cross is the mine-reeve’s; the two marks are in the carter’s own mud. A slate with four hands on it is the only kind a man at this gate will believe. A slate with one hand on it is a Court notice, and a Court notice has never yet told a man what the road would charge him.

At the head and in the gutter

The smut at the head is coal off the sleeve I wipe with; it is in the grain of the slate and no wet sleeve takes it out. The red cross is iron oxide from the mine-reeve’s point and stays red in frost. The dust in the gutter is slate dust off my own cutting; it gathers at the foot and is swept onto the floor, where the men tread it into stone. The warm-stone shaving in the fold came off a piece a man set down while he counted his marks and went away without it; it is still warm on the coldest morning and I have not sold it. The bead is lead out of the Ninth Gallery, where the rock sweats and the smell of hot lead gives the only warning there is; a man laid it on the slate and did not take it up. The frost along the edge is a feather that grows where the Warm has lately gone out of the stone, and it is gone by noon. The clamp-scratch across the two columns was made by a man showing his brother how a clamp bites; both were honest men afterwards and one has a dead hand. The thumb in coal dust at the foot is not mine and not the mine-reeve’s. I have not wiped it away. There is no Last Day at a gate.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The slate keeps the figure. The iron keeps the hand. The pass keeps the man who goes through it and does not come back.

I asked the tally-man at the quay, a surveyor come down from the Listening Stones, and a man who had been through the pass twice, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The slate goes back against the post. Tomorrow the carts come up with the winter’s coal and a lowlander will say his name before his measure, and I shall send him back to the foot of the post to begin again. The mountain sweats in the deep and the smell of hot lead gives what warning it gives, and the figures of the last tend show through the figures of this one, which is how a man reads a road: by what was cut here before him and not taken away.
