I keep the well-rope at Cold Acre and have drawn at this curb for thirty-one winters. I set this down because a man come up from Slate Row stood on the ridge in Stone and asked what the children said when they passed the stone round the well. He wanted a figure for the warmth. I told him there was a turn in it and no figure, which is the truth of the thing, and he went away and sent a clerk, which is what a man does when he has been given an answer he cannot carry back.

The leaf is the back of a mill tally whose figures had gone soft at one corner, and I wrote with a charred stick out of the barley kiln, because ink will not stay on a paper that goes into a bucket. The children’s words I have set as they came, half-sung and with nothing mended in them. What they do not say before a man with a clean cuff I have put at the end, where such things belong. I have left the clerk’s hand standing where he set it and the children’s words under it, as the curb keeps both the rope’s mark and the water’s.

Where it is played

In Stone and Frost, on the bare ring of ground that goes around the well, where the frost will not hold. The river lies ice at the Sill and the Singing Sill keeps its note under the ice, but the ground at our well stays dark and a little soft to the boot, and the children say it is because the well is breathing. I have heard men say it is a current under the ridge. Nobody has asked it with a slate in his hand, and I have not asked either.

They do not play in the Five, because in the Five a child stands and listens and there is no round for it. They do not play on a market-day while the barrow goes out of the north gate and along the ridge on its way to the Bone Orchard, heaped a finger above the sides where the load has shifted. The children stand and let it pass and say nothing, and take up the stone when the road is empty again. This is not a rule anybody taught them.

The choosing, said before the stone is taken up

The children stand in a ring with their hands behind their backs. One among them, facing the well, passes the stone from fist to fist behind the ring and says the words. She does not look at the faces. On the last word the child who has the stone takes the first turn. Said to the well. Said looking at a child, it becomes an argument, and there is always a child who will argue.

Six houses and a well,

a well and six houses;

stone in the fist and thumb on the stone,

and the one it stops with starts the round.

The passing, as it is played

Eight lines, one line to each passing of the stone from hand to hand round the ring. The words go low and are mostly sung. A child who passes late has let the turn go and is cold at once; a child who passes early has taken a turn that was not hers, and must give the stone back and breathe on it herself before the round can go on. At the eighth line the passing stops, whoever holds the stone. That hand is the untaken turn. The child shuts her fist on the stone, sets the fist on the curb, and does not open it. She is the turn that stands.

One for the north road where the barrow goes,

two for the gate that lets it through;

three for the turnips under the snow,

four for the stubble the frost holds to;

five for the rope too stiff to bend,

six for the breath that warms it again;

seven for the curb where the bare ground is,

and a turn for the water that comes without a hand.

The well gives. It does not ask.

Let the stone go. Let the turn stand.

The eighth is not a number. I have asked them why and they say it is the turn, and if I ask what the turn is they ask me why a woman wants to know what a well does when nobody is at it. There is always a child who will say the water comes up because somebody let the rope go, and there is always another child who will say no, the water came up first and the rope went after it. The argument never settles and the game goes on all the same.

The bringing back

A child who stands on the curb with a shut fist is brought back in one way only. Another child takes the stone out of her hand — she does not give it, it is taken — breathes on it twice, and puts it into her hand again. Nobody says who gave the warmth and the child who was cold says nothing when she comes in. If the stone is breathed on three times and still nobody takes it, the standing child stays where she is until the well-rope is let down and drawn up again, which the children call the ebb, and the ebb takes a while.

I have seen a child go to the curb on purpose on a morning when there was no fire in her house and the bare ring held more warmth than the hearth did. I have set that down and have not mended the sense of it. A gift on this ridge is not a thing that gets entered, and a child who has never been given a figure knows where to stand in a game about giving.

The verse they do not say while a man from Slate Row is on the ridge

From the child who keeps the curb, with her back to the road. She would not say it to the clerk and would not say it within sight of his cuff. She said it to the well.

The barrow goes out and the well stays in,

the slate says six and the ridge says none;

a breath is a gift and a gift’s a debt

in the hand that writes in the town.

The turn stands where the fingers let go;

nobody took it, nobody owes;

the water comes up when the river is ice,

and the Court has never asked its price.

Pass it behind and pass it back,

and warm the one you left in the cold;

the stone says nothing, the well says less,

and the well gives still, and asks no more.

In the margin, in the hand of the woman who winds the rope

Against a turn for the water that comes without a hand, in a small hard hand pressed close to the gutter:

Not without a hand. I have let go at this curb and the bucket has come up all the same, full, with nobody standing to it. There is a hand somewhere, only it is not mine.

Underneath, in mine: She has put a hand back into a line where I put none, and both stand. A well that gives when nobody winds is a thing a woman will argue about, and a thing a woman will not let a clerk enter.

What the clerk wrote above the eighth line

A clerk of the factor’s house at Greybank borrowed the leaf on a morning in Ilmen, being a man who cannot let paper lie. He has a cuff gone soft from washing and the ink sits on the first joint of two fingers and will not come out. He wrote above and a turn for the water that comes without a hand: one turn unperformed; unmeasured yield; presumed to accrue to the Keeping. Against The well gives. It does not ask he set source unentered; no deduction arises. At six for the breath that warms it again he wrote warmth passed between vessels without slate; no value attaches. At the foot, where the child is brought back with a breath, he put a round nought and wrote one restored, nil.

I have put the children’s words back underneath and left his standing above. He takes the verbs out because a thing always true has no need of them. I put them back because a child with a shut fist on a cold curb does not know she is hearing something always true, and wants to know whose hand will warm the stone. He has made a giving into a yield and a turn into a nought, and the nought belongs to the Keeping while the turn belongs to nobody. That is the whole art of that hand. He said the game was an account of the Return taught before a child could read a slate, and he entered it. The children stop when the soft cuff comes along the ridge. They take it up when he is back down the road, and they never once change the eighth.

At the head and in the gutter

The thumb of well-clay at the head is mine. I sign in clay because a wet hand will not take ink and because this curb is the only thing in Cold Acre that keeps a print the whole winter through. The grey along the gutter came off the barrow road, where I laid the leaf down while the Ash went past on its way to the north gate; I have not swept it, and there is no Last Day on a ridge. The white ring took two words off the fourth line one night when the leaf lay outside the door on the frost; I have not written them back, because a rhyme with two words gone is a rhyme said slowly, and all of ours are said slowly. The barley awn in the fold came off a sack by the door and has gone stiff as a splinter. The nick at the fore-edge is the turnip knife’s, and the thread in the second leaf is sacking, which is the only sort of clothing this hamlet has ever had. Frost is the only clerk Cold Acre ever had, and it takes two words where the factor’s clerk takes the verbs.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The well kept its own account before there was a slate. It has never entered it, and it has never once run short.

I asked the rope-woman, the children, a man come up from Greybank and a neighbour who carries turnips to the north gate, and had four answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The leaf goes back under the loose stone in the nick where the rope is coiled. Tomorrow the children will draw their ring on the bare ground and pass the stone and leave the turn standing where it stands now, and the bucket will come up with nobody at the rope, and the Court’s figure will stay where the Court put it, which is not at the well.
