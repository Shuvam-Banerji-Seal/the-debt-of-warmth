I am the second dipper at the pans and I have written this where I was told not to, on the back of a tally Kett spoiled with a wet thumb. A dipper’s business is nets at dawn and the draw; a net is blessed by being trailed in the sea, not by having words said over it. The women of the boats asked me for a saying they could keep in a dry place, because a prayer carried in the mouth changes in a wet season and a prayer on salt-paper goes the way of a law, which everybody in this port calls a virtue and I call weather. What follows is what we say over a boat going out on the ebb. The clerk’s words are left standing under mine, where he put them.

The tar at the head is my own; I hold the leaf with the hand I use for the net-rope and did not know I had marked it. The rope print came off the bollard when a woman leaned over to see what the fourth station said. The hook nick is not mine and I have left it. The green bead came off the Glaze Cliffs in a girl’s palm and stayed in the fold when she closed it. The gull took the upper corner while the leaf lay on the stone and brought back a peck the width of a nail; he took no word, which was a mercy and cost somebody a crust. The damp has eaten the sixth line of the fourth station. I know what it said. Knowing a thing is not the same as having it, and I have not written it again.

The manner of it, written small at the head

  • The boat is sealed before anyone speaks. Salt in the seams, weight in the belly, and not a word over the salt until the seams are stopped.
  • Read at the turning of the ebb, not at the flood. The flood brings things back. We are not asking for that.
  • The dipper stands at the bollard with the net-rope in her hand. Do not trail the net in the ebb. Nets are blessed at dawn, when the draw runs against the wind.
  • The answer belongs to the women who held the share. A man come aboard has half an opinion; say his answer twice and count it once.
  • Watch until the boat is out of sight. That is held to be enough. Watching longer is another trade and has no name in this port.
  • If an officer of the Court is on the Quay, give him the tally and not the leaf. The leaf is not a document and the tide will not authenticate it.

The litany

The dipper reads. The women answer. The answer is the same at every station, which is why it is a litany and not an argument.

I. Of the salt

The Sallow was before the quay and will be after it.

It lies the colour of skimmed milk and gives back nothing white.

Salt was here before anybody entered a share.

The pan takes what the wind leaves and writes no figure.

A thing with no figure cannot be owed.

Answer: The sea keeps its own account. We have watched him out of sight.

II. Of the draw

The warm goes out on the draw, against the wind, every year,

and every year the Council is told it is a surprise.

At dawn we trail the net and do not ask it to come back full.

What goes seaward is not stolen. It has gone.

A man who has gone is a man who is not here to be entered.

Answer: The sea keeps its own account. We have watched him out of sight.

III. Of the boat

Seal him in a boat and not in a slate.

Salt in the seams, weight in the belly, his share at the stern.

A boat has no house and no roll, only a share and an ebb.

What kept him up while he lived now carries him where he goes.

Do not scrape the salt off when the boat comes back empty. It was his.

Answer: The sea keeps its own account. We have watched him out of sight.

IV. Of the iron

On land they bring a cold tube and call it the Return.

Here is no arm to set the iron to and no slate that will stay dry.

Write him on the water, where the writing is true and goes.

The clean hand cannot follow him. The wave has no cuff to wash.

Here the damp has taken the sixth line. I have left the space the length of it. A clerk put a figure in that space once and the rain had it away before noon, which is the ordinary order of things here.

Answer: The sea keeps its own account. We have watched him out of sight.

V. Of what takes

The gull takes the coal and enters nothing.

The salt-thing taps the hull where the store lies.

The Waste takes what nobody kept and has no clerk to answer to.

A boat goes out warm and comes to nothing.

Nobody pays for the going. That is the whole of the bargain.

Answer: The sea keeps its own account. We have watched him out of sight.

VI. Of the watching

Watch until the boat is out of sight. That is held to be enough.

Watch no longer. A sea that keeps a man is not a sea that gives him back.

Say the tide-name over the water. If you have none,

leave the place where the name would stand and let the ebb take the place too.

A name kept by the water is kept somewhere, which is not the same as kept.

Answer: The sea keeps its own account. We have watched him out of sight.

VII. Of the coming back

A man who dies at sea has already returned.

He comes back as the tide comes: at the hour, not in the body.

He comes back to the Quay and not to a house.

Do not set a place for him. Do not sweep the salt where he stood.

We say the words, turn from the bollard and go home to our own fires.

Answer: The sea keeps its own account. We have watched him out of sight.

What the clerk did

A clerk of the Council borrowed the leaf on a market morning with ink in two fingers and the cuffs of his coat gone soft from washing. He took the verbs out, which is what they teach in the counting house, and set a mark against what was not for sale.

  • At I, against the sea keeps its own account, he wrote: No entry required. Under it, in mine: That is what the sea says. I have heard it said at this bollard for thirty years and never once by a clerk. Both stand.
  • At III, against a boat has no house and no roll, he wrote: one boat-share, current; holder deceased, share not returned. Under mine: The share goes to the daughter and the man goes to the Sallow. Those are two journeys and only one of them has an entry.
  • At IV, against no slate that will stay dry, he put a small stop and wrote: witness sufficient. Under mine: We watched him out of sight. The Council has made that sufficient, which is the same thing said by a man who was not there.
  • At V, against nobody pays for the going, he wrote: cost to district, nil. It is true in the figure and false in the room. I have left it.
  • At the answer, where I had set seven answers, he put a round nought and wrote: one answer, entered seven times, which is a true thing in a language nobody on the boats speaks.

I have left all of it standing. He takes the verbs out because a thing always true has no need of them; I put them back because a woman at the bollard wants to know which hand to take the rope with. He has entered the share and not the going, which is exactly what the Council does with both.

What the women put in the margin

The thick hand is Omra’s, who holds an eighth through her mother and will not say how many before company. I have copied it as it stands.

At III, against his share at the stern: The stern was her mother’s before him. Put him where she sat.

At IV, where the line is gone, she wrote a tide-name and pressed it with a nail so the damp would not take it. I cannot read it and she has not written it again.

At VII: We said this of my brother. We meant we should not have to enter him. That is a mercy and it is not one.

At the answer: He did not come back. We watched him out of sight. I have said both and will not make them agree.

At the head and along the gutter

The white along the gutter is salt and not Ash; there is no Ash in Vhal that has not been bought and sold. A factor come up from Greybank asked me once whether it was the grey the northern houses leave in their corners. I told him it was salt. He said he could sell me either, and laughed as though he had invented the weather. The green at the lower corner is not glass from our furnaces, which is blown clear; it came off the Cliffs and was sweet on the girl’s tongue before she put it in the fold. I have not washed it. A girl who tastes the Cliffs is either very young or very tired of being warned.

At the foot

The ebb takes and does not keep.

The sea keeps its own account.

Both lines are struck and both stand. I have asked three people whose hand it was and had two answers and no hand. The leaf goes back into the tin with the tide-name pressed deep and the damp where the damp has always been. Next ebb we shall read the seven stations and watch a boat out of sight, and the clerk will come with his wet thumb and take the tally, and the tide will do what it has always done, which is go out and not come back the same.
