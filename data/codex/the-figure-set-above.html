I keep the book of the figure set above. It is not an account. An account has one figure in each place and one hand to answer for it; this book has two figures in most places and no hand that will own either. When a clerk strikes a figure and writes another over it, the lower one does not go away. It goes on standing beneath the upper one the way an old repair shows through a new one on a vessel, and a man who knows to look for it can read the history of the repair by the shape of what is showing.

I was set to copy corrections in the third year of my teaching. The College teaches that a correction is the removal of an error. I have copied corrections for nine years and have yet to see an error removed. What is removed is the hand that made it. A figure with nobody in it is a figure that can be read aloud in a clean voice and carried home by a household that believes it has been told what it paid.

Twelve copper bits to the silver mark; twenty marks to the Sere crown, which comes north with the factor and does not stay in Grath. The Court books an ember at five marks and no man has exchanged one for the other. I have copied that figure four hundred times and have never once seen the exchange, which is a fact about figures and not about embers. One in twelve is printed on the notice and read twice at the Cross, once for the moot and once for those who came late. I have copied the notice and I have not added it, because adding is a thing done in the Weighing Hall where figures are read aloud and not inspected.

At the head of the register, in mine, written small: The upper figure is copied as it stands. The lower is copied as it was set down. Let both stand, for I am not the hand that chose between them.

The figures, as they stand in the register

The first column is what was written. The second is what was written over it. The third is the hand that did the writing over. The remarks are as I copied them, in the hands that made the draws, and are not part of the account.

Where the figure stood — In the first hand — Set above — The hand that set it — Remark

Sill, Stone 12; a child three days returned, Low Kiln — 13 — 12 — The factor’s clerk at Greybank — A vessel three days returned cannot hold thirteen after the draw. Both figures stand. I have asked which is right and neither hand has told me.

Sill, Stone 12; the order of the draw — I drew — the measure was drawn; above that, the warmth was entered — A clerk of the College — Three sentences, one figure, no doer. The officer put I drew back underneath both.

The assessor’s house, Sill; a vessel returned three times — with memory — whole — A clerk of the College — Whole is the word the notice prints. With memory is what the house calls it when no notice is in the room.

The College’s articles, rule 4; the keeping of apprentices — the apprentice is fed — the apprentice is kept — The Provost’s clerk — A boy can be kept and hungry in the same sentence. The passive voice has room for both.

Collector’s clerk at Greybank; his own word struck — taken — drawn — The clerk himself, at the officer’s bidding — A taking and a draw are the same heat going the same way. The slate is the difference, and the slate is a piece of stone with a neat hand on it.

Keld Keep; a lowlander giving two-and-forty at the gate — two-and-forty, struck through — two-and-forty, in red — The mine-reeve — A check and not a correction. He found no lie and wrote nothing a man could thank him for.

The First Seam; a man with two sets of numbers in one arm — the earlier cut — the later cut, in red — The mine-reeve — The later is entered. The earlier stays in the skin. A kindness done with a knife.

The chronicle of the Cantons; the oldest ledger — went out in a season of no frost — were gathered into the Hearth — A hand that takes the verbs out — The smoother words go up the road in a clean voice. The rough ones stay where they were said.

The Waiting Room leaf; the column of what was said at the water’s edge — four rows of remarks — No such column — A clerk of the College — He counted every row before he wrote that there were none worth entering. A man does not count what he has not looked at.

The counting stone at the Nine Wells; the space in the warming-song — four cups left as they are sung — a round nought, and vacancy — A clerk come up from Slate Row — A nought is a number. The space held four cups.

The moot book at Sill; five rows of squares — squares, left as squares — nil, entered in each — The moot clerk — A square is where a figure stood and was taken away. A nought is a figure put in its place. The column balances, which is how I know it does not.

The consent-book at the watch; one word scraped with a clip — no — a score the length of the word — The keeper of the watch — The word is not entered. The length of it is.

Figures struck in this leaf: twelve. Figures set above: twelve. Columns struck: two, both drawn back underneath. Verbs struck: as many as the hand could find.

What the upper hand is for

The Provost says a sentence true of everybody can be argued with by nobody. It is the whole art of the house. The measure was drawn is true of every man who ever drew a measure, and a thing true of everybody can be carried into a Weighing Hall and read over a room without anybody rising to contradict it. I drew is true of one man on one morning in one house, and a woman in that house may one day come to the counter and ask which morning and whose house, and there is no column for the answer.

I have put the doers back underneath everywhere. A correction that removes the doer is not a correction of a figure. It is a correction of a person, and the figure is left to stand in the person’s place, which is a garment that fits badly and is worn anyway.

The red hand at Keld Keep is the one honest correction in this book. A cross in red on that slate means the figure has been checked and is to stand. It does not mean the stone is warm, and it does not mean the man stood it. The mine-reeve enters the later number and leaves the earlier in the skin, and a clerk who can keep two truths without calling either a lie has a busy morning and a quiet night. In this office a correction is a thing done with a pen and a cuff, and the cuff is washed until it goes soft, which is as near as a clean hand comes to a stain.

The column struck and drawn back

Beside each row I keep the name of the hand that set the upper figure. I set no heading over it. A heading would make it an account, and an account is a thing a man may be called to answer for, and this house has answered for enough this year.

The Provost’s clerk borrowed the register on a morning in Ash and struck the column through from the first row to the last, and wrote No such column above it in his narrow upright hand. He had to count the rows before he could write that there were none worth entering, and I watched him do it, standing as he stands, with his cuff gone soft and his ink settled in two fingers. I drew the column back underneath and left his words standing over it. He struck a verb also, where I wrote the clerk set above, and set the figure was corrected over it. I put my hand back beneath and left his figure where he set it. A sentence with nobody in it is a sentence no woman can ask about, and women ask about these corrections in the end, always at the door, always after the register is put away.

The other hand is a tall leaning one out of the factor’s house at Greybank. Against the row for the child of Low Kiln it wrote adjustment on re-examination, figure confirmed, and underlined the word twice. Both stand. Confirmed is true in a room with a door shut, and the child was worse before dusk.

The song the correctors carry

Sung low at the correction-bench in the third year, never at the Weighing, and the second verse left out when a clerk from the factor’s house is within hearing of the bench.

One for the slate and one for the hand,

one for the figure set above;

the bottom one stays where it came in

and the top one gets read aloud.

I drew, it was drawn, the warmth was entered ---

three sentences and one man;

the clip leaves red and the tube leaves grey,

and the cuff stays soft through the Weighing.

Four cups are stopped and the space is nought,

the square is a figure gone;

the hand that strikes does not ask whose hand,

and the hand underneath knows.

The woman who sang this to me at the bench would not sing the last line while the Provost’s clerk stood in the door, which is how I know the last line is the one that matters. I have copied it as it came and put no gloss on it. A corrector who explains a song has begun to write a sermon, and sermons get copied.

In the head and in the gutter

The iron-gall at the head is mine, made by holding a quire open through a market morning; there are clerks in this city who can tell a copyist by his thumb before he has spoken a word. The slate dust in the gutter came off a surveyor who leaned over my table with the south-road dirt still on his cuffs and would not sit down. The filings in the fold came out of the wool sleeve a collector set beside the register while he gave me a figure; there is always a little iron in the fold, and it has never once drawn the warmth out of the paper, which is the only kindness iron has shown this leaf. The tallow thumb at the foot is mine, from holding the lamp low while a woman in the sealed-file room said something I wanted the end of; she did not tell me the end. The streak of slip came off the forearms of a thrower come up from High Kiln who set her slate beside mine to wait for a figure and took it away again. The green bead is lead glaze and not slip; a girl leaned over to see the correcting with the sweetness still at one corner of her mouth, touched the paper, said nothing, and went back down the terrace. The red score across the two Keld rows was made by a man showing his brother how a clip bites; both were honest men afterwards and one has a dead hand. The tide-line along the foot came from the sealed-file room, which is colder than the rest of Slate Row and always has been. The small bare square in the lower margin is where I scraped a word away with a knife and put no other in its place. I have not written the word back, and I have not forgotten it. On the Last Day they sweep the corners and burn what they find in the morning and call it dealt with. There is no Last Day for a correction-register.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The College keeps the upper figure. The yard keeps the lower. The room where both were spoken keeps neither.

I asked the Provost’s clerk, a collector come up from the Cross, and a woman who stood at the counter about her house, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the line where it was written.

The register goes back into the tin beside the second file. Tomorrow a clerk will strike a verb in another book and set a smoother one above it, and I shall copy both, and put the hand back where it was taken out. The figure at the top will be read in a clean voice at the Weighing, and the figure underneath will stay where it came in, and the space between them will have no signature, as it has had none for nine years and will have none in the tenth.
