I keep the consent-slate at the watch beside the Cross. A collector comes down off the Clay Road with the tube in its wool and a name already on his own slate, sets the tube on my counter while he goes for a cup, and I read what is below to the man before the tube goes back into his hand. The ordinance is in Ledgerhand, with nobody in any of its sentences, which is the only way the Keeping has found to make a consent read as though it had been given by a room. I put the people back underneath. A consent with nobody in it is a consent nobody ever gave, and those are the only ones this Court has ever wanted.

Title the Fourth, as it stands at the watch

From the Ordinances of the Keeping, Title the Fourth: Of Consent, the Clip at the Wrist, and the Refusal That Is Not Entered. Set down in the first years after the Rising of Y.H. 668; confirmed to the factor at Greybank by the treaty of Y.H. 418; added to in Y.H. 782 and in the Thin Year, Y.H. 812.

I. Of the asking

  1. No measure is drawn from a vessel that has not consented. The collector asks, the answer is entered, and the tube is not set until the entry is made.
  2. The asking is made in the hearing of one witness besides the collector. The witness is not asked whether he heard, and his name is not entered.
  3. Consent is entered in the hand of the collector or his clerk and not in the hand of the vessel. The hand that writes is not the hand that consents.
  4. The question is put once. A second asking is a fresh entry and is entered as fresh, whether the first answer was given or not.

Margin: I asked a man come down from the terraces why the question is put once when a man may want a moment. He said a second question is a new account and a new account wants a new figure. I have set his words here, because the ordinance has no place for them and the slate has room at the edge.

II. Of the clip

  1. Where consent is withheld, the vessel is clip-held by the wrist until consent is given. The clip is the Court’s iron and leaves no figure.
  2. The clip is set at the wrist and not at the neck, over the vein and not over the bone. A clip set wrongly is a fault of the officer and is entered to the officer.
  3. The hand in the clip is not the hand that holds the slate. The collector writes with the other hand, so that the entry and the refusing hand are not the same hand.
  4. No hour is entered for the holding. What is spent in the clip is drawn from no account, and no column provides for it.

Margin: There was a pale ring at the wrist of a man who came to this watch in the Thin Year, where something had been worn tight and long before the clip was set. He would not say what had been there. I have entered the clip and not the ring, because the ordinance asks after the iron and not after what a man was wearing. Both are marks on the same wrist. I have left at the edge a place the length of the ring.

III. Of the refusal

  1. A refusal is not entered. What is not entered is carried forward, and a refusal carried forward is not called a refusal in the roll.
  2. Where a vessel refuses twice in one tend, the second refusal is entered as a second asking and not as a second no.
  3. No officer is corrected for a refusal that was not entered. An officer who enters a refusal has made a fault, for a refusal entered would want an answer, and no answer is provided for.
  4. The word no is not written in the consent-book. A clerk who writes it has begun to keep another book.

Margin, scored with the edge of a cold-iron clip: I wrote the word once and scraped it away with the clip when the collector’s man came in. The score is the length of it. A clerk who keeps a second book is a clerk who will one day be asked which book he was keeping, and I have only the one. I have left the score and not the word.

IV. Of the entry

  1. The entry stands when the figure is written, and not when the tube is set. A consent entered is a consent given, whether the vessel is still in the room or not.
  2. The figure is one in twelve, as the notice says, and is not changed for the holding, the hour, or the mark at the wrist.
  3. Where the vessel is a foundling with no house, the consent is entered to the house that has him, and where no house has him it is entered to the Keeping. The Keeping does not refuse for anybody.
  4. The slate is read back after the figure is written. The reader does not ask whether the reading was heard. What is read is not corrected by what was answered.

Margin: A woman stood here through a reading with her hand at the middle of her palm where a ring had once been worn tight and long, and said the figure had been read to her twice, once for the watch and once for the households that came late. I have entered it once, as the ordinance provides. The second reading was hers, and I have set it at the edge, where a second reading has no column.

V. Of faults and their correcting

Fault — As entered at the watch — What the clerk sets beneath

A verb in the consent — Consent entered — The man said it, or did not

The question put twice — Second asking, entered fresh — The moment the man wanted, not entered

A refusal entered — Fault of the officer — The word no, scored in the margin

An hour entered for the holding — Figure not affected — The hour, which no column holds

A clip set at the neck — Fault of the officer, entered — The wrist, which is where it belongs

The witness asked whether he heard — His name not entered — Nothing; he was asked, and is gone

  1. A fault copied twice in one tend is a fault the officer has not understood, and the senior clerk sits with him at the watch until he has. The clip is not a rod. It is a piece of iron with a place where a wrist was.

The consent-song

Said low at the watch while the collector’s man holds the tube in its wool. Never while the slate is open, and never within hearing of the factor’s clerk. The fourth line is said to the wrist and not to the man.

Iron at the wrist and a slate at the knee,

the tube in its wool and the man in the cold;

they ask you once and they write it twice,

and the no that you said is not in the book.

Four at the bell and a gap where the fifth should be,

the gap is the hour the clip was on;

the figure goes up and the mark goes down,

and the hand that wrote is the hand that’s gone.

Iron at the wrist and a mark at the palm,

one of them older than the other by years;

the slate keeps the figure, the wrist keeps the shape,

and the watch keeps the place where the no was.

In the narrow upright hand: One consent entered; no deduction arises. In mine: He has put a figure where I put a person. A person can be asked; a figure cannot. Both stand.

What the two borrowed hands did

The College man came down from the Hearthseat with ink in two fingers of a cuff gone soft from washing. He struck the vessel refuses out of rule nine and set consent is withheld above it, which is the same thing said by somebody who is not in the room. On rule eight he struck out what is spent in the clip and wrote the interval is not entered, which is true of the slate and false of the wrist. I have put both of mine back underneath and left his above, where a man must lean to read them.

The other hand is a factor’s from Greybank, leaning and brown at the edge, with a little gold in it. Against the ring at the palm he wrote occupational mark; no deduction arises, which is the same phrase a clerk of the factor’s writes against the green at a girl’s gum. I have not struck it through. A hand with gold in it can call a mark occupational, because an occupational mark is a thing that can be entered and handed on. I have set my ring underneath. Both stand.

At the head and in the gutter

The grey at the head is Ash, off my own thumb turning the leaf toward the watch-fire; it does not come out of the creases and I have stopped trying. The scorch is where the watch-fire was too near on a wet evening in Ash; it has taken the corner off the first rule without taking the sense. The dark score in the lower margin is the clip’s edge, set down while I decided what word to leave out; it has gone green at the bottom where the damp got into it. The pale along the gutter is slip from a swaddling cloth, laid here while a woman held her child still with one arm and waited for me to finish. The brown at the second fold is where a crust was set down by a man come up from the clay yard with a number in his fist and no figure to spend it on. The grey wool through the tie-hole is off a cuff gone soft from washing; it is mine and I have not untied it. The tide-line along the foot came up off the market boards after rain, when the Cross stood under an inch of brown water and the collector’s man would not set the tube down until the slate was dry. At the Last Day the houses sweep their corners and burn what they find and call it dealt with. This watch is not a house, and the Ash stays where it fell.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The slate keeps the yes. The wrist keeps the no.

The hour keeps nothing, and nobody is asked to enter it.

I asked a collector’s man, a woman who stood here with her hand at her palm, and a clerk come down from Greybank, and had three answers and no hand, as I have had before. I have left the lines where they were written.

The slate goes back under the watch-post. Tomorrow a collector will come down off the Clay Road with the tube in its wool and a name on his own slate, and I shall set this leaf where he can read it and put the verbs back where another hand has taken them out. A man will say something that is not entered, the clip will be set, and the figure will stand as entered. That is my trade here: to write down what was done and leave a place the length of what was not.
