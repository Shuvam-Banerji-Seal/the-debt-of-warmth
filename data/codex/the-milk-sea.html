I am a man of the Fishstairs. I sell eels at dawn and know a river the way a potter knows a wheel: by the feel under the hand and the sound it makes when going wrong. In Stone of last year I took two baskets of smoked eel, a cart of coarse pots and two bales of wool up the Salt Road, and came back at the turn of the year with salt, a glass bottle, a rope and a mark gone thin at the edge. Where I wrote one thing and saw another I have left both standing. A river-man can do that. A clerk cannot.

The road up

The Salt Road runs eighty leagues through Greybank and the coast pass. I went with a cart and came back with a cart, which is more than some do. At Greybank the Court’s granaries stand so tall that a man from the Fishstairs looks at them the way he looks at the falls: as a thing that will be cold and loud and go on without him. Past the pass the clay gives out. The road goes hard and white and the smell of wet wool goes out of it and the smell of salt comes in, and I have never got used to the exchange.

At the last stage I asked a man walking down what the sea was like. He said, Which tide? I said, The sea. He said there was no such thing in Vhal, only the one going out and the one coming in, and that a man who called them the same had never owned a boat. I did not tell him I had never owned one. He would have asked my account next, and there the question is not rudeness.

The milk sea

They call it the Sallow. It is the colour of skimmed milk gone yellow at the edge, and cold in a way I had not met in the Ilm. The Ilm goes one way; a man of Sill can stand on the north bridge and watch his spit go downriver, and all his life that has been the arrangement. The Sallow will not do it. The tide comes in against the wind and goes out against the wind again, and a man new to it stands on the Quay with his coat blowing one way and the water the other and feels something go loose in him he had not known was tied. They say the tides follow the Warm and not the moon. I asked a dipper what the moon was for and he said the moon was for the fish, which may be true and is not an answer.

There is a thing in the water called a salt-thing. It taps the hull from under and draws the boat’s store away while the boat goes on looking the same. A man finds his warm gone and no hole in him to show where it went. In Sill we would call that a leak and put a hand over it. Here they write it in a tally and go on sailing.

What they write on

Everything in Vhal is written on salt-paper, which is paper made to rot. A Council clerk told me this with a straight face and a cuff soft from washing. In the damp a contract goes soft and the writing comes up in pale islands, and the Council calls it a virtue, because a law that lasts too long has stopped earning. In Sill a thing left in the rain is spoiled. Here it is made nothing, which they hold a cleaner end. I have set the two side by side instead, the way our yard sets two figures on the heap of turnips without making them agree.

I kept a tally of my own on the back of a warrant the Court had spoiled. This is the account as I wrote it, and the Council clerk’s figure as he set it above.

What I took up — What I set down at the Quay — What I brought back — What the clerk entered

Two baskets smoked eel — sold before the third hour, at a figure I did not ask twice — one sack of salt, coarse, with the bloom on it — dried fish, current rate

Cart of coarse ware, Nine Pots — three pots cracked in the frost on the road, entered as broken — one bottle of cliff-glass, green at the lip — glass, as entered

Two bales of wool — paid in a silver mark, counted four times in my sight — a coil of tarred rope, which I have had six months and will not rot — wool at the crown rate

— — a tally-clerk read the draw against the contract; the contract had gone soft — one mark, thin at the edge — the figure is current

In the narrow upright hand, above the wool row: Wool entered at the crown rate. The figure is current. The warmth was retained in the pens and transferred against the contract.

Underneath, in my charcoal: I carried the wool. They counted it in a room that smelled of tar and I stood in the doorway with my wet boots on the boards. A sentence with nobody in it travels well and arrives empty, which is what they want of a sentence here. I have put myself back in front of mine. The mark went thin because four men counted it and each took a little off the edge with his thumb. That is not a rate. That is four thumbs.

The Council room smells of tar and wet rope. The Council of Tides is elected by ship-owners and salt-masters for a year, and its decrees are written on paper that will not outlast the season, which is thought a good arrangement by anyone who has wanted to deny one. In the tide-tally house a man named Kett reads the draws against the contracts. A sheet left in the damp is a debt made nothing. I saw a man come in with a folded paper and go out with nothing in his hand, and lighter in the face too. Whether the paper freed him or only made him forget, I could not ask.

The dippers and the draw

At dawn the dippers trail the nets and bless them and read the Sallow draw, which is the current of Warm going seaward, taxed by the Court and prayed over by the dippers, running against the wind as a matter of course. I stood at the pans before light and watched a dipper pull up a net and say a number, and the pan-master write it down and go on with his counting. Nobody thanked the water or the dipper. Here a man does a thing, the thing is entered, and that is the end of the conversation.

The salt-women at the pans sing while they rake. I asked one what the song was for and she said it was for keeping the count, and I asked what count, and she said that was the count the Court did not take, which is the nearest thing to an answer I got in eighty leagues. I have set the words down as she sang them, with the ends of the words let fall the way they let them fall.

We rake at the fourth and the crust comes thin,

and the gull takes a coal and the clerk takes a name;

the tide goes out where the wind comes in

and the warm goes seaward all the same.

Four for the pans and one for the draw,

and a place on the leaf where the ink won’t stay;

what the paper keeps is the paper’s law,

and what the water keeps, the water takes away.

She would not sing the last line while a man from the Council stood on the quay. That is how I knew it was the line that mattered.

Boats, shares and the half voice

Boat shares pass through mothers in Vhal. A man who marries into a boat-owning family is said to be come aboard, and his opinion on the boat is counted at half. I asked a salt-wife whether a half opinion kept a man from being wrong; she said it kept him from being believed, which she said was a better thing. There is no word in Vhaleni for a guest or a friend, only for a man whose account is current. I was current for nine days. On the tenth I went up the road, which I think was why they were so pleasant to me on the ninth.

They say a stranger’s coin warms before his face. I put a copper bit on the counter my first morning and the woman looked at the coin and then at me, in that order, and I understood I had been read wrong and would spend the morning putting myself right. In Sill a woman looks at the face first and the coin after, which is a better custom and a poorer country.

The young walk the pans barefoot at low tide at fourteen and name a tide they have never seen. I watched a girl come in off the pans with her feet white and her head high, and an old woman ask what she had named it, and the girl tell her, and the old woman say nothing but take the girl’s hand, which was the only warmth I saw given freely in that town.

The pulled benches

Condemned men row the salt galleys for seven years, or until their hands give out, whichever comes first. A Vhaleni hull is never long from the oars. I saw them at the pulled benches with their hands on the wood. I have not put down what I felt, only what I saw: seven years counted in advance and a man’s hands counted after.

Slaves may be bought in Vhal but may not be returned there. The Council will not pay for a thin return and will not house one. A man from Greybank asked a clerk what became of a bought person who died, and the clerk said the contract had ended, a sentence with the person taken clean out of it. I have set both down. In Sill we return a man thin to the district and the district pays thirty marks and goes on. Here they will not even pay. It is not kindness. It is a contract with the last line left out.

The cliffs and the glass

The Glaze Cliffs stand a few leagues along the coast, blue-green with old lead, and smell faintly sweet in the heat. Children are told not to lick them. The furnaces take sand and lead and melt them into glass sold as far as Sere, and the women who work them rub their gums with the cliff and say sweetness answers sweetness, and their hands go slowly and they blame the cup. My mother tastes the glaze at the rim of every pot before it leaves our yard and will not say what she has tasted. I said nothing to the glass women.

The bottle I brought back is green at the lip. I keep it where the light comes in and have not entered it. The clerk at Greybank wanted a figure against it; I told him it was a bottle, and he wrote glass, as entered above my words and left mine standing underneath.

What I brought back

The rope has lasted six months in the wet and will last another six. In the Clay Yard a rope rots in a season and nobody is surprised. I keep it coiled by the door and touch it now and then with the back of the fingers the way a potter touches a pot come well from the fire. It is a thing that does not rot, and somebody paid to have it last, and I do not know who, and have stopped asking. The salt went on the eels. The mark is thin at the edge and will not change at the Cross; I keep it because I cannot spend it. The bottle stands in the light.

The eels went on being sold over the top of it all when I got back, and the Ilm went one way under the north bridge, and nobody asked me what the tide was like, which is the proper order of things and costs nothing.

At the head and in the gutter

The salt bloom in the gutter came up out of the paper itself and I have not brushed it off. The tar at the head is mine, off the coil I carried in my coat. The green bead in the second fold is cliff-glass; a girl pressed it in with her thumb while I was writing and had the sweetness at her mouth and said nothing. Do not rub it, the edge will take you. The fish-hook nick came off a man paying me for eels with the hook still in his hand. The peck-hole is a brine-gull’s; he took the leaf for a crust and I took it back with one word gone clean through, and I have not written the word in again, because a word with a hole in it is a word said through the teeth, which is how a great deal was said in that country. The wine-ring near the song is small and belongs to the third hour. The rope-burn along the edge is where the coil lay against the leaf on the road up. The grey print nobody will own came off a cuff laid where no cuff has business lying, and I have not swept it. There is no Last Day in Vhal and nothing anywhere gets swept.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

I went up with a river and came back with a sea.

The river has one direction and the sea has two.

The debt was entered. The thumb was not.

Ask the rope what it kept, and the bottle what it held.

I asked the dipper, the tally-clerk and a woman at the pans, and had three answers and no hand. I have left the lines where they were written. Tomorrow the eels will go on being sold at dawn and the Ilm will go the one way it has always gone, and I will not say which of the two I trust more, because a man who says it has begun to choose, and choosing is a thing the Court can enter against him.
