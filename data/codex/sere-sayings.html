I teach the young of this house their names and their manners, and have done it for nineteen years. At sixteen they go up to the Bowl to empty a cup of warm wine and name a debt they intend to carry, and the court claps. I am not allowed to stand where they stand. I am allowed to tell them beforehand what the room will do, which is a kindness of a particular southern kind: it saves them the surprise and does not save them the debt.

A clerk come down from the factor at Greybank asked what we said over the cradles and the bowls. He wrote down two lines and went away believing he had learned the south. So I have set the whole of it here, with the ends put back where the court lets them fall. I write in charcoal. Gold is for what a house means to keep; a saying is for the mouth, and a mouth has no cuff for a clerk to take ink from.

Say these with the vowels opened and the last of the thought thrown away, so that the listener supplies it. Say them to the bowl, the fire, the wine, the door; say them to a child last of all. Said looking at a person, a saying in Sere becomes a lawsuit, and there are lawsuits enough in this house without carrying one out of the room.

Of the bowl and what it is for

  1. “Empty the bowl and name what you will carry. The clapping is for the carrying, not the drinking.”

Gloss — At sixteen. The cup is emptied in the open court and the debt is named, and the court applauds the debt. A child who names a small debt is praised for modesty and given a larger one before the year turns. I have stood at the foot of the steps nineteen years and never seen a cup refused.

  1. “The wine comes warm to the hand and cold to the account. Both are true and neither is entered at the same table.”

Gloss — The warmth is in the cup and the figure is in a room the cup never enters. A guest who asks which is the real one has asked a question with two answers, which in Sere is not a question but an invitation to choose.

  1. “Three bowls go round. The fourth goes back to the shelf, and a house that knows why is a poorer house than one that does not.”

Gloss — At every feast a fourth bowl is set out and carried away full. Nobody pours it and nobody says whose debt it holds. If the house knew, it would pour it. Not knowing is called a mercy of the Spending. I asked the woman who carries the bowls what was in it and she told me to ask the bowl.

Of names, and the seven

  1. “Give the child seven names. Keep the one that costs the house least.”

Gloss — A child of a great house may gather seven names by adolescence and keep whichever the household prefers. The six are not thrown away; they are spent on the child, which is how a house speaks of having used them. I say this to the fire.

  1. “A bought woman is given one name. The one she brought is put under the glaze.”

Gloss — In some houses the forbidden name is cut into the inside of a bowl before the lead goes on, and the bowl goes into the rack with its rim turned outward. She is never permitted to look into it. The fire shuts the word in more carefully than the house shuts anything else.

  1. “A name under the glaze keeps longer than a promise written in gold.”

Gloss — The house writes its promises in thin gold on prepared paper and men with clean cuffs read them aloud. A word cut under lead before the firing is shut in for the life of the bowl. I have set the two where I heard them and have not joined them; a woman who joins them has begun to draw a conclusion, which I am not paid to draw.

Of the slate and the burning

  1. “The slate goes into the fire and comes out in a clean hand. The debt does not go anywhere.”

Gloss — At the end of a feast the debt-slates are burned by lot and written again before the wine is cold. The room claps at the new writing. A woman three places down from me wept at the burning of hers and then applauded the new slate with everyone else, and was called generous for it.

  1. “Buy the ash back. A house that cannot afford quiet ash has no business burning slates.”

Gloss — The Court taxes the ash and the family buys it back at the price of quiet ash. That is how a feast can ruin a house. The smoke goes up for nothing and what is left of it does not.

  1. “Burn the slate before company and the whole room has agreed to forget it. Ask the room, not the slate.”

Gloss — The burning and the forgetting are public acts done in the same hour with the same wine. Nobody is forced to remember. That is the mercy the priests speak of, and the reason the north calls our generosity by another name.

Of the house and the door

  1. “A woman may own the bowls, the slaves and her husband’s debt. She may not own the door.”

Gloss — The counting room is barred and no woman of the house goes through it; her numbers are read there by a man. I have stood outside with a wine-list and heard figures read in a low patient voice. The wood is ordinary wood and the latch is on the inside.

  1. “Gold ink keeps a threat as long as a promise. Choose which you are writing.”

Gloss — The house writes in thin gold and the corrections stand above in the same hand, so that what a man meant and what he was told to mean lie in the same ink and last equally long. Charcoal is safer. It rubs off on the sleeve and nobody has thought to enter a sleeve.

  1. “The tally-clerk writes above and the factor’s clerk writes above him. The woman at the press writes underneath and nobody reads her.”

Gloss — This is the order of hands in this house and in the copy that went north. I have let every borrowed correction stand and put my words beneath it, the way a repair lies over an older repair in a bowl still used at table.

Of the north and what is bought

  1. “The north buys what we pour out and calls it a return. We sell it and call it a province.”

Gloss — Some houses buy poor northern measures cheaply and make of them one beautiful return to display at a feast. The distance between the buying and the displaying is the profit, and nobody walks it. A northerner at the lower table with three copper bits under his palm is not there to be pitied, though he often is.

  1. “A crown that will not change beyond the Peat Line has been spent twice already.”

Gloss — Southern crowns will not change at Greybank. The northern traders who come south with wool and go back with a figure in their heads complain of this, and are sold to anyway. A coin a man cannot spend has already been counted for him.

Of the dead and what is kept

  1. “We burn the dead in an open bowl, tax the ash and buy it back. No other people makes so costly a thing of quiet.”

Gloss — Cremation in a public bowl, with wine and music; the warmest send-off in the world. The Court taxes the ash and the family buys it back at the price of quiet ash, which is how a Sere funeral can ruin a good house. I have seen a woman set her hand on the phial and not open it.

  1. “A cup that keeps is called generous here and a cup that pours is called spent. Ask the cup which it would rather be.”

Gloss — The priest says a vessel was made to pour and the cup that keeps is the cup that sours. He says it over the wine and not over the accounts. I have set it where I heard it and asked the cup nothing.

The song of the carrying

Said at the Bowl by the young going up the steps, going up and not coming down. The fourth part is said to the bowl and not to the company. There is a silence before it the length of a name under the glaze, and I have left it the length it is kept.

Up the long steps with a cup in the hand,

the household behind and the court set wide;

name what you’ll carry and carry it whole,

and they clap at the name and not at the soul.

Three pour at the third and the fourth stands still,

the slate goes up and the clean comes down;

buy back the ash, buy back the quiet,

and the house stands where it stood before.

Seven names over the cradle and one kept,

a door for the numbers and none for the name;

gold for the promise and lead for the word,

and the word keeps best where no eye has read.

[Here the bowl stands. No sound.]

One for the bowl and one for the fire,

one for the debt that the room will forget;

the wine goes round and the name goes up,

and the number goes in where no woman is let.

A girl of the press asked what the silence was for. She was told to ask the bowl. The tally-clerk put a small round mark in it afterwards and wrote not named beside it, which is a thing a house can enter and a name is not.

What the gold hand did

A tally-clerk of this house borrowed the leaf in the scullery while I was still finding the end of a line. He writes very small, in thin gold, and takes the verbs out as though a verb were a thing a woman could spill from a cuff.

  • At the clapping is for the carrying, he wrote obligation acknowledged by acclamation and underlined it twice.
  • At buy the ash back, he set ash repurchased at assessed valuation; no deduction arising, which leaves the price standing and takes the quiet away.
  • At a woman may own the bowls, he wrote property enumerated; access restricted, true of the inventory and silent about the latch.
  • At the silence he put the round mark and not named. I have left it where he set it and put the name back underneath, which is where it was before.

I have put my words back beneath his everywhere, as the word under the glaze lies under the glaze. He takes the doer out because a thing always true can be carried into a counting room. I put the doer back because a child at the foot of the steps wants to know whose hand will pour.

What the northern hand did

A factor’s clerk at Greybank wrote above the gold on the copy. He has never been south, and writes in the narrow upright hand of the College.

  • At seven names, he set six designations struck; one current, which is the whole of his trade with a person.
  • At the north buys what we pour out, he wrote pooled measures exported; no deduction at point of sale, and did not ask who had been pooled.
  • At the fourth bowl, he put reserve unentered and a figure of no account against it.
  • At a crown that will not change, he wrote currency not current beyond the province, a true sentence in a language nobody at the press speaks.

I have left his hand above both. There is no Last Day in a scullery and nothing here is swept; the corrections lie one over another and are not asked to agree.

In the margin, in the charcoal of a girl of the mills

My mother burned her slate at the feast and wept, and the room clapped. I have learned to clap at the burning and to keep the date of it in my head. A date has no figure and does not go up in smoke.

She would not sign it. I have left it where she wrote it.

At the head and in the gutter

The gold at the head came off the tally-clerk’s cuff and will not wash out of the grain; a contract is meant to last and a saying is meant to be carried. The yellow in the fold is the dust of the burning winds and I have not shaken it out. The wine-ring is the third hour. The green bead came off a bowl chipped when a girl set her cup down too hard on the rim; the sweetness is in the edge and the sweetness is not a kindness. The flake of quiet ash I found shut in the gutter and have left there. The dress-thread is gold and stiff, drawn out of the girl’s name-day sleeve. The crown between the third and fourth leaves is the only coin here nobody has entered, which is not the same as nobody having paid for it. The pale thumb is the girl with the sweet sickness. The grey along the outer fold is not mine and not the tally-clerk’s. I have not swept it.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The bowl keeps what it is told to keep.

The slate keeps what the fire lets.

The house keeps the name it chose and the debt it did not choose.

Nobody keeps the person who carried either.

I asked the woman who carries the bowls, a girl of the mills, the tally-clerk and a factor come down from Greybank, and had four answerings and no hand. The leaf goes into the silk and the silk into my sleeve. Tomorrow is a name-day in a house I will not name; three bowls will go round and one will stand, and a child will be told what she is to carry, and I shall say these sayings to the bowl and not to the child, and put the verbs back where the gold hand took them out.
