I have drawn the baskets at the Weir since I was twelve and I have never known a morning when the fifth one was not put back. A girl who brings the creels down from the Fishstairs asked what the women said over the traps and wrote my answer on the back of a salt-tally while the water went over the posts under our feet. She put the verses in the order of the lifts. I have put the rubrics back, which she left out because she has never had a basket come up heavy in her own hand.

The traps are drawn at first light, before the coats come down the planks. You lift, swing, tip, shake, set the basket back on its stake: five movements, and the song has four verses and a fifth that is not sung. The fifth is said into the rail after the last basket and never over the water. Said over the water it becomes a prayer, and there is no priest at this Weir who would have it. We say the words down into the basket the way you speak to a child who is doing what you have told it. Said up into the air they grow a tune, and a man with a slate can carry a tune away.

The count is in the knuckles of the left hand: four to the creel and the fifth over the rail. I asked my mother why the fifth goes back, and she told me to ask the river, which is what her mother told her. I have asked the river. It has not told me. So I have put the words here in charcoal, on the back of a tally the salt has already spoiled, because a song kept only in the wrist goes the way wrists go.

How it is drawn

  • Draw at first light, when the water is lowest and the boards still hold the night’s cold. Not at noon, when a buyer is on the planks and the song will be taken for an answer to a question nobody has asked aloud.
  • Set your foot where a foot has been tried. There is a board in this walk that will not carry a man when the river comes up, and nobody has gone down to find which one it is. We step over that uncertainty every morning as a matter of course, and do not make a name of it.
  • Lift with the knees and not with the back. A basket of eels is mostly water, and the water is honest about its weight; the eels are the part that lies.
  • Tip into the creel from the height of your own hip. Set the basket down into the creel and the small ones climb out over the rim before you have counted them, and then the count and the creel disagree.
  • Four on the knuckles and the fifth on the thumb. Do not say the fifth aloud while the basket is in your hand. A number said over an open basket has a way of staying in it.
  • If the note comes up from the sill below the bridge, let the basket stand where it is and stand still with it. The eels go down when that note comes, and a basket drawn on it comes up with weed and a twist of reed.
  • Do not sing when a man comes along the boards at the going of the light. He does not ask for a figure and does not tell us to be quiet; the boards go quiet of themselves, and we follow them.
  • Say the fifth once, into the rail, when the last basket has been set on its stake. Twice is for a death-house, and this is a working song.

The song

The call goes down into the basket. Refrain after every verse, said into the rim and not out over the water. One line of the refrain to one lift.

The basket comes up with the night still in it,

a hand’s breadth of water and a twist of reed;

hold it to the light before you tip it in ---

the dark keeps a shape that the light must read.

Draw. The basket comes up dark.

Tip. The creel takes what it can.

One goes back over the rail.

The river keeps it. That’s the count.

Sort them on the board with the back of the hand,

long to the creel and small to the stream;

say nothing when a coat comes down the planks ---

a figure said aloud is a hook for a man.

Draw. The basket comes up dark.

Tip. The creel takes what it can.

One goes back over the rail.

The river keeps it. That’s the count.

I count on the knuckles, four and a thumb;

four go to the creel and the fifth goes back.

There’s a knot in the cord for the four that I keep

and a place at the end for the one I don’t.

Draw. The basket comes up dark.

Tip. The creel takes what it can.

One goes back over the rail.

The river keeps it. That’s the count.

A brine-gull waits on the post where the bait was,

feet white with a white that is not from our pans;

it takes what we leave and it takes what we kept,

and it takes the warm thing first if a man turns his back.

Draw. The basket comes up dark.

Tip. The creel takes what it can.

One goes back over the rail.

The river keeps it. That’s the count.

What goes down to Greybank is entered in figures;

what goes over the rail is entered nowhere.

One in twelve, says the notice, read twice at the Cross,

and the bell goes four, four and a gap over both.

Draw. The basket comes up dark.

Tip. The creel takes what it can.

One goes back over the rail.

The river keeps it. That’s the count.

Draw them while the light is as thin as a thread,

tip them in, set the basket back on its stake;

the river will keep what the river has taken,

and the creel will keep what the hands have let go.

Draw. The basket comes up dark.

Tip. The creel takes what it can.

One goes back over the rail.

The river keeps it. That’s the count.

The fifth, said and not sung

After the last basket, low, into the rail. The girls lean in for it and are never allowed to say it back.

One goes back and nobody says why;

not to the pot, not to the creel, not to the man with the slate.

The river keeps a place the way a house keeps a chair ---

you sweep around it and you do not sit down.

I have asked three women what the fifth is for. One said it was for the run, and one said it was for the water, and one said it was because her mother did it, which is the only answer of the three that has an end to it. The girl who wrote this for me asked me and I gave her the answer I was given. A verse lost at the Weir goes the way an eel goes in silt: quietly, and with nothing entered.

What the clerk did

The factor’s clerk borrowed the leaf on an Account-day, with ink in the first joint of two fingers of a cuff gone grey from washing. He took the verbs out and set his figures above ours, and I have left his hand standing and put ours underneath, as the water goes over the stake and the stake stays where it was.

  • At four go to the creel and the fifth goes back, he wrote four retained; one returned to water; no deduction arises. True in the figure. In the figure a hand is a hand and a basket is a basket and nobody has ever put one back.
  • At a place at the end for the one I don’t, he set a round nought and wrote surplus, uncollected beside it. A nought is a number and the place is not one. It is the length of a thumb, which is where the count stops.
  • At the river keeps a place the way a house keeps a chair, he wrote riparian retention; not a chargeable item, and underlined it twice. He has made the chair an item and left the house standing empty, which is the whole art of that hand.
  • At the bell goes four, four and a gap over both, he struck out and a gap and wrote eight strokes. I have heard that bell every tenth day for nine-and-twenty years. It has never once gone eight.

What the girl with the creel put in the margin

The thick charcoal is hers. She wrote at the boards with the creel between her knees and pressed the words in with the end of a burnt stick. I have copied it as it stands.

At say nothing when a coat comes down the planks: I have seen a buyer given a figure and the same figure given back to him with one taken off, and neither of us said which was which. It is not lying if the figure does not ask to be told.

At the fifth goes back: My mother put one back every morning and I thought it was the small ones. It was not the small ones she put back. I have watched and I am certain of this and I have not asked her.

At a man comes along the boards at the going of the light: He stands where the cord is and looks at the water. He never asks and never tells us to stop. The boards go quiet and we go quiet after them, the way you stop pulling when the rope goes slack.

At the fifth, said and not sung: I asked what it is for. I was told to ask the river. I have asked the river at the rail in the dark and it has not told me either.

At the head and in the gutter

The grey in the grain at the head is eel-slime, dried in with the charcoal, and is the only part of this leaf that was put there by the trade and not by a person; I use that thumb to sign because it will not wash out. The salt white at the fold came off both my wrists on the morning after the blessing, when the salt that had been swept from the flat stone went over the edge and into the Ilm and left our sleeves crusted. I did not brush it off the tally. The silt tideline along the gutter came up over both of us on a morning in Silt when the river was off its banks and the posts stood in brown water; it took two words off the fourth verse and I wrote them back from my own mouth, which is how I know the girl was listening. The scale in the lower corner came off a fish laid on the leaf while a woman counted and did not notice she had done it. The bone between the third and fourth leaf is the length of a thumbnail and I have kept it there to remind me how small the ones we keep are when they are dead. The reed-fibre in the seam came off a bundle going downriver past the hut. The tar thumb nobody will own came off the rail where every hand on this Weir has rested at some time, and I have not tried to find which. There is no Last Day on a plank walk; the salt has already had most of it.

At the foot, in a hand nobody will own

The creel keeps four. The river keeps one.

The slate keeps nothing and is read aloud twice.

I asked a girl with a creel, a woman who salts at the hut, and a man come up from Greybank with brine on his boots, and had three answerings and no hand. I have left the line where it was written. The tally goes back under the salt pot in the hut. Tomorrow I shall lift, swing, tip, shake, set the basket on its stake, and count four on the knuckles and the fifth on the thumb, and say the fifth once into the rail, and the clerk may have his copy of the nought. The river will be where it was when we came, and the place at the end of the cord will be the length of a thumb.
