I set the coarse pots to the drying racks, and I have written this because a man with a slate stood in the yard in the dry week and asked what the children counted when they put their knuckles to the pots. I told him a sound and not a figure, which is the truth of it, and he went away and sent me a clerk, which is what happens to a sound in this country. What is below is what the children said before the clerk came and what they said again after he had gone back over the north bridge with the verbs taken out of it. I have set his hand above mine where he put it, and theirs underneath where they stand, as a rack keeps the wet mark under the dry one.

The slip at the head is my own thumb. I set the tally on the lowest rack before I had done with the wet ones, and slip goes into the grain and will not come out, which is why we use it to mark a place. The grey came off a cuff that had no business lying in a yard, and I have not swept it. There is no Last Day in a Clay Yard and nothing here gets burnt in the morning.

The row and the knuckles, as the children keep it

Played in Tallow and Ember, when the pots stand rim-up on the lower rack and the slip has gone firm enough to take a tap. Not in the Five, when the children are made to stand and listen and there is no rack for it; not in rain, because a wet pot answers flat whatever ails it, and a child who counts a wet pot learns the wrong count. In Stone they tap the pots on the shelf by the banked fire, which is the same game with the cold taken off it.

The row is as long as the day’s drying gives. Pots stand in it rim-up, belly out, with their feet on the slat where the wheel left them. One child is the ear, because she puts her head down to hear what the knuckle brings up; the rest are the row. They are called the ear and the row and never their own names, for a name said in this yard travels up the clay road in a cuff and comes back entered.

The ear taps each pot once on the belly with the middle knuckle, not on the rim. A tap on the rim gives a high thin note before the pot has earned one, and the yard calls that a pot saying a thing too soon. One line of the verse is one tap, and the verse is said to the pots and the rack, never to a face; said to a child it becomes an argument, and there is always a child who will argue.

When a tap comes back flat the ear does not stop and does not say the pot’s number. She sets that pot at the far end of the rack where the crooked ones stand, and taps the next. Which pot answers flat is the day’s business and changes with the day; no child keeps a list of them, and the rack keeps no list either. When the eight lines are done the hands come down together and the pots are left standing as they stood. There is no winning and no last tap. The flat one is not thrown out and is not counted in, which is the whole of the game and the thing the clerk could not hear.

The counting-out, said over the rims

Right fists held out, knuckles up, the child who says it touching each fist in turn and not looking at the faces. The fist it lights on at the last word is the ear, which is not a prize: the ear has to kneel and listen and the rest only stand in the row. The children accept this as a punishment and the rack-women accept it as practice.

One for the lip and two for the foot,

three for the thumb and four for the soot;

five for the rack where the wet ones lean —

the one it lights on taps the first note in.

The listening verse, as it is tapped

One line, one tap, on the belly of the next pot in the row. The eighth line is tapped like the others; the flat one is set aside when it comes, without a word and without a figure, and the verse goes on over it.

One for the foot that came off the wheel,

two for the belly that keeps its meal;

three for the lip where the slip ran thin,

four for the ear that was never put in;

five for the ridge where the thumb went round,

six for the sound that comes up from the ground;

seven for the seam that the kiln shut fast,

eight for the one that the yard calls whole.

The flat one goes to the crooked rack.

It keeps its sound. We do not count it back.

The last two lines are said low, to the rack, while the ear sets the pot down. The children will not be told which pot is the flat one before they tap. Ask them and they say the pot tells you; ask what it tells and they ask you why a man wants to know what a pot tells.

What is said at the flat one

Said to the pot as it is set at the end of the rack, with the head down and the knuckle resting on the belly. All the row says the last two lines together.

The note’s gone out. The pot’s still.

The yard says listen and the rack says wait.

Say it to the clay and not to me —

a flat’s a body standing, not a debt.

The verse they do not tap while a man in a soft cuff is in the yard

I have this from the child who was ear, with the space left before it, which is the space the children leave when the flat pot comes. She said it to the rack with her back turned and made me set it down with none of my verbs in it. A verse tapped to a pot wants nothing added, and a verse tapped to a man wants everything taken away.

Eight go round and eight come back,

and one sits flat on the crooked rack;

it went up the road with a whole note in it

and came back answering flat to the knuckle.

The slate says sound and the slate says eight;

the yard says listen and the yard says wait.

The flat one keeps what the ring forgot —

a name said at home and never entered.

The child who said this had a brother who went up the clay road in the spring and came back with a note gone flat, and would not say whether the house had known him before he went, which is a child’s way of keeping two things apart without putting a word between them. I have put no word between them either.

What the clerk did

The factor’s clerk borrowed the tally at Greybank, being a man who cannot let paper lie, with his cuff gone soft from washing and the ink sitting in two fingers of it and will not come out. He set the row down as a batch of vessels submitted for inspection. Against the eight lines he wrote eight sound vessels, passed. At the flat one he struck the children’s words through and set above them, one vessel defective, rejected and entered nil; replacement issued. At the foot, where the pots are left standing and nothing is said, he put a round nought and wrote no balance outstanding.

I have put their words back underneath and left his standing above. He writes of a thing always true and so has no need of a child kneeling in it. I write for a child with her ear to a pot who wants to know which hand to lift next and whether the pot she set aside will still be there tomorrow. He has put a figure where a pot was standing, and a figure travels up the clay road in a cuff and a pot does not. A nought is a number. The flat one is a place at the end of a rack, and it is not the same pot twice. He has written one pot where there are as many as the day gives, which is the whole art of that hand.

He asked what the children learned by it. I told him the count and the listening and the setting aside, and he said that was an account of pot gone wrong taught before a child could read a slate, and he entered it. The children stop when a man in a soft cuff comes into the yard. They take it up again when he is past the gate with his satchel under his arm, and they never once count the flat one.

The gloss in charcoal, by the woman at the kiln

The woman who fires the Vail kiln pressed one line into the margin beside the sixth tap. She has kept that fire thirty years and does not write often, and when she does she writes small, the way a person writes where somebody is sleeping.

A flat pot is a pot that tells you where it broke. The dangerous one is the one that rings and is hollow behind the glaze. I have mended the loud ones and thrown out nothing in thirty years.

I have left it where her thumb put it. She said a pot that keeps its break where you can see it is a pot you can carry, and that the yard’s way of setting the flat one at the end of the rack is the only honest repair she has ever seen in clay, and she would not say whether she meant pots.

At the head and in the gutter

The slip at the head is mine and has worked into the grain, which is why this leaf takes a mark and never gives one back. The green bead came off a coarse pot broken at the third rack and rolled as far as the fold before anybody saw it; do not touch the edge, the sweetness is in it and the sweetness is not a kindness. The pale thumb at the second corner is from a girl of the glaze room with the pale line at her gum, who leaned over to read and touched the tally and went back up the terrace. The grey in the gutter came off the cuff that had no business lying there, and I have not swept it. The tideline took two words off the back of the tally and I have not written them in again, because a rhyme with two words gone is a rhyme said slowly, and all of ours are said slowly. The twist of grey wool through the hole is mine, tied for the word I could not put down. I will not untie it.

At the foot, in grey slip, in a child’s hand

I like the flat one. It’s the only one that tells you where it broke.

No hand will own it. The letters are set down as they were made, hard at the th and one of them backward, which is how a child writes who has learned off a tally and not off a book. I asked whose it was and had three answers and no hand, as I did with the grey print. I have left it where it was written.

The leaf goes back into my apron and the pots go back to the rack. Tomorrow the row will stand as long as the day’s drying gives, and eight will ring and one will not, and which one will not is a thing nobody will enter and nobody will keep, and the children will set it at the end where the crooked ones stand and tap on. If the clerk comes again with his pen I shall put the verbs back. He may keep the nil; the pot comes back to the rack at the end of every count, and no tally in this yard has ever had a place for it.
