The bestiary of Sarn

An account for the reader of the Compendium: vermin, beasts, blights, spirits and calamities, in the order in which a traveller is likely to meet them. Threat is measured against the named ranks of vessels, which are trades rather than prizes. A thing that can kill a Thin man may do nothing at all to a Held one, and a thing that can kill a Held one may be met by staying indoors. Folklore is what the hearths say. Truth is what is found when the thing is opened.

The rule of Sarn’s monsters is the rule of its vessels: a monster is a vessel whose ways are in the wrong place, or a vessel filled past its proper measure by somebody else’s error. The horror is clinical.

I. Vermin and small nuisances

Ash-mite

Habitat. The grey ridge along the skirting board in every poor room in Sarn; thickest in Low Kiln and the Empty Cantons. Behaviour. Swarms in the Ash where promises are unkept. Invisible in daylight, plain by tallow-light, and most active on the Last Day when the Ash is not swept. How it fights. It does not bite. It settles in the seams of a sleeper’s clothes and drinks the thin edge of the measure over weeks. A household with mites is a household that is quietly getting thinner. Threat. Thin. It cannot kill a whole man, but it can keep a poor one from ever becoming whole. Uses and price. Ground into a paste and sold by Fenfolk as quiet paste, a cheap cousin of quiet ash that makes a person forget a year. A thimble costs four bits in Mud End and sixty marks in the Hearthseat, where it is taxed. Folklore vs truth. Folklore says it is dirt that breeds in cold. Truth: it eats the residue of selves and passes the memory on in its own body. A man who has been mite-bitten for a year will sometimes dream in another person’s voice.

Cupworm

Habitat. Clay yards, pottery hamlets, Old Kiln. Bores through unfired and fired clay alike. Behaviour. Follows the damp. A cupworm in a drying rack will hollow a pot from the inside so that it looks sound and breaks at the first pouring. How it fights. It does not fight. It ruins. A vessel with a cupworm has a hidden flaw that no knuckle-tap will find until it is too late. Threat. Thin to Whole, to vessels rather than people. A kiln-house can be ruined by one summer of cupworm. Uses and price. The silk it spins seals hairline cracks in green clay and is worth two bits a yard in Nine Pots. The worm itself is fed to pigs. Folklore vs truth. Folklore says a cupworm is the ghost of a badly fired pot. Truth: it is a animal, blind, and it prefers clay that has been handled by many people, which is why the pots of a busy household go first.

Silt-leach

Habitat. The shallows of the Ilm and Ferr, in summer when the water is low and smells of iron and mud. Behaviour. Attaches to the ankles of waders and to the legs of eel-trappers. It is barely visible against silt. How it fights. Sets its mouth against a surface way and draws Warm slowly, the way a collector’s tube does. A wader feels only cold in one foot that does not go away when he leaves the water. Threat. Thin to Held, depending on the number. A child can be emptied by a handful in an hour. Uses and price. Dried leeches are used by collectors to make a draw without a slate, which is theft and punishable by a greyed hand. Three bits a dozen in the Fishstairs, no questions. Folklore vs truth. Folklore says the leach takes the warmth and gives it to the river. Truth: it gives it to the Sallow draw, and the river is only the road.

Ice-mouse

Habitat. Frozen fields, river ice, the eaves of Grath in Stone. Behaviour. Lives under ice and in the thatch. Comes into houses in the deep cold and nests in clothing. How it fights. Its bite is cold and leaves a small numb patch that spreads if the wound is not warmed. Frostbite by another road. A sleeping infant bitten six times will not wake. Threat. Thin to Whole. Uses and price. The fur is warm beyond its weight and is sewn into the linings of collectors’ coats, which is considered a joke by the collectors and not by the poor. A skin is worth one bit in Sill and a mark in Keld. Folklore vs truth. Folklore says the ice-mouse is the cold made small enough to be killed. Truth: it is a field mouse whose ways have been pushed outward by living under ice; its bite steals head rather than causing it.

Tallow-wasp

Habitat. The Tallow Works, rendering sheds, anywhere sheep fat is rendered. Behaviour. Swarms in the sweet smoke. Nests in the warm cracks of rendering houses. How it fights. The sting swells the ways near the skin so that Warm pools and cannot circulate. A man stung twenty times becomes feverish and then cold, in that order. Threat. Thin to Held; a swarm can drop a strong man. Uses and price. The nests yield a resin that holds a graft until the vessel takes, much valued by bowl-surgeons in Sere at four marks a nest. In Grath the nests are burnt. Folklore vs truth. Folklore says the wasp is drawn to the smell of rendered fat. Truth: it is drawn to the warmth of the rendering, and the fat is only where the warmth has been.

II. Beasts of the bowl

Reed-wolf

Habitat. The reedbeds east of Mud End and Reedholt, and the banks of the lower Ilm. Behaviour. Hunts in packs of five to nine. Does not howl; it makes a low clicking with the tongue that carries over water. How it fights. Goes for the throat and the inside of the elbow, where a person’s ways run closest to the skin. It does not worry a victim; it opens one way and waits. Threat. Thin to Held. A pack can pull down a Held man in mud, which is why the reed-cutters go in pairs. Uses and price. The teeth are set into clamp-handles, for a tooth remembers the shape of a way. A good tooth is two marks in Stonemouth. The pelt is worthless: the hair carries Ash. Folklore vs truth. Folklore says the reed-wolf is a drowned man’s grudge. Truth: it is a wolf whose ways have been pushed outward by living in water, and it kills the way a collector draws — quickly, at one place, and without malice.

Glass eel

Habitat. The Ilm below the sill, the Ferr, the eel traps at the Weir. Spawns in the Sallow and comes upriver in Ilmen. Behaviour. Nocturnal, drawn to lamplight and to warm stone. Lies in the silt of the Fishstairs in the first warm days of the year. How it fights. A discharge through the water that stops a man’s ways for a moment, like a clamp with no iron. Long enough to drown a wader; not long enough to kill a man on dry ground. Threat. Thin to Opened in water; nothing on land. Uses and price. The eels are eaten, smoked, and sold for three bits at the Fishstairs. The skin, dried, is used by Drawn practitioners to carry a flow a little farther than the hand. A skin costs a mark in Greybank. Folklore vs truth. Folklore says glass eels are the shadows of the star-rivets fallen into the river. Truth: the eel is an ordinary eel that has learned to store a little Warm in a sac behind the head, and the Sallow draw does the rest.

Brine-gull

Habitat. The Sallow coast, the Glaze Cliffs, Vhal’s salt pans. Behaviour. A thief of anything warm: a baked loaf, a warmed stone, a coal that has not gone out. Flies inland as far as Greybank in hard winters. How it fights. It does not fight. It steals the thing a man is holding against the cold and leaves him colder than before. A flock over a fishing boat is a real danger in a Sallow winter. Threat. Thin; a nuisance to any vessel above it. On the water in numbers it can empty a small boat’s store. Uses and price. The feathers hold salt and are used to salt a wound, which is a brute method and often works. A bundle costs a bit in Vhal. The meat is not eaten; it is said to make a man thin. Folklore vs truth. Folklore says the brine-gull carries the dead out to the Sallow. Truth: it carries anything it can lift, and the salt on its feathers is from the pans, not from the sea’s dead.

Kiln-hound

Habitat. Around old kilns and the Old Kiln south of Sill. Feral dogs that have lived near firing for generations. Behaviour. Keeps to the smoke side of a kiln. Will not cross running water, which is why the people of High Kiln consider the river a wall. How it fights. Its breath is hot and carries the kiln’s own poison; a man overcome by it wakes with a headache and a lasting thirst, or does not wake. It bites at the face when the man is down. Threat. Thin to Held; the breath kills a sleeping man of any rank who has not learned to keep a window open. Uses and price. The tongue, dried, is used by the Fenfolk to test a room for kiln’s breath, since it discolours. Worth two bits. The hide is too smoky to sell. Folklore vs truth. Folklore says the kiln-hound is the kiln’s own breath given legs. Truth: it is a dog, altered by living where the air is bad, and the danger is the air and not the dog.

Lead-rat

Habitat. The Lead Run, the Ferr valley, the old workings above Stonemouth, the lead-glaze stores of High Kiln. Behaviour. Greedy for the sweet taste of lead glaze; gnaws the lips of cups and the seams of glazed bowls. How it fights. Its bite puts a little lead into the wound and makes the ways sluggish over a season. A poor household with rats in the cupboard is a household whose children are going thin by degrees. Threat. Thin; cumulative. No single bite matters and a hundred do. Uses and price. None. Rat-catchers in High Kiln are paid by the tail, two bits a score, and the tails are burnt. Folklore vs truth. Folklore says the lead-rat is the sweet sickness made small. Truth: both come from the same lead, and the glaze is the carrier in each case. This connection was made in Y.H. 641 and then forgotten, which is how Sarn forgets things.

Bone-hound

Habitat. The Bone Orchard outside Sill’s north gate, the burial grounds of Greybank, the thin-return graves. Behaviour. Scavenges the shallow graves of children. Follows the smell of Ash rather than the smell of decay. How it fights. Does not attack the living unless they come between it and a grave. Its bite carries Ash from the bodies it has eaten, and the wound refuses to close. Threat. Thin to Held; chiefly a danger to gravediggers and to those who return at night to the orchard. Uses and price. The Ash in a bone-hound’s stomach is sold as cheap quiet ash in the Fenmarch, and is the reason so many Fen returns are thin. Five bits a phial, no questions asked. Folklore vs truth. Folklore says the bone-hound guards the graves. Truth: it has learned that the children in the shallow graves still have a little Warm in them, which is true, and it eats that first.

Fen-leech / Bog-cow

Habitat. The peat bogs of the Fenmarch and the marsh east of Reedholt. Behaviour. A large, slow, horned beast that stands in peat until only the head shows. The peat keeps it warm and it does not move for days. How it fights. It lowers its head and pushes. In a bog a pushed man cannot brace, and a man who falls into peat with a bog-cow standing over him drowns slowly. It does not eat him; it simply holds him there until he stops wanting. Threat. Thin to Held; to Drawn and above it is an obstacle, not a fight. Uses and price. The hide, dried over peat, makes the best water-proof coat in Sarn, worth three marks in Vhal. The horns are carved into count-sticks by the Fen reeves. Folklore vs truth. Folklore says the bog-cow is the Marsh Mother’s own cattle. Truth: it is a beast that has found a place where the Warm comes up through the mud and has learned to stand still and take it in through the hide.

Warm-stone grub

Habitat. The deep mines of Keld, in the seams where warm-stone is cut. Behaviour. Eats warm-stone from the inside. A block that looks sound will crumble to dust in a man’s hands if the grub has been at it. How it fights. It does not fight. A mine full of grubs is a mine that sweats in the wrong places, and a Warm-flood follows. The Keldish call the grub the mine’s own appetite. Threat. Indirect; a calamity’s harbinger. Keldish mine-reeves will stop a shift for one grub. Uses and price. The grub is fried and eaten by the under-vessed slaves, who say it is the only warmth they are given that they get to keep. No market price. Folklore vs truth. Folklore says the grub is sent by the mountain to punish greedy digging. Truth: it is a beetle larva that has learned to digest stone which has held heat for a thousand years, and it is older than the mines.

III. Spirits, blights and the half-returned

Hearth-wisp

Habitat. Hearth-fires, banked coals, the Weighing Hall’s grating of cold iron. Behaviour. A small light that appears where a fire has been banked and left. It moves toward warmth and away from iron. How it fights. It sits on a sleeper’s chest and draws the fire out of him while he dreams that he is warm. In the morning he is cold and does not know why. A hearth-wisp in a child’s room is a common cause of the second chill, or so the midwives say. Threat. Thin to Whole; usually a nuisance, sometimes a death. Uses and price. A wisp can be coaxed into a clay cup and carried as a light that needs no fuel. Such cups are sold in Vhal for two marks and go cold after a tend. Folklore vs truth. Folklore says a hearth-wisp is the soul of a fire that was put out unkindly. Truth: it is a small gathering of Warm that has learned a shape and keeps it, which is almost the definition of a vessel. The Court’s keepers consider wisps vermin and crush them.

Ash-wraith

Habitat. Where many have been spent: the Old Kiln, the Empty Cantons, the corners of houses in Low Kiln after a levy, the Bone Orchard. Behaviour. A grey shape seen from the corner of the eye by tallow-light. It does not approach a person who is looking at it. It follows the Ash ridge from room to room. How it fights. It presses against the sleeper’s measure where the wall is thinnest and tries on the shape of the life that made the Ash. A man who dreams of someone else’s childhood wakes with that childhood available to him for a day. This is called ash-sickness in the Cantons. Threat. Thin to Held; a handful of wraiths can unmake a person’s memory over a winter. Uses and price. The Ash they leave behind is the strongest quiet ash there is, and a jar of it is worth twenty marks to the right buyer in the Hearthseat. The right buyer is always rich. Folklore vs truth. Folklore says the wraith is the ghost of a person who was returned thin and remembers being someone. Truth: it is what was left of the self when the Warm was taken, and it has learned to move. It remembers because selfhood does not evaporate.

The Second

Habitat. Any house where a graft was set from a donor whose measure was shaped differently. Commonest in Sere and in the back rooms of the Hearthseat. Behaviour. A person who walks and speaks and has a name, who wants a life that was not theirs. The want arrives in the Afterdrop and does not leave. Sometimes the person is the donor and sometimes the recipient; the law does not ask which. How it fights. It does not usually fight. It hesitates at the wrong moment, reaches for a hand that is not there, and says a word in a language the household does not know. In a crisis it may open a way in its own forearm and not know why. Threat. Measured as Thin to Held, but the danger is to the idea of a self rather than to a body. The Court strikes the word from its records and calls the condition a settling of the graft. Uses and price. In Sere, a Second with a beautiful face is exhibited at feasts and given small coins. Its parts are not sold; a person is not a part, in law, if they have no measure. Folklore vs truth. Folklore says the Second is a person returned twice by mistake. Truth: it is a Second Want with a body, which is a different thing and worse, because it is someone who wants and cannot say what.

Seam-blight

Habitat. Old repairs: the rim-seam, the under-seam, the walls of very old houses, the clay around the Old Kiln. Behaviour. Travels along a crack and widens it by a hair’s breadth each season. It is not alive in any ordinary sense. It is the repair failing. How it fights. It does not fight. A house with seam-blight loses heat through the wall in winter and is eventually condemned, which in Grath means the household is moved and the crack is left where it is. Threat. To places, not people, until the place fails. A seam-blight in the rim-seam is a calamity measured in years. Uses and price. The grey dust scraped from a blighted seam is used by the Vorr to mark counting-stones. No market price; the Vorr do not sell it, only numbers. Folklore vs truth. Folklore says the blight is the First Breaking continuing to happen. Truth: it is a patch coming loose, which is the same thing said by someone who has looked closely.

Frost-bloom

Habitat. Frozen ground, the banks of the Ilm in Stone, the wall of a room where a thin person has slept for a winter. Behaviour. Grows overnight in the shape of a branching flower on any surface where Warm has recently left. It is ice, but ice in a pattern that no cold alone would make. How it fights. It does not fight. Where it grows, the wall holds less. A household that finds frost-bloom on the inside of its outer wall will be cold all winter no matter how much fuel it buys. Threat. To vessels that are places. A frost-bloom on a person’s skin around a graft means the graft is failing. Uses and price. The patterns are copied by potters onto kiln apples and by the Vorr into their counting, for a frost-bloom is a map of the leak that made it. No price. Folklore vs truth. Folklore says frost-bloom is the cold’s own flower. Truth: it is the shape of the Warm that has gone — the branching map of a loss. Dan will one day look at a frost-bloom on a saint’s cold skin and recognise the spiral.

The Hollow (thin-child)

Habitat. The poor districts of every town; the waiting rooms of the Court; thin-return children’s graves. Behaviour. A child returned so thin that there is almost no shape in it. It eats, breathes, and looks at people, but does not want anything. It is not ill by any physician’s measure. How it fights. It does not fight. Its danger is to the adults around it: a household with a hollow child will spend warmth on it that the household cannot afford, for years, to keep something that does not know it is being kept. Threat. None to a vessel; a great deal to an account. Uses and price. None. The Court books a hollow child as a successful return. Its parents are told the account stands. Folklore vs truth. Folklore says a hollow child is one whose name was written wrongly. Truth: it is several people divided among one body, so that none of them is present enough to want. It is the poor person’s version of a whole return, and the Court’s cheapest product.

The Uncollected

Habitat. Anywhere a person has died without a collection: battlefields, the Waste, the road between Sill and Greybank, the far bogs. Behaviour. A dead vessel whose Warm has not been drawn walks until the Warm leaks out into the Waste. It does not speak and does not seem to know it is dead. It walks in the direction it was facing when it broke. How it fights. It pushes. A man who is pushed by an uncollected thing feels a cold place on his chest for days afterwards and may find a little of his measure missing. Threat. Thin to Held; more frightening than dangerous. The Court sends no collectors into the Waste partly because there is nothing there worth collecting and partly because the uncollected there do not stop. Uses and price. There is no market. The Ash they leave is pure and is gathered by Wastelings, who give it away. Folklore vs truth. Folklore says the uncollected are the dead who were too poor to be returned. Truth: that is exactly right, which is why hearth-maids cross themselves at a birth-flower and say nothing.

Salt-thing

Habitat. The cold tides of the Sallow, the Glaze Cliffs, the salt pans of Vhal in winter. Behaviour. A shape made of salt and cold water that follows a boat at night and taps the hull where the Warm is stored. How it fights. It draws through the hull, the way a silt-leach draws through an ankle. A boat’s store can be emptied in a night. Sailors who sleep on deck wake with grey fingertips. Threat. Thin to Banked, to stores rather than to people. A salt-thing cannot cross fresh water, which is why Vhaleni captains carry a barrel of rain. Uses and price. Salt taken from a salt-thing’s body is the finest curing salt in Sarn, worth four bits a pound in Vhal. It is said to keep a wound from turning. Folklore vs truth. Folklore says the salt-thing is a drowned sailor who could not pay the ferryman. Truth: it is a gathering of Sallow salt around a small knot of Warm that has forgotten how to be a vessel. Dippers bless nets against it, which is a reasonable thing to do.

Mire-fever

Habitat. The Fenmarch; the marsh east of Reedholt; anywhere the peat has been drained badly. Behaviour. Not a creature but a blight of the water. A person who drinks or wades develops a fever that burns and then leaves them colder than before, each cycle taking a little head. How it fights. It fights by being endured. There is no treatment in Sarn beyond keeping the patient warm, which the poor cannot do. Threat. Thin to Held; a Held vessel can survive three cycles where a Thin one dies in the first. Uses and price. Fever-mud from a patient’s bed is used by Fenfolk to seal a peat wall, because it dries hard. No price worth naming. Folklore vs truth. Folklore says the Marsh Mother sends the fever to those who drain her peat. Truth: draining the peat lets something in the mud that was formerly held down come up into the water, and the fever is that thing’s appetite.

Sweet-sickness

Habitat. High Kiln, the Glaze Cliffs, any house with lead-glazed cups. Behaviour. A blight of the glaze. The first sign is a pale line at the gum; the end is a man who cannot hold a cup. Takes two to five years. How it fights. It does not fight; it accumulates. Each year the ways run a little slower and the hands shake a little more, until the vessel cannot keep a grip on anything, including itself. Threat. All ranks; no vessel is immune, and the rich have the sweetest cups. Uses and price. None. The cure is to stop using the glaze, which no pottery household in Sarn can afford to do. Folklore vs truth. Folklore says the sickness is the price of beauty. Truth: it is lead, and the lead was noted in Y.H. 641, blamed on the glaze, and then quietly unlearned. The Court’s finest ware is still glazed with it.

Kiln’s breath

Habitat. Enclosed kiln houses, cellars, any room with a fire that has not enough air. Behaviour. An invisible blight. It kills in sleep and is called a merciful death by people who have not had to identify the body. How it fights. It does not fight. A man goes to bed and does not wake; his lips are a little too red, which is all the evidence there is. Threat. All ranks equally, which makes it the most democratic danger in Sarn. Uses and price. None. Kiln-hounds are said to smell it, which is the only useful thing about them. Folklore vs truth. Folklore says the kiln takes a life in exchange for a good firing. Truth: it is the fire using up the air in a closed room, and the potters know this and leave the window open anyway, because a draft spoils the glaze.

IV. Monsters and calamities

Seam-serpent

Habitat. The under-seam; the deep shaft of the First Seam; the cracks beneath the Nine Wells. Behaviour. A long thing that moves along a crack the way an eel moves along water. It is rarely seen whole. Keldish surveyors have entered its passing as a change in the numbers. How it fights. It presses against a way from the far side of a wall and draws Warm out through the stone. A miner who has been “touched by the seam” will be thin by the end of the shift. Threat. Held to Welled; to a single man it is a cold place in the rock. To a mine it is a slow emptying. Uses and price. The shed skin is sometimes found in the First Seam and is said to be the oldest repair material nobody will name. The Vorr will look at it and say only that the numbers are correct. Folklore vs truth. Folklore says the serpent is the First Breaking still moving. Truth: it is a vessel that lives in the world’s crack and drinks what the world loses, which is a useful arrangement for everyone except the world.

The Sweating

Habitat. The deep mines of Keld; the hot seams under the Rim. Behaviour. A calamity rather than a creature: the Warm comes up through the stone in a lethal flood, and the only warning is the smell of hot lead. How it fights. It fills the shaft. Men in the deep cannot climb out fast enough. The Keldish mine-reeves count every life that goes down and every ember of iron that comes up, and the two numbers are never the same. Threat. All ranks; Hearthward and above can survive it briefly by drawing from the flood instead of being drowned in it, which is one reason the Court employs them. Uses and price. The stone after a sweating is rich in Warm and is cut into warm-stone that holds heat for a generation. A single block is worth ten marks on the Keel. Folklore vs truth. Folklore says the mountain sweats when it is angry. Truth: it is the Rim current finding a thin place in the wall, and a warm-stone grub is usually found in the seam afterwards.

Cold-eater

Habitat. The Waste, and the last passes beyond the Rim. Behaviour. A Wasteling shape, tall and thin, that moves toward any vessel with a head. It has no language and no appetite a visitor can recognise. It does not kill; it takes. How it fights. It comes close and stands. The visitor’s Warm goes out to it, slowly at first and then faster. Travellers who go far enough into the Waste come back older and thinner and do not remember what they went for. A cold-eater that follows a man back over the Rim can empty a house in a tend. Threat. Drawn to Hearthward; a whole village may be needed to drive one off. The Court sends no collectors into the Waste, and the Wastelings do not fight them, because there is nothing there worth owning. Uses and price. Nothing that can be counted. A Wasteling may give a stone, a name, or a day’s warmth and expect nothing back, which is how the Unbanked differ from the uncollected. Folklore vs truth. Folklore says the cold-eater is the Waste itself come to fetch what is owed. Truth: it is a vessel that has leaked so completely that it has become a sink, and what it takes goes nowhere. This is the closest thing in Sarn to a true monster.

Mender’s failure

Habitat. Not fixed. Appears in the records of the chain of broken worlds, and occasionally in the present one. Behaviour. A person with a surgeon’s attention and no surgeon’s mercy, recalled in an earlier age to solve the same problem. One made the repair beautiful and lied about it. One made it honest and let the world break. One became the Court. How it fights. It does not fight; it builds. It makes clamps, slates, institutions, and worlds that hold. The mark it leaves is a branching red flower across the ribs and forearm, and the same mark appears on men who have never met each other. Threat. Beyond Kept in consequence, though its personal vessel may be no stronger than a Held man’s. Uses and price. None. The attempt to use one as a tool is the whole history of the Unbanked. Folklore vs truth. Folklore has no name for it. The Keldish call such marks birth-flowers and consider them unlucky. The truth is that the process has been run before, and the failures were not killed. They became the machinery.

Hearth-tongue

Habitat. The Weighing Hall, behind the grating of cold iron, where the flame has never gone out. Behaviour. The Hearth-fire drawn up through the Keel. It has never been touched by a priest and has never gone out. When a keeper draws on it wrongly it reaches back. How it fights. It takes a memory from the drawer and gives nothing in exchange. A Hearthward practitioner who draws too often becomes a polite, empty person who no longer knows why they came into the room. The Court calls this a vocation. Threat. Hearthward and below; even the Kept are not asked to stand near it. Uses and price. The fire heats the Hearthseat and lights the Weighing. The price is paid by whoever holds the keys to the Nine Wells, and by the Kept, who are kept near it because they cannot enjoy it. Folklore vs truth. Folklore says the tongue of the Hearth is the breath of the Nine Who Kept. Truth: it is the leak made visible, and the room it burns in is the mouth of a wound.

Star-rivet

Habitat. The rim-seam; the sky, where the stars are the rivet-heads of the great repair. One is said to have fallen in the Ash Times. Behaviour. A rivet that has worked loose and come down through the sky. It is old, bright with handling, and too hot to hold for long. How it fights. It does not fight. Where it lands, the ground holds more than it should for a season and then less than it should forever. The clay around the Old Kiln will not take a glaze, and the ash in its throat is deeper than a man is tall. Threat. A calamity to a place; nothing to a person who stays away. Uses and price. A fragment of a rivet would be the most valuable object in Sarn, if anyone could bear to look for one. No market has ever existed in it. Folklore vs truth. Folklore says the stars are rivets driven by the Nine Who Kept to hold the lid on the clay. Truth: the seam shows many repairs laid over one another, and the oldest of them are not made of anything that can be named. The rivets are part of a repair that failed before Sarn was made.
