Four at the Teeth

Days 132–134 · Reed 13–15, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, the Weir and Sill

The cord knows what the mouth will not say, and the mouth knows what the cord will not count. Keep the two apart. Laying them together is a trade no honest house makes.

— A saying of the Weir, told sideways

— Day 132 —

Reed 13, Thirdday, Y.H. 813

The reed month was in the middle of its middle tend and the morning came up over Low Kiln the way a pot comes up into the light from under a bench — a little at a time, with the shadow going first and the rim after it and the whole belly last. Sella Vail carried the boy out at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, going wide around the stones and the laid-down pans and anything that had been set out to dry, and gave the instruction to the wool and to the river wind and never to the basket. Keep it off his ears; it had come down with them and would go back up with them, and a basket set down was a pot set down and left. The little-door burn on the back of her hand closed on the wicker’s edge and then on nothing. Woodsmoke went before her down the yard and wet wool went with her, and under both the faint sour mineral tang of the Ash that had gathered in the corners of this house for two years and would not be swept. He was one hundred and thirty-two days old, four months and twelve days, and nobody in the yard said so and nobody under any roof in the district knew there was a number to say.

The yard received the morning the way a floor receives a bucket set down in it: without remark, keeping the shape of what had been there before. the Clay Yard had the colour of last week’s mud worked into a brown skin, and the reeds stood beyond the drying racks in long grey ranks where the barley had stood a month before, broken ends green, frost-bitten tips pale as old bone. A woman at a tub lifted her head as the basket passed, saw a basket, and went back to her work without appearing to have seen it. Sella went around the clean dark line where the Ash ridge had turned, and around the new finer hairline of grey that had gathered beside it and been called the thaw, with a care that looked like not seeing either one. She did not sweep them. A line left where it was was still a line kept, and the house said the grey would go when it went, and the boy had learned by now that a thing the house said would go was a thing the house had decided not to carry out.

At the drying racks she went wide around the space beneath the lowest rack where the broken birth-slates lay, as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and entered it no more than she had entered it on any of the hundred and thirty-one days before. Then they came to the edge of the worked strips, and there was the other place.

It was not a hole and it was not a mark. It was a space in the returning line where, on Seed 15, a hand had gone in with the seed and on Seed 27 had not come out with the rest. Sella went around it with a look and a wide step and no number, the way the yard had gone around it every morning since, and the boy felt the half-pace change by the width of a hand as she shifted the basket away from it. No name went with the step. No cause, no body, no figure, no payer. If a hand came up out of the ground in a month or a year a slate would be found and a figure made to stand at the foot of it; until then the space would go on being gone around, and the month would go on standing short. A cost with no figure was still a cost. He had spent a hundred and thirty-two days learning that this was so, and had no word in Grathic in which to say it to anybody and no voice with which to say it if he had.

He set the going-around on the place where it already lay, beside but never among the shelf’s two gaps and the crust in the fold two rooms away. There was a saying of the loaf-shelves of Low Kiln that two gaps stay two gaps until a finger lays them together and an account then wants a name, and a saying could be carried a long way on one breath if a person were careless with it. A neighbour had once laid a planting and a child’s appetite under one cause and gone off with her pot without seeing that she had joined anything. A line drawn through this ground and that shelf and that fold would be a cause with a clean hand at the foot of it, and the clean hand was the thing he had spent his whole first life declining to be and the whole of this one with no hand to be it with. He kept the three places apart without once deciding to, the way a man keeps his feet on a floor that is not level, and the keeping bought nothing and was the only work in the yard that anybody had not asked him to do.

His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far. Nobody had been given an instruction about that sound. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had been given an instruction about the thread either, so it stayed where it had been put, stiff and doing nothing. The river wind came off the Ilm and found the bare joint where the wool had ridden back, and cold arrived in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist. The child in him was cold at the ear and wanted what was two inches off and could not be reached; the man in him read the half-pace and the wide step and the order in which the yard went around its absences. Both were true. The second was quieter and the first was louder, and neither could be handed to anybody.

The reeds came in armfuls from the beds below the yard, going-downriver and coming-back, green at the broken ends and grey at the frost-pale tips, laid in the ruts and judged by the arm that carried them and by nothing set on a slate. A bundle went past on a girl’s shoulder with the butt of it wet against her hip and the cut ends nodding as she walked; a man behind her carried two and swung them so the tips cleared the rack, and a third came along with one arm loaded and the other held in against his coat for the wind. Nobody counted them where a figure could be taken down. A day’s cutting was a going and a coming-back, and the cost of it was in the shoulder and not in the hand that wrote.

A cutter come up from the beds set a bundle on the ground and waded into the middle of a girl with a creel who had been looking at it as a pot is looked at before it is lifted. “Lift it,” he said. She put both hands under it and got it up, and the green ends shivered. “Your arm’ll tell you. It’ll tell you what went into it, and that’s the whole of what it knows.”

“And what it cost?” she said.

“It doesn’t know cost. Ask it how heavy it is. That’s a question it can answer.” He took the bundle back off her and set it where it had stood, with the cut ends toward the yard. “There’s your figure. There’s the whole of the account.”

He went off downriver with the question unanswered behind him, and the girl took up her creel and went after the others with it bumping her hip.

The boy watched the arm that carried the bundle. It was a working arm, corded at the forearm and pale where the reed-water ran off it, and it knew exactly what it held: how many green ends, how much weight at the butt, how far the load sat from the shoulder and where it would balance if the ground rose under it. That was a record, and it was exact, and it told the truth about what had been carried. It told nothing at all about what the carrying cost. The man with two bundles would be stiff at the shoulder by dusk and would not set that down on any slate, and the girl’s creel would bruise the same place on her hip tomorrow and there was no figure in the world for it. Exact, complete, worth nothing. He had once stood at the head of a table and read a body the way these people read an arm, and the reading had told him what was wrong and had not told him who would pay for the fixing. The register worked the same here. He noted the order of reaching — the arm to the bundle before the eye to the cutter, the hand to the record before the hand to the person — and had no standing to enter it. A surgeon’s eye in a basket that could not close two inches on its own rim. He kept the observation and let it go, as he had let a hundred others go, because an observation kept where nobody could enter it was the only kind he had ever been permitted to keep.

A man come up from the strips with dry mud worked into his wrists and standing in the creases, and stood at the edge of the going-downriver with his hands loose and his mouth open on the question. “What’ll the work want before the tend’s out?” he said. “There’s hands enough today. There’ll be hands enough at the end of it. Somebody wants a figure.”

The wind took the last of it toward the beds. Sella did not turn from putting the fold straight at the boy’s ear, which she did without once looking at his face. “The weather and the river,” she said. “Ask them. They’ll tell you when they’ve had enough.”

“That isn’t a figure.”

“The house has said it.” Her hands went on with the wool. “A number goes into a slate, not into a strip and not into a bundle. You want a number, go ask the river to give you one. It’ll give you the weather.”

The man stood a moment with the unanswered thing in his mouth, the way a man stands with a cup he has not set down because there is nowhere on a bench to set it. Then he shut his hands and carried it away downriver, going the way the reeds went, with the weather in his mouth and no figure in it. A cost asked in the form of a direction had been answered with a direction and had left no entry. The boy had watched that done in a market and at a well and at a loaf shelf, and here it was done again in the open yard, as ordinary as sweeping and just as final. The man’s wrists went grey with drying mud as he walked off. Nobody called after him. Nobody would have entered his question if they had.

The district had one word for what it would not carry and one past tense for what it would not own. The word was weather and the past tense was the thaw that was, and between them a person could go home warm at dusk without ever having owned the day that made them so. Hands went into the strips and did not come out and it was the weather; a ridge grew an inch in a corner and it would go when it went; a room shut in the last war with the damper closed and men dead of the breath was eighteen leagues east and a curiosity with a date, not the war’s hand. The boy kept the man’s unanswered question on a place of its own as a shape and not a rule, because a shape laid beside three other shapes will stand up and call itself a doctrine if there is nobody to hold them apart, and he had spent a hundred and thirty-two days being the person who held them apart. It was an invisible labour and it bought nothing anyone could have entered, and the yard went on with its going-downriver as if nothing at all had been decided in it.

The yard went back to its going-downriver. The girl’s creel went bumping after the others toward the beds, and the reeds went over the ruts in armfuls, and the place in the returning line where the hand had not come out went on being gone around by anybody who came near it, with a look and a wide step and no number. The sun came higher and the frost-pale tips lost their pale and went green in the light. A pot somewhere under the Vail roof gave its note as it was set down and the note went where it sounded and was not made into a seventh. Six hearings stood where they had stood, five from four sources and the sixth from a person, beside and never among; a pot under a roof was a pot under a roof and not an answer to a question nobody had asked it. The boy kept them where they were and went on being carried.

His small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water coming off the Ilm, and came down. It could not reach the bundle going past on the girl’s shoulder, or the pot under the roof, or the clean dark line and the finer grey in the corner, or the space beneath the lowest rack, or the empty place in the returning line that Sella had just gone around. It could not close on the creel, or the wool at his own ear, or the crust two inches off in the fold. One hundred and thirty-two days old, four months and twelve days, he was told by nobody and would not have known what the number counted if somebody had. The man who had asked for the figure walked off downriver with the weather in his mouth, the girl’s creel bumped her hip and went on bumping it out of sight among the ranks of reeds, and the hand went up an inch toward the water and came down with the day exact and unspent and spoken by nobody.

Inside the Vail kiln-house the light came in low through the finger’s-width door and travelled along the floor until it found the bench leg and stopped there, as though it too had been given an instruction to go around. Sella Vail carried the boy in at the half-pace and set the basket low and a little back, near the leg, at the angle at which a man lying on the floor sees the underside of a board that a man standing sees only the top of. It was not a place she chose for him. It was a place the light chose, and she knew the light’s habits better than she knew the habits of anybody under this roof. She gave the instruction to the wool and to the fire and to the pot and to the broom and never to the basket — keep it off his ears, feed it, turn it, go around what the water left, it cost enough to come in and would cost more to take out — and told the fire it was the thaw. The little-door burn on the back of her hand closed on the wicker’s edge and then on nothing, and she did not look down into the basket. Woodsmoke went before them into the room and wet wool went with them, and under both the faint sour mineral tang of the Ash that had gathered in this house for two years and would not be swept.

He lay with cold iron at his cheek where the bench bracket had been bolted into the wood and left, and the grain of the board above him ran the length of his body the way a furrow runs the length of a field after the plough has gone over it. From down here a shelf was a roof, and a roof had a second face, and the second face was the one a house turned down when it wanted something kept where nobody thought to look for it. He had once come into rooms where the instruments were laid out on a towel before the patient was brought in, and had read the order of the day from the order of the tray; here the room told its order in loaves pushed along a board and a glance at a jar on the wall, and he read that order too, with the same attention and the same entire lack of standing to act on it. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had been given an instruction about the thread. The child in him was cold at the ear and wanted the crust two inches off in the fold; the man in him read the angle of the light and the second face of the board before he read either. Both were true. The second was quieter and the first was louder, and neither could be handed to anybody.

Above him the loaf shelf held its row the way a row is held in a house that keeps its days badly: loaves pushed along it, a glance at the clay jar cold under its glaze, and spaces where a loaf should have stood. The row kept two gaps and no third. One was the old space at the end, the one that had come in with the planting and that a neighbour could name in one breath without ever seeing it as a thing that was missing; the same neighbour had called the other the price of a wet thing set down wrong, hearing two sayings and not one absence. That other was a palm’s breadth of bare board that had come in on the night of Seed 6 with no scrape, no crumb, no print and no word, as if a loaf had lifted itself off in the dark and gone. A row comes up short and a body gets used to it. A house that kept its days in loaves could carry a whole tend without ever calling a space a number; a gap pushed along with the row was a gap the house had decided to live beside. No finger in Low Kiln had laid the two together, and a saying of the loaf-shelves held that two gaps stay two gaps until such a finger comes and an account then wants a name. The fold at his ear kept one stiff brown-edged crust and an empty place beside it that had no name, and that record lay in the other room. The two rooms did not touch. He had no word in Grathic for the second face of a board and no voice to say it if he had.

Sella stood at the shelf with her back to the basket. She moved her lips along the row, giving nobody a figure, and her glance went once to the clay jar and came back; the glaze was cold in the warm room and she did not touch it. The grey streak at her temple showed pale off the coals as she bent a little at the waist. Then, without looking at the board and without a word, she reached a finger beneath its front edge, to where the charcoal tally lay. The little-door burn closed on the wood’s lip and opened again. The finger went in far enough for the knuckle to set and for the thumb to come up against the grain, and stayed a breath longer than a hand stays when it is reaching only for a loaf. The dry warm smell of the shelf was crossed for that breath by the sharper smell of charcoal, and then by nothing. The wood’s lip kept the small clean shape of the burn where it had closed, and the hand went away with the feel of it, the little door opening on air. She brought the finger away. She did not go back to the shelf.

From the low angle the boy saw the hand go under and come back and saw nothing of what the marks said. A hand that goes under a board without looking either knows the board by heart or has been told not to look, and he left the reason where it stood. The second face of the board was a page kept face down against the grain; a woman who kept a page face down did it because she meant to come back to it where nobody stood reading over her shoulder. In the other life a chart turned down on a tray had meant a hand would come back for it, and the hand had been his; here there was no tray and no hand of his that could turn one. It was a second account kept where nobody thought to look, and the care of the hiding told him more than the strokes would have told him if he could have spelled them. He did not read the clusters or count the strokes or hold their shapes in the dark where he could have kept them. He did not press a thumb where her thumb had been, or lay the underside of the board beside the two gaps above it, or beside the one stiff crust in the fold in the other room that the shelf did not touch. He made no cause of the hand. A cause always wanted a clean hand at the foot of it, and he had no clean hand and had spent both lives declining to be one. He set the object down on the place it already occupied and left it lying, unread and spent by nobody.

His small hand outside the wool could not have reached the front edge of the board, or the pot, or the crust two inches off in the fold, or the jar on the wall whose glaze was cold and which Sella had not touched. It could not close two inches on the rim of the basket that held him. An observation kept where nobody could enter it was the only kind he had ever been permitted to keep, and he kept this one as he kept the others, without expecting it to buy anything. The light went off the underside of the shelf and the marks went dark in the corner where the grain turned, and the hand did not go under any board again that afternoon.

A neighbour come up from the worked strips stood in the doorway with dry mud worked into her wrists in the colour of the clean dark line and water standing in the creases, and a smell of river water coming off her colder than the room. She held a wet pot against her hip the way a woman holds a thing she has already set down in her mind, and did not set it down, because a pot set down in this house was a pot left and she had come for an answer and meant to carry it away with the pot. Her eyes went to the shelf and not to the basket, because the shelf was where a woman looked for an answer here and the basket was a pot set down and left. “What’ll the row want before the tend’s out?” she said. “There’s hands enough today. They’ll say there’s hands enough at the end of it. Somebody wants a figure.”

Sella’s hand went toward the clay jar on the wall and stopped, and came away without touching the cold glaze. The end of her instruction went to the pot instead. “Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there,” she said. “The house has said it.”

The neighbour stood a moment with the unanswered thing, the way a woman stands with a pot she has nowhere to set down, then carried it off as level as she had carried it in, with nothing slopped and nothing given. Water went on dripping from her sleeves into the trodden ash behind her and was gone into it. The boy set the asking and the answering on a place of their own. A cost asked in the form of a direction had been answered with a direction and had arrived nowhere as a figure; the two lines met nowhere and left nobody entered. He had watched the same trade done in the yard that morning, with a man’s mud going grey as he walked off downriver, and here it was done again indoors, as ordinary as sweeping and just as final. A woman could carry an unanswered question out of a house as level as a pot if she had been taught that a question set down was a question owned, and this house had taught it to everybody who came through the door. He had no standing to enter the question or its answer, and the keeping of it spent nothing and was worth nothing to anybody who might have asked what it was he did all day.

The widow’s mark hung where it hung, a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it and worn bright where a thumb had gone over it in other years, and Sella did not touch it or turn it toward the light. She touched it before an opening and at no other hour, and a shelf was not an opening and an afternoon in the middle of a tend was not an opening, so the mark stayed on its cord against her throat while the dry smell of the loaf shelf went up past it and the sharper smell of charcoal went under. The Ash lay in the corner where the ridge had turned, the clean dark line and the new finer hairline of grey beside it, unswept and called the thaw. She told the fire it was the thaw again and did not look toward the corner, and turned the coals with the back of a broken pot, and gave the end of the instruction to the broom. The house went on around its two absences without entering either one. The fire took its four low pulls under blind ash and kept the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for a hundred and thirty-two days, and nobody in the room called it a count or set it beside the marks under the board. A shelf, an afternoon, a strip, a basket and a bed are not openings, and the mark hung on its cord where it had hung since the last one.

The afternoon went on at the half-pace, which is the pace of a house that has decided not to hurry toward anything. Sella moved the pot and set its rim out and its handle toward the side of the bench where a reaching hand would find it, and did not look toward the basket, and did not go back to the shelf. The boy’s hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth of the fire and came down. He was one hundred and thirty-two days old, four months and twelve days, and nobody under the roof had said so and nobody under the roof knew there was a number to say. Above him the shelf kept its two gaps. Under it the marks waited where they had been put, and the finger had come away without a word, and the two rooms of the house did not touch.

The afternoon went downriver with the reeds, and the boy went downriver in the basket, at the Weir on the plank walk where the boards ran out over the water on posts. Sella Vail set him at the edge of the work with no slate within a half-pace, and gave the instruction to the wool and to the river wind and never to the basket — keep it off his ears, it had come down with them and would go back up with them, and a basket set down was a pot set down and left. The little-door burn closed on the wicker’s edge and then on nothing. Salt had dried white on the posts where the flood had touched them and gone away; the traps stood with their mouths upriver; the smell of eel, cold fish and tallow smoke came up off the Ilm and went under the wet wool. A league of the river road lay between these boards and Low Kiln, and the whole of it had been walked at the half-pace.

The judgement of the morning held into the afternoon. A bundle was what the arm that carried it said it was, going-downriver and coming-back, green at the broken ends and pale at the frost-bitten tips; nothing set on a slate anywhere along the boards contradicted the arm. The boy watched a girl with a creel take a bundle up and let her shoulder settle under it before she moved, and watched a man behind her swing two so the cut ends cleared the rack. Their arms knew what they held. Their arms did not know what the holding cost, and there was no figure in the district for the stiffness that would come into them by dark. Exact, complete, worth nothing. He had the same register here that he had once had at the head of a table, and the same empty authority to enter what it read.

A reed-cutter came along the boards with a bundle on his shoulder and a short length of grey wool or reed-fibre cord looped inside his coat. The wool had gone grey with the oil of a working thumb, the grey a stitch-hole takes after a thread has been drawn through it a dozen times. He set the bundle on the flat blessing stone and straightened the cord on his thumb, drawing it through his lightly closed fingers the way a man draws a measuring line and not the way a man opens a book. Then he turned it once so the knots stood in the small light, tied one low with his teeth and a pull of the thumb, bit the end off and put the cord back inside his coat, and drew the coat closed over it.

A weir woman with salt white at her wrists stopped beside the stone. “What does that cord hold?” she said.

“You can ask the cord.”

“I can’t read a cord.”

“No more can the Court.” He said it level, with the bite of reed-fibre still at the corner of his mouth, and went to lift a trap with the question going down into the water behind him.

No figure came out of the exchange. No knot was counted where a man could write it down, and the cutter gave the cord no name and made nothing of the bundle that lay on the stone with its cut ends nodding in the wind. The boy kept the object where it was. A cord was a page that could be carried inside a coat; the wear of the thumb was its only ink, and the knots stood up off it for anyone with eyes, and not one Court thumb in Grath could spell a word of it. There was a saying of the Weir that the cord knew what the mouth would not say and the mouth knew what the cord would not count, and the two were kept apart because laying them together was a trade no honest house made. He had watched a man go around a place in a returning line with a look and a wide step and no number that morning; he had watched a woman’s finger go beneath a board at home without a word; and now a third private record stood on a stone in the small light with its end bitten off. He set them side by side and not across, the way an instrument is set beside an instrument on a towel and not laid upon it. He did not count the knots. He did not name the cord’s keeping or join it to the place in the strip where the hand had gone in and not come out, or to the marks under the board, or to any word that had once been let fall into a basket and not repeated. Four likenesses in a row will stand up and call themselves a cause if nobody holds them apart, and holding them apart was the work he had been given and had never once been asked to do.

Lower down the boards Orra Weir stood with her board on its side and a cord at her waist, turning the board with the wrist that had turned it ten thousand times. Her hands were like old rope and the pale eel scars lay uncovered across both forearms; the grey streak showed first through the black hair when she moved her head, and the nasal half-song of the Fen came into her voice where she was tired. She pulled one knot down with her thumb and set it among the others.

“What’ll the levy take now the hands are in the reeds?” a woman at the rails said.

“The ninth of Ilmen,” Orra said. “The collector’s boat came round the bend on the flood and had six oars. One man stood at the stern and never touched them.”

“That isn’t the levy.”

“It’s a date. The river takes what the Court doesn’t. You can shout at either one. I’ve tried.” Her thumb went to the cord again as the sentence ended, and the boy could not tell whether that was a lie or the habit of counting, and made no cause of it. A date given where a figure was asked was an answer with the question taken out of it, a chart closed over a note nobody had opened; the man at the stern who kept his hands off the oars went on a place of his own, apart from the midwife’s reaching hand and the thane’s twice-wiped thumb, and none of the three touched.

The last knot on the cord showed the shape it had shown every morning since the year of the flower: a double turn, tail tucked, tied left over right, with the small extra weight of a thumb pressed in. The shape was legible. The name of it was not spoken, and the boy had no word in Grathic for the shape and no voice to say it if he had. He did not count it. He did not lift a hand to it; his hand could not have closed two inches on the rim of the basket, let alone reached a cord at a woman’s waist a league upriver from the bed where he had first heard the word that went with it. The cord would go back to the Eel House where no clerk could spell it and no boy could read it, and the watching would go on getting no answer, and the knot would stay a thing a woman had made to hold and not a figure anyone had paid.

Her board lay beside the eel board at the outer edge of the shade and did not touch it.

At the outer boards Mira Silt worked the eels with slime dried grey in long strokes on her sleeves and silt pale in the hollow of her neck, her head held a little to the right where the top of her left ear had been and her small smile kept on her face the way a door is kept on the latch. A woman come down from the traps asked whether the run would be thin now that the hands were out of the reeds.

“Thin? Who’s to say thin?” Mira said. “It comes when it comes. Ask the river.” She flicked a string of eel-bones against her thumb and moved two from hand to hand while she decided whether to lie, and put one away when the deciding was done. She said something low about a boat that had come up on the flood and nothing about the hand that had not come out of the strips.

A buyer in a coat with the river still in its hem named a figure for two pair. Mira’s thumb went under her apron to the grey wool cord and stayed longer than the figure required, and shifting a creel showed the second cord twisted twice beneath it, never carried upriver to Nine Pots, never lifted at a door, the knots on neither counted by any thumb but her own and the knot for the thin child upriver untouched.

“You’ve said it twice and I’ve counted it twice,” she said. “There’s nothing in the sentence that wants an answer.” The buyer took the eels and went, and her smile did not move, and she did not once look into the basket where the boy lay.

He kept her not-looking on a place of its own. It was a keeping, the opposite of the pricing face turned the same way across a market, and he had no word in Grathic for either and no standing to ask either woman what she held. On the boards in the small light stood three records in wool and thumb and teeth: a cord gone grey inside a coat, a knot-string at a waist, a second twist beneath an apron. All were visible. None was enterable. A Court thumb could spell a slate and could not spell one of them. He had once kept a tray of instruments and known every one by the wear on its handle, and here the wear was the record and the handle was a working woman’s thumb, and the tray was a plank walk above the river with the water going under it and taking the sound of every figure before it reached the far bank. His small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the water and came down. It could not reach the cord on the stone, or the knot-string, or the twist under the apron, or the bundle on the blessing stone with its green cut ends. He set the three records down where they already lay and went on being carried, and the going-downriver went past him and the coming-back went past him and no figure went with either.

At the going of the light Sella Vail carried him up the clay road at the half-pace, the river smell going off her as they climbed and the woodsmoke of Low Kiln coming down to meet them. She pulled one knot down with her thumb and set it among the others. He could see every one and enter none. He had no standing to ask either woman what she kept, and the keeping, which in this district was the most subversive act available to a person with no slate, went on in materials no Court thumb could spell.

Below the sill, where the tubs stood and the water had gone down into the last wrinkle, a child’s foot stayed dark inside three turns of wool. It was the cold to the woman who had wrapped it and weather to the yard, and the two facts went past each other as they had gone past each other every morning of the child’s life and never met. The woman said nothing over the wool that any slate could have taken; a neighbour at the tubs said the water had been weather and would go when it went. The extremity went before the core. The small almost-smile on the child’s face was not peace. The afterdrop waited on the far side of a warming nobody would perform. He knew the whole order of it as he had once known it under lamps that were not these lamps, and the reading worked perfectly and bought nothing: no beast, no cause, no cure, no recognition, no word for it in the language of the house, no name to carry to any door, and no way to close two inches of his own hand on a fold. It was the most exact thing in the shade and the least spendable. He did not lay it beside any story heard in a yard, or beside the two unswept lines up at the house, or beside the green that had gone off with the light across the square. A foot below a sill and a colour at a mouth were two things in two places, and he had spent a hundred and thirty-four days learning the cost of drawing the line and had never yet been paid for it.

At the out-of-line landing below the bridge the soft-cuffed clerk was still set for a measure nobody had called. His slate lay against his forearm with the writing turned where nobody else could see it, and his hand went to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls, every time. He had not been taken off since the month turned, or if he had been taken off somebody had put him back in the dark. The market sold its reeds and its eels over the top of him as water goes over a stone that lies where a stone lies, and did not lower its voice. The buyer’s figure went into the noise of the stalls and was gone. Back under the roof the afternoon had come down to the low angle, where the light lay along the floor and stopped at the bench leg as though it too had been given an instruction to go around. Sella Vail carried the boy in and set the basket low and a little back near the leg, at the angle at which a man lying on the floor sees the underside of a board that a man standing sees only the top of. It was not a place she chose for him. It was a place the light chose, and she knew the light’s habits better than she knew the habits of anybody under this roof. Woodsmoke went before her and wet wool went with her, and under both the faint sour mineral tang of the Ash that had gathered in this house for two years and would not be swept. She gave the instruction to the wool and to the fire and to the pot and to the broom and never to the basket. Keep it off his ears. Feed it. Turn it. The little-door burn on the back of her hand closed on the wicker’s edge and then on nothing, and she did not look down into the basket.

Above him the loaf shelf held its row the way a house keeps its days badly: loaves pushed along, a glance at the clay jar cold under its glaze, and two spaces where loaves should have stood and no third. One was the old space at the row’s end, the one that had come in with the planting and that a neighbour could name in one breath without ever seeing it as a thing that was missing; the same neighbour had called the other the price of a wet thing set down wrong, hearing two sayings and not one absence. That other was a palm’s breadth of bare board that had come in on the night of Seed 6 with no scrape, no crumb, no print and no word, as if a loaf had lifted itself off in the dark and gone. A row comes up short and a body gets used to it. A house that kept its days in loaves could carry a whole tend without ever calling a space a number, and a gap pushed along with the row was a gap the house had decided to live beside. No finger in Low Kiln had laid the two together, and a saying of the loaf-shelves held that two gaps stay two gaps until such a finger comes and an account then wants a name. The fold at his ear kept one stiff brown-edged crust and an empty place beside it that had no name, and that record lay in the other room. The two rooms did not touch. A keeping put where nobody expects to be read tells more by the care of the hiding than the writing would, and a hand that hides a thing in front of a child is either careless of the child or trusts him not to read, and the two are very different rooms and he had no standing to enter either. In the small hours he turned out his file the way a man lays instruments on a towel before he begins, each thing in its place and nothing laid across anything else. He had kept six hearings of the full note: five from four sources, a cup across a birth room, a pot under the wheel-shed wall, the district’s slate twice at the Cross, a man giving at the Drawing; and the sixth, the small flat knock out of a woman as the men lifted her, filed beside the five and never among them. No seventh had come in a hundred and thirty-four days, and he had not invented one to fill the place where a seventh would stand if there were sevenths to stand. The told bell stood beside the heard bell and the two did not agree or disagree, being two objects in two places, four, four and a gap in each, with nobody crossing himself at either. Three near-full answers stood on their own tray, advanced and not paid: the cup, the pot, a rack pot lifted a hand’s breadth too high and set down again, each answered so nearly that the want under the note could be felt, and none of them filled.

The interval tray held its older intervals, Stone 13 and Stone 17 and Stone 25, with the half-beat behind a standing finger at the damper, the stroke that went on over the flagstones without the check, and the breath a man drew at the top of a reach and did not spend. And now a fourth stopping of the near-saying lay among them: the sentence about an ear under this roof being worth more than the debt that holds it, brought all the way to the word that would call the loan and stopped there, no figure, no name. Four likenesses lay on that tray, and a man who lets four likenesses stand up together will find they have stood up and called themselves a doctrine by morning; he held them apart, which was an invisible labour and bought nothing anyone could enter. The damper’s double bite was filed as an object joined to no kiln, the bite that holds a vessel whole and the bite that can take the hand off a man, with no word in Grathic for both mercies and no standing to improve either. The two tellings of the shut counting room stood side by side with no third, breath through the nose and breath in the throat, both saying not the war’s hand, and the room off the shed kept its finger’s width of open and gave up nothing.

He was the only adult under this roof and the least use in it: a man who had once laid out instruments on a towel and known every one by the wear on its handle, now a child who could not close two inches of his hand on the rim of the basket two paces off, with a tray of records he could not lift to any table and no table that would have taken them. Cold arrived in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, the wool riding back from the small bare joint so the cold found the bare place and went under the fold where nobody had been given an instruction to put a hand. One hundred and thirty-four days old, four months and fourteen days, he was told by nobody, and if somebody had said the number aloud nobody under this roof would have known what it counted, and he would not have known either what it counted, only that it was his and exact and unspent.
