The Note Let Go Early

Days 315–317 · Bone 16–18, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, Sill

A note let go before the note has finished is a letting-go all the same. The river does not ask the hand why it stopped, and the hand does not ask the river why it went on.

— A saying of the Clay Yard, told over the flat stone by the well in Bone’s last working week

— Day 315 —

Bone 16, Sixthday, Y.H. 813

The hand lay against the fold as a tool is laid down where the next hand will find it without looking. It had gone up an inch toward the low note under the finger’s-width door, taken its small intake from the room whose floor does not touch his, let it go whole on that same note, and come straight down; and there it lay, answer two hundred and four, exact, complete and worth nothing at two inches of air. Put away in the dark. Not taken up again. Nothing under the Vail kiln-house roof had seen it go up and nothing had seen it come down, and the keeping of the number was the only honest work there was to do and it bought nothing at two inches of air.

The small hours went on the way water goes on under ice, without a line drawn across them and without anybody standing at the near side to say that the sixteenth had begun. The older women of the district named their months by the weather and the slatemen named them by the count of Account-days, and neither habit was set right or left wrong under this roof; the month carried both names the way a pot carries two handles and is none the worse for it. The boy came into his three hundred and fifteenth day with nobody saying so. Six months and one hundred and thirty-five days were told by nobody in Low Kiln, Sill and Grath. The count went from three hundred and fourteen to three hundred and fifteen exact, complete, unspent, spoken by no man under any roof. The carried fire’s fifth stood unpaid the same number of days with its payer unnamed, and Garl Vail’s name had not been spoken under the roof the same number. No market, no Cross, no bell on a Sixthday; nothing in the dark had lifted a hand to mark the turning. He had stopped expecting anybody to tell him what the number counted, which is not the same as not wanting it, and the wanting went on under the expectation the way a coal goes on under ash.

He read the room by its small sounds first, because the small sounds do not lie. The coals breathed under blind ash with the dry, sour smell of hot clay gone cold and tallow and wet wool over it all; the river went on water over water under the finger’s-width door, a low thing over a lower thing, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming; the little door of the fire’s warmth stood shut on air, and under it all lay the faint sour mineral breath of the Ash ridge along the skirting board, which nobody had swept in two years and nobody had named in three and which he set down as a smell and nothing more. He took the inventory in its old order, the way a man reads the numbers at the edge of a chart before the writing in the middle, and entered it nowhere, not even on the place where he kept the things he did not enter.

One stiff leathery brown-edged barley crust lay two inches from his ear, gone stiffer in the dry heat of the kiln, its corner standing a hair off the wool. The empty place beside it kept its name to itself and no third had come in to make the row come out even. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen and was unwiped, for nobody under any roof had ever been given an instruction about the thread and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. Cold had stopped at cheek, temple and the small bare joint of the wrist where the wool rode back, as though it had come to the end of a line written out for it, with neither an order to go further nor an order to go away. The Afterdrop waited beyond all three, past a warming nobody in this house would perform.

The surgeon in him took the room as he had once taken a field — by the small sounds first, then the shape of the loss, then the question of what could be done and how little of it there was — and the infant in him took the same room as a warmth that was not where he wanted it. The two readings were of one room and agreed on nothing, and both were true, and the older of the two had long since learned to keep his hand steady while the younger one wanted.

He knew the ease a cold face puts on for what it is not — the composed face that is not composure — and he knew that a cold patient cannot clot and will seem better before he is worse, and must be warmed from the outside and slowly, never rubbed, never set near the fire. The knowing bought nothing at two inches of air. The infant underneath wanted the crust lifted the two inches to his mouth and the thread at his mouth wiped, and the wanting had no words and did not need any. Both were true and the second was louder and neither won, and the two voices argued without a word between them the way a man argues in his sleep, and the argument was the work.

Three counts stood in three places and did not become one. Under the floor the carried fire gave four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have stood, its payer without a name. Overhead the boards kept four nails on the near board, four on the next, and a thumb-width knot where a fifth nail might have been driven and was not. At the finger’s-width door the river was a low thing over a lower thing and was not numbered among the hearings. Agreeing any two would have wanted a payer put under both and no payer stood to any, so the three stayed three. The bell’s gap left behind in the square was not fetched in to make a fourth: the heard bell stayed in its square beside the told bell of the Long Night. Six hearings of the full note stood beside and never among, five from four sources and the sixth, the small flat knock out of as the men lifted her, filed where a thing out of a person must go and never with the things that came out of vessels; the seventh place kept its emptiness and none was invented to fill it, for a finding filled to make a row come out even is a finding forged. The told figure stood beside the paid.

Sella Vail went to the hearth before the basket, as she went to it before everything, and told the coals it was the thaw. It’s the thaw. Go when it goes, she said to them, and turned them with the back of a broken pot, and fed them. The house will keep it, she said, where another woman would have said I. She stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand and counted something under her breath toward no opening — small intake, hold, letting-go — and gave neither gap a figure, because a thing said aloud becomes a thing the Court can enter and she had decided long ago what she would not give the Court. Her hands were never still; when they had nothing to do they counted spoons, nails, the breaths of a sleeping child, and the counting was not a prayer and went to no opening.

At the loaf shelf she kept her finger on top of the front edge so the charcoal tally stayed unread beneath it, and moved her lips along a row standing with two gaps and figured neither gap, hearing two sayings and not one absence. She glanced once at the clay jar standing cold under lead glaze on the wall shelf and shut her hand half over nothing; the little-door burn on the back of her left hand closed on air, two straight sides and a lintel and a threshold with the pale puckered skin inside it, and nobody asked what the jar was for. The jar was not a door and a morning was not an opening, and she gave no instruction to either.

On the pot board the twice-mended pot stood with its repair showing: two staples, one the colour of old brass and the second a wire drawn tighter and left longer than it needed, with a hairline of the original break showing dark between them. She lifted it with her thumb first where the wire crossed the crack, felt what the thumb had to feel, and only then let the weight come on the handle. Leave the crack showing. People trust a thing they can see has been handled, she said to the pot; and If it’s worth mending it’s worth mending twice. The second time is the real one, she said to the fire; and never said either to a person. The sayings were a man’s who mended pots for a living and would not let anyone mend him; they had improved in the keeping and were very likely not as good as he had said them. The pot’s foot came down on the board with the small flat sound of a vessel set where it belongs, and she gave neither the repair nor the half-beat after it a figure. Repair is not restoration, and a mended vessel carries its break, but she did not say that either, because it would have wanted a person standing under it to be asked.

She set the pot rim two inches beyond the reaching hand, handle toward it, with a half-beat and no look toward the bed; then said Keep it off his ears. It’s the river wind to the fold and the wool and the river wind and never to the basket, and went and put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without a look at his face, her hand resting a half-beat longer than the setting needed before it went away, and left the thread where it lay. The grey streak at her temple showed pale where she pushed the coal-black hair back with the wrist that carried the burn. The widow’s mark hung where it hung on its cord at her throat, a thumb-length of clay under lead glaze with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and she did not touch it, because a morning and a hearth are not openings and the hand goes to the mark before the kiln and at no other hour. Five openings in five months are five and not a rule; the next stands far off in a later month’s first firing, not hoped for and not counted toward.

Garl stayed in objects and not in a story. The salt-stiff coat kept his broader shape on its peg behind the door with the crease at the left shoulder where the wheel had leaned on him for twenty years; the hollow seat in the shed kept its twenty-year lean and no one filled it; the clean dark line at the Vail corner and the finer grey beside it called the thaw stayed unswept both. The debt against a firing of tiles circled without a name and was worse for being uncalled, and no collector had come up the clay road or gone down it. His name had not been spoken under the roof three hundred and fifteen days, and the keeping of him was maintenance and not a story, and no lesson was made of any of it.

Four arithmetics of the year stood behind one printed figure. The printed one in twelve was unchallenged, the figure read was still not the figure paid, and no signature stood on the space between; four readings were four and not one account. The four true subtractions rode locked up the clay road in Berr Clay’s box toward High Kiln, unread under this roof. He kept the finding on a place of its own with no payer under it, not a lesson, grievance, mending, doctrine or rule, and did not lay it across the house’s not-doings, or across the dressed grain covered under wool at the rack end and read over by a number that did not know its cost, or across the four held changes on four places, or across the stayed-at-the-inch object, or across his own kept gaps. The edges came near to touching and the finger went between, because a finding with no payer can be carried a long way and a doctrine is only an account that has found a payer, and none stood to this one.

The stayed-at-the-inch object stayed one object on its own place on the sixth, one staying and not a habit, not made again and not a fifth held change and not a rule. One staying is a keeping; two would stand up and call themselves a habit, and a habit would want a payer put under it, and none stood. The four held changes on four places were four and not a rule, and the returned half-check kept its own stone with a finger between it and every other object on the tray. Tarn’s six at the kindling sill and Jorra Vail’s dusty forearms on their own tray stayed where they had been left, and the house kept both silences without an order; neither man was under the roof this morning and neither silence was fetched in to make a fifth of anything. He turned all of it over the way a man turns instruments on a towel at the end of a list and asks nothing of any of it, and nothing stood up as a reach, a hearing, a seventh, a stopping, a payment, a gift, a power, a doctrine or a rule.

Then, in the first light, the hand outside the wool came up an inch toward the river’s note. It took a small intake from the room whose floor does not touch his and let it go whole on the same low note, a restraint and not a payment, borrowing no house count and supplying no payer; it came straight down and laid itself back against the fold. The small intake was no larger than the one before it and no smaller, and the note it went out on was the note it came in on; the hand did not stay at the inch and did not reach the two inches to the crust, but came down as it had come up, without witness and without haste. He filed it as answer two hundred and five: exact, complete and worth nothing at two inches of air, standing up as none of the things it refused and with no payer named to it. The older man took a clean satisfaction in the keeping of the number, and the infant underneath wanted the crust lifted and the thread wiped, and both were true and the second was louder and neither won, and the argument between them was the work. He who had once reduced a thousand shoulders in a room with hard light could not close two inches on the handle of a pot set beyond his reach, and the knowing and the wanting went on under the same roof without a word between them.

Two hundred and five honest answers stood on the sixth place, each an object and not a power, and neither the number nor the small hand-movings that made it stood up and called themselves a doctrine, for a doctrine is an account that has found a payer and none stood. The file lay open on the towel at the head of the bed, turned out the way instruments are turned out on a towel at the end of a list, every gap left gap, every tray parted by a finger’s width and nothing moved. The row stood at five askings with no witness, the well-saying at three shapings, the burned slate at five tellings with a finger between the fifth and the four, the shut-room tellings at two, the pot sayings at three with the Smoke 14 note on its own place, nine hands with the strip unread in a sleeve and no tenth, and nine stoppings with no tenth before the seventh year at its very end; the seventh green was a colour and the seventh shape a shape with no eighth, and the fifth opening stood where it stood with the next far off. Three hundred and fifteen stood exact, complete, unspent and spoken by nobody in Low Kiln, Sill or Grath; six months and one hundred and thirty-five days were told by nobody; the carried fire’s fifth was unpaid the same number of days with its payer unnamed; and Garl’s name had not been spoken under the roof the same number. Nothing on the sixteenth had been opened, joined, named, called, paid or ended, and the keeping of the number was the only honest work there was to do and it bought nothing at two inches of air.

The hand lay back against the fold on the two hundred and fifth answer, and the file lay open on the towel with every gap left gap, and the river’s low note went quiet under the finger’s-width door while the morning came on without a boundary drawn.

The morning went on without drawing a line across itself, and the afternoon came up behind it in the same unmarked way, the light leaving the wall for the floor and then the floor for the yard. The boy lay at the edge of it under a fold of wool, carried, silent, the crust two inches from his ear and the thread at his mouth frozen in the dry heat where nobody had thought to wipe it. Under the Vail kiln-house roof the day kept the fire and kept the silence and put the fold right without a look at the face inside it. Sella Vail came for the basket after the middle of the day. She gave no instruction to the basket. The instruction went past it to the wool and the river wind — Keep it off his ears. It’s the river wind — as though the wool had ears and the wind could be told where to stand. She took the basket up as she took up the twice-mended pot, settling the weight before the move, carried it down at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, set it level where no slate lay, and left it there as a pot is left.

The half-pace was not haste and it was not fear of slopping. It was a distance she kept from open slates the way another woman keeps a distance from a sleeping dog, not because the dog will wake but because a thing left alone is worth leaving alone. She did not look into the basket and did not say whose child it was, and by the time the yard went back to its work he had been set down level, out of the draught, and given nobody’s name.

The yard went around its seven places with a look and a wide step and no number.

At the Vail corner where the Ash ridge turned there lay a clean dark line, and beside it a finer grey hairline that the older women called the thaw and the slatemen would have called something else if they had called it at all; both lay unswept, the old dark under the new fine as a seam lies under a later stitching, and a broom went around the two of them together and took neither. Beneath the lowest rack the broken birth-slates lay where they had been laid, their edges paler than the rack dust sifted over them, and the sweeping went wide of the space beneath as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. On the stone beside the yard well a round ring marked where a shallow clay pan had once stood, and the ring held no scrape, no crumb, no print, no chip, no word — only the darker circle where the pan’s bottom had kept the dust off — and nobody pointed at it and nobody swept it and nobody said what had been set there. Then there was the water in the trough, skinned from edge to edge; the field’s empty place out on the worked strips, which was a direction and not a thing standing; and the returning-line place, where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out, which the yard went around wider than all the rest and entered with a look and a wide step and no number over it.

Seven places, and no eighth stood up to make a row. The boy took them in the order the carrying arm took them — the corner, the rack, the ring, the trough, the strips, the returning line — and kept each where it lay with a finger between every pair. He did not number them. A number spoken over seven absences would have wanted a payer put under them and none stood; a row of eight likenesses would have stood up and called itself a rule, and there was no account in the district that had not already found a payer for a rule. The yard went around and went on, and nothing was mended by the going around, and nothing was paid.

At the well three coverings stood and were called weather.

The stayed frost had gone off the north lip while the yard looked elsewhere — gone in the way a thing goes when no hand takes it and no word is spent on its going — and the north lip was bare, and the yard said the thaw had taken it, and entered it nowhere. The trough kept its skin, a grey membrane drawn tight from edge to edge, and when a pail knocked it the skin broke and closed again and was called weather. On the flat stone by the well, out of the path of feet, Sella had set a plate of ice clouded with silt, lifted off the trough with the back of a broken pot and carried three paces and set down where no foot went, and the plate stood there clouded and going nowhere and was called weather. Three coverings, and a finger between every pair: the frost that had gone without a payer, the skin on the water, a piece of the depth lifted and set where nobody would step. No fourth likeness was made a cause and no figure came off any of them. The boy kept the three as three acts of three different hands and would not let them stand up as one hand’s habit, because a habit wants a payer and none stood to any of them.

At the rack end the dressed grain lay under wool away from the hearth, and Sella turned it with a progressively lighter touch — a corner lifted, a handful let fall back, the wool drawn over again — and gave it no figure. It had been read over on Bone 10 by a number that did not know what it cost; the number had gone up the clay road in a locked box and had not come back as a lesson or a mending, and the grain lay as it lay, changed in state and uncounted. Repair is not restoration and a count is not a cost, but she did not say either, because a saying would have wanted somebody standing under it to be asked.

The afternoon’s hunts and repairs went on as directions judged by the carrying arm and the weather, and never as figures. A man came down with a cracked jar in a sling of rope and asked what the mending of Bone would want, and a woman at the well told him to ask the pot, and the pot, being a pot, said nothing, and the man went off with his broken jar and no entry standing behind him. Another, shifting a burden from one collarbone to the other, said the cost was the weather — it had carried the snow, it could carry the price — and went off toward the clay road with nobody weighing what he carried and nobody opening a slate. The boy watched the shoulders rather than the burden, because the shoulders told the truth and the burden was only what the man chose to show; both tellings arrived nowhere and neither was entered.

Tarn worked at the kindling with the small knife lying at the very edge of its sill, where a house could check it and where a man’s hand could also slip it off. He had put it there before the light and had not announced it, because the yard had already been told and a man does not say the same thing twice to a house that does not answer. Six years stood in the worn thumb-mark where the house could check, and the seventh stood at the lip beyond the mark where the house could not, and beside the seventh lay Garl Vail’s unforged collector sentence — the one about a man who had turned back at a frozen ford — with the loan uncalled and no collector come up the clay road or gone down it.

Six counted. The next I don’t count, he told the kindling, in the tone of a man stating the weather. He set the knife back where it could be checked and did not look toward the house.

The ear-sentence came up again as he reached for the wood: the sentence about an ear under this roof being worth more than the debt that held it. It came all the way to the word that would call the loan and stopped there, with no figure and no name on the man who had turned back. He drew the breath that should have gone in at the top of the reach and did not spend it; the small interval had stood at the top of every reach since Seed 16, and his chest had been tight since the dark. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line at the corner, and no scrubbing lifted it. The pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what little light there was under the shed edge and gave it back, and he held the wrist up a moment to the grey as though to see whether the light had come to anything. A ninth stopping, remembered, with no tenth before the seventh year at its very end and still ungiven; the loan that could hear was worth more than the debt that held him and was unsafe for that, and he said neither sentence to any man.

The boy, carried past at the edge of the work, took the stopping apart as he takes apart a reaching — by what was there and by what was not — and filed it as an object on its own place, an interval and not a payment, a ninth stopping and not a tenth, with no payer standing under it and no rule standing up out of nine. He did not look toward Tarn’s wrist and Tarn did not look toward the basket. Two ears kept their equality under one yard, a loan that could hear and had never been called, and the two silences were of one shape with nobody putting a word between them.
