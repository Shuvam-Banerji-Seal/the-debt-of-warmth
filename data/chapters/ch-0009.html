The River Under the Ice

Days 31–37 · Ilmen 2–8, Y.H. 813 · The Clay Yard, Low Kiln, Sill

The ice goes soft from underneath and nobody says the word for it. A woman coughs into her hand and tells the yard it is weather. The river takes what was left in the corner, and the count goes on as though nothing had been taken.

— A saying of the Low Kiln yards, Sill

— Day 31 —

Ilmen 2, Y.H. 813 (Secondday)

The bucket went down on the end of the rope before there was light enough to see the water take it. The woman at the yard well let it down as a woman lets down a thing she has let down ten thousand times, arm over arm, the rope running through her palm with her eyes on the dark under the roof, and the water took the bucket with a small hollow suck. Frost lay on the stone lip in a rim as white as the glaze inside a cracked cup. Above them the little roof of the well kept the last of the dark under it, and cold came off the stones in a breath that had weight and the taste of iron in it. It was the hour when the yard had not yet decided to be awake: a door going somewhere at the far end, a cough, the small sounds of a woman reaching for what she had set down the night before.

A man stood a little apart with his boots still wet from the bank below the sill. He had come up from the Ilm and had not gone home to change, and the river was in the creases of his face and in the way he kept his hands to himself, as though he had carried something all night and was not yet rid of the feel of it. He watched the bucket go down.

“Tastes different,” the woman said.

She said it to the water, or to the ground, or to nobody; it was not a sentence that wanted an answer. She drew the bucket up hand over hand and set it on the lip, and a little of the water slopped over and ran dark down the stone, and she tasted a finger and made a small sound with her mouth that was not a word.

“It’s the ice,” the man said. “Honeyed under the mud. That’s what it is.”

He said it the way a man says a thing he has already said twice that morning and expects to say again before the day is out. The woman did not ask him what he meant. Nobody at the well asked him what he meant. They took the word honeyed and passed it around the way a cup is passed, and went on with the drawing and the carrying and the waiting of turns. A girl ahead of them shifted a full bucket from one hand to the other and set it down again and said nothing at all.

Corin lay in the basket at Sella Vail’s hip with the fold tucked under his chin and listened to a thing the whole of Low Kiln could taste and not one person in it would name. He was thirty-one days old. He had no word for the change either, and no way to put a word down if he had one, and so he kept the shape of it instead: the small sour note spoken over the bucket, the man’s honeyed, the way the sentence went around the well and came back without anybody claiming it. In Grathic there were words for weather and words for water and a whole district’s stock of words for the cold, and none of them had been laid on this. A thing everybody could hear in the water had no name anybody would give it. He had known rooms where a thing nobody would name was given a number and written at the top of a page in a clean hand, and the room went on afterward as though the writing had settled it. His head went where it wanted and made its small wet sound and came back; the thread at his mouth froze a little in the small hours and nobody wiped it. Cold arrived in its known order, cheek and temple and wrist. He kept the shape of the unnamed thing because keeping a shape was the only work available to him.

❦

The small signs had come in a row all week and the yard had put a name on none of them. At the Vail kiln-house the oiled paper over the little window had lifted hard through the deep cold, snapping back when the draught took it, and this morning it lifted less hard, sagging in the frame as though the hand that had held it stiff had opened a little in the night. Tarn noticed it before he noticed the light. He had been out before first light, as he was out every morning, with Ash worked into the creases of his knuckles that no scrubbing lifted, and his broom went over the boards in the direction of the slip pits as though sweeping toward a thing would put it off. Six years of the seven stood counted; the seventh he held back, the one entry in this house the house could not check, and he said nothing about it to the paper or to the boards or to anybody.

Behind the drying racks the Slip Brook had lost its stiff skin. It moved now under a thin grey film that broke and closed again around the stones, and the sound of it had changed with the skin: less like a thing tapped, more like a thing breathing through a clog. He had heard the brook from his bed under the roof for thirty-one days and had filed its stiff note with the other things the house kept without entering. This morning it had gone and something quieter had come in its place, and nobody had said so. The grey film went along under the bank and did not hold, and the clay of the pits came down in it now, a thread of the same colour as the forearms of the boy at the wheel.

Down the Clay Yard a woman said it was a warm spell. A man said it was the weather. By the time the bucket went around a second time the warm spell had become the weather in everybody’s mouth, the word that lets a person go home without owning a day of it. The boy had learned the shape of that word in a house where it was used to close a shelf and name a cough and set a ridge on fire. Where the house could not measure a thing it called it weather or mercy, and the accounts came out even and everybody went on with their work. He set the taste at the well and the sagging paper and the moving film each on its own place and did not make a cause of them, because a likeness laid on a likeness will grow a cause whether or not anybody puts one there, and he had made that mistake once in a room with better light and carried it for the rest of a life.

Jorra Vail came up from the Vail wheel-shed with slip dried the colour of old bone on both forearms and the bent little finger of his left hand leading the way to the damper, which he moved before Sella had finished telling him the fire was low. To a pot going past on a board he said, “You’ll do,” in the flat voice he kept for pots, and did not look toward the basket. He agreed with nobody about the thaw and contradicted nobody; his silence went around the yard as its own kind of weather signal, and the yard straightened around it without an order. Tarn agreed with everything so thoroughly that there was nowhere for an argument to go — with the fire, with the ground, with the neighbour who said the thaw would be early and the one who said it would be late. He agreed with both and swept on, and wanted the late one because a late thaw puts off the paying, and had not once that morning said the word for what was coming.

❦

Sella drew her own bucket when her turn came. She did not hand the basket to anybody or set it down where the rope might swing over it; she kept it at her hip with the fold at his chin and drew with her free hand, the little-door burn on the back of her hand closing on the rope and then on nothing as she changed her grip. The burn had the shape of a door, two straight sides and a lintel and a threshold, and it shut when the hand had nothing to hold. He had watched it close that way a hundred times and it still told him nothing except that the hand had let go.

“That’ll do,” she said to the bucket, not to the woman who had let hers down. And to the wool at his ear, “Keep it off him. It’s the river wind.” She said the house where another woman would have said I, and did not say where the warmth in the household dish had come from or who had paid for it. The instruction went to the fire and the wool and the bucket and never to the basket, and the basket was where he was, and so the keeping went on without an entry, as it had for thirty-one days. She did not wipe his mouth. She did not look into the fold.

When she carried the full pail back toward the kiln-house the boy saw, past her shoulder, the peg behind the door where Garl Vail’s coat kept its salt shape, and the hollow in the wheel-seat worn by twenty years of a man’s leaning, and the place under the skirting board where the grey ridge had turned its corner for a year and two days and was now a clean dark line. The ridge had been burned in the morning of the day before and called dealt with by a neighbour with a pail of water by her knee, and Sella had stood over it with a broom and said nothing. The proverb went to the pot as she passed: leave the crack showing; people trust a thing they can see has been handled. The crack had been showing all year in a grey line along the wall, and now the line was gone and the proverb went on being said to the pot, as though a thing left in plain sight could not be made to disappear by a woman with a pail of water. The seat stayed hollow. Nobody filled it. The coat kept the shape of a broader man, and the debt against the firing of tiles lay where it had lain through another turning of the year, neither paid nor settled nor done with.

❦

Mira Silt came up from the eels halfway through the morning with pale slime dried on her sleeves and river silt in the hollow of her neck, her head held a little to the right against the cold where the top of her left ear had been. The smile was on her face in the way she kept it, on the latch, shut without anybody seeing the movement. She had come up the yard in a hurry and with nowhere to be, which was how she came up the yard, and the slime on her sleeves had dried pale in the cold before she reached the well. A woman at the well asked her whether the eels ran early this year, and Mira answered with a question.

“Do they run early? They run when they run. Ask the river. It’ll tell you the same as it tells me.”

She flicked an eel-bone against her thumb and put it away. The woman at the well said a figure for something — the levy, or the feast, or the price of eels by the pair — and the boy felt rather than saw Mira’s thumb go under her apron to the grey wool cord at her waist and stay there while she decided which answer the woman would get. She shifted the basket’s weight from one hip to the other as she passed, and under the grey cord the second cord showed twisted twice beneath it, a short length of wool with knots in it that had never been taken upriver to Nine Pots and never lifted at a door. The thumb went always to the first cord. The knots on neither were counted, and she did not look into the basket.

“Early or late,” she said, “it’s weather. I’ve eels to sell.”

The boy kept the two cords on the tray where he kept the things no slate could enter, and kept the figure that had gone past on its own, and did not let them touch. He had learned that a question answered with a question was a door with a latch on it, and that the hand under the apron was the part of the sentence the woman at the well would never hear, and that the second cord was a record no Court’s thumb would think to take up. He had no word in the district’s tongue for any of it and no hand to lift a finger toward the cord. She went back down the yard the way she had come, brisk, with the smile still on the latch and nothing said about knowing him.

❦

He was thirty-one days old and a month old tomorrow. Nothing in the yard marked the day, and tomorrow would not be marked either; the district had entered a year on its slates that did not contain him, and one in twelve stood printed where it had stood last year and nobody challenged it. The month turned under a fold he could not lift. In the fold at his chin the two crusts lay where they had been put. One had been stiff since Stone 15 and gone past smelling of anything; the other still gave a thin breath of barley smoke when the wind turned off the yard, though the wind had turned a hundred times since the smoke left it. Neither had been eaten, lifted or named, and no third had come in six days. Bread there was in the house, and an oven that had been hot five days ago, and the crusts lay where they lay.

The bucket cleared the lip and the full weight came onto the woman’s arm. He watched the whole of the draw the way he had once watched the give of a body under his hands in a room with better light — the set of the shoulder, the angle of the forearm, the rope shortening hand over hand, and then the small dip and recovery of the full bucket as it came over the stone and the water slopped down it. It was a clean piece of mechanics, head and rope and the give of the full thing, and he could have read it in the dark and said what it would do next. The sour smell of the water came to him with the iron in it, and the cold off the stone went up his sleeve.

He could hear it changing. That was the exact and useless thing: the ear that had held the full note five times from four sources and kept the count without a sixth was taking in a change in the water that nobody in the yard would put into words, and he had no word either, and his hand was the size of a fold. The surgeon in him read the draw and the weight and the give. The infant in him wanted the breast and wanted the crust even cold and got neither, and the blanket would not stay on, and the wet thread at his mouth was not wiped. He was the only adult at the well and the least use in it.

The bucket came up cold over the well’s stone lip, the water tasting different to everybody who watched it come and to nobody who would say the word for it, and the boy’s hand outside the wool went up an inch toward it and came down.

— Day 32 —

Ilmen 3, Y.H. 813 (Thirdday)

Before light, the Vail kiln-house was a room of edges. The bench had an edge, the sill had an edge, and the little knife lay along the sill with its own edge turned inward the way a man turns his face to the wall when he is not yet awake. Tarn had set it there before anybody stirred, and now he stood over it in the half-dark with Ash worked into the creases of his knuckles and said to the Clay Yard what nobody had asked him to say.

“I put it back. You can check.”

The yard gave nothing back. The oiled paper at the little window sagged in its frame, lifting less hard than it had a week ago, and a neighbour’s door went somewhere at the far end. Tarn waited a beat, as though the yard might answer, then took up his broom. It was the same sentence he had said every morning since the year turned, and he would say it again tomorrow. Six years of the seven stood counted. The seventh he held back, the one entry in this house the house could not check.

The boy could see the pale ring at the man’s left wrist where an iron clip had sat six years ago, and the bend in the shoulders that six years in the slip pit had put there, and the way the smallest tool went into a pocket when Tarn thought nobody was looking. He read the body the way he had once read a chart left face-down on a desk: the shape of a life entered against a house. A bed under the roof for the foundling and a loft above the wheel for the debt; the household had performed a triage on its own members and expected nobody to notice, and Tarn had said the thing once in one sentence and had never said it again.

Corin lay in the basket at the edge of the bench with the fold tucked under his chin and heard the sentence go out and come back with no answer on it. He was a month old today. Nothing under the roof marked it. No loaf had been set apart, no number said, no hand laid on the little board where a household might have written the day; the district had entered a year on its slates that did not contain him and would go on not containing him through all twelve months of it. A month was a thing that happened to a body. It was not a thing that happened to an account.

Tarn agreed with everything so thoroughly that there was nowhere for an argument to put its foot.

“Fire’s low,” Sella Vail said from the bench.

“It’s low,” Tarn said, and put another turfcake to it before the sentence had settled.

Jorra Vail came in from the Vail wheel-shed with slip dried the colour of old bone up both forearms, the bent little finger of his left hand going in ahead of his hand at the damper, which he moved before she had finished. “That wants opening,” he said to the damper.

“It wants opening,” Tarn said to the fire, and swept on.

The ground wanted sweeping toward the slip pits and he agreed with the ground. He did not contradict the neighbour who said the thaw would be early and did not contradict the one who said it would be late; he agreed with both and swept on, and wanted the late one because a late thaw puts off the paying. An argument needs a place to stand, and Tarn had removed his. The boy had known a man who agreed like that, long ago, in a room with better light and a file in his voice; the man had never raised it and the room had straightened around him all the same. The memory came in the ordinary past and went out again without a name put to it, and the boy kept it on the tray where he kept the other shapes of silence.

❦

The broom went over the boards in long strokes that started at the door and ended at the bench. Tarn swept in the direction of the pits, as though sweeping toward a thing would put it off. At the place by the bench where a pot had once been turned toward and given nothing, the broom checked half a stroke. It went on. His head did not turn and he did not look toward the basket and he did not call the place by any name.

The boy’s hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the passing broom and came down. He could not have touched it; the broom was two paces off and a month’s reach is shorter than that. He kept the half-check of the stroke on the same tray as the interval it answered, and kept Tarn’s not-turning beside his own not-turning, and did not let the two touch. Two silences under one roof kept their equality, and neither man had a word for it, and the house went on turning as though two people holding the same sound were an ordinary thing. The ear that had held the full note five times from four sources had taken in nothing this morning that it could add to the count, and the count stayed five. He had learned that a pause was not a hearing and a gap was not a hearing and that a thing which did not happen still left a shape, and he kept the shapes because keeping a shape was the only work available to a hand the size of a fold.

❦

Sella came to him with the little dish in one hand. It was the house’s own dish, the one that went from the fire to the bench and back again and was never set before a neighbour, and she fed him barley gruel thinned with milk from it, the spoon wiped on her sleeve before it went to his mouth and wiped again on the same sleeve when it came away, and two fingers behind his ear held his head where it wanted to go. His head wanted to go everywhere at once, and the two fingers kept it in the world of the dish. He swallowed. The gruel was warm in a way that had been arranged somewhere he could not see, and the warmth had a payer, and the payer was not named under this roof. The pressure of the two fingers was exact: not the clumsy cradle of a girl who had never held a small skull, but the pressure of a hand that had held a child through a second chill and knew to the ounce how much a thing this small would bear before it turned its face away. He knew that pressure from the other side of a table, in a room with better light, and here he was under it.

“The house feeds you,” she said, which was the sentence she used where another woman would have said I. To the fire she said, “Keep it off his ears. It’s the river wind.” To the wool at his ear she gave the same instruction in the same flat voice, as though the wool and the fire were the two things in the room that could be told what to do. The instruction never went to the basket, and the basket was where he was.

Her little-door burn closed on the rim of the dish and then on nothing as she lifted the spoon. It had two straight sides and a lintel and a threshold, and it shut when the hand had nothing to hold. He watched it shut. He had watched it shut a hundred times and it told him nothing except that the hand had let go. She did not wipe his mouth. The drool at his chin froze a little in the small hours and nobody wiped that either; a basket set at the edge of a board was a pot set down and left. She put the fold right without looking at his face, and did not say where the milk had come from or who had paid for the warmth in the dish.

❦

When the light cleared the sill Tarn took the small knife from its sill and scraped at his knuckles with it. The mark on the handle was worn almost flat by six years of a thumb in the same place, and the knife brought up a grey the colour of the line along the skirting board where the ridge had turned its corner for a year and two days and was now a clean dark board. Ash worked into the creases that no scrubbing lifted; he had scrubbed at them with lye and with the rough edge of a broken pot and with the edge of this knife, and the grey came back the way a stain comes back in a board that has been planed. A neighbour with a pail of water had burned the ridge in the morning and called it dealt with, and Sella had stood over the place with a broom and said nothing, and Tarn had offered to check the width with a thumb as good as any and had taken his eyes off it. He wiped the knife on his thumb and set it back on the sill where it could be checked. The boy kept the grey of the knuckles and the clean dark board under one hand without putting one over the other, as he had kept the left thing and the burned thing the morning the grey went; a thing called gone is not the same as a thing gone, and smoke clings longer than the thing that made it.

He still wanted the late thaw. A late thaw was a longer week before the paying, and the paying was a year nearer now than it had been when the year turned, and the ice was going against him anyway. He said none of this. He said to the ground, “I’ll sweep it again,” and swept, and the boy heard the wanting in the broom the way he had once heard it in a man’s voice, and kept it on its own place with the other things the house had entered nowhere.

Garl Vail’s proverb went to the pot and the fire and never to the basket. Leave the crack showing; people trust a thing they can see has been handled. The crack had been showing all year in a grey line along the wall, and now the line was a clean dark board, and the proverb went on being said to the pot as though a thing left in plain sight could not be made to disappear by a woman with a pail of water. The salt-stiff coat kept its broader shape on its peg behind the door. The wheel-seat in the shed kept its hollow on the left, worn by twenty years of a man’s leaning, and nobody filled it. The debt against the firing of tiles lay where it had lain through another turning of the year, neither paid nor settled nor done with, and the house went around the word for it as Sella went around Ash in a corner.

❦

At the bank she turned the coals with the back of a broken pot. She did not touch the widow’s mark at her throat, because a bank is not an opening; the mark was for the lid and for nothing else, and a reading was not an opening and a dusk was not an opening and a month turning was not an opening either. The hand went to the fire and not to the clay at her throat. The carried fire that had come up the yard on Kindling five days ago was still in the house under blind ash and the back of the broken pot, warming the boards by degrees and missing one breath in every four. Four, four and a gap. The boy had counted it since the coal came into the room and had added nothing to it and taken nothing away. He set the missing breath on the tray beside the empty shelf where a fifth of the firelight would stand and beside the bell told in the dark, and kept the three in three places, because a likeness laid on a likeness will grow a cause whether or not anybody puts one there, and he had made that mistake once in a better-lit room and carried it for the rest of a life.

They set him on his bed under the Vail roof at the turn of the dark, one month old and unentered, with the two crusts in the fold where they had been put and no third come in seven days. The small knife was back on its sill. The fire behind him took its breath in fours and missed one. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the broom leaning by the wall and came down, and the count went on into a year the district had entered and had not entered him, and the two silences under one roof kept their equality without a word passing between them.

— Day 33 —

Ilmen 4, Y.H. 813 (Fourthday)

The the Vail wheel-shed smelled of wet clay and tallow and, when the wind came wrong off High Kiln, the sweetish edge of lead glaze. Corin was carried in under the fold before the light had properly reached the bench and set at the edge of the drying racks in the Clay Yard where the baskets went, a pot set down where a pot would not be slopped. He was thirty-three days old. The fold sat at his ear. Cold came in its known order — cheek, temple, wrist — and the wheel was turning already under hands he could not yet see.

Carried, he was a thing among things. The basket’s handle knocked against a hip and the fold slipped and his head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound it made when it went too far; two fingers came behind his ear without a face looking down and put the head back. He read the order of the shed the way he had once read the order of a field — where the light came from, where the cold collected, which way a body would fall — except that the field was a room and the body was his own and small. The wheel stood in the middle of it with its seat hollow on the left, and the shed was built around that hollow the way a wound is built around the thing that made it. He counted what he could, the wheel’s tread, the boards under the basket, the hands going to the damper, and let the rest stand as the household stood it, unentered.

He heard Jorra Vail before he saw him: not the step, which was quiet, but the small catch of breath a man makes when he goes out of a cold room into a warm one and decides not to notice the cold. Then Jorra was in the shed, the light behind him, grey dust worked into the creases of his forearms past the bend of the little finger.

It was not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel. Slip dried the colour of old bone sat on a thrower like a second skin and cracked at the knuckles when the hand opened; a boy could read the morning’s work in it the way he had once read a cast left out too long, by the lines the stuff had settled into. This was finer. It went into the creases and stayed, grey as the ridge the neighbour had burned and called dealt with, the colour of a thing that has been in the corner of a room for a year and is still in the room though the corner has been cleaned. Jorra said nothing about where he had been. Nobody asked him. The men whose names were not spoken had names — the boy knew this as he knew a chart has a name at the head of it even turned face-down — and knew that in this house a name left off a slate was the same as a room left off a roll: present by the shape of the absence, and kept the safer for it.

He kept the two states of the same forearms on two places in the private file, the forearms with grey dust in the creases and the forearms with clean bone-coloured slip. They were the same arms and the same morning and he filed them as two entries, because a face that refuses to report a thing still leaves the report on the sleeve. He wanted to know which slate had been broken. He wanted to know whether it had been a birth-slate. The wanting was exact and went nowhere; a question asked at thirty-three days comes out as a wet sound and is taken for hunger, and hunger is answered with the dish and not with the slate. So he kept the two questions where he kept the others, in the place questions are allowed to last for years, and went on watching the forearms he could not reach.

❦

“She’ll want a thinner slip,” Jorra said to a pot on the bench. He did not look toward the basket; he had not once looked toward it, not on any morning, and the not-looking was as regular as the damper. “The wheel doesn’t care whose hands. It cares if they’re steady.”

The wheel agreed, going over on its steady hands. Jorra put a palm to the pot to feel the shoulder of it, and the boy knew a thing nobody in the shed had told him and that Jorra would not have said if they had: the full note was in a Vail pot before it left the wheel. Jorra could hear it in the wet clay while it was still turning, the way a man reads the level of a measure by the sound it makes going into the pot. He would not say so before a kiln-master. A kiln-master would call it early and make something of it, and a thrower who heard a note before anybody tapped it was a useful thing to a house and a dangerous thing in a district. Jorra kept the knowing for the pots. The boy kept his knowing of the knowing where questions can last for years, which was nearly the whole of the room he had.

Once while the wheel turned the boy felt rather than heard a note come up through the boards into the basket — a pressure in the wicker under his back, the way a vessel’s note goes through a table before it goes through the air. He kept it as a pressure and not as a hearing. A sound felt through the floor was not a sound taken under a knuckle; the count was a count of hearings, and the count was five. He had learned to be exact about that difference in a room where being nearly right had once cost a life.

“That wants opening,” he said to the damper, and the bent little finger of his left hand went in ahead of his hand and moved it.

Sella Vail had got as far as “The fire’s —” before the damper moved. She let the rest of the sentence stand where it was, the way a woman sets a dish down and leaves it, and went to the fire with her own hand. “Feed it before you feed yourself,” she said to the fire. “It’ll still be there in the morning. You won’t.” It was Garl’s saying and she had improved it in the keeping, and she said it to the fire as she said all his sayings, never to a person and never to the basket. She did not take up the silence about the grey; she never had. The damper was the one thing in the shed that got moved before anybody finished a sentence, and the household had long ago stopped finishing them.

❦

Under the drying racks there was a space where whole slate should have stood. The boy could not see it from where he lay — the rack’s shadow went down to the floor a hand’s breadth past his reach and the space was under the far end of it — but he had learned the yard by the way work went around a thing, and work went around this. A woman setting a pot to dry shifted her weight and set it down two boards over. A man with a barrow of slip took the long way past the end of the rack rather than the short one through it. Nobody swept under there and nobody said why. The door off the shed stood a finger’s width open and nobody pushed it wider, and the open dark of it was another thing the yard went around, as a woman goes around Ash left in a corner. The space kept its shape the way a hollow in a wheel-seat keeps the shape of a man who has sat in it for twenty years.

He planted nothing. He had no hand to plant with and no slate to plant and no word he had heard for the thing that had stood there. But he set the shape of the space on the tray beside the grey dust and the two questions and left it there toward a day when the thing might be named, without naming it now, without a slate shown or a man in the back room described. A room could be present in a house only as dust on a sleeve. A broken thing could be present under a rack only as the shape of the work that went around it. He knew the difference between a likeness and a cause; he had made that mistake once in a better-lit room and carried it for the rest of a life, so he set the dust and the space side by side and did not let them touch.

❦

Tarn swept near the bench. He had come up from the Debt Loft with the broom and the small knife already back on its sill, and he had told the empty shed where it was, though nobody had asked him and nobody was there to check. Six years of the seven stood counted; the seventh he held back, the one entry in this house the house could not check. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles the colour of the clean dark line where the ridge had turned its corner and been called gone, and no scrubbing lifted it.

“Yes, house,” he said to the ground, though no house had spoken. “I’ll sweep it. I swept it this morning. Ilmen 6, Y.H. 813 (Sixthday)

The yard had come awake by the time the basket was set down, which was a different thing from being awake in it. Bristles moved on boards somewhere down the racks and a woman worked with her hands full, and the morning lifted the grey off the poles strip by strip and laid light along the ground where the pots stood in rows. He had been carried out under the fold and set at the edge of the drying racks in the Clay Yard where the baskets went when the women wanted their hands for other things, a pot set down where a pot would not be slopped. The fold sat at his ear and the two crusts lay where they had been put, one gone past smelling of anything and one that gave a thin breath of barley smoke when the wind turned. Cold came in its known order — cheek, temple, wrist — and he had stopped expecting anybody to do anything about it.

A woman at the racks lifted a pot off the board and set it down again a hand’s breadth to the left, to get it out of the way of her own shadow. It was a common motion, made ten thousand times in this yard and in every yard along the row. She did not knock it. She did not listen to it. “That slip’s thick at the shoulder,” she said to the pole she was working, and pushed her sleeve higher up her arm, and went on with what she had been doing. A stripe of wet clay lay across one forearm the colour of old bone.

The pot answered her.

It answered under nobody’s knuckle, while her hands were already away and her attention back at her work. The answer came up out of the wet clay of its own accord, as a pot will answer when it is set down on a board that knows it — a small sound in the throat of it, round and going toward full. Almost full. A thumb’s breadth of air still in it. It was not the flat answer of a cracked vessel, that dead knock a coin makes on wet boards, heard five times from four sources and filed without a sixth. It was not the silence of pots turned toward on a bench that gave nothing and left the space where a note should stand empty as a door opened into a cold room.

It was a third sound. He lay with his ear under the fold and listened to it the way a man listens in the dark to a thing he has been waiting thirty-five days to hear and did not know he was waiting for.

He knew the difference between nearly and full before he knew that he knew it, the way the body gets there first and the mind comes along afterward to write it down. Full closed; the note went all the way down and stopped, with nothing left over. Nearly left air under the note, a looseness just below the rim where the sound had not settled. It was a note with a want in it. He had no word in Grathic for the want, no word for nearly, and no hand to put on the pot to ask it to say the thing again. He could not have lifted the fold, could not have turned his head toward the woman and said set it down once more. He was thirty-five days old, and the ear that would have been a gift in another house was a thing that merely happened to him here, in a yard where nobody had asked it to hear and nobody would have known what to do with the answer if it had.

He had reduced a thousand shoulders and here could not lift a hand an inch to tap a pot giving him the first answer anyone had given him in thirty-five days. Ilmen 7, Y.H. 813 (Seventhday)

Ilmen 8, Y.H. 813 (Eighthday)

Sella Vail carried him three doors down the Clay Yard in the grey before the smoke had cleared from the morning fires, with the basket at her hip and the fold tucked at his chin, and set him in the shade of the doorway at the end of the nineteen steps below House Silt. It was a pot’s place, level and out of the draught, chosen with her knee and not her eye; she had set him in a hundred such places in thirty-seven days and had never once asked him where he would rather be. Corin was thirty-seven days old and saw the doorway from the low angle of a thing that cannot stand, which is the angle at which doorways are largest. The oiled paper at the Vail window had lifted less hard every morning of this thaw, and the cold came at him in its known order — cheek, temple, wrist — and the little-door burn on the back of her hand closed on the handle and shut on nothing as she set him down.

“Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. “It’s the river wind.” She did not say it to the basket and she did not look into the shade.

The nineteen steps ran from the slip pits up to the rack, and the yard went up and down them all morning with wet slip on its boots and no figure in its mouth. A girl came past with a barrow of grey and did not look at the basket; a woman at the tubs straightened and went back to her work without appearing to have seen it. This was the courtesy of the district, not to see what had been carried past, and the boy had learned to read it as a form of attendance: a whole yard standing aside so that one house could keep one child without a speech. Thirty-seven days of it had built a wall no slate could enter and no collector could take up.

At the end of the nineteen steps Dessa Silt stood with her shoulder against the jamb, as though the jamb were the only thing holding her up. Wet clay had dried at her hem in the shape of a hanging petticoat, and her nails were the colour of slip mixed too thin. The cough came up out of the pits in a low short way, shorter than a cough should be and lower, and she put her hand over her mouth and kept it there while the sound finished with nothing after it. When it passed she took the hand away and said to the ground, “It’s the pits. Comes up in the cold. Weather.”

It was worse than it had been on Stone 28, and she gave it the same name. That was the whole talent of this district: to put an old word on a new thing and go home warm.

Because a truth had come near, she got specific. “Nineteen out and nineteen back,” she said to the ground. “Four loaves on the seventh of Stone in eight hundred and twelve. Three to the levy and one eaten with my feet up. Bits to the woman who came with a basket.” She laid the figures out the way a woman lays nails on a bench, small and separate, so that nothing larger can be fitted onto the bench. Her thumb went once over the pale sieve-wire scar that ran up the inside of her left forearm, and the door-shut smile came down over her face and shut. “A body can be warm and still be gone,” she said. “You learn that in the pits.”

He had been carried past this doorway often enough to know the shape of her standing, and he read it now with the part of him that had once stood on the far side of a room and decided which bodies were worth opening. The hand over the mouth had gone down too late. The shoulder kept more of her weight than one shoulder should have to keep. Under the smile, at the throat, something beat faster than the rest of her admitted to. Her hands were small and quick and never clean, and the grey had gone into the nails the way the years had gone into the door-shut smile. Severe cold put a look on a face that passed for composure; he had seen it in a bay with the lights too bright and had learned not to trust it. He had once watched a man come off a cold street into a warm room and grow worse in the ten minutes after while everybody said the man was settling. He had learned to distrust the word settling. It was what people said when they wanted the room to go on being warm. He knew how a vessel went thin — not by breaking, but by handing its work to the next thing and the next, until the skin held more than it had ever been meant to hold and the face went on arranging itself in the ordinary way. In the language he had lost there were words for it, and a row of them would have gone into a chart in a room with better light, and there was no chart here and no hand to write one. He had no word in Grathic. He could not name what he saw in her face. He kept the looking, which was the only part of it he could keep.

The nineteen steps went out of her feet and into her thumb. She counted the nineteenth into the thumb again as though counting a thing already counted, because the count kept a larger thing out of the room, and the larger thing was the reason she had come to the doorway and could not say why she had come. He had a count of his own that kept objects on separate boards — four, four and a gap in the kiln, four, four and a landing at the Fishstairs, five hearings of the full note from four sources and no sixth — and he knew without being told that the two counts were doing the same work: a row of small exact things laid across the mouth of something too large to look at. He did not let the knowledge turn into a likeness with a cause in it. He had put three things into one sentence once in a room with better light, and the rest of a life had been the cost.

She did not look into the basket going past her shade. She believed him spent among children she had never seen, and the belief had the weight of a fact because she had laid it down so often; a thing laid down every morning became the floor. She did not say the number and she did not say the name. The number lay in her thumb and the name under the scar, and neither came up, and the boy was carried through the shade with the drool freezing at his mouth and nobody wiping it, because a basket set down was a pot set down and left.
