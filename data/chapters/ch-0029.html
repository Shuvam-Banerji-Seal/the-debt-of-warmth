The Word Stopped Twice

Days 126–128 · Reed 7–9, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, the Weir and Sill

A cord tied twice is one knot. A word stopped twice is one debt. Look again all you like; nothing has been paid.

— A saying of the Weir, Low Kiln

— Day 126 —

Reed 7, Seventhday, Y.H. 813

The reeds had a row at last, standing in the ruts where the barley had stood a month before, and the yard at Low Kiln took them in the way a yard takes in anything that has been carried a long way: with the eyes, with the arm, and never with a figure. The stalks went down green at the broken ends and came up pale where the frost had bitten the tips, so that every armful held two colours of the same month, the cut and the cold, and a man judging a bundle judged it by the weight settling into his own forearm and by nothing that could be set on a slate. Sella Vail carried the boy across the mouth of the Clay Yard at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, and gave the instruction to the wool and the river wind and never to the basket, as she had given it on the day the reeds went in and would give it on the day they were cut and gone. A day’s cutting was still a going-downriver at first grey and a coming-back at the going of the light, and the going and the coming had a shape and an hour and no cost that anybody had entered.

Cold came at the boy in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist. The wool rode back from the small bare joint so the cold found the place it had come for and went under the fold at his ear, and the thread at his mouth had frozen white in the small hours and nobody under any roof had been given an instruction about the thread, so it stayed where it had been put. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and nobody had been given an instruction about that sound either. River silt dried in the boot prints around the well, pale in the hollows where the water had stood, and the clay dust of the ruts came up underfoot and settled on everything the yard held, on the racks, on the pots, on the backs of the women’s hands, as if the whole district had been handled and set down wet.

He was one hundred and twenty-six days old, four months and six days, and nobody said so. The number stood in him exact as a set of instruments laid out on a towel, and the surgeon who had once read a body by what it would not hide now read an armful of reed by the same law: the arm that carried a bundle recorded what had been carried, not what it had cost, and a record kept in muscle is the hardest kind of account to enter, because it leaves no writing for a Court thumb to find. He laid the going-downriver and the coming-back on a place of his own, and did not lay a likeness across the shelf’s two gaps in the next room, the one stiff crust in the fold at his ear, or the empty place beside it.

A cutter come up from the beds with a bundle on his shoulder and set it down at the rack end for the women to sort, wiping his palms down his thighs as he straightened. A girl with a creel asked him what a bundle held, and he said, “Ask the arm that carried it. It’ll tell you,” and lifted the next armful up before she could get the second question out. The girl went off with the creel on her hip, and the boy set the saying on the tray beside the others, because it was the same answer in another mouth: a record a man could feel settling into his forearm and could not enter anywhere. The rack took the weight and gave back nothing that could be written down, and the women went on with the sorting.

A woman come up from the worked strips with dry mud worked into the creases of her wrists stopped at the basket’s edge and asked what the reeds would cost. She put the question to nobody in particular, the way a woman puts a pot down where there is room for it, and the hands she asked it with were the hands that had cut the stalks and carried them in, so the question had a direction even where it had no figure.

Sella’s glance went once toward the house wall, where the clay jar stood on its shelf cold under its glaze, and came back.

“Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there,” she said. “The house has said it.”

The woman stood a moment with the answer arriving nowhere, then took it up as she would take up a pot by the handle, level, and went off toward the racks with the unanswered thing held steady so that nothing slopped. It was the same care she would have used with a full dish, and the boy, watching from under the fold, set the care on one place and the question on another, because the two things were not the same and no word had joined them.

Before she went the woman lifted two fingers out of a fist and shut them again without showing what they had held, the shape a woman makes when she has a number and thinks better of giving it. Sella did not look at the fist. The boy watched the fingers go back into the hand and set the small shutting beside the number come up out of a man’s fist and put back in his sleeve, two holdings of the same kind in two places that did not touch, and wrote no cause across them.

❦

A man come up from the sluice with his sleeves wet to the elbow asked what the beds would want before the week was out. He asked it as a man asks the time: not for an answer he could carry, but to have said the thing that was on his mind. The water had been in the ruts since the flood went, and the beds lay east of the yard in the direction of the river, and the direction was all the figure the district had ever given him.

“Ask the beds,” Sella said. “They’ll tell you when they’ve had enough. The house has said it.”

“And if they tell me weather?”

“Then it’s weather.” She gave the instruction to the wool and the river wind and not to the man, and went on holding the basket level. “It’ll come when it comes. It always does.”

He went off satisfied with the weather and the river in place of a number, wiping his wet forearms on his thighs as he went, and the boy watched the shape of it: a cost asked in the form of a direction and answered with a direction, the two lines meeting nowhere and leaving no entry, an account kept in materials no Court thumb would read — mud on a wrist, water on a sleeve, the tilt of a basket carried level. He had seen a man bring a number up out of a fist and put it back in his sleeve rather than give it; here the number was never brought up at all, and the withholding was called the weather, and everybody went on with their work.

❦

The yard went around what it would not name. Sella went around the clean dark line where the Ash ridge had turned and the new finer grey hairline beside it that had been renamed the thaw after the fact, both unswept since the water went, both standing where they had stood; she went around the space beneath the lowest rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, the broken birth-slates lying where they lay under it, entered by nobody. Beyond the racks the worked strips held their own place: the place where a hand had gone in with the seed on Seed 15 and had not come out with the rest when the last hands came up on Seed 27. The yard went around it with a look and a wide step. The armfuls of reed passed it. There was no number anywhere in the returning line, no name, no cause, no accident, no body, no figure and no payment, and a slate would be found and a figure made if a hand came up in a month or a year, and nothing had come up.

She carried him at the half-pace from every open slate, gave the instruction to the wool and the river wind and never to the basket, and put her hand to the widow’s mark at no rack, strip, well or shed wall, because a strip, a rack, a shelf, a well, a yard and a basket are not openings and the mark is touched before an opening and at no other hour. The rule held as exactly in the morning light as it held at dusk under the roof.

❦

Outside at the edge of the yard Tarn took up the broom only after the broom had been given its instruction. Go around, it cost enough to come in, it will cost more to take out; the instruction went into the broom and the flagstones and never into the man, and he took up the tool as a man takes up a thing already named.

“Yes, house,” he said to a woman who wanted the thaw early, and “Yes, house,” to a man who wanted it late, agreeing so entirely that no argument could find a foot to stand on. He kept wanting the late, because a late thaw puts off the paying, though the paying had stood in the ruts since the water went and would stand there through the cutting and after it.

His seventh year was pressed past the third tend of the planting and still held. The small bench knife lay at the very edge of its sill with six years standing in a thumb-mark his thumb had worn almost flat and the seventh where the house could not check it, and the unforged collector sentence lay beside it where he kept it, neither entered. His chest had been tight since the dark; the breath he had decided not to spend on Seed 16 was still unspent, and there was a small interval at the top of each reach where it should have gone in, an empty place in the order of a thing a man does without thinking. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line and no scrubbing lifted it, and the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back. The near-saying stayed where it had been left under the roof, on the tray with the intervals as an object among objects, and he did not bring it to the word this morning; something must give at the end of the seventh year and had not given yet, and the loan that could hear was uncalled.

The boy watched the top of each reach where the broom went across the flagstones. The interval stood there, a breath’s width, the place where a man would draw in and did not; he had seen the same shape in his own chest at the market and behind the wheel-keeper’s standing finger in the yard, the half-beat of a check that came too late and the stroke that went on afterward without it. Three objects on one tray and not a rule; an interval was not a payment and a near-saying was not a hearing and neither was a seventh. He kept them apart by knowing what each one was, which was the only instrument he had ever had and the only one he had never been able to lay down, and his hand could not close two inches on the broom handle to save him.

❦

The armfuls went past the basket all morning, judged by the arms that carried them. The boy watched the forearms come down under the weight and the weight settle and the man nod once, and he read each one as a record kept in muscle that told what had been carried and not what it cost, exact, complete, worth nothing. He laid the going-around on a place of its own with the green ends and the pale tips and the mud in the wrists, and laid no likeness across the loaf shelf’s two unnamed gaps in the room he could not see from here, the one stiff brown-edged crust in the fold at his ear, or the empty place beside it. Two neighbours had once laid the old gap and the empty place under one cause in a single breath and gone off with their pots, and he had set their sayings on places of their own and made neither his own. No fourth likeness had made a cause. No finger of his had drawn the line.

Beyond the yard the river’s note of water over water went on at the edge of the hearing, a fifth low thing kept on a place of its own, never a hearing and never among the six; under another roof the carried fire took its coal and missed the fifth pull, its payer unnamed for a hundred and twenty-six days. Three counts in three places, and none became one.

Nobody in the yard said he was a hundred and twenty-six days old. His small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water beyond the yard and came down. The armfuls went past him judged by the arms that carried them, and one hundred and twenty-six stood where nobody had said it, exact, unspent and spoken by nobody, and the going of the light was still a long way off.

The going of the light was still a long way off when Sella Vail took the road down with the boy in the basket under the fold. She carried it level, as a woman carries a pot filled to the lip, and the instruction went to the wool and to the river wind and never to the basket: keep it off his ears, it’s the river wind, and the river wind had found the square already. She went at the half-pace from every open slate, around a board set out to dry, around the low table where a clerk’s hand lay flat on a sheet, going wide as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. The basket kept what it held. She did not look into it, and because she did not look into it the boy had the whole of the walk to look out.

Cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist. The wool rode back from the small bare joint and the cold found the place it had come for and went under the fold at his ear, and the thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody under any roof had been given an instruction about the thread, so it stayed where it had been put. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and the road went on under the sound the way a mill goes on over a stone turning. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the noise ahead and came down. Nothing within two paces could be reached.

The road kept the morning’s grammar. A man come up from the reed beds with a bundle on his shoulder let the weight settle into his forearm before he judged it, and the boy watched the nod come after the settling, the same record kept in muscle that the yard had kept all morning. It told what had been carried. It did not tell what it had cost, and the man did not turn it into a figure for the woman walking beside him who asked him something with her hands as they went. She took the answer in the shape of a shrug and went on. The boy laid the shrug on the place where he laid the morning’s directions, and did not lay a likeness across the shelf’s two gaps in the room he could not see from here or across the one stiff crust in the fold at his ear. No fourth likeness had made a cause. No finger of his had drawn the line.

At the north end of the Cross she set the basket down in the moot-post’s finger’s width of shade, the only shade at that end of the square, choosing the place with her knee the way she chose a place for a pot, setting it level and out of the draught and letting go only when the whole weight had committed itself to the ground. There was no slate within a hand’s reach of it and she had made sure of that before she bent. The cold iron of the post came through the wool into his cheek and stayed there, a cold with a shape, flat and without mercy, and he measured it against the warm side of his face as he had measured iron under other roofs. Her little-door burn shut on the wicker’s edge and then on nothing. Her hand did not go to the widow’s mark at her throat, because a reading was not an opening and a market was not a door and there was no opening anywhere in the square that morning. She stood a half-pace off with the burn shut, facing the square and not the basket, the stance of a woman who has set a pot down where it may be seen and will not be answerable for what is in it.

The stalls had come up along the north side in the ordinary way: reeds laid over eels in one long noisy row, with salt dried white on the boards and slime grey in the cracks between them, a brazier going sour under a pot of something, turnips heaped where figures had been left standing side by side without anybody making them agree. the bell went over the square, four, four and a gap, longer than any bell hung in a chapel, and nobody crossed himself at it. The boy counted the strokes and the place where the closing stroke should have stood, and kept this bell, heard under the post, in its own place beside the told bell of the Long Night. Two shapes with the same gap, and no hand to lay one over the other. A likeness laid on a likeness will grow a cause whether or not anybody puts one there; he had watched that done in a better-lit room, and had carried the correction into every room since.

Across the square a woman stood looking at pots.

She had set herself at the pot board where the glazed cups stood in a row, and she was not buying. She stood with her hands loose at her sides and her head a little down, turning nothing over until she had looked at it a long time. The sweetish green of lead glaze sat at one corner of her mouth, in the thin dry skin where a lip had been rubbed by a cup or by a thumb, and it did not move when she moved her jaw. Clay was worked under her nails. Across the back of one hand ran a pale line where something had once been worn tight and long enough to leave its mark. The boy took the green. He took the line and the clay and the stillness of her hands. He did not take them into one another.

She put out a hand and turned a cup a quarter on the board, not to show it to anybody and not to choose it, only to bring the light round to the place she wanted to see, and then set it back where it had stood. The cup went back with its handle to the same quarter of the sky. She did not lift it. She said nothing at all. A potter’s boy went past with an armful and neither gave ground to the other; the armful went between them and neither made anything of it. When the light went off the tops of the pots she went with it, one of two women going together, and the green went with her at the corner of her mouth and was gone.

He called the colour green and nothing else. It was the third time he had seen that particular green at a mouth. The first two had been given no name either: one under this same post at the last market of Silt, one at the last market of Seed; and there was a fourth green of the same colour at Berr Clay’s own mouth when Berr came up the clay road with his slate and the red cord of his office folded under his coat. Four shapes in four places. He set this one on a place of its own beside the other three and drew no line between them and wrote no name over them, because there were three of them in a row and a man who wants an answer will do the rest of the work himself if he is let. There was no word in the district’s speech that would turn the colour into a message, and no ear in this world to ask, and he kept it as a colour and not a message. The pale line on the woman’s hand went on its own place too. It was the shape a cord leaves and it was not joined to any cord he had seen on any board, or to eels, or to a door, or to anything that had been sold.

Beyond the post the road went down under the north bridge, and at the bottom the Fishstairs ran four steps, four, and a place where a step should have been, a hand’s breadth out of line since the Breaking and walked carefully ever since. The soft-cuffed clerk was set at that landing as he had been set every market since the month turned. His cuff was grey from washing and grey again by midday, the same grey as the silt on the boards. He held his slate to his forearm. His hand went to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls, every time, so that the reaching came before the looking and the looking never changed what the slate held. He did not speak. Nobody asked what he was set there for. A man selling reeds passed in front of him with a bundle on his shoulder; a woman with a basket of eels passed the other way and the slime from her creel left a trail across the boards that the clerk’s boot would take up when he moved, and he did not move. The market sold its reeds and its eels over the top of him as though a man with a slate were a thing every landing had.

The boy had seen a hand go to a slate before eyes went to a face in a birth room, at a Court table, across a square, and now here below a bridge. He kept the four orders on four places and did not make one clerk out of four men, because a use wants a payer and a payer wants a name and he had no name to give any of them. A man who washes his own cuff has a hunger or a habit; a man who holds a slate where nobody else can see the figure has a use. He set the hunger on one place and the use on another and joined neither to the green across the square.

A buyer in a coat with the river still in its hem stopped at the eel boards, put out two fingers and named a figure. The figure went into the air between them and the seller named a different one back, and the two stood side by side for a breath without agreeing, and then the buyer passed on with his figure still in his mouth and the eels still on the board. Neither figure was written down where the boy could see. He set the two on two places and did not make the smaller of them the price of anything. The seller wiped his thumb once on his apron after touching the eels. It was not Berr’s thumb and he did not file it with Berr’s: one man wiped twice before touching nothing and this man wiped once after touching fish, and the difference was the whole of it.

A girl with a creel came down the steps sideways at the out-of-line landing, not down onto it as onto a step but across it as across a gap, one hand out to the rail. The rail took her weight. She went on selling with the creel bumping her hip and did not look at her feet, and nobody behind her made a fall of it, and nobody ahead called it weather. The boy watched the place where her hand had gone to the rail and put it with the other careful goings he had kept: the yard going around the clean dark line, a cup set back a quarter where it had stood, the market going over a step that was not a step. Care was a kind of account. No Court thumb would read any of them.

At the edge of the shade Sella shifted her weight from one foot to the other and gave the end of her instruction to the river wind. She did not look into the basket and she did not look at the clerk. When a woman came near with a slate under her arm she took one half-pace back so that the slate and the basket did not touch, and said nothing about either, either to the woman or to the wind or to the house. The boy lay with the cold iron at his cheek and held the third green where it belonged, on its own place, with no cause written across it and no name on it and no line running from it to the pots or to the man with the slate or to the eel boards or to a box up the clay road. He had learned in one life and then in another what three likenesses in a row will do to a man who wants an answer, and the labour of the whole morning had been to refuse it, a labour nobody in the square could see and that would have been worth nothing to anybody who could. His ear had six hearings in it and no seventh, and the green was not a hearing and went on no hearing tray. The told bell stayed beside the heard bell. The cup stayed where the woman had set it back. The market did not know what he had kept. It went on selling reeds over eels and eels over a man with a slate, and the light went off the pots and the cups stood where they had been set, and nobody in the square turned a colour into a cause or a cause into a figure. That was the whole of the labour and nobody was the better for it. The clerk’s hand went to his slate once more before his eyes went to the stalls, and the market went on over him.

The woman went off with the light with the green still at the corner of her mouth, and the boy held the colour on its own place while the market closed over the man with the slate as though a man with a slate were a thing every landing had.

The market closed over the man with the slate and the light went off the last of the pots, and Sella Vail came back to the moot-post’s finger’s width of shade for the basket as a woman comes back for a pot she has set to cool. She put her burn-shut hand to the wicker and lifted it level, the instruction going to the wool and to the river wind and never to the basket: keep it off his ears, it’s the river wind, and the river wind had come all the way down with them and would go all the way back up. She went at the half-pace from every open slate, around the low table where a slate lay flat, around a board set out to dry, going wide as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. The basket kept what it held. She did not look into it, and because she did not look into it the boy had the going-home to look out.

Cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist. The wool rode back from the small bare joint and the cold found the place it had come for and went under the fold at his ear, and the thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody under any roof had been given an instruction about the thread, so it stayed where it had been put. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and the road went on under the sound the way a mill goes on over a stone turning. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the noise behind him and came down. Nothing within two paces could be reached.

He was one hundred and twenty-six days old, four months and six days, and nobody on the road said so. The armfuls went past in the going-down and had gone past in the coming-up, judged by the arms that carried them, a record kept in muscle that told what had been carried and not what it had cost. He laid the going-home on a place of his own beside the going-down and laid no likeness across the shelf’s two gaps in the room he could not see from here, or across the one stiff crust in the fold at his ear, or across the empty place beside it. A likeness laid on a likeness grows a cause unless a hand holds them apart, and his hand could not close two inches on the wicker, so he held them apart by knowing what each one was, which was the only instrument he had ever had and the only one he had never been able to lay down.

The yard received them as a yard receives a basket, with the going-around. She went around the clean dark line where Ash ridge had turned and the new finer grey beside it that had been named the thaw after the fact, both unswept since the water went, and she went around the space beneath the lowest rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, the broken birth-slates lying where they lay, entered by nobody. Beyond the racks the worked strips held their own empty place, the place where a hand had gone in with the seed and had not come out with the rest when the last hands came up on Seed 27; the yard went around it with a look and a wide step, and there was no number anywhere in the line. It had gone around the place every day since and put no figure to the going-around, and it would go around it on the morrow and the day after and call the going care. The boy set the going-around on a place of his own. He had read it for a month as a chart closed with a neat line over a note nobody had opened: exact, complete, worth nothing. In Low Kiln that evening nobody put a number to any of it.

Back under the Vail kiln-house roof she set him on his bed and not in the Debt Loft, the way a woman sets a pot down where no passing hip will find it. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without once looking at his face, and left the thread of drool frozen and unwiped, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left and nobody under this roof had been given an instruction about the thread. Her hand rested a half-beat longer than the setting needed before it went away, the way a hand rests on a thing it has carried a long way and cannot bring itself to let go of, and then it was gone and the burn on the back of it was shut on nothing. She did not look toward the bed. She had not said whose child he was in all his days and would not have known how to say it if she had wanted to.

In the next room the loaf shelf kept its days the way this house kept most of its days, badly, and in materials no Court thumb would read. Loaves pushed along the board, a glance at the clay jar standing cold under its glaze on the wall, and spaces left where no figure stood. She moved her lips along the row without a figure, her hand going past the old empty place at the row’s end and past the newer palm’s breadth that had come in on the night of Seed 6 with no scrape, crumb, print or word, and closing on a loaf beyond both. Two gaps; no third. The fold at his ear kept one stiff brown-edged crust and the empty place beside it, and that place was not the old gap and not the new one and was not laid across either by any finger of his. Two rooms that did not touch. When she had finished with the shelf she did not go to the widow’s mark at her throat, because a shelf and a loaf and a bed and a dusk are not openings, and the mark was touched before an opening and at no other hour.

At the fire she told it what it was. “It was the thaw.” The words went down into the coals as instructions went into the bucket and the broom and the pot, and the coals answered nothing. She turned them with the back of a broken pot, the broken edge lifting and settling, and fed them, and the house kept its warmth the way a cup keeps water with a crack in it nobody mentions.

“The house will keep it,” she said, where another woman would have said I.

Then she stood with the back of the broken pot in her hand and counted something under her breath toward no opening. The lips moved; the count arrived nowhere; there was no slate and no threshold for it to be entered at. In the yard beyond the wall Tarn had taken the broom only after she had given it its instruction, and had said Yes, house to an early thaw and a late and kept wanting the late because it put off the paying, and the small knife lay at the very edge of its sill with six years in a worn thumb-mark and the seventh where the house could not check it, and the breath from Seed 16 sat unspent in a man’s chest. None of it came into the room and none of it was asked about. A house straightens around a silence the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted, and this house had straightened twice in two days without anybody putting a word to either.

She pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carried the little-door burn, the skin inside the door puckering as the wrist turned, and the grey streak at her temple showed pale in the dying light off the coals. She had said once that it came in the winter Garl died and had not said it again. She set the pot where it could be found in the dark, rim out and handle toward the reaching hand, the way a woman sets a cup for a man who will come in late and not look where he puts his fingers. She did not look toward the bed, and went to her own bed, leaving the fire to keep what it kept and the door standing as it stood, a finger’s width open.

Through the finger’s-width gap came the river’s note of water over water, beyond the reed beds and the Weir and the long grey rows of the cutting, a fifth low thing kept on a place of its own since he was six days old, never a hearing and never among the six. It came nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, as it had come nearer every night since the flood went out of the yard, and he had never once mistaken it for a vessel’s note or given it a source it did not have. Through the floor under blind ash the carried fire took its coal and gave back four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for a hundred and twenty-six days. Above him the boards kept four nails, four, and a fifth that was a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts in three places, and none became one.

He turned out his file as a man lays instruments on a towel, in the dark, with the count standing at one hundred and twenty-six where one hundred and twenty-five had stood the night before. Every object was where he had left it. Six hearings of the full note stood beside and never among: five from four sources — a cup across a birth room, a pot through a wall, the district’s slate twice at the Cross, and a man giving at the Drawing — and the sixth, the small flat knock out of a woman as the men lifted, which came from a person and was filed beside the five and never counted with them. No seventh had come. He had not made one to fill the empty place. The bell that had gone over the square in the morning was a told bell, and it sat in its own place with the heard bell in another, and the two places did not touch.

Three near-full answers stood advanced and not paid: a cup across a birth room, a pot under a wheel-shed wall, a rack pot lifted a hand’s breadth too high and set down again. The boy took the shape of the hand in as a man takes the measure of an instrument laid on a towel before anyone names what it is for: a record kept in a material no thumb of the Court could read, exact, complete, worth nothing to anyone who wanted a figure. It was grey with the oil of a working thumb, the grey a stitch-hole takes after the thread has been pulled through it a dozen times, and he read the greyness as the record of a hand that had been there long before any figure was needed, and would be there after the figure was forgotten.

A weir woman with salt dried white at her wrists stopped at the stone with a basket on her hip. “How many went up yesterday?” she said.

The reed-cutter did not turn the cord out of his coat. “You can ask the cord.”

“I can’t read a cord.”

“No more can the Court.”

He lifted the bundle off the stone and went to the traps with it, and the question went down into the water with his arm. No figure came up. Nobody counted the knots; nobody gave the cord a name; and the hand that had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out on Seed 27 was not brought up out of the strip and set beside it, nor was the word forty spoken over the boards. The boy set the cord on a place of its own and did not join it to either.

Orra Weir stood at the lower boards with her board on its side and a cord at her waist, turning the board with the wrist that had turned it ten thousand times. Her hands were like old rope and the pale eel scars lay uncovered across both forearms; the grey streak showed first through the black hair when she moved her head. The nasal half-song she had never lost came into her voice where she was tired, and she gave a date where another woman would have given an opinion. She pulled one knot down with her thumb and set it among the others.

“What will the levy take now the hands are out of the reeds?” a woman at the rails asked, with her own answer already in her mouth.

“The ninth of Ilmen,” Orra said. “The collector’s boat came round the bend on the flood and had six oars. One man stood at the stern and never touched them.”

“That isn’t the levy.”

“It’s a date. The river takes what the Court doesn’t. You can shout at either one. I’ve tried.”

She went back to the knots. Her thumb went to the cord again as the sentence ended, and the boy could not tell whether that was a lie or only the habit of counting, and did not make a cause of it. A date given where a figure was asked was an answer with the question taken out of it, a chart closed over a note nobody had opened, and he set it where it belonged. A man who stands at the stern and keeps his hands off the oars is a man whose work is the counting of other men’s pulling, and the boy set that untouched hand on a place of its own, apart from the midwife’s reaching hand and the thane’s twice-wiped thumb, and wrote no cause across the three. She watched what there was to watch at the edge of the shade and got no answer from the basket; she did not ask and nobody asked her.

Her knot-string lay beside Mira Silt’s board lower down the shade and did not touch it. The last knot on the cord showed its shape to the boy as a thing showable and not spendable: a double turn, the tail tucked, tied left over right, the small extra weight of a thumb pressed in. He knew the shape and had no name for it in any tongue he had, and the word that would have named it stayed where it had stayed since the year of the flower, unspoken on a morning that had a great many figures in it and not one he could enter. Three accounts of the district stood on the boards in front of him, kept in wool and teeth and thumb, and the Court’s neat slate stood ready a league downriver for a measure nobody had called. He could see every one of them and enter none. The keeping was care and refusal in the same gesture, the double bite of the damper under a roof downriver, the iron that holds a vessel whole and the iron that takes the hand off a man.

Lower down the shade Mira worked the eels. The boy had watched her hands at this for more mornings than he could count and still could not tell, from the hand alone, which movement was the lie and which the truth; she made them the same movement on purpose. Pale slime dried in long grey strokes on her sleeves and river silt had dried pale in the hollow of her neck. She held her head a little to the right where the top of her left ear had been, and kept her small smile on her face the way another woman keeps a door on the latch: it could be shut without anybody noticing the movement.

“Will the run be thin now the hands are in the reeds?” a weir woman asked from the steps.

“Thin?” Mira said. “Who’s to say thin? It comes when it comes. Ask the river.”

She flicked a string of eel-bones against her thumb while she decided whether to lie, moved two from hand to hand, put one away and said something low about a boat that had come up on the flood, and nothing about the hand that had not come out of the strips. The decision was taken and the slate was not written, and the boy set the hand on its tray and made no cause of it.

A buyer in a coat with the river still in its hem came down the shade and named a figure for two pair. Mira’s thumb went under her apron to the grey wool cord and stayed there longer than the figure required, the way a hand stays on a vessel after the note has been heard, listening for the crack that does not come. She shifted a creel from one hip to the other, and the second cord twisted twice beneath the grey showed for a moment and went back under the apron. It had never been carried upriver to Nine Pots and had never been lifted at a door; neither knot on it had been counted by any thumb but her own, and the knot for the thin child upriver was untouched.

“Two marks the pair and two short,” she said. “You’ve said it twice and I’ve counted it twice. There’s nothing in the sentence that wants an answer.”

The buyer took the eels and went. She did not look into the basket at the end of the shade. Her not-looking was a keeping set on a place of its own, the opposite of a pricing face across a market, and the boy had no word in Grathic for either. Her board lay beside Orra’s knot-string and did not touch it; the string of eel-bones went back into her pocket, and whatever the grey wool held went back under the apron with no figure read aloud over it. The smile had stayed shut while the figure went past and while the hand went under the apron, and the boy, who had watched a face held like that in a room where a number was being read, understood that it was not a mask put on but a door kept held, and that a door held shut is a thing somebody must stand beside all day.

Below the bridge at the Fishstairs, on the out-of-line landing, the soft-cuffed clerk was still set for a measure nobody had called. His cuff was grey from washing and grey again by midday, and he held his slate to his forearm with one hand while the other went to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls, every time, as though the figure were due out of the market and not out of a man. Nobody had taken him off since the month turned, or if somebody had taken him off somebody had put him back in the dark. The boy had watched that order of the hand for many mornings and had never once seen it reversed; a clerk whose hand reaches the slate before his eyes reach the stalls is a clerk who already knows what he is waiting for, and a wait with no figure in it is the longest kind. The market sold its reeds and its eels over the top of him. A girl with a creel took the landing sideways, caught the rail and went on selling, and nobody made a fall of it. The steps below the bridge kept their four, four and a landing a hand’s breadth out of line, and a whole market walked over them carefully without turning them into an accident.

The boy set the clerk on a place of his own. A measure set down before anybody called it was a measure set down ahead of something; that was a shape, not a rule, and he had learned not to make a rule of it. Hand to slate before eyes to stalls was the order a man uses when the writing is the work and the people are the interruption, and he had seen that order in a midwife reaching for a slate before a child, in a thane wiping his thumb twice before touching nothing, and in a woman reaching for a bare board before she looked at him. Three likenesses in a row will make a cause in a man who wants one, and he had learned in two lives what a cause grows from: a hand laid on a hand, a clean line drawn at the foot of a page. He laid no line. The clerk went on holding his slate for the measure nobody had called, and the measure went on not coming.

The cord went back inside the reed-cutter’s coat with its knots unread and its figure given to nobody. Orra’s knot-string lay beside Mira’s board and did not touch it. Mira’s second twist lay under the grey wool with its knots uncounted and the thin child’s knot untouched. The clerk’s slate lay on his forearm with a figure on it that belonged to no hand in the strip. Four accounts in materials the Court could not spell, and the boy held them all, exact and complete and worth nothing, with one hand outside the wool that could not close two inches on the rim of the basket. Through the finger’s-width of shade beside the boards came the river’s note of water over water, a fifth low thing on a place of its own, never a hearing and never among the six hearings of the full note. The market went on selling over the man with the slate as though a man with a slate were a thing every landing had, and the count went on where it had stood all morning, unspent and spoken by nobody.

The boy was carried up from the Fishstairs in the slope of the afternoon, when the light lay along the yard in long thin strips and the river wind had come round to the east. Sella Vail carried him at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, and gave the instruction to the wool and the river wind and never to the basket: keep it off his ears, it had come all the way down with them and would go all the way back up, and a basket set down was a pot set down and left. The wool rode back from the small bare joint and the cold found the place it had come for and went under the fold at his ear in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist. The thread at his mouth had frozen the night before and stayed where it had been put, because nobody under any roof in the district had been given an instruction about the thread. He was one hundred and twenty-eight days old, four months and eight days, and nobody on the road up had said so.

Three doors up the yard she set the basket just inside the finger’s-width-open door of House Silt, at the edge of the light and away from every place a slate might lie, and stood a half-pace off. She opened no slate and turned no errand into a walk to Slate Row. The front room smelled of soap-and-milk gone cold under river silt, and under the soap the sour mineral tang of lye, and under that the faint dry tang of Ash that had worked into the floor and would not come out of it with scrubbing. The floor was trodden ash packed until it had the spring of old bread, and a grey ridge of it ran along the skirting from the bench under the door into the next room, where a woman’s low song came up through the boards with its words flattened out by work. Above the abandoned board-bed a round pewter tin kept its pale fold at the seam and a thumb-bright rub on the lid, shut, and the fold of grey wool below held only the hollow where a small pale river stone had once lain. Nobody said either thing was gone.

At a tub in the back room a neighbour scrubbed at a board with lye water and did not look up at the bench, the tin or the fold pressed too long. The song went on under the door and along the floor with its words worn flat, and came up through the soles of the room the way warmth comes up through a cracked pot, by degrees and with a loss nobody entered. Sella gave the instruction to the pot on the bench and never to the basket, and said the house where another woman would have said I, and stood with her little-door burn shut on nothing.

Alda Reed came short into the room with the birth-slates under her arm, smelling of soap and warm milk gone cold under river silt and lye sour under the soap, her nails scrubbed to the quick with clay still under them. She went to the bench before she went to the basket. That was the order of her hand, the record before the person, and he had watched it so often that he could have told the number of steps between the two without counting them: a hand to the slate, then a hand to the child, never the other way about. She laid the birth-slates writing-down on the bench, in the place within her right hand, and only then turned to the fold.

She pressed the wool flat at his shoulder a breath too long. She pressed it with the same two fingers that had made the sign, and the sign came before she knew she had made it, two fingers crossed at the red edge of the birth-flower where it ran from the third rib down the forearm and stopped short of the wrist. Grathic had a name for the mark and no name for what she did at its edge; he had learned the one and never found the other. She covered the sign with the date she had given the woman: Stone seven. The account went up on the eighth, before the paper went out. The date lay over the mark the way a clean line lies over a note nobody has opened. It was exact. It was not true. It stood now over a woman more mornings dead than any living person keeps count of, and no living person could take it off.

“Warm and breathing, that’s all I’ve set down,” Alda said, in the tone a woman uses to state the weather.

She went on with dates after the question had stopped, because dates were a handle on a thing too large to carry: a cooper’s seventh in Mud End, a potter’s third child in Stonemouth, the last high water, the exact day of a birth in a house nobody in that room would ever enter. The dates came faster than dates had any business coming, and the room arranged itself around them the way a market arranges itself around a reading it has not understood. He watched her hand go to the tin once and come away, and knew the shape of that gesture as the shape of a hand that has learned to stop short of the thing it wants to open. A date laid over a sign is a chart closed over a note nobody has read; the clean writing at the foot of it says nothing about what lies under, and a man who trusted the writing would go away satisfied. He had seen a hand cover a field with a cloth before bringing the instrument down, and had seen the same hand lift out of the field before the dressing went on, and had learned from both that the covering and the taking-out are one motion performed on a person who cannot object. Alda’s date was such a cloth, laid down over a woman who could no longer pull it off.

On a narrow slate she wrote what she would not leave lying, in a second hand that no physician of the Court had ever seen, and turned it writing-down into her grey shawl toward a floorboard in a house the boy could not name. Six pages lay there turned toward the floor and read by nobody, written one at a time across three years, and no seventh had been added on this day.

He kept the three things in three places. The pewter tin above the empty bed held one record in a material no officer’s thumb would open, the little tin under the shawl held another, and the grey wool below the first held the shape of a warmth that had gone out of a fold without ever being carried to a door. The hollow in that wool was the only object in the room his sorrow could attach to; in the life he had lived before this one a grief with no name in the country it was spoken in went looking for a thing it could hold, and here the thing was a pale shape pressed into grey wool by a small stone that had long since been carried away. He laid none of the three together. The surgeon in him noted the pressure of the fold at his shoulder, a breath over what a fold required, and the exact instant the two fingers moved; the child in him was cold at the ear and wanted the crust that was not in this room. Both were true. The second was louder, and neither could be handed back.

At the going of the light Sella carried him up the clay road at the half-pace and set him on his bed under the roof and not in the Debt Loft. Under the lowest rack the broken birth-slates lay where the yard had gone around them for a year, entered by nobody, and she went around the space as she would go around a pail set where a child might knock it: a look, a wide step, no number and no name. The boy read the going-around as a chart closed over a note nobody had opened, and set it on a place of its own beside the place in the strip where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out on Seed 27; he laid no likeness between them and gave neither a figure. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without once looking at his face, and left the thread of drool frozen and unwiped. Her hand rested a half-beat longer than the setting needed before it went away, the way a hand rests on a thing it has carried a long way and cannot quite bring itself to let go of; the little-door burn closed on nothing. The clean dark line where the Ash ridge had turned lay unswept at the corner, and the new finer hairline of grey beside it lay unswept too, and she told the fire it was the thaw and would go when it goes.

She turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and fed them, and said “The house will keep it” where another woman would have said I, and stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand counting something under her breath toward no opening. She pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn, and the grey streak at her temple showed pale off the coals, and she set the pot where it could be found in the dark, rim out and handle toward the reaching hand. She did not look toward the bed. She did not touch the widow’s mark, because a dusk is not an opening, and went to her own bed.

The fire had come up into this house in a clay dish before he was a month old, passed from hearth to hearth along the yard with a coal under ash, and no house had said where it was first lit or who had paid for the burning. It had kept its four low breaths through every morning since and missed the fifth in every four, warming the boards by degrees and taking its coal from somewhere it would not name. She fed it and told it the weather and went to her bed, and the house held what it held without ever entering the warmth.

Under the roof the dark came on in the order it always came on, first the light going out of the oiled paper at the window, then the fire’s low red, then the sound of water. Through the finger’s-width of the door came the river’s note of water over water, beyond the reed beds and the Weir and the whole length of Low Kiln, a fifth low thing he had kept on a place of its own since he was six days old, never a hearing and never counted among the six hearings of the full note. Through the floor under blind ash the carried fire took its coal and gave back four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, and its payer had been unnamed for a hundred and twenty-eight days and would be unnamed on the morrow. Above him the boards kept four nails, four, and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts in three places, and none of them became one.

The bell at the Cross was a told bell and stayed beside the heard bell in a place of its own; the note of water was a fifth low thing and stayed beside the four pulls in another; the knot in the boards was a knot the width of a thumb and had never been counted as a nail. He had learned in two lives how readily three likenesses in a row will stand up and call themselves a cause, and he kept the three counts lying flat where they lay, as a man keeps instruments separated on a towel so that no edge is mistaken for another.

He turned out his file as a man lays instruments on a towel before anyone calls what the operation is for. the loaf shelf in the next room kept its two unnamed gaps, the old space at the row’s end and the palm’s breadth that had come in on the night of Seed 6 with no scrape, crumb, print or word; the fold at his ear kept one stiff brown-edged crust and an empty place beside it that had no name. Two rooms that did not touch, and a saying of the loaf-shelves of Low Kiln that two gaps stay two gaps until a finger lays them together and an account then wants a name; no finger in the district had laid these together. Neighbours had laid the shelf’s old space and the place at his ear under one cause in a single breath more than once, their hands going to the board before their eyes found him, and he had set each saying on a place of its own and had not made their joining his own. No third gap had come. No fourth likeness had been made into a cause.

Six hearings of the full note stood where they had stood: five from four sources, a cup across a birth room, a pot in the wheel-shed through a wall, the district’s slate twice at the Cross, a man giving at the Drawing, and the sixth, the small flat knock out of a woman as the men lifted her, filed beside the five and never among them. No seventh had come in three days, and he had not invented one to fill the empty place. Three near-full answers stood on their own tray, advanced and not paid: a cup across a birth room, a pot under the wheel-shed wall, a rack pot lifted a hand’s breadth too high and set down again. On the interval tray lay the intervals from Stone 13, Stone 17 and Stone 25, with the half-beat in a man’s broom behind the standing finger, the stroke that went on over the flagstones without the check, his own held breath from the market, and the two stoppings of the near-saying: the sentence about an ear worth more than the debt that held it brought twice to the threshold of the word that would call the loan and stopped twice there with no figure and no name. They lay as objects among objects and not as a rule; the loan that could hear was uncalled and the seventh year had not given. The word that would have named the shape of the last knot on Orra Weir’s cord stayed unspoken with the shape of the forty-first knot still legible beside it. The cold foot below the sill was still the cold to one woman and weather to the yard. The green at a mouth’s corner was still a colour and not a name. The clerk at the landing still held his slate for a measure nobody had called.

The reed-cutter’s cord had gone back inside his coat with its knots unread and its figure given to nobody; Mira’s grey cord and the second twist twisted twice beneath it had not been lifted at a door, her board lying beside Orra’s knot-string without touching it; Berr’s narrow slate held a figure that did not know the hand that had not come back. Every one of them was a record kept in a material no Court thumb could spell, and he held them all, exact, complete and worth nothing, and could spend none of them.

He was one hundred and twenty-eight days old and nobody had said so. The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down. The count went on where it had stood the night before, exact, complete, unspent and spoken by nobody, while the dark went on keeping what it kept and the day crossed into the morrow without being opened, joined, named, paid or ended.
