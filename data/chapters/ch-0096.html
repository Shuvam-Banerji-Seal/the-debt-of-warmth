Seven Behind One Figure

Days 339–341 · Ash 10–12, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, Sill

The figure goes up seven times and the crack stays showing; the runner carries what she will not read, and the house keeps its grain under wool without a number.

— A yard saying of Low Kiln, on Ash’s first Account-day

— Day 339 —

Ash 10, Tenthday, Y.H. 813

The hand lay against the fold where it had been laid down on the two hundred and fifty-second answer, as a man puts away a tool where the next hand will find it without looking. It was a small soft hand with milky nails and no strength in the wrist worth speaking of; it had once reduced a shoulder in a room that smelled of disinfectant and cold metal and had since learned the exact weight of a barley crust at two inches. In the small hours under the Vail kiln-house roof the boy lay awake and took the room as a surgeon takes a field before the light, by the small sounds first, because the small sounds do not lie and the large ones are mostly weather: the dry tick of clay cooling behind the wall, the settle of a coal under its ash, the river going low over lower under the finger’s-width door. The room smelled of wet wool and tallow and, under them, the faint sour mineral tang of the Ash ridge along the skirting board, which had not been swept in two years. Three hundred and thirty-nine stood exact behind him, six months and one hundred and fifty-nine days told by nobody in Low Kiln, Sill, Grath. Under the floor the carried fire gave four low pulls and kept the silence where the fifth should have stood, its payer unnamed three hundred and thirty-nine days; on its peg behind the door the salt-stiff coat kept the shape of a man whose name had not been spoken under this roof the same number. Nothing from the seventh, eighth or ninth of Ash crossed the dark toward him as sign, lesson, mending, cause or payment. The pot-crack resting of the ninth sat on a place of its own beside the five held objects and was expressly not a sixth; an empty place kept empty is a finding and a finding filled to make a row come out even is a finding forged, and he had kept rows honest longer than anybody under this roof could count, which was not a thing he could say to anybody. The ear gave a man the truth before the eye did, because the eye is always being told what to see and the ear is not; he had learned that in a room where nobody had ever thought to tell him what he was hearing.

On the bed at the low angle of the loaf shelf the inventory came in its old order, because an order is the one thing a field will give a man who cannot move in it. One stiff leathery brown-edged barley crust lay two inches from his ear, gone harder through the dry kiln heat, its corner standing a hair off the wool; the empty place beside it kept its name to itself and no third had come in the small hours to make the row come out even. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen and was unwiped, because nobody under any roof had ever been given an instruction about the thread, and a thing nobody has been told to wipe is a thing that stays. Cold had come to cheek and temple and the small bare joint of the wrist where the wool rode back, and had stopped there as at the end of a line written out for it, with neither an order to go farther nor an order to go away; the Afterdrop waited beyond the stopping for a warming nobody in this house would perform. He knew the face that cold puts on a man, the composed face that is not composure, the ease that is no ease. He knew a cold patient cannot clot and will seem better before he is worse, and must be warmed from the outside and slowly, never hurried, never rubbed, never set too near the fire; he knew the order in which a body takes heat back, the small joint first and the core last, and the order in which it gives it up, the reverse, which comes sooner than anybody likes, and that the pink returning to a face is the surface still cold and not the core. He wanted the crust lifted the two inches to his mouth, the thread wiped away, and a hand that would close on the pot handle brought within reach. Both were true and the second was louder and neither won. The argument went on under the wool without a verdict, as it had gone on through every morning of his life in this house: the surgeon taking the inventory exactly and entering it nowhere, the infant underneath taking none of it and wanting, the two of them quarrelling in a body that could not so much as lift its own chin from a fold. He had reduced a thousand shoulders and could not lift a crust; the joke cost him nothing and was not funny. When he turned his head past a point a small sound came up out of him that was not a word and would not be commanded; he had once told a junior that a patient making that sound was already in trouble, and now the sound was his own and there was no junior to hear it.

Sella Vail went to the hearth before she went to the basket. She was a broad woman who took up room by being impossible to move, with forearms the colour of dried slip and a burn across the back of her left hand shaped like a little door — two straight sides, a lintel and a threshold, the skin inside it puckered and pale. “It’s the thaw,” she told the coals, and turned them with the back of a broken pot, not with the poker, because a pot’s back knows how much a coal will bear. The ash over them was the colour of a day-old loaf and broke where she turned it, showing a red that went out again almost at once. “The house will keep it,” she said where another woman would have said I. She stood a moment counting a small intake, a hold and a letting-go toward no opening, and gave neither gap a figure; the breath went in, stood, and went out, and she did not call the space between the two ends of it anything. She counted the way another woman might count spoons on a shelf, where the count is a way of knowing the room is as it was and not an entry in anybody’s book. The little-door burn shut on nothing after the coal turned, and nothing in the room corrected it. Her finger lay on top of the loaf shelf’s front edge so the charcoal tally stayed unread; her lips moved along a two-gapped row and figured neither gap. Her eye went once to the clay jar standing cold under lead glaze and her hand shut half over nothing, the little-door burn closing on air. She did not look at the bed. She had one grey streak at her temple and she pushed coal-black hair back with the burn-wrist, and the instruction went to the fire, the wool, the pot and the river wind and never to the basket.

From the pot board she lifted the twice-mended pot, and her thumb went to the wire where it crossed the crack as it had gone on every morning for three years. The hairline of the original break stood dark between the old-brass staple and the tighter wire, and she left it showing. “If it’s worth mending it’s worth mending twice. The second time is the real one,” she said to the pot; and to the fire, “Leave the crack showing. People trust a thing they can see has been handled.” The sayings went to a pot and a fire and never to a person, and had improved in the keeping and were very likely not as good as the man who had first said them. Repair stayed apart from restoration: a restored thing hides the hand that handled it, a mended thing leaves that hand showing, and here the hand was left showing in the dark hairline between brass and wire. He read the mending as a wound closed in a place the hand would not have chosen, by what the hand had done and by what it had left undone, and kept the two readings apart: a seam that holds is not a seam that never broke. The dressed grain lay turned under wool away from the hearth, and she lifted a corner with a touch lighter than the morning required and turned it once and gave no figure; her fingers went in and came out without closing, so that no handful was lifted. Wool over turning grain gave its small dry rasp, and the smell of the yard came up through it. It was the lightest touch she used all day, lighter than the setting of a fold, and it told him nothing he could carry out of the room because there was no number on it. The pot’s rim went down two inches beyond the reaching hand, with a half-beat at the setting and no look toward the bed. The widow’s mark hung where it hung and was not touched, because a morning and a hearth are not openings; six openings in six months were six and not a rule, and no seventh was made due on a day that had not earned one.

Under the floor the carried fire kept its four and its gap; overhead the boards kept four nails on the near board, four on the next and a thumb-width knot where a fifth might have been driven and was not; through the finger’s-width door water over water went low over lower, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, and was never numbered among the hearings, for a hearing wants a source standing and none stood. Three counts in three places, and none rose into another, because agreeing any two would have wanted a payer put under both and none stood to any. The room held the three the way a body holds three old injuries, each with its own weather, and none of them would agree to be one pain. Six hearings of the full note stood beside and never among, with the seventh place empty, the sixth the small flat knock out of a woman as the men lifted her, filed where a thing out of a person must go and never among the things that had come out of vessels; an empty place kept empty was a finding and a finding filled to make a row come out even was a finding forged. The heard bell stayed in its square beside the told bell of the Long Night, four, four and a gap with nobody crossing himself, and was not fetched in to make a fourth count or a seventh hearing. The told figure stood beside the paid figure with no signature on the space between. The file lay open on the towel at the head of the bed with every gap left gap: the staying at the inch of Bone 15, the early letting-go of Bone 17, the handful let fall at the rack end on Bone 22, the resting of a thumb on the uncut seventh on Bone 26 and the sixth opening at the kiln mouth on Ash 2, five objects on five places with a finger between every pair and none made again; the pot-crack resting of the ninth stood beside them and not among them, with a finger between it and each, and no payer stood under any of them. He did not look at the sixth place longer than it took to know it was six, for a finding stared at too long starts asking to be promoted.

Six arithmetics of the year — the three of Frost and the first, second and third of Bone — rode locked up the clay road in Berr Clay’s box toward High Kiln, one in twelve printed and unchallenged, the figure read still not the figure paid and no signature on the space, six readings six and not one account. Ash 10 was a date that had arrived and must earn its reading; Ash’s first arithmetic belonged to its own morning, to go up in the locked box and stand with the six behind one unsigned figure, and was not carried into the small hours. The finding, once the morning had earned it, that seven are seven and not one account would be kept on a place of its own with no payer, not laid across the house’s not-doings, the dressed grain covered under wool, the five held objects, the pot-crack resting, the handful, the thumb’s resting, the two small objects of the hand or his own kept gaps; the edges would come near to touching and the finger would go between. Ash 20, the month’s second Account-day, stood ten mornings ahead and was named nowhere under this roof; it belonged to its own day and was not brought into one that had not earned it. Berr had wiped his thumb twice on his apron the last time and taken himself out of his own sentence, and the boy had filed the taking-apart as a hand drawn from a field before the dressing goes on; nothing from it was due this morning, and no eighth arithmetic was owed on a day that had not earned one. The box had a lock and the lock had a key that went up the road in a coat pocket and did not come back down, and the boy had no key and made no figure out of a reading he had only heard.

In the small hours, with the river’s note low and going lower under the finger’s-width door, the hand outside the wool came up an inch toward the sound. It took a small intake from the room whose floor does not touch his, held the interval whole, let the intake go on the same low note, and came straight down and laid itself back against the fold as a man puts away a tool where the next hand will find it without looking. The note had done before the hand came down, so the letting-go was neither early again nor stayed, and the hand did not stay; it was the note’s own finishing that ended the interval and not any decision of the hand, and that was the whole of the honesty in it. The satisfaction was not comfort. Comfort wants a giver standing, and none stood, so the feeling had to be taken small and without a name. The inch was not a reach and the intake was not a breath kept for warmth; the interval was not a stopping of the river and the note was not his. He filed it as answer two hundred and fifty-three: exact, complete, worth nothing at two inches of air, a restraint and not a payment, borrowing no house count and supplying no payer, laid across neither the six arithmetics, the bell’s gap, the covered grain, the handful, the thumb’s resting, the stayed object, the early letting-go, the sixth opening, the Ash 9 pot-crack resting or his own kept gaps. Two hundred and fifty-three honest answers stood on the sixth place, each an object and not a power, and neither the number nor the small hand-movings that had made it stood up and called themselves a doctrine, for a doctrine is an account that has found a payer and none stood.

The surgeon took his small clean satisfaction in a field left as found and an answer entered where nobody could see it; the infant underneath took none of it and went on wanting the crust lifted, the thread wiped and a hand on the pot handle, both true, the second louder, neither winning, the two registers quarrelling without verdict in the small hours. Three hundred and thirty-nine stood exact, complete, unspent and spoken by nobody in Low Kiln, Sill and Grath; six months and one hundred and fifty-nine days were told by nobody; the carried fire’s fifth was unpaid the same number with its payer unnamed; and the name of the man the salt-stiff coat kept was unspoken the same number. Nothing on the seventh, eighth or ninth of Ash had been opened, joined by a standing hand, named, called, paid or ended, and nothing had crossed into the tenth to make any of them a sign. The first reading of the dark month was coming up the clay road without anybody under this roof having made it happen, and no hand here would go down to meet it as a question. The hand lay back against the fold on answer two hundred and fifty-three, the file open on the towel with every gap left gap, the twice-mended pot standing on its board with the hairline of the break showing between the old-brass staple and the tighter wire, the fired kiln standing cold behind the kiln mouth, and the tenth morning at the door with its arithmetic still unread. Forward, not closed.

Sella Vail carried him down before first light. The yard was a room with no light in it yet and the river wind had not yet come up off the water; the clay underfoot held the cold of the night and gave it back in the small dry rasp of a boot. She went at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, the basket level on her hip and the fold laid over his ears against the wind, and the instruction went to the wool and to the river wind and never to the basket. The boy took the carrying apart as he took everything apart, by the small sounds first, because the small sounds do not lie and the large ones are mostly weather: the settle of her breath under the load, the rasp of the pail rope at the yard well, the tick of clay going on cooling behind a wall. Three hundred and thirty-nine stood exact behind him, six months and one hundred and fifty-nine days told by nobody in Low Kiln, Sill, Grath. Under the floor of the house he had left the carried fire kept its four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have stood, its payer unnamed the same number of days; on its peg the salt-stiff coat kept the shape of a man whose name had not been spoken under that roof the same number.

She went wide around the seven walked-around places with a look and a step and no number: the clean dark line at the Vail corner and the finer grey beside it called the thaw, unswept; the space beneath the lowest rack where the broken birth-slates lay; the round ring where the shallow clay pan had once stood, with no scrape, crumb, print, chip or word; the skinned trough; the field’s empty place out on the worked strips; the returning-line place where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out. Seven places, and not one of them given a figure. The three coverings at the well stayed weather entered nowhere. Nothing from the seventh, eighth or ninth of Ash crossed the yard with them as sign, lesson, mending, cause or payment. The pot-crack resting of the ninth sat on a place of its own beside the five held objects and was expressly not a sixth; an empty place kept empty is a finding and a finding filled to make a row come out even is a finding forged, and he had kept rows honest longer than anybody in this yard could count, which was not a thing he could say to anybody.

At the Cross she set the basket in the moot-post’s finger’s width of shade where no slate lay, with his cheek turned toward the cold iron and his ear to the wool. Two hundred people were already leaning toward the reading block under the moot-post, and the pigs were being driven over the wet boards, eight of them, though nobody in the shade counted them aloud. She gave no instruction and no reason to the basket. She stood half a pace off and did not look into it, and the half-pace was not a distance she had chosen for feeling; it was the distance at which no man could read a figure over him without her seeing the hand that read it. The market came up around them in mud and straw and the smell of wet pig and turnip. The cold iron of the post put its own small cold into the air beside his cheek, the third cold of the morning and the one that had not come from inside him.

Berr Clay came up short off the clay road. The red wool cord of his office was folded square under his coat instead of worn across it, and the sweetish green of tasted lead glaze stood at one corner of his mouth; under the hot clay of him was the smell of nothing at all, which is the most expensive smell in Grath. His hands were large and clean. He wiped his thumb twice on his apron before he touched nothing, once for the slate and once for the figure that would be on it, and held a narrow slate unopened against his forearm with the writing inward. A finger went over the figure through the cloth of his own sleeve, and his eyes rested on the writing a breath longer than the writing required. The boy took the order apart as he took apart a field before the dressing went on: hand to the record before eye to the person, eye before mouth, and no hand at all to the basket. He filed the twice-wiped thumb on a place of its own with a finger between it and every other hand he had ever filed, and filed no tenth.

“One in twelve, as the notice says,” Berr read low into the goers-by, in the tone of a man setting a cup where a cup belongs. He sent the figure twice over the basket, once for the moot and once for the late households, and the figure went over the top of it as water goes over a stone lying where a stone lies. He did not look into the basket. He never did. “The levy is called. It is not called by me,” he said. A man at the rail brought a number up out of a fist and let it stand there; Berr looked at the fist, did not take the number, and said, “Your house wants it again, there’ll be a slate at the watch.” Your house. Never I. He took himself out of his own sentence as a surgeon takes a hand out of a field before the dressing goes on, leaving the work clean and the hand that did it nowhere in it. Then he said something more low to the block about kindness being entered, in the same cup-setting tone, and the block, being a block, answered nothing.

There was no anger in the reading and no triumph in it and nothing in the face of the man who read it that a crowd could have fastened on. That was the terrible part, and the boy had learned early to be afraid of the banal before he was afraid of the cruel: a reasonable man with a ledger and a good reason is the most frightening thing in Sarn, and the reason is the part that will be remembered against him. He had once stood in a white room and watched a man say a number over a body on a table and go on to the next body, and the number had been right and the hand that wrote it clean. Here the number was one in twelve and the print had not been challenged in any of the year’s readings. Seven arithmetics of the year now stood behind that one printed figure: the three of Frost, the first, second and third of Bone, and now Ash’s first, going up in the locked box. Seven were seven and not one account. The figure read was still not the figure paid and no signature stood on the space between.

The finding came where findings came, on a place of its own with no payer under it. He did not lay it across the house’s not-doings, the dressed grain covered under wool at the rack end, the five held objects, the pot-crack resting beside them, the handful let fall, the thumb’s resting on the uncut seventh, the two small objects of the hand or his own kept gaps. The edges of these things came near to touching, so near that a less careful hand would have called the touching a cause and the cause a payment; the finger went between. No doctrine stood up, for a doctrine is an account that has found a payer and none stood. The reading answered nothing for the hand that had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come back out, nothing for the man carried out on Day 188, nothing for the carried fire’s fifth with its payer unnamed. It answered nothing for the crust two inches from his ear or the thread frozen at his mouth, because it was not put there to answer anything.

Berr went off up the clay road with the cord folded, the narrow slate under his arm and the box locked, Ash’s first true subtraction riding inside beside the six that had gone before. He believed he had done the district a kindness. The district’s arithmetic had changed for Ash and could not be changed back, and no payer stood where the boy could see. The box had a lock and the lock had a key that went up the road in a coat pocket and did not come back down; the boy had no key and made no figure out of a reading he had only heard. Ash 20, the month’s second Account-day, stood ten mornings ahead and was named nowhere in the square; it belonged to its own day and was not brought into one that had earned only this.

Hessa Rale took the slate up the road before noon. It came to her hand first: the small flat correction of the strap, the turn of the wrist bringing the edge round, the thumb along the grain of the frame with nothing pressing where the writing was. Only then did her eyes go to the crowd. The writing lay turned to her sleeve, the slate’s face edge-on to her forearm, and it was not concealment but the manner of carrying a slate so that the writing was not rubbed and no goer-by took a figure off it without a hand to put there. The second strap was let out for the long stretch and dried pale scurf of road-mud stood unbrushed on the skirt she had shortened for the wet. She moved at the pace of a person who has made a bargain with distance and keeps it without thinking.

A woman by the rail asked what she carried. “I don’t read it. I carry it. There’s a difference, and the road doesn’t care which,” Hessa said, answering the question put and not the one behind it, and went on up toward Greybank with no rudeness and no apology and no figure coming off the slate. She was a runner and not a reader. Her hand to the slate before her eye to the crowd stayed the eighth record-first hand on a place of its own, eight and not a rule, with no tenth and no figure made of the slate’s passing. The Ash 6 turning of her writing toward herself stayed a sighting on its own place beside the fourth shaping of the well-saying, with a finger between; the reading of a figure that had named a person she had once known did not make her a reader of it, a witness, a cause or a payment. She was not joined to any voice standing in no doorway, not to Marra, not to the soft-handed man of Slate Row, not to the clerks, not to Garl Vail, not to the woman with the count in wood, and the far-off day a hand like hers would lay the district’s separate keepings together was not brought one step nearer by the morning’s arithmetic.

The bell went four, four and a gap. Nobody crossed himself. The boy kept the heard bell in its square beside the told bell of the Long Night, four, four and a gap longer than a church bell, and did not fetch it in to make a fourth count or a seventh of the full note. Six hearings stood beside and never among, the seventh place empty, the sixth the small flat knock out of a woman as the men lifted her, filed where a thing out of a person must go and never among the things that had come out of vessels. An empty place kept empty was a finding, and nothing the bell did this morning invented a seventh to fill it.

Below the north bridge the soft-cuffed clerk stayed set at the out-of-line landing, where a fifth step should have stood, for a measure nobody called. His slate lay inward to his forearm and his hand went to it before his eyes went to the stalls; the market sold over him as water over a stone, and nobody took him off. The boy put the order of his reaching on a place of its own and made no figure of it and no joining to the runner’s eighth hand or to Berr’s twice-wiped thumb. Three record-first hands in one morning and still no tenth and still no rule; a rule wants a payer put under all of them and none stood.

Across the square at the pot board a woman stood with clay dried pale in the crescents of her nails and the sweetish green of lead glaze at one corner of her mouth. She turned a cup a quarter without lifting it and set it back exactly where it had stood, and there was a pale line across the back of one hand where something had once been worn tight and long. She said nothing. At the market’s edge a small shape in cast-offs caught the light on a green sliver at its throat and went dull again. The boy called the one a colour and the other a shape and gave them each a place of their own, a seventh of each with no eighth, and nothing more. He did not name either. He did not join the green at the pot board to the green at Berr’s mouth, or either of them to the sliver, or the woman to the shape, or any of them to a loaf, a slate, a sale, a hearing, a cause or a romance. No second look was given and none was made into a saying; a sighting is a sighting and becomes a witness only when somebody stands up behind it to be asked, and nobody stood.

Sella had not finished telling him the fire was low when his bent finger went into the cold iron of the damper, took the cold out of the fingertip, moved the tongue a hair’s breadth and was left standing; the end of her instruction arrived at the pot and the household straightened around his silence the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted. One piece of iron had two bites, the holding and the taking, and there was no Grathic word for both mercies. “He’s not Vail” stayed sealed as a lid nobody had tried to lift in three years, and the promise of the boy’s name to the men who burn slates was neither given nor withdrawn. The burned-slate word stood at five with a finger between the fifth and the four and no witness standing; the joining said over the boards on the fifth of Ash stood beside it on a place of its own, not a sixth telling, not a third telling of the shut room, not a cause, not a confession and not a rule. The shut-room tellings stood at two and the row of askings at five with no witness and no sixth, and no seventh was made due on a day that had not earned one. The boy set the five down where they lay and did not look for a sixth standing beside them.

Garl stayed in objects and not in a story. The salt-stiff coat with the crease at the left shoulder kept his broader shape on its peg behind the door; the wheel-seat in the shed kept its twenty-year hollow on that side and nobody was allowed to fill it; the ridge that had been his ash-marker was now the clean dark line at the Vail corner with the finer grey beside it called the thaw, unswept both; the twice-mended pot stood on the pot board with the hairline of the original break showing between the old-brass staple and the tighter wire, and both proverbs had gone to the pot and the fire and never to a person, improved in the keeping and very likely not as good as the man had said them. The debt against a firing of tiles circled without being called, neither paid nor settled nor done with and worse for being uncalled. His name had not been spoken under this roof three hundred and forty-one days, and no lesson had been made of any of it. The keeping of him was maintenance and not a story.

At the going of the light Sella carried him up at the half-pace and went wide around the seven walked-around places, and set him on the bed under the Vail kiln-house roof and never in the Debt Loft. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without a look at his face, let her hand rest the half-beat longer than the setting needed before it went away, and left the thread where it lay. A sound came from above and behind and no interior was entered and no name was given to the sound; the instruction went to the fire, the wool, the pot and the river wind and never to the basket. She said “The house will keep it” where another woman would have said I. The widow’s mark hung where it hung and was not touched, because a going of the light and a bed are not openings; six openings in six months were six and not a rule, and no seventh was made due on a day that had not earned one.

She went to the hearth before the basket, told the coals it was the thaw and turned them with the back of a broken pot. The fire answered four low pulls with the silence where the fifth should have stood, its payer unnamed three hundred and forty-one days, and she did not supply the pull. Her finger lay on top of the loaf shelf’s front edge so the charcoal tally stayed unread; her lips moved along a two-gapped row and figured neither gap; and her eye went once to the clay jar standing cold under lead glaze with the hand half over nothing, the little-door burn on the back of her left hand closing on air. From the pot board she lifted the twice-mended pot with her thumb first where the wire crossed the crack and only then let the weight come on the handle, and the crack was left showing. At the rack end the dressed grain lay turned under wool away from the hearth; she lifted a corner with a lighter touch and turned it once and gave no figure, and her fingers went in and came out without closing, so that no handful was lifted. The pot’s rim went down two inches beyond the reaching hand, with a half-beat at the setting and no look toward the bed. The boy took the half-beat apart by what was there and by what was not: the distance between the hand’s intention and its arrest, a small exact interval that bought nothing at two inches of air and was not a gift, for a gift wants a giver and nobody had given.

Under the floor the carried fire kept its four and its gap; overhead the boards kept four nails on the near board, four on the next and a thumb-width knot where a fifth might have been driven and was not; through the finger’s-width door water over water went low over lower, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, and was never numbered among the hearings, for a hearing wants a source standing and none stood. Three counts in three places and none rose into another, because agreeing any two would have wanted a payer put under both and none stood to any. The finding that seven are seven and not one account stayed on a place of its own with no payer under it and was not laid across the not-doings, the covered grain, the five held objects, the handful, the thumb’s resting, the two small objects of the hand, the sixth opening, the three Ash 5/6 movements, the pot-crack resting or his gaps; the edges came near to touching and the finger went between.

In the small hours the hand outside the wool came up an inch toward the river’s note. It did not reach; the inch was not a reach and he had long ago stopped calling it one. It was the distance a hand may move in a field without disturbing the dressing, and he took it the way he had once taken a hand into a cavity, slowly, with the whole of the attention and the knowledge that most of what a hand does in a field is wait. The note under the finger’s-width door went low over lower, a thing over a thing, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming. The hand took a small intake from the room whose floor does not touch his, held the interval whole, and let the intake go on the same low note. It came straight down and laid itself back against the fold as a man puts away a tool where the next hand will find it without looking. The note had done before the hand came down, so the letting-go was neither early again nor stayed, and the hand did not stay; it was the note’s own finishing that ended the interval and not any decision of the hand, and that was the whole of the honesty in it. The intake was not a breath kept for warmth, the interval not a stopping of the river, the note not his. He filed it as answer two hundred and fifty-eight: exact, complete, worth nothing at two inches of air, a restraint and not a payment borrowing no house count and supplying no payer, laid across neither the seven arithmetics, the bell’s gap, the covered grain, the handful, the thumb’s resting, the stayed object, the early letting-go, the sixth opening, the Ash 5 joining, the fourth shaping, the turned writing, Ilva’s wood, the pot-crack resting nor his own kept gaps.

Two hundred and fifty-eight honest answers stood on the sixth place, each an object and not a power, and neither the number nor the small hand-movings that had made it stood up and called themselves a doctrine, for a doctrine is an account that has found a payer and none stood. The surgeon took his small clean satisfaction in a field left as found and in an answer entered where nobody could see it; the infant underneath took none of it and went on wanting the crust lifted the two inches to his mouth, the thread wiped and a hand on the pot handle. Both were true, the second louder, neither winning, the two registers quarrelling without verdict in the small hours. Three hundred and forty-one stood exact, complete, unspent and spoken by nobody in Low Kiln, Sill and Grath; six months and one hundred and sixty-one days were told by nobody; the carried fire’s fifth was unpaid the same number with its payer unnamed; and the name of the man the salt-stiff coat kept was unspoken the same number. Nothing on the twelfth of Ash had been opened, joined by a standing hand, named, called, paid or ended. The keeping of the number was the only honest work there was to do, bought nothing at two inches of air, was not for sale, and went on because attention is the one thing no institution can tax. The file lay open on the towel at the head of the bed with every gap left gap; the twice-mended pot stood on the pot board with the hairline of the break showing between the old-brass staple and the tighter wire; the runner’s writing from the sixth of Ash was turned toward herself up the clay road and no figure had come off any slate; Ash’s first arithmetic rode locked and unread in Berr Clay’s box behind the six, seven behind one unsigned figure; and Ash 20, the month’s second Account-day and market, stood eight mornings ahead and was not brought into a day that had not earned it. The Chronicle of Chapter 96

On the tenth day of Ash, the dark month’s first Account-day, Sella Vail carried the basket down before first light at the half-pace she keeps from every open slate and set it in the moot-post’s shade. Two hundred people leaned toward the block; eight pigs came over the boards, uncounted aloud. Berr Clay came up short off the clay road with the red wool cord of his office folded under his coat and green of tasted lead glaze at one mouth-corner. His hands were large and clean. He wiped his thumb twice on his apron before he touched nothing, passed a finger over the figure, and read One in twelve, as the notice says, sending it twice over the basket. “The levy is called. It is not called by me,” he said, and spoke of your house, never I; then something low to the block about kindness being entered, and the block answered nothing. He went off with the box locked and Ash’s first subtraction inside, believing he had done a kindness. Seven arithmetics now stand behind one printed figure, and seven are seven and not one account.

Hessa Rale took the slate up the road before noon, her hand to it before her eyes to the crowd, the writing to her sleeve. To a woman who asked what she carried she said, “I don’t read it. I carry it.” The bell went four, four and a gap; nobody crossed himself. Below the north bridge the soft-cuffed clerk stayed set for an uncalled measure, the market selling over him as water over a stone. At the pot board a silent woman turned a cup a quarter and set it back; at the market’s edge a shape in cast-offs caught light on a green sliver. Each was a seventh with no eighth, and neither was named.
