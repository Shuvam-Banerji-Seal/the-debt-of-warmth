What Nobody Tells the Hand

Days 261–263 · Smoke 22–24, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, Sill

The broom has its instruction and goes around. The lip keeps its white and is called weather. What nobody tells the hand to do, the house keeps by leaving it undone.

— A saying of the yards of Low Kiln, told at the going of the light in Smoke’s third week

— Day 261 —

Smoke 22, Secondday, Y.H. 813

The hand lay where it had been put down, against the fold at the low angle of the loaf shelf, and the room under the Vail kiln-house roof took it back the way a bench takes back a tool laid down at the end of work. the boy had set it there on the ninety-sixth answer and had not asked it to remember what it had done. An inch was an inch, a low note was a low note, and the two intervals he had laid apart on the sixth place were two objects and not one likeness; he put the hand away with the same hand that had put away the others, where the next hand would find it without looking, and made no reach of it, no hearing, no gift, no rule for a morning to obey. Two hundred and sixty had stood exact at the going of the dark. In the dark it became two hundred and sixty-one; six months and eighty days became six months and eighty-one; and nobody under any roof in Sarn, Sill or Grath said so, or knew there was a number to say. Under the floor the carried fire went on giving its fifth unpaid day the payer it had given all the others, a payer without a name for two hundred and sixty-one days. Smoke 22 would be earned by the morning’s own light, when there was light enough to earn it; Smoke 30 stood eight mornings beyond that and was not reached for. There was no market, no Cross and no bell on a Secondday, and the district’s two names for the months were not set right or left wrong.

The first smell was hot clay under tallow, the dry smell of a kiln that had been fed and left to work. Under it came wet wool, sour with the lodging of a body that had lain long in it. Under that, finer and more persistent, came the sour mineral breath of the unswept corner the house called the thaw, a smell so long in the room that it had ceased to be an event and become a condition of the air. The first sound came up from the kiln mouth behind the wall — the kiln drawing in fours, four low pulls, and the silence where the fifth should have stood. It was not a bell. Nothing in this house struck four, four and a gap and called it a summons; the heard bell stayed where it had sounded, beside the told bell, and was not folded into the morning’s work.

There were three counts in three places and the boy kept them in three places. Under the floor the fire pulled four times and kept its missing fifth. Through the finger’s-width door the river kept its note of water over water, a low thing over a lower thing, going on with its own business and never numbered among the hearings. Overhead the boards kept four nails on the near board, four on the next, and a thumb-width knot where the fifth nail should have stood. Three counts, and none rose into another; agreeing any two would want a payer put under both, and no payer stood to any of them, for a knot is not a nail and a river is not a fire. Six hearings of the full note stood beside and never among, five from four sources and the sixth, the small flat knock out of a woman as the men lifted her, filed where a thing out of a person must go and never with the things that came from vessels. No seventh stood in the empty place and none had been invented to fill it, for an empty place kept empty is a finding and a finding filled to make a row come out even is a finding forged.

He took the body in the order the facts presented themselves, as a surgeon takes a field in the order the wounds present themselves, and entered none of it. One stiff leathery brown-edged barley crust lay two inches from his ear, hardened past the point where a child would have eaten it, and the empty place beside it kept its name to itself; no third had come in the night to make the row come out even. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the dry heat and was unwiped, because nobody under any roof had ever been given an instruction about the thread and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. Cold had come to cheek and temple and the small bare joint of the wrist where the wool rode back, and had stopped there as if a line had been written out for it to that length and no farther; it neither went forward nor went away. The afterdrop waited beyond all three, past a warming nobody in this house would perform. He knew the face severe cold wears, the composed face that is not composure; he knew a cold patient cannot clot and will seem better before he is worse, and that a man must be warmed from the outside and slowly. He knew all of it, and he wanted the crust lifted the two inches to his mouth. Both were true and the second was louder in a body of two hundred and sixty-one days, and the surgeon took the inventory and let the boy want, and entered neither, not even on the place where he kept the things he did not enter.

In the small hours the house was only its objects. Garl Vail’s salt-stiff coat kept a broader shoulder’s outline on its peg behind the door, with the crease at the left shoulder still where a man had set his weight against a bench to reach a high shelf. The wheel-seat kept its twenty-year lean and its hollow on that side. Nobody had spoken his name in two hundred and sixty-one days and nobody spoke it now; the keeping of him was maintenance and not a story, and a line left where it is is still a line kept. The boy lay under the low angle of the shelf and listened to a house keeping itself in the dark the way a pot keeps heat — without comment, and with one crack in it that nobody would mention until the thaw.

Sella Vail went to the fire before she went to the basket. That was the order of the house and had been the order since the first cold, and she did not have to decide it in the morning any more than a woman has to decide which hand takes the pail. She told the fire it was the thaw, saying it to the coals and not to any person, and turned them with the back of a broken pot so the ash broke over them in a fine grey dust and the heat came up again without a flame to show for it. The house will keep it, she said, where another woman would have said I. She stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand and counted under her breath toward no opening — the small intake, the hold, the letting-go — setting a row of tools in order on a board and would not have the row called a number. Then she pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the burn, and the one grey streak at her temple showed pale off the coals; the burn shaped like a little door closed on nothing and opened again to the work.

Her eye went once to the clay jar cold under its glaze on the shelf by the wall, and her hand shut half over nothing. It was a glance with no errand in it and no errand came of it. The widow’s mark hung where it hung on its cord at her throat, a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and she did not touch it, because a morning at the hearth is not an opening and the hand goes to it before the kiln and at no other hour. It had been touched once before the first firing of Smoke, held one breath, let go, and touched at no shelf, well, rack, road, basket, morning or dusk since. The one opening before Smoke 1 lay behind the household and was not looked at again, made into no lesson, no sign, no mending and no payment, with no payer named to any of it. The fire answered her with four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have stood, and she did not count the silence and did not ask who had left it there.

At the loaf shelf she kept her back to the basket and her finger on top of the front edge, so that the charcoal tally beneath the board stayed unread. She had reached a finger beneath that board on other mornings and brought it away without a word, and the boy had kept the reaching and the not-reading on two places with a finger between them. She moved her lips along a row of loaves standing with two gaps and gave neither gap a figure; the old space at the row’s end and the newer one beside it stood where they stood, and she did not join them to the empty place at his ear in the room that did not touch this one. Then she set the pot rim out on the shelf with its handle two inches beyond the reaching hand, rested her hand a half-beat longer than the setting needed, and did not look toward the bed. Two inches was a measurable thing and a child’s body was the whole of his reach, and the half-beat went where the half-beats went, on a place of its own with no cause and no owner.

The dressed grain lay covered under wool on the cool stone at the rack end, well away from the heat of the hearth. She went out to it before the yard was properly awake and lifted a corner of the wool for damp, the way a woman lifts a cloth from a sleeping child to feel whether the brow is wet, and put the corner back without looking a second time. Under the fold the grain had changed its state since the handfuls went under the wool — threshed, dressed, dried to the arm’s knowledge of its weight — and changed in state it would stay. It carried no figure. There was no slate within reach of it and no hand had entered it since the gathering, and the arm that had gathered it knew what it weighed and not what it had cost. On Smoke 30 the district would read a figure over that covered grain, and the figure would not know the grain it lay over; it would know a printed number and a levy and the space where a subtraction ought to stand, and it would pass over the heap under wool as water goes over a stone that lies where a stone lies. He kept the figure read and the price of the grain on two places and did not average them, because a thing covered is not therefore paid for and a thing not entered is not therefore free. The space between the two had no signature to lay on it. He did not make a grievance of it, for a grievance wants a debtor standing under it and none stood; he made a finding of it and put the finding on a tray of its own. Nobody under the roof found it remarkable that a number should pass over a heap of grain and not know it. That, too, he kept.

Out in the Clay Yard the frost had come thin and white on the north stone lip of the yard well in the small hours, fine fronds the like of frost-bloom, written where the Warm had lately gone out of the stone. Then the rope came over it with the cold in it, the cold iron of the well rope taking the cold and giving it to the hands that pulled, and the bucket knocked the heads off it, and the grey of the wet stone showed through. The yard called the whole of it weather and went on drawing. Whatever white had been there he kept on a place of its own with no cause — not a payer, not the shape a vessel makes when heat goes where it was not meant to go entered into a reason, not the widow’s mark, for a well is not an opening and the hand goes to the mark before the kiln and at no other hour, not the first firing of Frost, which stood far off and unbrought. Ash had turned the corner at the Vail wall into a clean dark line a year ago and a finer grey hairline had gathered beside it; the two lines lay unswept and were called the thaw, and he did not lay the white lip across either of them. Three absences in three places are three absences, and a row of them is not an account until a payer stands under it.

A neighbour came up with a pot hitched against her hip and asked what the keeping and the storing would take now the grain was under wool and the visits coming. Sella did not turn her head toward the drying racks or toward the basket. “Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there. The house has said it,” she said, and went on with the bucket, carrying the question to the weather and the pot and leaving it there. The neighbour went off level with the question still on her, nothing priced, nothing refused, nothing entered. A man came down the clay road with dried pale scurf of mud on his coat skirt and brought a number up out of a fist toward the well, the way a man brings a thing up to the light to see what it is worth. He looked at it. He put it back into his sleeve. The figure arrived nowhere, and because it arrived nowhere it was not a missing figure and no answer was owed to it; a figure supplied to a fist that had shown nothing would have been a forged one, and the boy had no hand to supply one and no wish to. The river went on with its note of water over water through the finger’s-width door, low over lower, and the man walked on up the road with his number where his number had been.

The afternoon came to the kindling as a voice before it came to a person. Tarn took the broom only after it had been instructed, the way a man takes up a tool already named, and said Yes, house to an early thaw and a late, agreeing with both so thoroughly that no argument could find a foot to stand on, and keeping the late because it puts off the paying, though the paying had stood in the ruts since the water went.
