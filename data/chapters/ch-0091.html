The Uncut Notch

Days 324–326 · Bone 25–27, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, Sill

You can lay your thumb on the notch you have not cut and leave it lying there. The knife knows where it was; the wood keeps its place; and nothing is owed for the touching.

— A saying of the kindling, told in the last tend of Bone

Bone 25, Fifthday, Y.H. 813

The hand had come down against the fold and lay there as a tool lies on a bench after use, not because the work was done but because the next hand would find it without looking. There was no sound in the putting-down, and the room went on with its own quiet, and the boy let the quiet be. Three hundred and twenty-three exact stood behind him and three hundred and twenty-four stood in front, and neither was spoken, and six months and one hundred and forty-four days were told by nobody under the Vail kiln-house roof in Low Kiln, Sill, Grath. Under the floor the carried fire gave four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have stood, its payer without a name for three hundred and twenty-four days; the salt-stiff coat with the crease at the left shoulder kept a broader man’s shape on its peg, and the sayings went to the pot and the fire and never to a person, and the name of the man the coat kept had not been spoken under the roof the same number of days. Nothing from Bone 20’s reading came in as a sign, a lesson, a mending, a cause or a payment, and the reading was not read again. Bone went on without a boundary drawn. The older women named the month by the weather that had not come and the slatemen by their count of Account-days, and neither habit was set right or left wrong; the month carried both names the way a pot carries two handles and is none the worse for it.

He took the body as a wound is taken, in its old order, because the order was the only thing in the room that had not changed. One stiff brown-edged barley crust lay two inches from his ear, gone leathery in the dry kiln heat, its corner standing a hair off the wool as though a hand had lifted it and set it down again badly; the empty place beside it kept its name to itself and no third came in the small hours to make the row come out even. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the dry heat and was unwiped, because nobody under any roof had ever been given an instruction about the thread, and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. Cold had stopped at cheek, temple and the small bare joint of the wrist where the wool rode back, as though it had come to the end of a line written out for it with neither an order to go further nor an order to go away; the Afterdrop waited beyond all three, past a warming nobody in this house would perform.

He knew the ease that cold puts on a face was not ease but a composition, and that a patient who looks settled at the end of a line may be worse before he is better: a cold vessel cannot clot, and must be warmed from the outside, and slowly, never rubbed, never set too near the fire. He knew it as exactly as he had once known where the third rib would tear the lung, and the knowing bought him nothing at two inches of air. The infant underneath wanted the crust lifted the two inches to his mouth, the thread wiped away, a hand that would close on the pot handle set beyond his reach. Both were true and the second was louder and neither won; the argument between them was the work, and it went on in the dark without a verdict and without a witness.

He had reduced a thousand shoulders in the dark and could not lift his own chin; he had packed cavities with both hands and could not close two inches on the basket’s handle. He lay in a basket and was carried like a pot filled that must not be slopped, and the discussion of him went on over his head in the third person while he understood every word and could make none of them. Before the light he read the room by its small sounds, because the small sounds do not lie and the large ones generally do: the boards giving back their heat by degrees, the fourth pull under the floor not followed by a fifth, the river through the finger’s-width door keeping its low note of water over water, a low thing over a lower thing, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming. He let the sounds arrive in their own order and did not arrange them, because arranging them wanted a hand to put under them and none stood.

Sella Vail went to the hearth before she went to the basket. That was the order of the house and had been the order before any day the boy could count, and she kept it the way another woman might keep a habit of crossing herself. The coals lay grey under their blind ash with a little heat at the heart of them, and she told them it was the thaw in the flat tone she used for the pot and the broom and the wool, and turned them with the back of a broken pot so the ash fell where she meant it to fall. The house will keep it, she said to the fire, where another woman would have said I, and the house kept it, and the coals answered four low pulls from under the floor with the silence where the fifth should have stood. She stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand and counted something under her breath — a small intake, a hold, and a letting-go — toward no opening, and gave neither gap a figure. The grey streak at her temple showed pale where she pushed the coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the burn.

At the loaf shelf her finger lay on top of the front edge, resting a little longer than the checking needed, so that the charcoal tally beneath stayed unread; her lips moved along a row standing with two gaps in it, and she figured neither gap. She glanced once at the clay jar standing cold under lead glaze on its shelf by the wall, and shut her hand half over nothing, the burn on the back of her left hand — two straight sides and a lintel and a threshold, the skin inside puckered and pale — closing on air the way a little door closes on a room nobody goes into. She did not touch the widow’s mark. A morning was not an opening and a hearth was not an opening; five openings in five months were five and not a rule, and the sixth stood at Ash 2, in the first firing of Ash, far off, not hoped for and not counted toward.

On the pot board the twice-mended pot stood with the hairline of the original break showing between the old-brass staple and the tighter wire. She put her thumb first where the wire crossed the crack, felt what the thumb had to feel, and only then let the weight come on the handle. If it’s worth mending it’s worth mending twice. The second time is the real one, she said to the pot, and Leave the crack showing. People trust a thing they can see has been handled, she said to the fire, and never to a person. The sayings were Garl Vail’s and had improved in the keeping and were very likely not as good as he had said them. The crack showed. Repair was one thing and a whole vessel another, and the house had long ago decided which of the two it would keep.

He read the pot the way he had once read a wound closed in a place the hand would not have chosen: the first staple set honestly, the second wire drawn tighter than it needed and left longer, the hairline of the original break showing dark between them. A vessel that has been mended carries its break and is the truer for showing it, which was the whole of the saying and was not a lesson anybody had paid for. The man who had first said it had gone into the Long Cold and had not come out, and the sayings had gone on being said to a pot and a fire and had not mended him. Nobody under the roof had made a doctrine of this either, and the boy did not.

Out at the rack end she turned the dressed grain beneath the wool with a touch lighter than the last time and gave no figure to it, and nothing about it that morning lifted a hand or let anything fall. The handful let fall back through her fingers on Bone 22 sat on a place of its own and was not made again; the empty fingers of the mornings since went beside it as a keeping and not as a second, for two would have been a habit wanting a payer and three a way wanting one, and none stood. She set the pot rim two inches beyond the reaching hand, with a half-beat before the setting and no look toward the bed, and went on to the next thing as though the half-beat had not been spent.

The bell’s gap left behind in the square was not fetched in to make a fourth, and the heard bell stayed in its square beside the told.

At the very end of the day, when the note had come down to its lowest under the finger’s-width door, the hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the sound. It took a small intake from the room whose floor does not touch his, held the interval whole, let the intake go on the same low note, and came straight down and laid itself back against the fold as a man puts away a tool where the next hand will find it without looking. The note had done before the hand came down, so the letting-go was neither early again nor stayed, and the hand did not stay. The inch was not a reach and the intake was not a breath kept for warmth; the interval was not a stopping of the river and the note was not his. He had merely put his small hand into the order of things for the length of one note and taken it out again, and the order went on whether he did it or not. He filed it as answer two hundred and twenty-eight: exact, complete, worth nothing at two inches of air, a restraint and not a payment, borrowing no house count and supplying no payer, laid across neither the five arithmetics, the bell’s gap, the covered grain, the handful, the thumb’s resting, the stayed object, the early letting-go nor his own kept gaps. Two hundred and twenty-eight honest answers stood on the sixth place, each an object and not a power, and neither the number nor the small hand-movings that had made it stood up and called themselves a doctrine, for a doctrine is an account that has found a payer and none stood.

Three hundred and twenty-six stood exact, complete, unspent and spoken by nobody in Low Kiln, Sill and Grath; six months and one hundred and forty-six days were told by nobody; the carried fire’s fifth was unpaid the same number of days with its payer unnamed; and the name of the man the coat kept was not spoken under the roof the same number. Nothing on the twenty-seventh had been opened, joined, named, called, paid or ended. The keeping of the number was the only honest work there had been to do all day, and it had bought nothing and was not for sale, and it went on because attention is the one thing no institution can tax. The silt-clouded plate of ice stood on its flat stone out of the path of feet; the file lay open on the towel with every gap left gap; the hand lay back against the fold on answer two hundred and twenty-eight; and the river’s low note went quiet under the finger’s-width door while Bone went on without a boundary drawn, three mornings short of the month’s third and last reading and not brought into a day that had not earned it.

The Chronicle of Chapter 91

On the twenty-fifth of Bone the house woke by the small sounds: boards giving back heat, the fourth pull under the floor with no fifth, the river going low over lower under a finger’s-width door. The boy lay with one brown-edged crust two inches from his ear, the thread at his mouth frozen in the dry heat and unwiped, cold stopped at cheek and temple, the Afterdrop waiting past a warming nobody would perform. Sella Vail went to the hearth before the basket, told the coals it was the thaw, turned them with a broken pot’s back and said The house will keep it where another woman would have said I. She lifted the twice-mended pot thumb-first where the wire crossed the crack and set its rim two inches beyond the reaching hand.

For three days the yard went around its seven walked-around places with a look and a wide step and no number --- the unswept dark line and its grey thaw, the broken birth-slates, the pan’s gone ring, the skinned trough, the field’s empty place and the returning-line place where a hand went in on Seed 15. Three coverings at the well stayed weather, a finger between every pair. A man down from Cold Acre answered the price of his hare with the weather; a man with a cracked jar was told to ask the pot, which said nothing. Three counts stood in three places and did not become one, and five arithmetics stood behind one unsigned figure up the clay road.

On the twenty-sixth Ilva drew at the well. To a woman who asked what the firings had taken she put her hand toward her sleeve and stayed; the notched birch strip did not come out and no figure came off the wood. Ask the pot what it cost. I keep the count, not the account, she said, and went back to the pail. The boy filed her reaching as a ninth hand and no tenth. At the kindling Tarn rested his thumb on the seventh notch at the lip for one breath, did not cut it or call the loan, drew back to the six and said Six counted. The next I don’t count. The boy kept the touch on a place of its own. Garl Vail stayed in objects, his name unspoken three hundred and twenty-six days.

Each day gave two answers: a small intake let go whole on the river’s note, the hand coming straight down as a tool put away where the next hand will find it. Six were entered, two hundred and twenty-three through two hundred and twenty-eight, each worth nothing at two inches of air. At the close three hundred and twenty-six stood exact, complete and spoken by nobody, six months and one hundred and forty-six days told by nobody. Nothing had been opened, joined, named, called, paid or ended. The file lay open on the towel with every gap left gap while Bone went on without a boundary drawn.
