The Water Goes Around

Days 68–74 · Silt 9–15, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, Sill

The river goes around a thing a house has set down and leaves it standing, and the yard says the river was never coming for it. Count what is left on the shelf. That is the whole of the account.

— A saying of the drying racks, Low Kiln

— Day 68 —

Silt 9, Y.H. 813

Before light the river was already over the lower steps at the Fishstairs, and coming up the road beyond them in a sheet so thin it seemed the ground had sweated it out rather than the river given it. It was not a crash. Nothing broke. The water went over the Ilm’s stone lip and across the landing where a step should have been, and then across the four steps below that, and then across the road, brown water going over brown water with a small steady sound like a cloth drawn over a table, and the sheet kept coming. Corin was carried down to the edge of it in the basket under the fold, level as a pot filled that must not be slopped, with Sella Vail’s knee turned against the wicker to keep his face out of the draught. She gave every instruction to the wool and the fire and the bucket and never once to the basket. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. “It’s the river wind.” She said the house where another woman would have said I, and stood a half-pace from every open slate, and did not touch the widow’s mark at her throat, because a road is not an opening and a basket is not a door.

The steps had counted four, four and a landing where a step should have been since the boy first came under the bridge. Now the water put them back a hand’s breadth out of line and went on over them, and the district read the water as weather and went on using them. The low houses of Low Kiln below the stone sill stood with their doorsteps in the thin brown sheet, and women set their pots on higher stones and their children on the window ledges, and nobody said what the water was doing, only what it had done to the step. A woman at a door with her skirts gathered said to the water, “You’ve come the wrong week for it,” and a woman at the next door said, “Weather,” and the first went in and shut the door on her own sentence, which was the way the district made a thing nobody owned. Another, lifting a child onto a ledge, told it to keep its feet off the step; the child asked when the step would be back, and the woman said it would come back when the water went, and when the child asked when the water would go she said weather and lifted it in through the window. At the well behind them the rope had mud in it to the last strand and came up under the fingernails of every woman who drew. The air below the bridge smelled of tallow and wet wool and river mud. The sheet went up the road past the last of the houses and kept going, and the boy, carried at the half-pace, saw brown water over brown water and the road going away under it.

Cold came in its known order — cheek, temple, wrist — and the wool did less than the wind wanted, riding back from his wrist so the cold found the place. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody wiped it, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far. The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the sound of the water and came down. Sixty-eight days. The number sat in him with the weight of a small stone he could neither set down nor lift, and he had no one to give it to and no word in Grathic in which to give it even if he had.

The soft-cuffed clerk sat at the landing with his slate held to his forearm and his cuff gone grey from washing, and brought his hand to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls. He did not speak. He had sat there for a measure nobody had called, and the eels went on being sold over the top of him, and word had come up more than once that nobody had taken him off. Once a woman laid a mark down on the stall in front of him and waited, and he did not lift his eyes from the slate, and she took the mark away again and went on with her eels; the measure he kept his place for was not called that morning either. The boy filed the order of reaching on its own place, as he filed all record-first hands, apart from the woman in the grey coat at the flare, apart from the man who had priced him, apart from the clerk at the Court’s table at the Cross and apart from the Drawing collector’s ordinary hand with no pause in it. Four hands reaching for four records and none of them joined. No fifth was made. He had learned what a row of likenesses laid too close together would do in a better-lit room, and he did not lay them close.

It was under all of it that he heard the note. It was not the small flat knock of one vessel going all the way through; he knew that sound and had kept six hearings of the full note, five from four sources and the sixth out of a person, and no seventh had come. This was a low turning, felt in the roots of his teeth and against the roof of his mouth before the ear placed it out in the water and the road and the houses. Many small failures moved inside one slow certain one. He had once stood at the head of a table and read a body that was going at the edges while the middle still held, and known which part would go first if nothing were done, and here the failing thing was a whole district, a vessel of such capacity that no hand in it could have got around it, with a thin place nobody would name. He wrote no cause across it. He set it on the place where he kept the intervals and the told bell and the heard bell and the early note out of the stone under the falls at the Singing Sill and the river’s own note going over itself behind the drying racks, and he let none of them touch. There was no word in the district’s tongue for the sound and no hand in it to point at the thin place, and the only work available to him was to file it, which he did, and which bought him nothing.

Word came up the road from the Weir before the eels were done, carried by a man with salt dried white at his wrists and river mud the colour of old rope on his boots. He said it to the boards and to whoever stood within hearing, the way a man says a thing he does not want owned. “It’s trodden, or it’s gone under,” he said. “Nobody’s been down to see which.” The weak board in the plank walk was either trodden or gone with the water; nobody had seen it go, and nobody had gone to look. A woman at the tubs said the walk would be mended when the water fell and went back to her wringing, and that was the whole of what was said about it. Nobody made anything of the board. Nobody said anybody had been on it. The boy set it where he kept the missing Fishstairs step and the hairlines that ran into the ice and stopped a thumb’s breadth short of the open water — a gap in a thing that would one day have to carry weight, a gap in a flight of stairs, a gap in a sheet of ice — and he still let nothing touch. He had been made to remember a thing that had not happened, and he kept it the way a man keeps a blade shown to him in its sheath and never seen drawn, with no hand of his own to lift it and no standing to ask who had sharpened it.

Far upriver a woman’s board lay beside a grey wool knot-string where no clerk could spell it, and the watching went on without an answer, and the word that belonged to the shape was not spoken. The day’s thin brown sheet went around both boards and reached neither.

Sella carried him up the road at the half-pace and set the basket on the higher stone where the water had not come, and turned it with her knee so the light fell where she wanted it, and gave every instruction to the fire and the wool and the bucket and never to the basket. “That’ll hold,” she said to the coals. “The house keeps it.” She told the fire it was the thaw, and the fire took the sentence the way a man takes a thing he has no answer to. She said the house where another woman would have said I. She had not touched the mark that morning and would not touch it at dusk, because there had been no opening, and she went around every open slate as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it.

Tarn was at the yard end with the small knife back on its sill and Ash worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line. He said “Yes, house” to the ones who wanted the thaw early and to the ones who wanted it late, agreed so entirely that no argument could find a foot, and went on wanting the late one because a late thaw puts off the paying, though the paying was already in the road below him. His broom went over the collector’s road without once turning along it, and his head did not turn at the note a carried vessel gave past the door. Six years were counted; the seventh was nearly gone and still held, the one entry in this house the house could not check. He did not look toward the basket, and the boy kept his silence beside his own without letting the two touch.

Under the Vail kiln-house roof the afternoon went its ordinary way, as though the road below had not moved all day. The carried fire lay under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees, and the kiln’s breath went four, four and a gap through the floor, keeping the missing breath as carefully as the four that came. Above it the boards kept a count of their own, four nails and four and a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts did not meet, coming as they did from two different places under one roof. The told bell of the Long Night, given in a dark room where no fire was lit and taken on trust; the heard bell at the Cross, four, four and a gap, under which nobody crossed himself; the early note out of the stone under the falls; the kiln’s gap through the floor. Four low sounds in four places, kept apart for two months. The district’s new note went on underneath them, enormous and unnamed, and the boy set it beside them without laying it on them, for to call it anything would have been to write a cause across five separate places. The neighbour’s saying, brought from eighteen leagues east about a room shut in the war with the damper closed and the men inside dead of the breath and received here as history with a date in it, lay on a tray of its own and had not crossed the floor into this room.

In the corner the clean dark line where the ridge had turned its corner for a year and two days lay as it had always lain, and the new hairline of grey beside it lay there still, finer than the old line, unswept, because the house said it was the thaw and it would go when it goes. A line left where it was was still a line kept, and a new line left beside it was kept the same way, and the boy set the two lines in two places and did not let one become the other. Garl Vail was kept by his salt-stiff coat on its peg, his hollow seat with its twenty-year lean, and the proverb said to the pot and not to a person; the debt against a firing of tiles lay where it lay, neither paid nor settled nor done with.

When she set the basket down at last it was on his bed under the roof and not in the Debt Loft, and she put the fold right without looking at his face. One brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold with the empty place beside it still without a name. He wanted the crust and wanted what had been taken out of the fold and got neither; the hunger was louder and the older man’s sorrow had nowhere in this house to put its hands, so it went where all of it went, into the counting. Sixty-eight days, and nobody under any roof gave a figure for it, so he kept it himself. The house kept its days in loaves moved along a shelf and a space at the end of the row that nobody called a number; the district kept its flood in fists and mouths and the one word that let a person go home warm. The boy kept sixty-eight, and the three accounts did not meet, and none of them was entered where the water had been.

The door stood a finger’s width open. Through the gap came the low note of the river moving in its thin brown sheet up the road past the last low house, nearer than it had been at the well or under the boards in the afternoon, and through the floor came the fire, four, four and a gap. The two kept their separate places as they had kept them for two months. The sheet went up the road past the last house and kept going, and the note that went with it had no word in the district’s tongue and no hand to point and no cause written across it. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the sound of it and came down. Nothing had been joined, nothing paid, nothing explained, nothing ended. The count went on.

First light found the water sitting on the second step at the Cross and going nowhere, and Account-day holding itself on the step above it, which was dry. The tenth of Silt came into the square with mud on its boots, and neither the bell nor the slate waited for it; the market arrived the way water arrived in this district, by spreading, and took its place around the standing water the way a pot takes a place on a shelf that has been cleared for it. Below the reading block the step lay under an inch of brown water gone flat and glossy in the thin light. Above it two hundred people leaned. A man selling turnips set his heap on a flat stone and went on with his morning; a woman at the eel trays lifted the rim of a creel an inch higher and set it down again. That was the whole of what the flood had done to trade so far. Beyond the square the Ilm had come up in the night and stopped, and the place went on being a place with water in it, as a cracked cup goes on being a cup and holds what it can.

Corin was carried down in the basket under the fold, level as a pot filled that must not be slopped, and set in the moot-post’s finger’s width of shade with the cold iron of the post at his cheek. Sella Vail chose the place with her knee, went around every open slate at a half-pace going down, and gave every instruction to the wool and never to the basket. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. “It’s the river wind.” She said the house where another woman would have said I. Her hand did not go to the widow’s mark at her throat, because a reading was not an opening and she had carried that rule through a year and into this one. Sixty-nine days. The boy held the number with no hand to set it down and no word in Grathic in which to hand it over. Cold came in its known order — cheek, temple, wrist — and the wool did less than the wind wanted, riding back from his wrist so the cold found the place. His head went where it wanted and came back with the small wet sound. The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the standing water and came down.

Marks passed along the stalls and bread went over the boards, and nobody counted what passed except in the fist that closed and the mouth that thought better of speaking. Two half-loaves went one way and a string of eels another, and the marks laid beside them did not agree, and nobody laid them straight. A woman counted her change twice and put it in her sleeve without the second count changing anything. The boy lay with the shade on his face and the drool freezing at his mouth, and kept the arithmetic of the square in the only place where it could be kept, which was inside him, and it bought him nothing he could lift a hand to.

Berr Clay came up short at the edge of the shade, where the step was dry and the crowd had to lean in to hear him. The red wool cord of his office was folded under his coat instead of worn across it, and there was green of tasted glaze at one corner of his mouth, and hot clay under the smell of nothing at all, which is the dearest smell in Grath. He wiped his thumb twice on his apron before he touched nothing. He held a narrow slate unopened against his forearm and passed a finger over what was written and let his eyes rest on it a breath longer than the writing required, as though to make certain the figure had not moved in the night.

“One in twelve, as the notice says,” he said, low, into the leaning top of the crowd. He said it twice, once for the moot and once for the households that had come late, and the second reading went over the water and over the basket as the first had, and he never looked into the basket and never had. The levy was called, he said, and it was not called by him. A household that had heard otherwise had heard wrong. He put himself out of his own sentence as neatly as a hand taken out of a field before the dressing goes on, and the sentence stood up by itself afterward with nobody in it to answer for it. The crowd leaned and took it, and the pigs went over the weighing board with their legs tied and their small hard eyes shut, going where the string took them.

The true subtraction went over the top of the basket and into the noise of the stalls and was spoken by nobody. What the flood would cost went from a fist to a mouth to a shoulder and arrived nowhere; a man shut his hand on a door-latch and stood a moment with it shut, and the boy watched the fist open onto nothing and the shoulder go away without the number on it. Nobody asked the slate to hold it. The boy had once stood at the end of a table where a chart closed a matter while the note that would have opened it again lay turned down beside it, and he had learned there, with an older man’s anger that had never once reached his mouth, what a figure does when it is read aloud in a room that has already decided it is settled. A figure read aloud was not the figure paid. The printed one went into the morning and stayed where it was put. The other one went around in sleeves and shoulders and got spent on nothing. He set them on two trays as he set the low sounds on their own places, and spent neither, and there was no word in the district’s tongue for the difference between them.

A man at the edge of the crowd, with the water to his boot-top and a number shut in his fist, began to ask what the rise would take out of the households below the sill. Berr did not turn toward him. “A number goes into a slate, not into a well,” he said to the step and the notice and the top of the crowd. “If the house wants it again, there’ll be a slate at the watch.” He said the house and not my account, and took himself out of the sentence again; the man put the fist back in his sleeve and went home with his question still standing where he had left it, which was the same place it had stood before he asked. The boy kept the soft sentence and the shut fist on two places and did not lay the one over the other, though they had come out of the same breath.

The bell went four, four and a gap over the square, longer than any bell hung in a temple, and nobody crossed himself. It held on after the pigs were through the board and let go of the square only when the moot had settled back into its leaning. A woman at the turnip heap started at the third stroke and put her hand to the neck of her dress, and the hand went to her apron instead and wiped it, and she went on pricing turnips; the moot leaned at the slate as though nothing had sounded. The boy kept the bell he had heard in the square beside the told bell of the Long Night, given in a dark room where no fire was lit and taken on trust, and the early note out of the stone under the falls at the Singing Sill, and the missing breath of the kiln’s breath coming up through the boards under his own roof; four low things in four places kept apart for two months, and now the new note of a whole district under water going on underneath all four, enormous and unnamed. That nobody had crossed himself lay on a place of its own and did not join any of them, and he wrote no cause across the four low sounds and the new note and the missing sign. Six hearings of the full note stayed six, five from four sources and the sixth out of a person, filed beside the five and never among them; no seventh had come. Three nearly-full answers stood advanced and not paid. The word that belonged to the shape of a double turn stayed a saying on its own tray with the shape beside it and the weight of a thumb apart from both, and was not spoken in the square. He did not lay one of these on another. A likeness laid on a likeness will grow a cause whether or not anyone put one there, and he had learned in a better-lit room what a man can do with an answer he wants too much.

Word came up the step from the direction of the Fishstairs that the soft-cuffed clerk was still set there, with his cuff gone grey from washing and his slate held to his forearm, his hand going to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls, for a measure nobody had called. Nobody had taken him off. The eels went on being sold over the top of him. The boy filed the order of reaching on the place where he kept all record-first hands, apart from the woman who had written at the flare, apart from the man who had priced him, apart from the clerk behind the Court’s table and apart from the Drawing collector with his ordinary hand and no pause in it. Four hands reaching for four records and none of them joined; no fifth was made. Behind the clerk’s soft sentence stood the old anger at a chart signed and closed and the note never read, and it stayed behind, because there was no one under any roof in Sill to whom he could say it and no Grathic word in which to say it if there had been. He had the printed figure and the unspoken subtraction on two trays that did not touch. The clerk’s slate, still waiting for its uncalled measure, went on a third, and the three places stayed three.

Berr closed the narrow slate without opening it and went off toward High Kiln with the red cord folded away under his coat and the box in his house still locked and the true deduction inside it, believing he had done the district a kindness. The crowd opened and shut behind him. From the post’s shade the boy watched the back go up the road, the clean hands carrying a figure that would not be spent where it had been read, and the warmth of the afternoon came off the wet boards in a thin smell of clay and iron. One brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold at his ear, and the empty place beside it went through another day without a name. He wanted the crust and wanted what had been taken out of the fold and could lift a hand to neither. The trays did not touch. Nothing had been joined, nothing paid, nothing explained, nothing ended.

Morning came up over the yard without clearing it. The water stood where the night had set it down — in the ruts of the Clay Yard, in the shallow pans the slip pits had worn, along the foot of the drying racks where the poles went into mud the colour of a pot washed out into a bucket and left to settle. It had stopped coming. What it did now was go around. It went around the heap of spent slip, around the upturned barrow, around the stone a woman had set for a step, taking a little from each and leaving the rest, the way a hand goes over a table and does not lift what lies on it. There was no crash in it and no breaking. Corin was carried to the edge in the basket under the fold, level as a pot filled that must not be slopped, with Sella Vail’s knee turned against the wicker to keep his face out of the draught. She gave every instruction to the wool and the fire and the bucket and never once to the basket. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. “It’s the river wind.” She said the house where another woman would have said I, and kept her half-pace from every open slate, and did not touch the widow’s mark at her throat, because a rack is not an opening and a basket is not a door.

The rack-women worked around it the way they worked around everything in this yard, with their feet and their skirts and a word spoken to the mud. One laid a board across two stones and set her wet slips on it, and a girl held the end down with her toe. Another pushed a creel an inch higher and let it stand. It came up around the legs of the rack and did not take the rack, and went on around the next pole, and the next. A woman come up from the lower yard with her skirts gathered said it to the poles and the mud and the sky, as people say a thing in this district that they do not want owned: the water had gone around the ridge and left it standing. A woman at the next rack said the house was saved. That was the whole of what was said about it. Nobody fetched a broom. Nobody went to look closer. A man with river mud to his calves came up, looked at the standing water, spat into it, and said it would go around again when it came again, and went back down the yard. The girl kept her toe on the end of the board. The slips dried where they lay.

A man come up from the water’s edge with salt dried white at his wrists shut his hand on a figure and said the flood would cost the district a number; the number went from the fist to a mouth and from the mouth to a shoulder and arrived at nobody, and he went off with it still shut in his hand. The boy kept it on the tray where he kept the other numbers that went around and were never paid. Cold came in its known order — cheek, temple, wrist — and the wool did less than the wind wanted, riding back from his wrist so the cold found the place. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody wiped it, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far. The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the standing water and came down.

Sella did not say it. She had carried him to the edge at the half-pace, and she stood with the burn on the back of her hand shut on nothing and said the house where another woman would have said I, and gave the instruction to the fire and the wool and the bucket, and turned the basket with her knee so the light fell where she wanted it and not where the draught came. “That’ll hold,” she said to the coals, though there was no fire under the racks to hold. She told the water nothing. She told the fire it was the thaw.

Seventy days. The number sat in him with the weight of a small stone he could neither set down nor lift, and there was no one under any roof in Sill to give it to and no word in Grathic in which to hand it over even if there had been.

They went down the yard. Sella carried him at the half-pace past the house three doors down where the door stood a finger’s width open and the mud lay against the step, and past the death-house two doors down, and the water had gone around that corner too. The ridge there had once been a finger’s width thicker than Vail’s and had turned the corner of the room without a break; now there was a line of old dark where it had been burned and called dealt with, and the water stood off that line as it had stood off everything else, a brown sheet gone flat in the thin light with a skin on it that the wind moved and did not break. Nobody had swept the line. Nobody had set a bucket under the door. The boy took it in from the height of the basket: the dark line, the brown water standing a hand’s breadth short of it, the wet shine where the mud had come up and stopped. He had learned in a better-lit room what a man can do with a likeness laid too near to another likeness, and he set this line on a place of its own and did not lay the water on it or let either touch the other rooms of the same shape he kept, and wrote no cause across any of them. Garl Vail’s ridge was a different ridge. The line two doors down was a different line. He set them in two places, and neither place was a third.

At the corner of the Vail kiln-house the water had come up to within a hand’s breadth of the wall and turned. The clean dark line where Ash had turned that corner for a year and two days was dry above it, the colour of a board that has been rained on and dried, and the mud came to its lower edge and stopped as if a hand had drawn the line and told the water to keep to the other side of it. The boy lay with his cheek against the cold of the basket bow and looked at the narrow strip of dry wall between the mud and the line. It was no wider than two thumbs. It was the whole width of the house’s standing.

Beside the clean dark line, higher up the wall where the damp had not reached, the new hairline of grey lay where it had lain since Silt 2, finer than the old line had ever been, a scratch a man might have taken for a hair caught in the lime. It had not been swept. The house said it was the thaw and it would go when it goes, and it had not gone, and no hand had gone to it with a broom. The boy set three things in three places that did not touch: the line that stood, the water that had gone around it, the new grey that lay beside it unswept. Three objects in three places. He had no word in the district’s tongue for the difference between a thing left where it lay and a thing taken away, and the only work available to him was to set them down inside himself where nobody could read them, which he did, and which bought him nothing he could lift a hand to.

And here, without a word and without an argument, something went into the file that was not a finding and not a rule. The water had gone around a thing the house had set down and the thing was still there. To leave a thing where it is and have it still kept: that was what the water had done. Nobody in the yard made anything of it. The woman at the rack had said the house was saved and gone back to her slips, and the man with mud at his calves had spat and gone down the yard, and no one had stood over the line with a proverb or a slate. He had once stood at the head of a table and watched a wound left open take a man, and here a house left a line standing and kept it, and he would not lay the two on one tray; he had learned what an answer wants too much will do to honest witnesses. He felt the pull of it where his old life had taught him the opposite — that what is left unentered goes bad, that a wound left open is a wound lost, that a chart turned down is a chart already closed — and felt the infant in him want the crust two inches from his ear and the breast under the wool, and both wanted, and neither was loud enough to become a cause. He filed the feeling as an object on its own tray, as he filed the intervals and the told bell and the low note of a district under water, and left it lying there unread. It was not an answer. It was a thing the water had done.

Sella came in with wet wool and woodsmoke on her and the sour mineral tang of Ash under both, and went to the fire before she went to anything else. The carried fire lay under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees, and the kiln’s breath went through the floor four, four and a gap, keeping the missing breath as carefully as the four that came. Above it the boards kept their other count, four nails and four and a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts did not meet, coming as they did from two places under one roof.

He held his broom in both hands and did not sweep with it. Fine grey slate dust was worked into both forearms past the bend of his little finger, grey and fine and not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel; the slip he came from ordinarily dried to the colour of old bone and sat on the surface, while this had gone into the creases and would not come away with a wipe of the hand. He said nothing about where he had been. He said nothing about the two men.

He spoke to the wheel before he spoke to anyone, as he always did. “It doesn’t care whose hands,” he said. “It cares if they’re steady.” The wheel made no answer. He set a pot on the head and turned it, and told it it would do, in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either. He knew the full note of a Vail pot before it left the wheel — knew it in the hand, before the knuckle told anybody else — and would not have said so in front of a kiln-master from High Kiln, who would have called it early and made something of it. The boy put the knowing on the tray where questions lasted for years, with no hand of his own to lift the lid.

There was wet clay in the shed and tallow on the shelf, and on a bad wind the sweetish edge of lead glaze came up from High Kiln and put a false sweetness in the mouth. The boy held the dusty forearms and the clean ones on one tray as two states of one morning. He asked neither which slate had been broken nor whether it had been a birth-slate. The two states did not become a cause, because three likenesses laid in a row will grow a cause whether or not anybody put one there, and he had learned in a better-lit room what a man can do with an answer he wants too much.

“The fire’s low, Jorra,” Sella said. She had not finished the sentence. His bent finger went in ahead of his hand at the damper and moved it, and the end of her instruction arrived at the pot instead of at him, so that the household straightened around his silence the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted off one corner. Nobody gave an order. The pots went on standing in their rows.

Tarn had set the small bench knife on its sill before light and left it there. Six years stood on the handle in a mark worn almost flat by his thumb, and the seventh was nearly gone and still held back, the one entry in this house the house could not check. He told the empty yard that the knife was where it could be checked, and did not tell the yard what he was counting, because the yard had heard it enough times to be tired of it and a man does not say the same thing twice to a house that does not answer. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line out at the ridge, and the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back.

His broom went over the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector, over and over, without once turning to look along it. A vessel gave its note somewhere past the door — a pot set down, or a cup put on a bench, he did not turn to find which — and his head did not turn either. He went back to his stroke. The loan that can hear stayed uncalled. The sentence that would call it lay under the roof where it had lain for six years, and the two men under that roof kept it without either of them saying it, Jorra’s silence and Tarn’s silence sitting side by side as the same shape and not joined.

The boy lay under the roof and watched the house sort its own members. It swept around what it would not remove, it entered what it could enter in a roll it showed, it counted whose forearms and whose years and did not count whose silence. He was the one thing in the room that was neither swept around nor entered nor counted: he was set down, like a pot set down, and left. He had once known the other side of that work, the side that decides which body is worked on and which is waited on, and here the deciding was done around him and he was the body waited on. He made no cause of it. He had no standing to ask why a house keeps some things by leaving them alone and others by writing them down, and the surgeon in him and the child in him wanted different things — the surgeon wanted the rule, the child wanted the crust two inches from his ear — and the child, as usual, was louder.
