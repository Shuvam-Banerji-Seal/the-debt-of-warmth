The River Stirring

Days 48–54 · Ilmen 19–25, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, Sill

The man who said the ice held has stopped saying it. The man who knows the number keeps it in his fist. Between them a board waits over the water, and the river comes up as it always comes.

— A saying of Low Kiln, Sill

— Day 48 —

Ilmen 19, Y.H. 813

The frost had written again on the stone lip of the yard well in the first thin light, white fronds standing up where the Warm had lately gone out of the stone, a branching map laid down overnight in a hand nobody had hired and no slate would keep. It was the same shape that ran down his own ribs under the wool, the red flower the midwife had crossed herself at and called the birth-flower; he had carried that shape for forty-eight days and had never been told what it counted. Here it stood on a well-lip in Low Kiln in white, as if a thing written on him had been written back onto the stone in the night. Corin lay in the basket with the fold turned right under his chin, and the fronds stood a finger high above the place where the bucket would come down.

A neighbour let the bucket down and the rope went over the lip without touching the fronds, the way a hand goes around a thing it has decided not to see. The woman at the tubs who had said all winter that the ice would hold stood with her wrists in the wash and said nothing at all now, and the man who had come up from the river in the warm spell and called the ice honeyed under the mud kept his counsel too and went off toward the road with his hands in his sleeves. They had had an argument once and the argument had ended in weather; now the weather had taken the argument and left nobody to carry it. frost-bloom was the word the yard gave the writing on the stone, and the word let every woman go home warm without owning the morning.

A rack-woman came for water with a pot under her arm and stopped at the lip. “It’ll go soft before its time,” she said, and looked at the fronds as if they had been written there by somebody who owed her money. The woman at the tubs did not answer. There was a place in the morning where a sentence used to stand, the one about the nights being cold and the ice holding, and neither woman filled it. The rack-woman set her pot down out of the draught, which is what a person does with a pot when there is nothing left to say over it, and let the bucket come to her. The boy registered the small silence with the exactness he had once brought to a chart: not an absence of sound but a space shaped like a sound, kept in its own place and not joined to the others.

Sella Vail came across with the little-door burn closing on the bucket handle and then on nothing, and set the basket down where the lip did not cast its shade. She looked at the white on the stone the way she looked at a thing that had been left where it lay, and gave the instruction to the fire before she gave it to the bucket. “It’s the thaw,” she told the fire. To the bucket: “Easy. There’s a skin on it.” She said the house where another woman would have said I, and she did not touch the widow’s mark at her throat, because a well is not an opening. The fire took the word and went on keeping what it kept, and the bucket came up with a little silt in it that everybody called the spring and nobody entered.

The window of the Vail kiln-house stood toward the yard with its oiled paper stretched in a frame of split lath, and the paper sat slack. It had lifted hard in a dry cold on the morning he came under the roof; now it hung in its frame without taking the wind, and the frame showed through it as a shape with nothing in it. Tarn saw the slack before the light did. He came past with the broom under his arm and the Ash worked into the creases of his knuckles that no scrubbing lifted, and his eye went a degree to the paper and back to the road, and he said nothing. A debt with a broom notices when a thing stops lifting and does not report it. The boy kept the slack frame and set it beside the honeyed ice and the white writing on the lip, three shapes the district had not entered and would call weather before the pot was cold. He had learned to read the edge of a thing without touching it, and reading was the whole of the work available to him.

The pale clip-ring at Tarn’s left wrist caught what light there was and gave it back, and he put the broom to the dust under the window as if the slack paper were a thing a man sweeps around and not a thing he looks at. Six years of the seven were behind him and he held the seventh where he always held it, and there was no word in the yard for a frame that had stopped taking the wind. He did not say the paper had gone slack. He did not say the ice had been honeyed or that it would hold. He agreed with the ones who wanted the thaw early and the ones who wanted it late and went on wanting the late one, because a late thaw puts off the paying. The boy kept the degree of his turning beside his own noticing and did not let the two touch. Two people under one roof had marked a thing and said nothing of it, and the house had no name for the equality.

Sella carried the basket a few paces into the well’s shade out of the draught and set it down as she set a pot that must not be slopped, and turned the fold right under his chin without looking at his face. The well stood at the low end of the Clay Yard, where the houses gave onto the racks and the ground went soft under the rope. One crust lay in the fold where two had lain. The spent one from Stone 15 was stiff past the smell of anything and lay where it had always lain, uneaten and unlifted and unnamed, and no third crust had come in all the days since. Beside the one crust was the empty place, and the empty place was the warmth he had most wanted and could least reach. He carried it into the new day as he had carried it across the last one, without a name and without a cause, the way a man carries a wound he has learned not to dress in words. Grathic had no word for a thing taken and not spoken of, and he had no word of his own.

When the wind turned off the water the thin breath of barley that used to come from the smoky crust did not come. He had known the smell on the afternoon it stopped and he knew the shape of its absence now on the morning after, and the absence had the exactness of a wound. It was not the loss of heat, which is a number on a slate, but the loss of a thing, which is a place in the hand. He wrote no cause across it. He set Sella’s hand, Alda Reed’s hand and Mira Silt’s hand on three places on a tray and would not let the tray become a cause, because he had learned in a room with better light what three likenesses laid in a row will do to a man who wants an answer. He did not think who had taken the crust, which was not the same thing as not knowing there were hands that might have taken it.

The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the crust and came down, which was the whole of the distance between the basket and the warmth he most wanted. He had once stood at a bedside and known, before anyone spoke, that a body had turned; the knowledge had come into his hands and not into his head. Here the knowledge came the same way and ended in the same place, an inch short. He did not try again. A man who has learned what an inch costs does not spend it twice before the fire is fed.

The ear held six hearings of the full note — five from four sources, a cup across the birth room, a pot in the Vail wheel-shed through the wall, the district’s slate twice at the Cross, a man giving under the iron tube at the collection, and the sixth from a person, out of Dessa Silt as the men lifted — and the six stayed six with no seventh at the well or anywhere this morning. Beside them lay the nearly full note of Ilmen 6 on its own tray, advanced and not paid, neither a hearing nor the third thing. The word for nearly was not in the district’s tongue and there was no word of his worth spending on it. He had no hand to tap either pot and ask it to say the thing again, and the ear that would have placed a flaw under a knuckle belonged to a body that could not lift an arm to its own chin.

The body humiliated him exactly, as it had every morning of his forty-eight days. A thread of drool froze at his mouth and nobody wiped it, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and cold arrived in its known order, cheek first, then temple, then wrist, the wool doing less than the wind wanted. The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the bucket and came down. Reducing shoulders had been ordinary work in the life before this one; keeping his own hand on a bucket’s rim was not, and the joke had not gone stale and had never once been funny enough to say aloud. He was the only adult at this well and the least use in it.

Sella gave every instruction to the fire and the wool and the bucket and never to the basket. “Keep it off him,” she said to the wool; “it’s the river wind.” To the bucket she said “That’ll do” when it came up, and to the fire that the thaw would have to be fed before it took the house. She never once put the boy’s name into a sentence that asked something of somebody. When a slate stood open propped at the end of the drying racks she went around it as wide as if it had been a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it over, and she did not set the basket near it and did not say why. Behind the racks the Slip Brook ran clay-heavy and a little higher than it should, and the road beyond went down toward the Fishstairs and the market, and neither the brook nor the road was entered in anything the boy could read.

A neighbour came toward the well with a slate under her arm and stopped a hand’s breadth short of the basket, the way a person stops short of a pot that is doing work. “Whose house?” she asked, not of him. “The house,” Sella said, and gave the question to the fire: “Feed it before you feed yourself. It’ll still be there in the morning. You won’t.” The neighbour looked at the fronds, which the sun had begun to take off the lip, and went back to her tubs with the argument still unsettled. She had got no I from Sella and no name for the boy, and she went home warm without owning a day of it. He kept the order of Sella’s reaching — fire first, pot second, the instruction to the wool third and never to the basket — on a tray of its own and did not let it become an answer.

At the turn of the wind she lifted the basket off the dust and carried him in. She gave the instruction to the wool on the way and said the house would be warmer inside, which was true of the house and was not a promise to him. Under the Vail roof she set him on his bed and not in the Debt Loft, and put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without once looking at his face. Behind the door Garl Vail’s salt-stiff coat kept a broader man’s shape on its peg and the hollow seat kept its twenty-year lean and nobody filled either, and the clean dark line where the Ash had turned its corner was not swept, because a line left where it is is still a line kept. One of his proverbs went to the pot and another to the fire, and neither went to the basket.

From the bed he could see the inner edge of the window and the frame standing with its paper slack, a rectangle with a little air in it. In the birth room and the death-house two doors down and the Court’s room heard through a doorway there were a bench and a pot and a draught along the floor, and here there were the same bench, the same pot, the same draught and the same gap. Six rooms with a gap in them, and he kept them in six places and had not written a cause across any one. The slack frame lay in a place of its own and did not join them.

Behind him the carried fire kept under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees and taking its breath in fours. Four, four and a gap. It had kept that count through the burning of the ridge and the taking of a woman into the water and the going-out of one crust where two had lain, and it would keep it after this house was cold and another house had the keeping of it. The coal that had gone up the river on the back of a brine-gull was entered in no slate and missed in no account, and the fire had not missed it and the house had not missed it. Nobody in the district had found a word for the not-calling of a collector for the woman the brook took, and nobody had made a doctrine of it; the omission lay where it fell and the morning came, and neither was entered.

The boy lay on his bed, forty-eight days old, with one crust in the fold and an empty place beside it, the oiled paper slack in the window behind him and the fire missing one breath in every four. The frost on the lip would be gone by noon and the yard would call it weather, the woman who used to say the ice held would go on saying nothing, and the man who had called it honeyed would keep his counsel. He kept the slack frame and the white writing and the missing smell in three places that did not touch; the six hearings stayed six, the nearly-full note lay on its own tray advanced and not paid, and the three hands stayed on their tray and did not become a cause. Nothing had been joined or paid or explained. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth and came down. The count went on.

The Vail kiln-house was still dark when Sella Vail lifted the basket off the bed and took the house down with her in the smell of wet wool and cold ash. She carried him the way a pot is carried when it is filled to the lip and the slip has not set: both hands under, elbows in, the little-door burn shut on the handle, her feet going where they had already decided to go and nothing slopping over. Corin rode level in a fold turned right under his chin, forty-nine days old and awake to every jar of the carrying. The instruction went to the wool. “Keep it off his ears,” she said. “It’s the river wind.” She did not say the boy and she did not look down.

They went down the Clay Yard at the half-pace she kept from every open slate — not the slow of a woman carrying and not the haste of one with a market to reach, but a pace measured off a thing she would not step between: a hand and the writing it was going to. Where a slate stood open propped at a doorway she went around it as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it over; if a woman had a slate under her arm and a finger already on its edge, Sella waited for the finger to finish before she passed. He kept the distance she kept. He had once known a man go past a chart with his hands in his pockets and his eyes on the door, and the distance had been the same distance, held for the same reason and given to a different god. She gave this one to the house and not to him, and there was no word for it in Grathic and no cause written across it.

By the road the sky had come up the colour of skimmed milk over the Ilm, and the frost that had written on the well-lip the day before was water in the ruts with nothing in it a person could read. Account-day had the yard by the throat already. Men went down before them with pigs on strings and slates under their arms, and the pig went where the string took it and the slate where the hand took it, and neither stopped for a basket. A draught came up off the water with silt in it and the thin smell of cold iron. The boy’s drool froze at his mouth and nobody wiped it, because a basket carried is a pot carried and a pot’s rim is not wiped in the street. His head went where it wanted and came back with the small wet sound, and cold arrived in the known order, cheek, then temple, then wrist. The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the fold and came down. Reducing shoulders had been ordinary work once; keeping a wool fold under his own chin was not, and the joke had not gone stale and had never been funny enough to say aloud. He was the only adult on this road and the least use in it.

At the Cross the market had settled itself around the block the way a pot settles into the hollow of a shelf: pigs at the weighing board, turnips in a heap with figures left standing on them side by side and never made to agree, eels and hoop-strips, the Court’s table set at the Slate Row fringe with its top slate laid ready. Sill had come down in its thousands, or so it sounded, and Low Kiln had come with it and brought its cold in the folds of its coats. A pig went over the board and made a sound like a slate dragged over stone, and the weigher called a figure nobody wrote down and went on with the next pig.

Sella set the basket in the moot-post’s finger’s width of shade out of the draught and turned the fold right at his chin without once looking at his face. The post was cold iron and his cheek took it first. She did not put her hand to the widow’s mark at her throat, because a reading is not an opening; the mark stayed where it lay under her collar while the market went on around it. “Keep the wind off him,” she told the wool, and to the basket she said nothing at all.

The printed notice hung from a nail in the post above his head, a square of paper soft at one corner, with a row of marks the wind kept turning over and back. He could see the row and could not read it. In a little while the row would be spoken aloud over the top of the basket, and he would lie inside the number it made and could not correct it, as a body lies under a chart nobody has read to it. A neighbour with a slate under her arm stopped a hand’s breadth short of the basket and asked what house had sent it.

“The house,” Sella said, and gave the rest of the sentence to the wind: “It’ll do till the pigs are through.” She did not explain the reading. The neighbour went off with her slate still under her arm and no more of an answer than she had come with.

Berr Clay came to the block a little before the bell, with the red wool cord of his office folded under his coat instead of worn across it and the green of tasted glaze at one corner of his mouth. He smelled of hot clay and, under it, of nothing at all, which is the most expensive smell in a district that sells its warmth by the measure. His hands were large and clean. He wiped his thumb twice on his apron before he touched the slate, and after the second wipe he did not touch it with the thumb at all; it went back to his side and the slate stayed clean. The crowd leaned in because he spoke low. That was how the figure got heard — not by shouting but by two hundred people bending their backs toward a man who would not raise his voice.

“One in twelve, as the notice says,” he said into the top of the leaning crowd. He let it stand a moment, then added the part that made it theirs: “If your household has heard otherwise, your household has heard wrong.” A finger went over the figure on the slate and came back, making sure it was still where he had put it. “The levy is called for the thaw. It is not called by me.”

The true subtraction went over the top of the basket and was spoken by nobody. It went the way a number goes in this district: a fist shut on a door-latch, a mouth that thought better of speaking, a shoulder lifted at a man who had done neither. It arrived nowhere. The boy lay under the notice with one crust and an empty place in the fold and the printed figure over him, and he could not spend the reading. The institution’s most ordinary sentence had been said in a market voice and accepted, and the acceptance had gone around the pigs like a passing dish.

A neighbour who had a figure of his own in his mouth began to ask it. “If the ice goes soft before —”

“A number goes into a slate, not into a well,” Berr said. “If your house wants it, there’ll be a slate at the watch.” He said the house and the slate and never I, and took himself out of his own sentence as neatly as a hand out of a field before the dressing goes on. The neighbour shut his mouth on the rest of the question. The crowd went back to the pigs, which was what crowds do when a man has given them a sentence they can carry home without owning it.

The bell went four, four and a gap over the market, longer than any church bell in Sill, and nobody crossed himself. It was a Court instrument doing a temple’s work with a Court’s hand, and the weighing went on under it as if a bell were only a bell. The boy kept the told bell of the Long Night on its own tray beside the bell he was hearing now and did not let the two touch: one had been spoken in the dark by warm people telling a story, and one was iron in open air over a weighing board, and both kept the missing stroke in the same place. He wrote no cause across the likeness. He had learned in a better-lit room what likenesses will do to a man who lays three of them in a row.

The five pauses stayed five objects on their own tray, unjoined; the three intervals from Stone 13, Stone 17 and Stone 25 stayed on another; and the nearly full answer of Ilmen 6 lay on its own still, advanced and not paid, neither a hearing nor the third thing. The neat dark hand had not shown itself a sixth time in all the morning, and the collector at the drawing with his ordinary hand and no pause in it stayed in the place the boy had put him, neither joined to the five nor called as a sixth.

Word came up from the north bridge with a neighbour who had been down for eels: the clerk was still set at the Fishstairs with his cuff grey from washing and his slate held to his forearm, and nobody had taken him off. It was the eleventh morning. The boy had not been carried past the steps this day and had not seen the man, but he knew the order of the thing as surely as if he had: the hand went to the slate before the eyes went to the stalls, and the slate was for a measure nobody had called. He filed the order on a tray of its own, with Berr’s twice-wiped thumb beside it and the hand at the well that had gone to a slate before a child, and did not make one hand of the three. He filed the absence of a draw for Dessa Silt, eleven mornings gone and uncollected, as an interval with the outline of a sound that did not come. The clerk did not speak. He was not the woman at the flare light and not the soft-cuffed man at the Court’s table; the boy had filed both elsewhere and did not bring them here. There was no sixth sighting to add and no name to give the man, and he was left a fact on his own tray for a measure nobody called.

Berr read the figure a second time for the households that came late, and the boy could not spend either reading. He had lain inside it twice and it had bought him nothing he could lift a hand to: no word, no slate, no warmth entered in any account that would own him. At home the arithmetic of the house would be waiting in loaves moved along a shelf and a glance at the clay jar on the wall shelf, with a space where a loaf should stand and no figure spoken; he would keep the count exactly beside it, one crust and an empty place in the fold. Tarn at the yard and Jorra Vail at the wheel had silences of their own to keep under one roof and were not here to hear a number read over a basket, and the boy did not need them to. A loan that can hear is a loan that can be called, but there was nobody in the market to call either of them and no sentence said under any roof about it.

At midday Sella lifted the basket off the post. She did not look into it and said nothing about where they had been or what had been read over the top of him; the instruction went to the wool again, and the house got the sentence another woman would have spoken to the boy. The pigs were still at the board behind them and the bell was still doing the temple’s work in the open air, four, four and a gap, dying by degrees as the yard took them back. Up the Clay Yard the ruts had dried to the colour of old bone and the draught had the silt out of it now. The fold was at his chin and one crust lay in it with an empty place beside it, carried through another day without a name; no third had come and nobody had said the one that went was gone. The figure he could not spend went up the yard inside him, and the house’s arithmetic waited at the end of it in loaves and glances with no number spoken. The the Clay Yard was still the colour of wet ash when Sella Vail set the basket down at the end of the the drying racks, out of the draught that came off the Slip Brook, and turned the fold right under his chin. Corin was fifty days old and awake to the setting-down, the way a pot is awake to the hand that lifts it and knows before the hand does what is going to be done with it. The instruction went to the wool. “Keep it off his ears,” she said. “It’s the river wind.” She said the house where another woman would have said I, and did not look into the basket, and went to the fire before she went anywhere else, because the fire came first in that house and had for four generations.

By the time the light got up over the racks the men had begun. The boy lay where a pot is laid to dry, at the edge of the floor where nothing would be set on him and nobody would step between him and the writing they had come to do. He had once known a man go past a chart in a better-lit room with his hands in his pockets and his eyes on the door, and the care here had the same shape, though it was given to a different god and cost a different kind of attention. He kept the distance because she kept it. The cold came at cheek, then temple, then wrist, and the drool at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had wiped it, because a basket set down is a pot set down and a pot’s rim is not wiped in the yard. There was no word in Grathic for going around a thing you would not step between, and he wrote no cause across it.

Then Jorra Vail came out of the room off the the Vail wheel-shed with fine grey slate dust worked into the creases of his forearms past the bend of his little finger. It was not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel. The slip dried pale and sat on top of the skin where a man could see it; this dust had gone into the creases and stayed there, grey as the inside of a slate, ground fine as meal and rubbed in with the sweat of a hand that had been splitting stone. He said nothing about where he had been. His eyes went to the door, to the fire, to the wheel, and came back without reporting, which was how his eyes always went.

The boy kept the dust and the clean slip of other mornings in two places on a tray and could not ask which of them meant what. The men in the room off the shed were not named by anyone, and the broken birth-slates under the racks stayed where they had been laid, gone around by everybody and entered by nobody. He had learned that a room a house does not enter is still a room, and that the space under a rack where whole slate should have stood is still a space, and that two facts laid side by side will invent a cause for a man who wants one badly enough. He did not want one badly enough. He had learned the cost of wanting in a better-lit room. He read the grey into the creases of the forearms as he had once read a stain under a nail on a hand that swore it had not been there — exact, unsentimental, and worth nothing to anyone who could not see it.

“You’ll do,” Jorra said to a pot on the bench, in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either. He turned the wheel with his palm and did not look up. “The wheel doesn’t care whose hands are on it. It cares if they’re steady.”

He knew the full note of a Vail pot before it left the wheel; the boy knew that he knew it, and knew that he would not say so before a kiln-master, who would call it early and make something of it. Two things he could keep and could not spend, and the keeping of them was the whole of his work. The note was in the pot before it left the wheel and no calling it early would change it. Questions could last for years in this house. He had already seen one last three.

Sella told the fire it was low. The bent finger went in ahead of the hand at the damper and moved it before she had finished the sentence, so that she shut her mouth on the instruction and gave the end of it to the pot instead. “That’ll do,” she said to the pot. Jorra did not say the room off the shed was cold. He did not look toward the bed under the the Vail kiln-house roof or the basket at the rack’s end. He’s not Vail stayed sealed as it had been on the hour it was spoken, and the foundling went on being carried under the roof without a name written on him that anybody would show.

Garl Vail was absent and kept by objects, which in this house is a way of keeping a man that no Court can enter. His salt-stiff coat held a broader man’s shape on its peg behind the door, the salt still standing in it in pale lines where the wet had dried. The wheel-seat in the shed kept its twenty-year lean to the left and nobody filled it. The clean dark line where the Ash ridge had turned its corner for a year and two days was not swept, because a line left where it is is still a line kept; there was a proverb in the house about leaving the crack showing, and the house had gone on saying it to the pot after the crack was gone.

His sayings went to the fire and the pot and never to the basket. “Feed the fire before you feed yourself. It’ll still be there in the morning. You won’t.” Sella said it to the coals as she turned them, and the boy lay under it with one crust in the fold and an empty place beside it and did not take it as a sentence spoken to him. The debt against a firing of tiles lay where it lay, neither paid nor settled nor done with, going around the talk the way a woman goes around Ash in a corner.
