The Sweet on the Wind

Days 186–188 · Ember 7–9, Y.H. 813 · The Vail kiln-house, Low Kiln

The sweet on a man’s breath comes from somewhere. The yard asks the wind, and the wind asks the pot, and the pot keeps its own counsel. What is carried out is carried out; nothing in the yard is entered.

— A saying of the Vail kiln-house, in the middle days of Ember

— Day 186 —

Ember 7, Y.H. 813 (Seventhday)

The morning came in under the Vail kiln-house roof in Low Kiln the way hot clay comes into a room — not through the door but around it, the heat arriving before the grey and settling into the wool while the dark was still whole. The boy lay on the bed with his back to the hearth and his face turned a little to the boards, one hundred and eighty-six days old and six months and six days, and nobody under any roof said so. The first smell was hot clay under tallow, and wet wool under that, and below both the sour mineral breath of the corner no broom had been given an instruction about in two years. The first sound was the kiln drawing breath in fours with a gap. It was not a bell. No bell had been rung in this house for a hundred and eighty-six days, and the district went on calling Tallow the firing month and Ember the kiln month without a word spent between the two names.

Through the floor the carried fire answered four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been. Its payer had no name and had had none for a hundred and eighty-six days. Through the finger’s-width door the river kept its note of water over water, and that was a fifth low thing but not a hearing, never counted among the six and never made to stand up as a seventh, for an empty place kept empty is a finding and a finding filled to make a row come out even is a finding forged, and a forged finding carried into a room with a signature at the bottom is a finding that will be believed. Overhead the boards kept four nails, four, and a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts in three places, and none rose up and became the others. The surgeon’s register would have laid them on three towels and left the towels apart; the body lying under the wool could not have told a nail from a note and did not try.

The crust lay two inches from his ear, one stiff brown-edged barley cake gone leathery in the dry kiln heat, with its empty place beside it unnamed and no third. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had wiped it, because nobody under any roof had been given an instruction about the thread. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and nobody had been given an instruction about that sound either. He knew the sound. He had once reduced a shoulder in the dark and counted the seconds between a breath and its consequence; now the consequence was his own ear going damp against the fold and he could not lift a hand to his own chin to stop it. The adult and the infant quarrelled in the dark, and the infant was louder. He could not roll. He could not reach the crust two inches off. He could not close two inches on the pot handle standing where it stood. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water through the finger’s-width door and came down against the fold.

Sella Vail came in with the basket against her hip and gave the instruction to the wool before she gave it to anything with ears. Keep it off his ears. It’s the river wind, she said; and the fire got its own instruction, and the pot got its own, and the broom leaning by the door got its, and the running water at the well got its, and the basket got none. She said the house where another woman would have said I, and kept her half-pace from every open thing, and did not once look into the fold. Her eye went once to the clay jar standing cold under its glaze and her hand shut half over nothing and came away without touching it. She pushed coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn so the grey streak showed pale off the coals, and set the pot rim out on the board with its handle standing two inches beyond the reaching hand, and let her hand rest a half-beat longer at the setting than the setting needed before it went away. It would have said, if a man could ask it, that the weight in the basket was the same on this morning as on the first, which was true, and that the cost was not the same, which was also true and not entered.

The widow’s mark hung on its cord at her throat, a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it, where it had hung since the first firing. She did not touch it. A morning was not an opening and a shelf was not an opening, and the one opening lay behind the household on the first morning of Ember, when the hand had gone to the mark before the kiln, held one breath, let go, lifted the lid and tipped the carried coal under the back of a broken pot; nobody had praised it and nobody had seen what it mended, and the fire had answered four pulls with the fifth still missing. People noticed the burn first and the mark last, which was the order she preferred. The rule wanted no saying aloud in this house: the hand goes to the mark before the kiln and at no other hour. Two rooms lay under the one roof, and neither borrowed warmth from the other that anybody could enter.

Presently she took him up and carried him out, and set the basket with its back to the kiln mouth. The yard came up around them in its long low bars of woodsmoke and its smell of hot clay, and the work had already taken the morning before the boy had any part in it. At the edge of the Clay Yard she went around the clean dark line where Ash had turned the corner for two years and the new finer grey called the thaw lay beside it, and neither had been swept and neither had an instruction. She went around the round place on the wall where the shallow clay pan had stood mouth-up to dry through Reed and was standing there no longer, with no scrape, no crumb, no print in the dust and no chip of a rim. She went wide beneath the lowest rack, where the broken birth-slates lay and nobody entered. She went around the well, where the trough stood bare and dark and the water in it was called weather. She went around the empty place in the returning line where a hand had gone in on Seed fifteen and had not come out. Each got a look that asked for nothing and found nothing, a wide step, no number, and no explanation to the basket of why a pot set down here should be carried wide of there. The surgeon’s register set the pan’s gone ring on a tray of its own and the field’s empty place on another, and would not let them touch, because three likenesses in a row will stand up and call themselves a truth if a man leaves them standing; he had spent a hundred and eighty-six days taking likenesses down one at a time with nobody to help and nobody to see.

The danger kept an order and no name that could not be carried into a chart. Hot clay lay under tallow; fine grey slate dust, ground small as meal in the room behind the finger’s-width door, sat white in the crease of a knuckle when a fist opened, and there was no scrubbing it out; the sweetish edge of lead glaze rode up from High Kiln on a wrong wind and touched one mouth-corner and went on; and a rack-worker set a pot down wrong on the edge of a rack, so that the pot gave its high thin note before its time. The woman who set it down did not hear it. She went on with her hands and made no lesson of it. The boy turned toward the note and got no further: he had no knuckle to set against the sound, no word in Grathic to give it, no hand that could reach a towel or a slate, and he filed it where a danger of the month goes, on a place of its own, not among the six hearings and not as a fourth near-full answer standing up to call itself a cause, for a rule is an account that has found a payer and no payer stood to this. No pot was lost. No lesson was made. The high thin note hung a moment over the racks and went where the woman’s hands had not followed it.

Tarn took the broom only after the broom had been given its instruction, as a man takes up a tool already named. He told the kindling what he told it at every going and coming of the light. Six counted. The next I don’t count. Six years stood in the worn thumb-mark where the house could put a finger and verify them; the seventh stood at the lip of the sill where no thumb of the house had gone, and the small knife lay at the very edge with Garl Vail’s unforged collector sentence beside it, the one about the man sent for a collector who turned back at the frozen ford, kept where the seventh year was kept and entered in nothing. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line and no scrubbing lifted it, and the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back. At the top of the next reach the breath from Seed sixteen stood unspent, with its small interval showing where a breath should have gone in and had not. He said Yes, house to a neighbour who wanted the thaw early and to another who wanted it late, agreed so entirely that no argument could find a foot, and went on wanting the late one because a late thaw puts off the paying, though the paying had stood in the ruts since the water went. His stroke went over the flagstones and out onto the clay road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector, over and over, without once turning along it. No collector came up the road and no collector went down it.

In the Vail wheel-shed Jorra Vail worked with slip dried to the colour of old bone up both forearms and the bent little finger leading, and kept the dusty forearms and the clean ones on one tray, as a man keeps two states of one morning in one account without writing a word over either. The dust was parted from the slip the way a surgeon parts a clot from the vessel that holds it — one the material of a room, one the material of the work — and the boy, carried past the edge of the door, took the two apart without a word spoken over either and asked neither which slate had been broken nor whether it had been a birth-slate; the broken birth-slates lay under the lowest rack and were entered by nobody. Jorra spoke to the wheel before he spoke to anyone. It doesn’t care whose hands. It cares if they’re steady, he said, flat and low, the way a man says a thing to an animal he means to work with, and the wheel took the words and went on turning. To the pot under his hands he said You’ll do, in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either. He knew the full note that pot would give three turns before it left the wheel, knew it in the forearm before the knuckle had proved it, and kept the knowing where it belonged, on the near-full tray beside the hearings and never among them, advanced and not paid.

A kiln-master come up from High Kiln on the wrong wind, with the sweetish lead edge at one mouth-corner and Ash on his cuffs, said that a pot singing before its time generally sings itself out early. Jorra did not answer him and did not look up, and the man took the silence for agreement and went off up the yard with his saying. The knowing stayed where Jorra had put it and did not become a hearing because a man in a clean apron had given the danger a name aloud. No pot was lost in the yard and nothing was paid. When Sella’s voice went low and unfinished in the middle of a sentence to the fire, his bent finger went to the cold iron of the damper ahead of his hand and waited. He did not look at her and did not answer her. He held the iron while the sentence found its end and the end of it arrived at the pot, and only then did he move the tongue a hair’s breadth and leave the finger standing in the cold. The household straightened around the silence the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted, and nobody spoke of the waiting and no payer was named to it. It was a remembered holding, not a change made again; it lay on neither the place of the Day 166 beat beneath the shelf nor the place of the Day 171 half-check on the flagstones, and the two shut-room tellings stood side by side with no third. The boy read the iron once more as one piece with two bites, the holding and the taking, with no word in Grathic that would carry both mercies in one mouthful, joined to no kiln and entered in no slate.

The sweet edge went on up the yard and off toward the racks and was gone from under the roof, and the morning went on being an ordinary morning that nobody marked. He wanted the crust two inches from his ear, with its empty place beside it unnamed and no third; he wanted the breast; he wanted the pot handle standing two inches beyond his hand; he wanted a number spoken by somebody who would have known what a number counted. He got none of them and could not ask for any, and had stopped expecting to be told, which was not the same as not wanting it. The surgeon’s register read the whole of it as an inventory that is not a key and a list that is not a hand, exact and complete and worth nothing at two paces, and he was the only adult under this roof and the least use in it.

One hundred and eighty-six stood exact and unspent in the dark of the wool, spoken by nobody under any roof. Nothing had been joined, named, opened, called, spent or ended: the fire’s fifth still missing under the floor with its payer unnamed, the six hearings still six with no seventh invented, the nine stoppings still nine, the Seed sixteen breath still held, the loan still uncalled, the three changes still on their three places, the mark touched once before the kiln and at no other hour, the stone back in its pot in the loft still cold and unreconciled, and the charcoal still waiting under the shelf for a hand that was not hers and a day that had not come. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water through the finger’s-width door and came down against the fold, and the pot’s high thin note from the rack’s edge went on hanging where the woman who had set it down did not hear it.

The afternoon thinned without ending. It lost its edges the way water goes off a stone that has held the sun — first the bright at the yard’s far side, then the small distinct heat at the kiln’s lip, then the last of the separate smells, each going off the stone and leaving the stone the same stone it had been. Hot clay lay under tallow under wet wool, and below the three the sour mineral breath of Ash in the corner nobody had swept in two years, which did not go with the light but stayed and gathered in the quiet. The work wound down into the smaller sounds a house makes once it has stopped being listened to: the rasp of a pot eased down on boards, the click of a tool put back where it belonged, the pull of a breath a man had not known he was holding, let go at last into the room. The boy lay at the low angle where the underside of the loaf shelf becomes a roof, his back to the fire and his face to the boards, and took the going of the light in as a shape rather than a colour. The new finer grey called the thaw lay beside the old clean dark line in the corner and was neither of them swept, and the surgeon’s register set the two lines down as a finding laid beside a finding and went no further with it.

Sella Vail stood at the shelf with her back to the basket and moved her lips along a row that stood with two unnamed gaps. The old space sat at the row’s end; the palm’s breadth that had come in on the night of Seed six sat beside it. She gave neither a figure and gave nobody a figure. Her eye went once to the clay jar standing cold under its glaze and her hand shut half over nothing and came away without touching it, the way a hand closes over an instrument it has decided not to lift. She kept her finger from beneath the front edge of the board, where the charcoal tally lay where it had lain since the night she held a finger under it for one counted breath. An afternoon was not that night. A woman’s back turned was not the same as a hand going in. The tally stayed unread, uncounted, uncopied, and the beat she had left under the board on that night stayed where it was left, on its own place under its own roof, and the two rooms under this one roof did not touch.

The widow’s mark hung on its cord at her throat where it had hung since the first firing, a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it. She did not touch it. A shelf and an afternoon and the going of a day’s light were not openings; the one opening lay behind the household on the first morning of Ember, and nobody had praised it and nobody had seen what it mended. People noticed the burn first and the mark last, which was the order she preferred.

Tarn had taken it out on the afternoon of Day 183 and held it and set it on the sill in the light and looked at it a long while and put it back; the debt had not moved by the width of a hair and he had not forgiven the stone. From below the boy heard nothing now, not a scrape, not a foot, only the house going on around its own kept things. He filed the remembered holding as a remembering and not as a holding made again. Jorra Vail did not ask about the ear and Tarn did not ask about the room.
