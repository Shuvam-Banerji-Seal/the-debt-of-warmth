The Figure Goes Up the Road

Days 267–269 · Smoke 28–30, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, Sill

The tenth day reads what the ninth would not. The reader goes up the road with the box shut, and the wool lies where it lay. Three readings are three and not one account.

— A saying of the rack end in Low Kiln, told in the last tend of Smoke

— Day 267 —

Smoke 28, Eighthday, Y.H. 813

The hand had come down on the river’s second low note and lain against the fold since, the way a man lays a tool on a bench at the end of a shift — not because the work is finished but because the next hand will find it where it was left without having to look. No one under the roof had been told to read it. No one under the roof had ever been given a reason to. The small hours went on around it as water goes around a stone that lies where a stone lies, and the boy lay awake inside a body two hundred and sixty-seven days old, and six months and eighty-seven days were told by nobody in Sarn, Sill or Grath, and never had been.

Two hundred and sixty-six had stood exact, complete, unspent and spoken by no mouth; two hundred and sixty-seven stood where it stood now, exact in the same way, with no number anywhere in the house to agree with it. The boy had stopped expecting to be told what a number counted, which was not the same thing as not wanting it. The wanting went on under the expectation the way a coal goes on under ash, giving out nothing and going out not at all. Under the Vail kiln-house roof the file lay open on the towel with every tray parted and every gap left gap, and the hand lay where the hand had been put down; the file was whole and the hand was still, and neither had been asked to stand up and mean something.

Through the finger’s-width door the river kept its note of water over water, a low thing over a lower thing, never numbered among the hearings; under the floor the carried fire gave four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have stood, its payer without a name for two hundred and sixty-seven days; overhead the boards kept four nails on the near board, four on the next, and a thumb-width knot where the fifth nail should have stood. Three counts in three places, and none rose into another. Agreeing any two would want a payer put under both, and no payer stood to either, and the boy had long ago stopped supplying one to make the row come out even. Six hearings of the full note stood beside and never among, with the seventh place kept empty and none invented to fill it; the heard bell stood beside the told, and the figure read beside the figure paid. He had learned the difference between an empty place kept empty and an empty place filled to make a row come out even. The first was a finding; the second was a finding forged, and an account that had found a payer. The keeping of the difference was its own small, cold exactness, and it bought him nothing he could lift or spend.

Heat had gone out of the fold and sat behind the pot, where it stayed the way a coal stays under ash. He took the body in the order the facts presented themselves, which was the order he had learned in a country that had no word for any of it. One stiff leathery brown-edged barley crust lay two inches from his ear, gone past the point where a child would have eaten it, and the empty place beside it kept its name to itself; no third had come in the night to make the row come out even. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the dry heat and was unwiped, because nobody under any roof had ever been given an instruction about the thread, and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. Cold had come to cheek and temple and the small bare joint of the wrist where the wool rode back, and had stopped there as at the end of a line written out for it, neither going forward nor going away. The afterdrop waited beyond all three, past a warming nobody in this house would perform.

He knew the composed face that is not composure. He knew a cold patient cannot clot, and will seem better before he is worse, and that a man must be warmed from the outside and slowly, and that you do not rub a frozen foot. He knew the order in which the cold arrives at the extremities and the order in which it leaves the core last, and knew that the knowledge was worth nothing at two inches of air. He wanted the crust lifted the two inches to his mouth. Both were true, and the second was louder in a body of two hundred and sixty-seven days, and he entered neither, not even on the place where he kept the things he did not enter. The surgeon took the inventory; the boy lay under the fold and wanted his breakfast. There was no third hand in the room to lift either thing for him.

Smoke came on its own morning, earned by that light and not carried into a day that had already spent its own; the light came first and the day took its name from it afterward. There would be no market, no Cross and no bell on an Eighthday, and the day did not become an occasion by being the first of the month’s last tend. Smoke 30, the third and last Account-day and market of the second firing, stood two mornings ahead and was not reached for; the first firing of Frost stood far off and unbrought, and neither of the district’s two names for the months was set right or left wrong as the yard went about its work.

The first smell was hot clay under tallow, and under that wet wool sour with lodging, and under that the faint sour mineral breath of Ash in the unswept corner called the thaw. The first sound was the carried fire drawing in fours with a gap where a man might have put a fifth pull and did not. It had answered four pulls and a silence since before the boy came under the roof, and it answered four pulls and a silence now. Sella had once told a pot that the house would keep it, and the house had kept it by leaving things where they lay.

Sella Vail went to the fire before she went to the basket, and gave no instruction at any hour to the basket. She told the coals it was the thaw. She turned them with the back of a broken pot and said The house will keep it where another woman would have said I. She stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand and counted under her breath toward no opening — the small intake, the hold, the letting-go — then pushed coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the burn, so that the one grey streak at her temple showed pale off the coals. The burn was shaped like a little door closed on nothing. When her glance went to the clay jar standing cold under its lead glaze, her hand shut half over nothing, and she went on with the fire as though the glance had been a thought about the weather.

At the loaf shelf her finger stayed on top of the front edge so the charcoal tally on the underside remained unread, and her lips moved along a row standing with two gaps and gave neither gap a figure. She did not touch either gap. She set the shelf’s bits right with the side of her hand the way a woman smooths a cloth and does not straighten the seam under it, and moved her lips past the two empty places as though a row with two gaps were a row like any other. The pot rim went out with its handle two inches beyond the reaching hand, with a half-beat at the setting and no look toward the bed. The half-beat was gone as soon as it had been spent; the handle stood where it stood and the boy’s hand lay where his hand lay, and two inches of air between them were the whole of his reach.

The dressed grain lay covered under wool on the cool stone at the rack end, away from the heat of the hearth. She drew the wool back only far enough to feel for damp, turned the grain once with a touch lighter than the day before, and laid the wool over it again. It had changed in state and gained no figure. The arithmetic that would be read over it on Smoke 30 would not know what it cost, and the space between the figure and its price stayed unsigned, as it had stayed through every turning and would stay through the reading. Nobody under the roof found this remarkable. There was no hand in the house to write the cost down and no slate that wanted it, so the grain lay under the wool and waited for a number that could not know it; the boy watched the corner of wool come down and kept the waiting as a finding with no payer, not a grievance and not a lesson, and laid nothing across it.

At the yard well the stone lip was bare and grey under the bucket’s knock; the frost that had written white on it in fronds was gone, and the yard called the whole of it weather. The rope was cold iron under her palm and the pail knocked the lip and went down and came up, and she gave the instruction to the bucket and not to the basket. The boy kept whatever white had been there on a place of its own with no cause — not a payer, not the widow’s mark (a well is not an opening), not the first firing of Frost, far off and unbrought. He had no Grathic word for the thing the white had been doing and nobody had asked him for one, so the white stayed where the white had been, a shape a vessel makes when heat has gone where it was not meant to go, and went into no account.

The widow’s mark hung where it hung, touched once before the kiln on Smoke 1, held one breath and let go, and touched at no shelf, well, rack, road, basket, morning or bed since, because a morning and a hearth and a rack and a bed are not openings. The one opening before Smoke 1 stayed behind the household, made into no lesson, no sign, no mending and no payment; the next real opening stood far off in the first firing of Frost.

A neighbour with a pot hitched against her hip came to the door and asked what the keeping and the storing would take now the grain was under wool and the visits coming.

“Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there,” Sella said. “The house has said it.”

“The pot doesn’t keep accounts.”

“No,” she said, and turned the coals with the back of the broken pot. “That’s why I told you to ask it.”

The neighbour stood a moment with her pot against her hip, waiting for the figure that does not come out of a pot, then went off level with the question still on her, nothing priced, nothing refused, nothing entered. Sella had sent it to the weather after the pot, and the weather had answered first, as it always did.

Toward mid-morning a man come down the clay road with dried pale scurf of mud on his coat skirt brought a number up out of a fist toward the well, his thumb tucked over it as over a thing that might be read. He looked at it. He put it back into his sleeve. The figure arrived nowhere, not a missing figure and not a withheld one: it had come up out of a fist and gone back into a sleeve, and the boy read it as a chart turned face-down, and supplied no figure to fill the space under it. The man went on up the yard and nobody took the number off him. No collector came up the clay road on Smoke 28 and none went down it.

The little silence was left empty again, on a place of its own, and was not crossed: an empty object laid beside the held one and apart from it, a keeping and not a miss. No fire-sitter stood over the coals to supply a count and no house count was there to borrow, and the boy borrowed none. Over his head the boards kept four, four and a knot; under the floor the fire kept four and a gap; through the door the river kept its low note over lower. Three counts in three places, and he laid no hand across any two of them, because agreeing would want a payer put under both and none stood.

The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the river’s low note. It held for the small intake only. It came down on the river’s next low note, borrowing no house count and taking no stroke from the kiln’s gap or the boards’ knot. Nothing was opened, named, called, paid or ended; only the ordinary work of the month’s last tend went on, and the hand was put down.
