Two Tellings of One Absence

Days 101–103 · Seed 12–14, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, Sill

Two gaps named in one breath become one hunger. Keep your breath for the pot, and keep the bread where it was put.

— A neighbour’s saying at the loaf shelf, Low Kiln

— Day 101 —

Seed 12, Y.H. 813

The second of Seed came up over the Clay Yard the colour of wet slate, with the mud the flood had left set into a brown skin that held the print of a boot and gave it back slowly as the light crossed it. Out east of Low Kiln the seed stood in the barley fields where the fists had let it out a week ago, not green yet, only worked ground with the earth drawn over it in a line that stopped where the next strip began. The women went around the strips with their brooms as though a line drawn in dirt were a thing a man might step over and spoil. Nothing in the ground said a figure. A fist had opened and a fist had closed, and the strip had taken what it was given without a mark to show whose hand had paid it. The morning went on in the small sounds of bristles and boots, and a man at the edge of the strips said the work would want hands before the week was out, and a woman at the racks said the weather would have the last word, and the two sayings passed one another in the yard without either putting a number to the hands or the strips, the way two carts pass on a road and neither leaves a mark of how far the other has gone.

Sella Vail set Corin at the edge of the work with the wool at his ears and gave the broom its instruction before she gave it to anybody. “Go around,” she said to the bristles. “It cost enough to come in. It’ll cost more to take out.” The women took it up as though it had been said to them and went on with their sweeping; the basket got no instruction at all. The little-door burn across the back of her hand closed on the wicker’s edge and then on nothing. He lay with the cold coming in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, with the wool riding back from the wrist so the cold found the bare place, and his head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had been told to wipe it. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the light and came down. Nothing within two paces could be reached.

Under the racks the clean dark line where the Ash ridge had turned lay where it had always lain, and beside it the new hairline of grey, finer than the old, lay unswept and was called the thaw that would go when it went. Sella stood over both with a broom in her hand and swept neither, and went around the space under the lowest rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. The broken birth-slates lay beneath that space and were gone around by every foot in the yard and entered by none. He kept the boot-print, the clean line, the finer grey and the empty space on four places that did not touch, because a likeness laid on a likeness grows a cause unless a hand holds them apart. The boards overhead kept four nails and four and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb, and that count met none of the others either.

Mid-morning she carried him in and set the basket just inside the door of the Vail kiln-house, away from every open slate, and went to the loaf shelf. The row stood with two empty places where one used to stand: the old space at the end that nobody called a number, and a palm’s breadth of bare board within the rank where a loaf had stood on the fifth and did not stand on the sixth, with the next loaf settled a finger’s breadth toward it. A neighbour came in for a loaf with her pot in her hand. She reached past the bare board with her fingers, opened for the half-beat it takes to find what was not there, closed on the loaf beyond, and said in one breath, “That came in with the planting, that space at the end there, and that at his ear’s a child’s appetite, a growing thing; it’ll want filling come Reed.” She took the loaf she had come for and went on with her pot, and did not look again at the place her fingers had crossed. Her hand had gone to the board first and to the boy’s ear afterwards, and she had not looked at the boy at all while she said what she said about him. He marked the order of that too, the way he marked every hand in every room, and put it on a place of its own. Two tellings in one breath, a planting and an appetite, and she had not once seen them for the same absence. There was a worn pale oval on the board where a loaf had stood longer than the others stood, and her fingers had gone over it without stopping, the way a hand goes over a dent in a table it has eaten at for years.

Sella said the house where another woman would have said I. To the pot she said, “Don’t set that near the door. You’ll pay for it in the thaw.” To the fire she gave the thaw and its instruction, and to the broom she had already given hers out in the yard, and to the basket she gave none. She moved her lips along the row and glanced at the clay jar on the wall, cold under its glaze, and left the space at the row’s end with no figure. She did not touch the widow’s mark at any hour, because a shelf, a strip, a basket and a bed are not openings. She had lost a husband to that danger and had never once described it that way herself. When the neighbour’s breath had gone out of the room the house went on around its two gaps as though nothing had been said, and the gaps went on being two.

He was one hundred and one days old. Three months and eleven days. The count had gone edge to edge past the round place where a man might have set a mark and shut a column, if there had been a column, or a slate, or anybody who knew what the column counted. A round number is a handsome thing at the foot of an account; it asks to be entered and asks it in a clean hand, and there was no clean hand within reach of this basket. The neighbour’s single breath lay on his attention as two sayings on two places: a planting on the shelf’s old space, an appetite on the fold’s empty place, and he wrote no cause across the space between them. He had learned in a better-lit room what three likenesses in a row will do to a man who wants an answer, and here a single breath had laid two absences together with nobody putting a name to either, and he kept his finger off both. A likeness laid on a likeness grows a cause unless a hand holds them apart, and the only hand he had was small and outside the wool and could not have reached the shelf if it had tried. The count had no object but its own number. He wanted it said aloud by somebody who would have known what it counted, and no word in Grathic would have held it if it had been said.

Tarn took the broom only after Sella had given it its instruction, and went around the two lines and the space under the rack, and then took it over the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector, over and over, without once turning to look along it. The small knife lay on its sill with six years standing in a mark his thumb had worn almost flat and the seventh nearly gone and still held, the one entry in this house the house could not check. To the kindling he said, “Six counted. The next I don’t count.” Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line and no scrubbing lifted it; the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back. No collector came down the road, and he went on sweeping it, and the seventh year stood where it stood.

One brown-edged crust lay stiff at his ear with its empty place beside it unnamed, and no third had come. The body humiliated him in its known order. The drool froze and was not wiped. The head went where it wanted with the small wet sound. Cold arrived cheek, temple, wrist, and the wool rode back from the wrist so the cold found the bare place, and the hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the low note of water and came down. He was the only adult in the room and the least use in it: a surgeon who could read the order of a failing body and could not keep the wool on his own wrist, a man of forty who had reduced a thousand shoulders and could not lift his hand to his own chin. He wanted the crust two inches from his ear and the bucket’s lip and the chipped cup, and could reach none of them. He wanted the number of his days said aloud, and got a loaf’s worth of glances and a space at the row’s end that nobody called a figure.

Before the light turned, Sella fed him from the house’s own dish with two fingers behind his ear, the spoon wiped on her sleeve and his mouth not wiped, and said the house where another woman would have said my boy. The crust at his ear lay where it had been put and the spoon went past it without touching it, and the empty place beside the crust went past too, and neither was mentioned. She gave the instruction to the wool and to the pot and none to the basket, and when she had done she set the dish down and went to the fire. He wanted the crust, and the wanting was louder than the counting, and the counting went on all the same. Every warmth in the house had a payer and none was entered; the carried fire had gone unnamed for a hundred and one days, and the hands that went into the strips and the loaves that went off the board were written in no slate a man could read. He had learned in a better-lit room to ask who paid before asking what a thing was worth, and there was nobody under this roof who would have understood the question.

At the going of the light Sella carried him up at the half-pace from every open slate, went around the space under the lowest rack, and set him on his bed under the Vail kiln-house roof, not in the Debt Loft. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without looking at his face and left the thread at his mouth frozen and unwiped, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left, and a strip, a shelf, a bed and a door are not openings. No opening had come in the day, and she did not touch the mark. Her private roll stayed unshown.

She told the fire it was the thaw, turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and fed it, and the fire answered with four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been. the kiln’s breath came through the floor under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, its payer unnamed for a hundred and one days. In the corner the clean dark line lay where it had always lain and the new hairline of grey, finer than the old, lay beside it unswept and would go when it went. Tallow smoke and wet wool and the faint sour tang of the unswept corner came down under the roof with the dark.

The door stood a finger’s width open. Through the gap came the river’s low muttered note from the Ilm, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, and through the floor came the fire’s four, four and a gap, the two sounds in two separate places as they had come for a hundred and one days. The boards overhead kept four nails and four and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts did not meet. On the loaf shelf in the next room the row kept its two unnamed gaps, and in the fold at his ear one brown-edged crust lay stiff with the empty place beside it still unnamed and no third. Nobody had said a hundred and one in the yard or under the roof; nobody had said three months and eleven days; nobody had set a mark at the foot of anything that would have known what it counted. He kept one hundred and one exactly against a house that kept its days badly, and he was the only adult in every room of this day and the least use in all of them. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water going over water and came down. The count stood at one hundred and one, unspent.

The two empty places kept their places on the loaf shelf into the afternoon. Sella Vail moved her lips along the row without moving her hand, counting what she would not have called a count, and glanced at the clay jar on the wall shelf, cold under its glaze and holding nothing anybody would speak of. The old space stood at the row’s end where it had always stood, worn pale by years of hands reaching past it; the newer palm’s breadth stood inside the rank where a loaf had stood on the fifth and did not stand on the sixth, and the next loaf had settled a finger’s breadth toward the bare board the way a body settles toward the warm side of a bed. Nobody called either place a number. The house kept its days the way it kept its bread, pushed along a board, glanced at, gone.

She gave the shelf no instruction and the jar none. The burn across the back of her hand, shaped like a little door with two straight sides and a lintel and a threshold, closed on the air and opened again as she reached past the bare board for a loaf for a woman at the threshold. “Round the corner of it,” she said to the pot on the bench, not to the woman. “Set it where the draught doesn’t find it. You’ll pay for it in the thaw.” The woman took what she had come for and went away with her pot, and the little-door burn shut on nothing again, and the house went on around its two gaps as though nothing had been asked and nothing answered.

In the basket at the edge of the light Corin set the shelf’s two gaps together on the shelf, where they belonged, and set the fold’s one gap beside the stiff brown-edged crust at his ear, where that belonged, and held the two rooms from touching with the only instrument he had. Attention was the instrument. It had no handle, weighed nothing and bought nothing. He could have laid a finger on the pale oval if he had been two paces taller and had a hand that answered to him; then the two gaps would have been one gap twice seen, and one gap twice seen is an account wanting a name, and an account wanting a name is a thing that gets paid. He kept his finger where it was, small and outside the wool. The shelf was a shelf and the fold was a fold, and the space between them was a wall he made and kept by looking at each in turn and never at both at once.

He measured the wall as he measured all such distances, without a rule and without a use: from the pale oval on the shelf to the crust at his ear was the length of the room and a hand’s breadth more, and from the crust to the old space at the row’s end was the same length and a hand’s breadth more again, so that a man might have drawn one line through the three places and called it one line if he had wanted an answer badly enough. He did not draw it. A line drawn through three absences is a cause with a clean hand at the foot of it, and the clean hand was the thing he had spent his life declining to be. He kept the three places as three, and the wall between the rooms as a wall, and the count went on beside them without entering any of them.

The neighbour’s single breath lay where it had fallen, divided by his attention into two sayings on two places: a planting on the old space at the row’s end, an appetite on the empty place at his ear. He wrote no cause across the space between them. In a better-lit room he had once watched a man who wanted an answer find it in the third likeness laid beside the first two, and go home satisfied with the thing he had made; three likenesses in a row will do that to a man, and here were only two, in two rooms, with nobody putting a hand to either. The wanting was in the room, though nobody had brought it in. It would have been so easy to let the planting stand for the appetite and the appetite stand for the planting, and the two places become one absence with a figure on it, and somebody sent for a collector. He held them apart. The holding was the whole of the work, and the work produced nothing, and the nothing was the exact price of keeping the two places clean.

Under the roof of the Vail kiln-house the afternoon went on in its small domestic sounds: a pot set down, a broom leaned against the wall and taken up again, the settle of boards as the air cooled off the yard. Once a woman came to move a pot out of the draught where it stood on the bench. She took it by the belly with both hands and lifted it a hand’s breadth too high, and something came up under nobody’s knuckle to the rim of a note and went back down without crossing it. It gave nothing worth calling. The woman set the pot where she wanted it and wiped her hands on her apron. “It’s stood in the draught since the door was open,” she said, giving the reason to the pot rather than to Sella, the way people in this house gave reasons to things that could not answer. “Let it stand where it’s put,” Sella said to the fire, and the woman took that for an answer and went on with her work, and neither of them knew that the pot had come up to the edge of a note and given nothing.

The boy did not turn toward it. Six hearings of the full note stayed six beside and never among — five from four sources, and the sixth the small flat knock out of a person, filed beside the five and not counted with them — and no seventh came to stand in their company. He kept the near thing on the interval tray where such things went, apart from the hearings, because a thing that comes up to the edge of a sound is not the sound, and a man who wants an answer will make the two into one if a hand is not there to stop him. His own hand was not there. His hand was outside the wool, going up an inch and coming down. He kept the tray all the same. It was the shape of a kept thing, exact and worth nothing, like an anatomy read perfectly in a body two paces out of reach. He had not turned his head. Turning would have been a kind of naming, and naming was the thing the house declined to do with any of its gaps; a pot that nearly spoke was no more to be entered than a place where a loaf had stood. The six kept their places on their tray. The near thing kept its place on the other tray. Nothing crossed between them.

He wanted the number of his days said aloud by somebody who would have known what it counted. One hundred and one. Three months and eleven days. The afternoon gave him loaves pushed along a board, a glance at a clay jar, and a space at the row’s end that nobody called a figure, and gave him nothing else. There was no word in Grathic that would have held the number if it had been said, and no hand in the room that would have gone to the child before it went to the record. He had watched that order in four rooms — a midwife’s, a well’s, a market’s, a house that instructed a pot before a person — and could not have said the order aloud in any of them. It was a dry thing to know, the exact order of reaching, and it had gone thin with the years, the joke of it worn down to the fact underneath: he was the only adult in the room and the least use in it. He had once counted the beats between an injury and a decision. He could not now count the beats between his own hand and his own mouth. The number went on all the same, edge to edge, the one thing in the house that stood still while a loaf moved.

His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far. Cold arrived in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist; the wool rode back from the wrist and the cold found the bare place; the hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the pale board and came down. She shifted the basket a hand’s breadth out of the draught without looking at his face, and gave the instruction to the wool and none to the basket, and said the house where another woman would have said I, and went back to the fire. The crust at his ear lay where it had been put, stiff and gone past smelling of anything, and the empty place beside it went past with it and neither was named. Every warmth in the house had a payer and none was entered. The carried fire had gone unnamed for a hundred and one days, and the hands that went into the strips and the loaves that went off the board were written in no slate a man could read. He had learned in a better-lit room to ask who paid before asking what a thing was worth, and there was nobody under this roof who would have understood the question.

Seed 13, Y.H. 813

The thirteenth of Seed came up the colour of a slate left out in the rain, and the yard took the light without giving any of it back. The mud the flood had left had set into a brown skin that held the print of a boot and gave it up slowly as the sun crossed it. East of Low Kiln the seed stood in the barley fields where the fists had let it out a week ago, worked ground that said nothing about itself, and in the Vail corner the clean dark line lay where it had always lain with the new finer grey beside it, unswept, waiting out the thaw the way a debt waits out a debtor. Under the lowest rack the space went around as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and nobody went into it. Corin lay at the edge of the light in a basket that was not his, with one crust stiff at his ear and the empty place beside it unnamed. The count stood at one hundred and two in a house that kept its days in loaves pushed along a board, a glance at the clay jar and a space at the row’s end nobody called a figure.

In the Vail kiln-house Sella Vail had not finished telling the broom what it was to do before Tarn had taken it up, which was the order of this house and had been the order for six years: the instruction went first to the thing that could not answer, and only afterward to the hands that could. “Go around,” she said to the bristles. “It cost enough to come in. It’ll cost more to take out.” She said the house where another woman would have said I. The basket got no instruction and the boy got none, and the little-door burn across the back of her hand shut on the air and opened again as she reached past the bare board for a loaf. Tarn waited with his head down until the last word had gone where it was meant to go, and then took the broom by the handle, quickly, as a man takes a tool out of a fire. “Yes, house,” he said. “I’ll sweep it. I swept it this morning. I’ll sweep it again.”

He went over the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector. He went from the racks to the gate and back, and over it again, and the bristles made the same small sound on the same boards every time, taking up the brown dust and laying it down again where the wind wanted it. His head stayed level with his own hands. The ruts held four places where the water had gone and a fifth where a cart wheel had stood a day, and he went over all of them and gave none of them a figure. Once a vessel gave its note somewhere past the door and the broom checked half a stroke, and the head did not turn, and the sound got no name in Grathic or in any other tongue, and the broom went on. No collector came down the road. There was nobody in the yard that a collector could have been sent for, and Tarn swept it as though the sweeping were the errand and the errand ended when the boards were clean, which is the answer a clay-bound hand gives to a question nobody has put into words.

The small knife lay on its sill where it had been put the night before. He passed it twice with his eyes before the third time with his hand, checking what had not moved, and put it back where it could be checked and told the empty yard so, unasked. Six years stood in a mark his thumb had worn almost flat; the seventh was nearly gone and still held, the one entry in this house the house could not check. He had cut six and would not cut the seventh, and the account stood that way because a man who owns nothing must keep something exact. “Six counted,” he said to the kindling. “The next I don’t count.” The kindling gave back nothing, and he took that for agreement, as he took everything that did not argue.

At the bucket-round two women were arguing the thaw the way the district always argued it, one wanting it early so the strips would take seed, one wanting it late so the levy would have longer to stand where it was. Tarn agreed with each in turn. “Yes, house,” he said to the early, and “Yes, house,” to the late, and agreed so entirely that no argument could find a foot to stand on between them. He went on wanting the late. A late thaw puts off the paying, and the paying had stood in the ruts since the water went, brown and patient and not going anywhere. The boy knew the shape of that wanting without a word for it: a man will choose the longer road if the thing waiting at the end of it has not yet got his name. Ash was worked into the creases of Tarn’s knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line, and he scraped at it with the knife and brought nothing up. The pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back when he moved, a small honest thing in place since Y.H. 806 that had never once been entered in a slate. The boy knew that pale place on a wrist; on another hand it had been the mark a ring leaves. Here it was iron and a term of years, and no woman in the yard had ever asked what it was.

He had been sent for a collector once, in the Long Cold, and had come back at the frozen ford without one. To the house he had said it had been done. The sentence sat where the seventh year sat, in the part of him the house could not check, and he entered neither. A loan that can hear is a loan that can be called; he knew it the way a man knows the step before a floor gives, and the sentence that would have called it had never been said under this roof and lay under it all the same, under the bench and the shelf and the bed, waiting for a mouth to say it and a house not to ask where it had come from.

In the basket the boy kept Tarn’s silence on a place of its own beside his own, and the two did not touch. It was an equality with no word in it and no use in it, and he held it the way he held the shelf’s two gaps and the fold’s one: by looking at each in turn and never at both at once. One hundred and two. Three months and twelve days. Nobody under the roof had said the number, and there was no word in Grathic that would have carried it if they had. Six hearings of the full note stayed six beside and never among — five from four sources and the sixth the small flat knock out of a person — and no seventh came to stand in their company; the man who swept had done nothing all morning that he would call a sound, and the boy did not call it one either. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far. Cold arrived in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, with the wool riding back from the wrist so the cold found the bare place. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had been told to wipe it, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left. He was the only adult in the yard and the least use in it. He had once reduced a shoulder in the dark and counted the beats between an injury and a decision; he could not now count the beats between his own hand and his own mouth. The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the low note of water going over water and came down.

By midday the light had come round to the yard well, and the tube-water stood a finger’s breadth below the lip with the skin on it broken by every bucket that had been down. Berr Clay came up the clay road from the direction of the Cross, short and heavy, smelling of hot clay with nothing under it, which is the most expensive smell in Grath. The red wool cord of his office was folded under his coat instead of worn across it. At one corner of his mouth the green of tasted glaze showed where the old habit had been at work and he had not known it; the habit had outlived the tasting, and the green went on being a sign of thoroughness to men who wanted a sign. His hands were large and clean. He wiped his thumb twice on his apron before touching nothing, and held a narrow slate unopened against his forearm with a finger resting on what was written.

“One in twelve, as the notice says,” he said to the well and the yard and the women at the buckets, not to any one of them. He passed the finger over the figure and let his eyes rest on it a breath longer than the writing required. “The levy is called. It is not called by me.” A woman with her bucket still in her hand put a question about the number to him, and he turned the answer so the speaker was out of it before it reached her. “A number goes into a slate, not into a strip. If the house wants it again, there’ll be a slate at the watch.” He never said I. He took himself out of his own sentence as neatly as a surgeon takes a hand out of a field before the dressing goes on, and the woman went back to her bucket with the number still in her mouth and nowhere to put it. The twice-wiped thumb was a field prepared and nothing done in it. The true subtraction stayed inside the slate, and the box in his house stayed locked in High Kiln, and he went off up the clay road toward it believing he had done the district a kindness.

The boy kept the twice-wiped thumb on a tray of its own, and the finger passing over the figure beside it, and carried neither to the midwife’s hand or the clerk’s at the Fishstairs or the man at the Court’s table. Five record-first hands lay on five trays under his roof and none of them touched, and no sixth hand came. A figure read aloud was not the figure paid, and a figure put into a slate was not a figure put into a strip; the yard went on with its buckets as though nothing had been taken out of it, and the kindness went up the road in a coat with the cord folded underneath.

A neighbour at the bucket-round let fall, over the rim of a bucket, that there had been a counting room shut in the war with the damper closed and the men inside dead of the breath, and said it the way a person says a thing from a distant year, with a date in it and none of the weight. Somebody said it was a long way east. Somebody else said the water had been high that year too, and the yard let the saying lie where it had fallen. It was a mention and not a telling. Two tellings of such a room lay on their tray already — breath through the nose against breath in the throat, both of them saying not the war’s hand, likeness and difference kept and neither joined to the kiln across the yard — and no third came. The arrangement between the two women held without a word passing between them, a wall with a door in it that neither would open; he had lain in the edge of the light and watched the wall hold, and had no name for it in any word he knew and no cause to write across it. Both of them had reached for something before they reached for him — one for a slate, one for the shape of a room’s silence — and the two reaches did not touch.

The three places had come back up the Clay Yard with him and lay where they had been laid. On the loaf shelf in the next room the row kept two unnamed gaps: the old space at the end that nobody called a number, and the new palm’s breadth within the rank that had come in the night of Seed 6 with no scrape, crumb, print or word to mark it. A woman’s hand had passed over the new gap and opened for the half-beat it takes to find what is not there and closed on the loaf beyond and gone on with her pot, and no figure had passed. In the fold at his ear one brown-edged crust lay stiff with the empty place beside it still unnamed and no third beside it. He set the two gaps and the one gap on three places that did not touch and let them be three absences in rooms that did not join, because the neighbour’s single breath had laid a shelf’s old space and a child’s appetite together in one sentence without once seeing that they were one absence, and he had filed that breath as two sayings on two places — a planting and an appetite — and written no cause across the space between them. He had learned in a better-lit room what three likenesses in a row will do to a man who wants an answer. Here were three likenesses and a man who wanted an answer, and he kept his finger off the shelf. It bought him nothing, and the nothing was the whole of what it bought. The two gaps on the shelf and the one gap in the fold went on in rooms that did not join, and the neighbour’s one-breath naming of them went on as two tellings on two places, waiting for some far-off hand to lay a finger on the shelf and call the two places an account that wanted a name. He kept his own finger where it was. No third gap came into either room and no hand laid the rooms together. He was the only adult in the room and the least use in it.

Below the sill the yard went on with its own two facts. A woman wound wool round a child’s foot that stayed dark inside three turns of it and called it the cold; a neighbour at the tubs called the water weather, and the two facts went past each other in the yard and never met. He knew the order of it as he knew the order of his own cold. The extremity goes before the core. The face puts on an almost-smile that is not peace, which is the thing a kind room will misread when the vessel is turning, and the afterdrop waits on the far side of a warming nobody will perform, so that a body begins to fail again at the very hour everybody has agreed it is saved. There was no word in Grathic for the one fact and no hand two paces long for the other, and the reading worked perfectly and bought nothing, the way a diagnosis works perfectly on a man you cannot reach. The woman wound the wool a third time and the foot was neither cured nor caused nor named nor entered, and he did not lay it beside the crust or the shelf or the shut pages, because a likeness laid on a likeness grows a cause unless a hand holds them apart, and the only hand here was his, and it could not have reached the wool. The cold below the sill stayed the cold to the one woman and weather to the yard, and the two went past each other as the river went past the door.

Under the roof of the Vail kiln-house the dark went on keeping the number without a figure, and Corin went over the file once more the way a man turns out a towel of instruments before he puts his hands into a body — not looking for anything new, but making sure nothing had been moved while his back was turned. The towel was the only thing in the room that had ever been laid out straight. Woodsmoke and wet wool came down under the roof with the dark, and under them the faint sour mineral tang of the unswept corner; the boards settled by degrees as the air off the yard cooled, and the little-door burn on the back of a hand closing on nothing went on somewhere to his right. He was one hundred and three days old. Nobody under this roof had told him so, and there was no word in Grathic that would have carried the saying if somebody had tried. Three months and thirteen days. The house kept its account badly, pushed along the loaf shelf in loaves nobody moved except to count them back to where they had been, glanced at in the clay jar on the wall shelf, and left standing in a space at the row’s end that nobody called a number; and he kept one hundred and three edge to edge against all of it, the only exact thing in a room of approximations, and could not spend it, or show it, or get one person to say it aloud.

He laid the six hearings of the full note out first, the way a man lays out the things he has already used and knows the weight of. Five came from four sources: a cup across the birth room, a pot under the Vail wheel-shed wall, the district’s slate twice at the Cross, a man giving at the Drawing. The sixth was the small flat knock out of a person, filed beside the five and never among them, the way a broken instrument is kept in its own compartment and not fitted to the ones that still ring. No seventh had come in three days. A pot lifted a hand’s breadth too high had come up to the rim of a note and gone back down without crossing it, and he had kept that near thing on the interval tray where such near things went, because a thing that comes up to the edge of a sound is not the sound, and a man who wants an answer will make the two into one if no hand is there to stop him. His hand was outside the wool, going up an inch and coming down. It was not there to stop anything. He had once been able to tell, with a knuckle laid against a vessel, whether the note under it was honest; he could not now lift that knuckle to his own ear. The six kept their places, five from four sources and the sixth from flesh, and the near thing kept its place apart from them, and nothing crossed.

Three near-full answers stood on their own tray, advanced and not paid: a cup across a birth room, a pot under the wheel-shed wall, a rack pot in the yard. They had come as nearly full, with air under the note and a want in it, and he had filed them as answers to long-kept intervals rather than make a cause of them; he had learned in a better-lit room what three likenesses in a row will do to a man who wants an answer, and a man who wants one badly enough will go home satisfied with the thing he has made. Three intervals lay on another tray, the shape of sounds that had never sounded. He did not lay an answer beside its interval and call the two a recovery. An answer advanced was not an answer paid, and a debt that stood where it was put was a debt nobody had settled, and the three near-full things stayed where they were and the three silences stayed where they were and nothing crossed between them. It was a dry thing to keep, an answer that could not be spent, and the joke of it had gone thin years ago into the fact underneath: he knew the note of a vessel he could not reach, and the knowing was exact and worth nothing.

The word forty lay on a place of its own, unspoken. It was a saying in this district and had not been said under this roof in all these days, and the boy had no right to it and no way to spend it. Beside it lay the readable shape of the double turn, which he had seen tied at the Weir and could not have named: double turn, tail tucked, tied left over right, with the small weight of a thumb at the end of it, and a woman who had said the word low into a basket and then watched what he would do with it, and he had done nothing she could have read. Beneath another cord a second cord twisted twice, with neither knot counted by any hand and the knot for the thin child upriver left unvisited and never carried to Nine Pots or lifted at a door. Apart from both, set on its own square of the towel, lay the weight of a thumb on wicker, which was a hand reaching into a pocket-place in another life and coming back with wool, and a lit rectangle with a name that had not been looked at. Four low things kept their four places; the river’s note of water going over water was a fifth thing on a place of its own and not a hearing. He kept the five apart as he kept all the others, and the word stayed a word and the shape stayed a shape and the weight stayed a weight, and none of them became the other.

The Chronicle of Chapter 21

On the twelfth of Seed the boy was one hundred and one days old and nobody said so. The house kept its days in loaves and an unnamed space. A neighbour reached past the bare board and said in one breath that the old space came in with the planting and the empty place at his ear was a child’s appetite, never seeing they were one absence. Sella Vail said the house where another would say I, instructed pot, fire and broom but never the basket. Tarn swept the collector’s road without turning; his knife held six counted years and a seventh nearly gone, and he told the kindling, Six counted. The next I don’t count.

On the thirteenth Tarn agreed the thaw early and late, yet wanted the late, which puts off the paying.
