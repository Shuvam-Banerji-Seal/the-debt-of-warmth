Where the Figure Went

Days 192–194 · Ember 13–15, Y.H. 813 · The Vail kiln-house, Low Kiln

The figure goes up the road in a box and comes back in a loaf, a glance and a space at the row’s end. Ask the pot, ask the weather; neither will give you the number.

— A saying at the well in Low Kiln, in the kiln month

— Day 192 —

Ember 13, Y.H. 813 (Thirdday)

The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water that came through the finger’s-width door, and came down again against the fold. It had done this every morning of his life and did it now without triumph or instruction, the small bare joint at the wrist riding back as the arm rose, the fingers opening on nothing and closing on wool; and the count at one hundred and ninety-one was carried whole out of the dark that lay behind him into this morning, exact, complete and unspent, a thing laid out on a towel that did not exist and could not now be put down. He was one hundred and ninety-two days old and six months and twelve days. Nobody under any roof in Low Kiln, in Sill, in Grath, in all of Sarn, said so. The house kept its days in loaves and glances and the space at the end of a row, and a day begun was a day begun, whether anybody counted it or not. The hand came down. It always came down. That was the whole of what his body knew about reaching: up an inch, down against the fold, and the fold did not move.

The morning came by the nose before it came by the eye. Hot clay lay under tallow, and wet wool lay under that, and below both came the sour mineral breath of the corner that no broom in Low Kiln had been given an instruction about in two years. The boy lay on the bed under the Vail kiln-house roof with the hearth at his back and his face turned a little toward the boards, and he knew the order of the smells before the grey showed at the window. The first sound was the kiln drawing breath in fours with a gap. It was not a bell. No bell had rung under this roof in a hundred and ninety-two days, and the bell that belonged to the square was behind them, tied to an Account-day that had gone off up the road with a box locked under a thane’s coat. Below the floor the carried fire answered four low pulls and kept the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer without a name for a hundred and ninety-two days; and through the finger’s-width door the river kept its note of water over water, a fifth low thing that had never been counted among the hearings and had never been invented to stand up as a seventh. Overhead the boards kept four nails, four, and a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts in three places, and none of them rose up and became the others, because a man who wanted them to agree would have to put a payer under them, and no payer stood to any of them.

The crust lay where it had been put, gone leathery in the dry heat of the kiln month, two inches from his ear, with its empty place beside it unnamed and no third. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and was still frozen; nobody under any roof had been given an instruction about the thread, and a basket set down was a pot set down and left. Cold had stopped at cheek, temple and the small bare joint at the wrist where the wool rode back, as if it too had reached the end of its instruction; the afterdrop waited on the far side of a warming nobody in this house would perform. He knew the afterdrop when he saw it, and he wanted the crust, wanted the breast, wanted the pot handle standing two inches beyond his hand, wanted a number spoken by somebody who would have known what a number counted. He got none of them. He had stopped expecting to be told, which was not the same as not wanting it. The body was allowed to humiliate him. A man who had once reduced shoulders in the dark could not lift a crust two inches to his own mouth, and the dry joke of it had long since stopped being funny enough to mention.

Sella Vail came in with the grey, bringing woodsmoke and wet wool ahead of her, and gave the morning its instruction before she gave it to any person. “Keep it off his ears. It’s the river wind,” she told the wool. She told the fire it was the thaw, told the pot to stand where it stood, told the broom what it would be wanted for before anybody had asked for the broom, and told the running water to come when it was called; she gave no instruction to the basket and no reason to the basket, and she said the house where another woman would have said I. She kept her half-pace. Her eye went once to the clay jar cold under its glaze on the wall, and her hand started toward it and shut half over nothing without touching it; she pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn so the grey streak at her temple showed pale in the low light, and the little-door burn closed on nothing when the hand had nothing to hold. At the hearth she turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and said The house will keep it to the fire, stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand counting under her breath toward no opening — the small intake at the top, the hold, the letting go — and set the pot rim out on the board with its handle standing two inches beyond the reaching hand. Her hand rested a half-beat longer at the setting than the setting needed, and then went away. She did not look toward the bed.

The widow’s mark hung on its cord at her throat where it had hung since the first firing, a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it. It had been touched once, before the kiln on the first morning of Ember, held one breath, let go; nobody had praised what it mended and nobody had seen it broken, and there had been no opening since. A quiet morning after a reading was not an opening. She did not touch it now at a hearth, and she would not touch it at a shelf, a well, a rack, a road, a basket, an afternoon or a dusk, because the hand went to the mark before the kiln and at no other hour. People noticed the burn first and the mark last, which was the order she preferred.

A neighbour came to the threshold with dry scurf of mud on her hem and asked what the firing would want now the account had gone up the road. Sella did not give her a figure. “Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there,” she said. “The house has said it.” The woman carried the question away level, with nothing slopped, priced, refused or entered. The printed figure still said one in twelve and nobody had challenged it; the true subtraction sat locked in the box that Berr Clay had carried off toward High Kiln, and a narrow slate had gone up the road in the hands of Hessa Rale with the writing turned to her sleeve and no figure coming off it, while a soft-cuffed clerk remained set at an out-of-line landing for a measure nobody had called. All of it had gone away or stood out of reach, and none of it had come back under this roof. A figure said over a basket was not a figure entered in a slate and a figure entered in a slate was not a figure paid; the cost of the man carried out of the yard earlier in the kiln month stood entered nowhere, and his pale line lay where it had been laid, on a place of its own that was joined to neither the sweet edge on a wrong wind nor any absence in the yard.

In the middle of the morning she carried him out, base first and the weight settled the way a woman sets a vessel that must not rock, with the instruction going to the wool and the river wind and never to the basket. The arm had carried him a hundred and ninety-one mornings and kept the height of the handle, the give of the shoulder and the turn of the wrist at a step’s end — a record in muscle that remembered weight and gave no account of cost. In the Clay Yard the kilns stood at their heat in the kiln month’s second working week, and the district went on calling Tallow the firing month and Ember the kiln month without a word spent between the two names. Woodsmoke went before her in long low bars out of the chimneys and the river wind came up behind with the taste of water over water, the two passing on the stair of the morning as they had passed every morning of his life without taking each other’s colour or spending a word on either.

She went around the places as she would go around a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. The clean dark line where Ash had turned the corner for two years lay along the skirting of the Vail wall, and the new finer grey called the thaw lay beside it, neither swept and neither with an instruction; the household went around both and gave the broom no word about either. Beneath the lowest rack the broken birth-slates lay where nobody entered them, and the yard went around the space above them as wide as if a hand had been set there and told to keep it. On the wall the shallow clay pan had stood mouth-up through Reed and was standing there no longer; its round ring waited in the dust with no scrape, no crumb, no print and no chip of a rim, and she went around the round place as if the pan still held water a child might spill. At the well the the trough stood bare and dark, and its water was called weather. In the returning line she went around the empty place where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out, and around the place the two carriers had crossed with the glaze-room hand between them, each with a look that asked for nothing and found nothing, a wide step and no number and no explanation given to the basket. The yard had learned to carry every absence with a glance and a wide step, and the boy read the space a hand did not go into as carefully as the space it did.

Under the wall Tarn took up the broom only after the broom had been told what it was wanted for, and his stroke went over the flagstones past the shallow bright patch and back again in the ordinary way; he gave the basket no word and the yard gave him none. Beyond the Vail wheel-shed the wheel turned at Jorra Vail’s hand, its low note coming through the wall and going out into the yard without anybody making a cause of it; the finger’s-width door of the back room stood as it stood and was entered by nobody the boy could see. No collector came up the clay road and no collector went down it. The two men kept their silences in the house’s ordinary grammar, and the boy kept them on their own places and laid no finger across them, because three likenesses in a row will stand up and call themselves a truth if a man leaves them standing long enough to want an answer.

A woman come up from the racks with slip dried pale in the creases of her wrists asked what the kiln would want now the figure had gone up the road, and Sella, standing half a pace from an open slate with the basket low at her side, sent her to the pot and the weather again. “Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there,” she said. “The house has said it.” The woman went away level with her question still in her hands, and nothing was priced, refused or entered. No third gap came into the fold, no likeness stood up out of the yard’s absences and called itself a cause; he took them down one at a time as he had taken down all the others, with nobody to help and nobody to see.

Back under the roof she set the basket low at the angle where the underside of the loaf shelf made a roof that stopped at the edge of his reach, and moved about the room with the ordinary work of a quiet morning, giving the wool and the fire and the pot their instructions and none to the basket. The afternoon had not yet begun to thin; it lay warm in the low places and had not yet started to go off the high air, and the shelf’s roof came down toward him in a slow slant and stopped where his hand stopped. The pot rim stood out on the board with its handle two inches beyond him, close enough to be a question and too far to be an answer. He wanted it the way he wanted the breast and the crust and the number nobody would speak, and he could no more close the two inches than he could have read the charcoal waiting unread under the front edge of the shelf for a hand that was not hers and a day that had not come.

Through the finger’s-width door the river kept its note of water over water; through the floor the carried fire gave four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been; overhead the boards kept four nails, four, and a knot the width of a thumb. He did not lay the three across one another. He had spent a hundred and ninety-one days taking likenesses down one at a time and had not yet learned to let one stand, and there was nobody under the roof who would have known what he was doing if he had. The six hearings of the full note stayed beside and never among, with the sixth out of a person filed where a thing out of a person must go; no seventh had come in a hundred and ninety-two days and none was invented to fill the empty place, for an empty place kept empty is a finding and a finding filled to make a row come out even is a finding forged. The three near-full answers stood on their own tray, advanced and unpaid. The nine stoppings stood as objects with no tenth before the seventh year was earned. Three held changes stood on three places, the loft-sill scrape on a fourth and the pale line on a fifth, and no word was spoken over any of them and no payer named. He wanted all of it to mean something and he would not let it stand up and mean something, because a rule is an account that has found a payer and no payer stood to any of it.

Sella moved her lips along the row above him, standing with her back to the basket, and gave nobody a figure. Her eye went once to the clay jar cold under its glaze without a touch. She told the fire it was the thaw and turned the coals with the back of a broken pot, and said The house will keep it where another woman would have said I; she stood with the back of the pot in her hand counting under her breath toward no opening, pushed coal-black hair back with the burn-wrist so the grey streak showed pale off the coals, and did not look toward the bed. Outside, the yard went wide around the place the carriers crossed, with a look and a wide step and no number, and the clay road lay where it lay with the box gone up it and nobody bringing the figure back.

Cold had stopped at cheek, temple and the small bare joint at the wrist where the wool rode back, and the afterdrop waited on the far side of a warming nobody in this house would perform. The thread at his mouth was frozen and unwiped. The crust lay leathery two inches from his ear with its empty place unnamed and no third. He wanted it, wanted the breast, wanted the pot handle standing two inches beyond his hand, wanted a number spoken by somebody who would have known what a number counted, and got none of them and had stopped expecting to be told. And his hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water through the finger’s-width door and came down against the fold, as it had every morning of his life, while one hundred and ninety-two stood exact, complete and unspent in the dark of the wool and the count went forward into the next day.

The world was full of two inches. Hot clay lay under tallow under wet wool, and below all three came the sour mineral breath of the corner that no broom in Low Kiln had been given an instruction about in two years; the household went around that corner and gave the broom no word about it.

Tarn took the broom only after the broom had been told what it was wanted for. “Six counted. The next I don’t count,” he told the wood. To a neighbour wanting the thaw early and another wanting it late he said “Yes, house” to both, agreed so entirely that no argument could find a foot, and went on wanting the late because a late thaw puts off the paying, though the paying had stood in the ruts since the water went. His chest had been tight since the dark. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line and no scrubbing lifted it, and the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back. He swept the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector without once turning along it. No collector came up the clay road and no collector went down it.

The breath from Seed 16 stood unspent at the top of every reach, with the small interval where a breath should have gone in and had not. The sentence about an ear under this roof being worth more than the debt that held it came to his teeth and to the threshold of the word that would call the loan, and stopped there with no figure and no name. He drew the breath he should have spent and did not spend it. Nine stoppings stood as objects with no tenth before the seventh year was earned; the seventh year stood nearer its end than it had ever stood and had not given, and the loan that can hear stayed uncalled. Beyond the Vail wheel-shed the wheel turned. He kept dusty forearms and clean ones on one tray and asked neither which slate had been broken nor whether it was a birth-slate; the broken birth-slates lay beneath the lowest rack and were entered by nobody. He spoke to the wheel before he spoke to anyone. “It doesn’t care whose hands. It cares if they’re steady,” he said, and told a pot “You’ll do” in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either. He knew the full note that pot would give three turns before it left his hands and withheld the knowing from a man come up on a wrong wind who said a pot singing before its time generally sings itself out early.
