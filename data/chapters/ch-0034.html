Seven at the Teeth

Days 141–143 · Reed 22–24, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, the Weir and Sill

Seven times a word may come to the teeth and go back, and seven times is a keeping and not a payment. A reed bundle is counted in the arm that carried it, and a year held to its last day is a year still held. What is held is owed, and what is owed waits for a hand that has not come.

— A proverb of Low Kiln, said over the banked fire in the last tend of Reed

— Day 141 —

Reed 22, Y.H. 813 (Secondday)

The reed month went into its last week the way water goes over a stone — without a boundary drawn anywhere, without a hand raised to mark the place where one thing left off and another took hold. It was only that the bundles coming up from the beds were shorter in the arms that carried them, and the broken ends showed green where the knife had bitten, and the frost had taken the tips pale as old bone. Nobody in Low Kiln said the month had turned a corner. The yard had never been one for announcing its own arithmetic. Sella Vail carried the boy down at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, and the pace did not change when the road went from clay to mud to the trodden ash under the yard’s feet; woodsmoke went before her and the river wind came up to meet it, and the two passed through one another about her shoulders without either giving way. She gave the instruction to the wool and to the wind and never to the basket — keep it off his ears, it had come all the way down with them and would go all the way back up — and he lay with his cheek against the fold and the small wet sound coming and going as his head wandered and was not set right. One hundred and forty-one days old. Four months and twenty-one days. Neither number was spoken under any roof that morning, and if somebody had said one aloud in the yard nobody there would have known what it counted.

The cold had come in the night and lay in the ruts like a thin skin drawn over the mud, and the wool at his ear was wet where the river wind had worked at it all the way down. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and was not wiped, because nobody under any roof had been given an instruction about the thread, and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. He had been a man who could tell, from the lie of a blanket and the movement of a lip, how much warmth a body had lost and how much time it had left in which to lose the rest; here he could not lift the wool off his own ear, and the small wet sound came and went as his head wandered and Sella did not set it right. Cold arrived in its known order — cheek, temple, wrist — and the order was the one thing about this morning that was the same in both lives. He noted it and wanted the wool pulled up. The wanting was louder than the noting, as it had been when he was smaller, and both were true at once.

The cutters had been downriver since before first grey and were coming back up with the last of the cutting across their shoulders, armfuls laid against the cold the way a man lays a child against his coat. They came around the pans set out to dry and the trough by the well with its skin of ice, and the ice was called weather and left where it lay. At the edge of the work Sella set the basket down level, as a pot is set down where it must not be slopped, and stood a half-pace from every open slate. The boy watched the ground go by above the basket’s rim: dry mud, a boot’s print held and let go, the grey reeds standing in the ruts in long ranks with nobody counting them.

Women worked the racks with their skirts kilted and their forearms grey to the wrist, turning wet pots out of the shade and setting them where the light fell; the light went along the rims and came off them and no woman called it a fine morning. Two men went past with a barrow that had lost its easy rolling in the flood, and neither put a number to what the barrow held. A girl younger than the creel girl stood at the slip pits with her hands in a pan up to the elbow and lifted them out to show the woman beside her that they were clean, and the woman looked at them and went back to her own pan. It was the last work of the reed month done in the ordinary way, with no more ceremony than a pot set down to dry. The boy watched it from the basket’s low angle, where the underside of every board was a roof and the top of no board was in his line of sight.

A cutter come up from the reed beds with a bundle on his shoulder and wiped his palms down his thighs before he set it down. A girl with a creel stood looking at the bundle the way a woman looks at a pot before she lifts it — measuring the weight with her eyes and finding the place her hands would go.

“Lift it,” the cutter said. “Your arm’ll tell you what went into it, and that’s the whole of what it knows.”

She got it up with the green ends shivering and the pale tips brushing her coat, felt the weight settle into the meat of her forearm, and the creel bumped her hip as she turned. “And what’ll it cost?”

He was a dozen paces down with another armful before the answer came back over his shoulder. “It doesn’t know cost. Ask it how heavy it is. That’s a question it can answer.”

There’s your figure, the yard said without anybody saying it; there’s the whole of the account. A low sound went along the racks and did not become a laugh. An older cutter passing the other way with his collar up said, without stopping, “A bundle’s counted in the arm that carries it,” and a voice from the racks put the old question after him: “And the hands that cut it?” He went around the pan set out to dry, and the question went down into the mud behind him and stayed there. The girl carried her question away with the bundle on her shoulder and no number going with it, and the boy watched the carrying arm go away down the yard, because the arm was the only part of the morning that kept a record, and the record was in muscle and would be gone by dark.

A woman working the near row of the drying racks straightened her back, with dry mud worked into the creases of her wrists and a strip of reed held in one hand, and asked what the cutters’ hands would be paid now the tend was nearly out. The man going past with the armful did not break his step.

“The weather and the river,” he said. “Ask them. They’ll tell you when they’ve had enough.”

She shut two fingers out of a fist as she went back to her strips, closing them on nothing and showing nobody what they had held. The question went down the yard ahead of her and arrived nowhere, as questions had arrived nowhere since the reeds first came in. She pressed her thumb against a strip until it bent and said to the ground that frost in the tips would tell its own tale, and a second woman along the row said something about the weather having the last word, and nobody asked what that would cost either. The words came to rest on the pots and stayed there. The boy kept the unanswered question on a place of its own, because a cost asked as a direction and answered with a direction leaves no entry for anybody to read.

The yard went around its three places and its one empty place as it had gone around them every morning of the month. At the Vail corner Ash had turned and left a clean dark line behind it, and beside the line lay the new finer hairline of grey the house called the thaw, thinner than the old mark and paler, gathering where nobody had put a broom. Sella carried past with her shoulder tilted and both lines where they lay, gave the broom no instruction for them and swept neither. Farther on she went wide around the space beneath the lowest rack where the broken birth-slates lay, as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and did not look into the space and did not enter it. And past that, in the returning line where the hands came back at dusk, there was a place the yard went around with a look and a wide step. A hand had gone in there on Seed 15 and had not come out with the rest on Seed 27. No name had come up, no body, no cause, no figure, and no hand had come up to be counted either. The place was gone around and given nothing.

He had seen it gone around in the planting and in the flood and in the first cutting, and each time the going-around was the same piece of work performed carefully and without comment: an eye laid on it, a shoulder turned, a step taken wide enough that no heel came near it, and then the ordinary work resumed as though nothing had been avoided. A surgeon knows the shape of an omission by the care spent on the surface around it. Here the care was spent in a step, and the step was the whole of the record, and the record named nothing. He did not lay the place under the empty space at the rack or under the two gaps on the shelf or under anything else; an absence in a line of hands is an absence in a line of hands until somebody puts a name to it, and nobody in the yard had put one in a year.

The boy noted the order of the going-around without deciding to, as he had noted it in the small hours under another roof and on a hundred mornings between: the shoulder went first, the eyes came after, and the place got no more than the wide step it had been given. He had learned that order in a room where a hand went toward a chart before anybody looked at the face on the table, and in a yard where a hand went toward a slate before anybody looked at the pig, and here it was again in a woman’s shoulder turning a half-beat ahead of her gaze. The hand went to the record first. The person came after. He had no standing to enter any of it anywhere, and the surgeon’s register noted the order and could do nothing with it, which was the whole of what the register had been able to do since he came into this body.

A reed-cutter came along the boards past the basket with a bundle and the short length of grey cord carried inside his coat, the wool or reed-fibre gone the colour of a stitch-hole a thread has been drawn through a dozen times, which is the grey a working thumb leaves and the colour of use and nothing more. He set the bundle down where no slate lay, straightened the cord on his thumb and turned it once, and the knots stood in the small light. He did not count them where anybody could hear him count, and nobody asked him what they held, and the question a weir woman would have put to him was not put here. He put the cord back inside his coat and drew the coat closed over it with the flat of his hand and went on downriver with the bundle judged in his arm. The boy let the cord lie where it lay. He did not lay it beside the charcoal marks under the loaf shelf three doors up, or beside the empty place in the returning line that the yard had just gone around, or beside any cord at a weir, or any string of knots on a board below the sill. Four private keepings in four places are four keepings until a hand lays them together, and no hand of his could have reached the first of them. He left it where it had been put, unread and not joined, as he had left it on the days before and would leave it on the days after.

The carrying arm was the record he could read best, and it was the one that told the least. A carrying arm knows what it has carried and never what the carrying cost. It can say how often it lifted, how the weight sat, which shoulder took the longer armful on a morning when the wind came upriver; it cannot say what the lifting took out of the woman who lifted. He had learned that before he could hold up his own head and he did not need to learn it again. Exact, complete, worth nothing to a thumb looking for a figure. The yard paid its last week’s cut in armfuls and weather and questions that were answered with directions, and no Court thumb would ever read the entry, because there was no entry to read.

Sella shifted the basket a hand’s breadth out of the draught and gave the instruction to the wool and to the river wind again, and never to the basket. She went around the pan set out to dry and went around the rack space again on the way back, and did not touch the mark at her throat, because a yard is not an opening and a basket is not a door. The competence was the whole of the helplessness: he could read a carrying arm the way another man reads a chart, and he could not lift a hand to the bundle the arm had carried. There was no dishonesty in the reading and no use in it, and he had learned to hold both facts in one breath without either cancelling the other. He wanted the bundle two paces off, and the pot under the kiln-house roof, and the place in the returning line that had been given a look and a wide step; he could not reach any of them. His small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water that came from behind the racks, and came down against the fold. One hundred and forty-one went on with nobody’s thumb upon it, and the reeds stood grey in the ruts with nobody counting them, and the cutter went back downriver with the bundle counted only in the arm that carried it.

Sella Vail turned in off the yard and came under the Vail kiln-house roof with the basket held level against her hip, the way a woman carries a pot filled to the brim and will not have it slopped. The light changed at the threshold. Out in the yard it had lain on the reeds in long grey ranks and on the skin of ice at the trough; under the roof it came down through the oiled paper in a soft brown sheet that reached the floor and went no farther, so that the far corner kept its own darker air to itself. Woodsmoke went before her and clung in the beams, and under the smoke lay the dry warm smell of the shelf where the loaves had stood all morning, a smell of crust and ash and old baking that was almost a warmth in itself. Crossed through it, for one breath, came the sharper smell of charcoal from beneath the board — clean, bitter, the smell of the inside of a banked kiln — and then it folded back under the shelf as though it had never been let out.

She set the basket low near the bench leg. There was an angle to the place and she found it without measuring, as a woman finds the lip of a cup in the dark: a person standing saw the top of the board and the row of loaves upon it, but a person lying on the floor saw the underside of that same board become a roof over his face, with the iron bracket that held it standing cold at his cheek. The boy lay at that angle. He had lain at it on other afternoons and knew the grain of the wood above him better than he knew the faces that stood over him. The cold iron of the bracket touched the edge of his ear and did not go away.

“Keep it off him,” Sella said to the wool, tucking the fold with her thumb. “It’s come all the way down and it’ll go all the way back up.” To the pot she said, “Don’t set that on the floor. You’ll pay for it in the thaw.” To the broom, upright in its corner with the bristles up, “Round it. Go around.” And to the fire, with her back already turned toward the basket and her mind gone somewhere else, “It was the thaw. That’s all it was.” She gave no instruction to the basket. A basket set down was a pot set down and left, and the thing in it was nearly four months old and could be trusted to keep its own heat, or not, without anybody naming which.

The hearth breathed behind her. It had given four low pulls through the morning and kept the silence where the fifth should have stood, and the boy lay with that silence coming up through the floor boards under him and the river’s note of water over water coming through the finger’s-width door to join it from another direction. Two low things in two places. He had long ago stopped making one of them. A man who had learned to tell one sound from another in a room where the difference mattered knew the difference between two noises that happened together and one noise with two halves; these were two. The river’s note came through a door that kept a finger’s width of open, and the kiln’s gap came up through the floor under blind ash, and neither had been counted under this roof for a hundred and forty-one days, and neither wanted to be counted. He kept them on two places and went on breathing.

Above him the shelf kept its two spaces. One was the old space at the row’s end, which the planting had brought in and which nobody called a number; the other was a palm’s breadth of bare board that had come in on the night of Seed 6, between a dusk and a light, with no scrape left on the wood, no crumb, no print, no word. A loaf’s worth had stood there and then had not stood there, and the row had gone on with one less in it as a row of loaves will. The shelf kept the two gaps in the room above, and the fold at his ear kept one stiff brown-edged crust and the empty place beside it in the room below, and the two rooms did not touch. He had held them apart on purpose for a hundred and forty-one days. A saying of the loaf-shelves of Low Kiln held that two gaps stay two gaps until a finger lays them together, and an account then wants a name, and no finger in the district had laid these together. He had never understood why the saying was needed at all, until he had understood it too well: a thing left where it is stays a thing left where it is, and the whole district could go on reaching past bare boards and never once put a hand to the space between them.

A neighbour came in out of the yard with a pot in one hand and the dry mud of the strips still worked into the creases of her wrists. She had come up from the worked strips east of the houses where the planting had gone in and the hands had gone in after it, and she carried the weather of that ground on her sleeves and a strip of dry reed caught in the crease of her thumb. It was the same mud she had had on her wrists when the seed went in, and the same question about the hands that had gone down the yard with it then had gone down the yard again with her now, unanswered. She went to the shelf for a loaf. Her hand went past the bare board toward the loaf beyond it — past the palm’s breadth first, because that was where her hand had learned to go, and past the old space at the row’s end as well, without either place stopping her — and her hand was at the bread before her eyes came down to the basket. In between, for the half-beat it takes to find what is not there and go on, she opened on nothing. The opening was so small that her own face did not change. She closed on the hard heel of the loaf beyond.

“That came in with the planting,” she said, meaning the space. She had the loaf in her hand and the pot balanced on her hip, and her glance went down at last and found the boy in the basket, the fold at his ear, the small wet sound coming and going as his head wandered. “And that one there—” She nodded at the basket, at the place where the wool rode back by his ear. “That’s a child’s appetite under an appetite. One hunger at the board and one at the ear.”

She said it in one breath, the way a woman says two ordinary things while her hands are full, and went off with the loaf and the pot level, nothing slopped. She never saw that she had laid the two bare boards under one cause, or that the hunger she had put at his ear was the same hunger she had just reached past on the shelf. She had not laid a finger across the two places. She had not named anything. She had made two rooms into one absence and was already gone with her pot.

The boy lay where she had left the sentence hanging in the air above him and took it in as a man takes in a second wrong reading of the same chart, without correcting either. He had kept the first telling on a place of its own since the planting, and the watcher’s turn a half-beat late behind a pot’s note on another, and the neighbour’s one-breath saying now went on the same place beside them, a further shaping of the same held absence, advanced and not paid. He did not let it lie across the shelf’s two gaps, or across the crust in the fold, or across the space in the returning line where a hand had gone in and had not come out. He did not make a third gap to match the two. He laid no finger of his own across anything, because he had no finger that could reach, and if he had had one he would not have used it. A likeness is not a cause, and an absence in a loaf row is an absence in a loaf row until a hand not his comes along and calls the two of them one account. He filed the saying where the others lay and left it there, exact, complete and worth nothing to anybody.

Sella stood at the row with the neighbour’s question still in the air and moved her lips along the loaves without making a figure. She counted them the way the house counted — in a breath here, a glance there, a space at the end nobody said aloud — and her glance went once to the clay jar on the wall shelf, cold under lead glaze even in the warm of the afternoon, and came away. She gave nobody a number.

“Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there,” she said to the doorway the woman had gone out of. “The house has said it.” The woman carried the unanswered question away with her as level as she had carried the pot, and the question went down the clay yard and arrived nowhere, as such questions had arrived nowhere since the reeds first came in. A cost asked in the form of a direction is answered with a direction, and the answer leaves no entry for anybody to read.

Sella’s lips finished the row. She stood a moment with her hand at her side and then, still with her back to the basket, reached a finger beneath the front edge of the board where the charcoal tally lay. The little-door burn on the back of her hand closed on the wood’s lip and opened again as the finger went under. She did not look at what her finger was doing. She held it a breath out and a breath back, the way a woman feels along the underside of a shelf for a thing she has put there and knows is there, and brought the hand away. She did not go back to the shelf. She did not wipe the finger or say what it had touched or why it had gone under. The dry smell of the loaves closed over the place and the sharper smell of charcoal went back into the grain.

From the basket’s low angle the boy had seen the hand go under and come back. He had seen the burn close on the lip of the wood and open on nothing, and the finger come away with nothing showing on it that he could read. He saw the motion and he saw nothing of what the marks said. He did not count the strokes, though the charcoal lay in little clusters a finger’s breadth apart with the ones at the row’s end faded nearly into the grain. He did not press a thumb where her thumb had been. He did not lay the underside of the board across the two gaps above it, or across the one stiff crust two inches from his ear in the other room, or across the free place in the returning line the yard had gone around that morning. He made no cause of the habit. Such a hand, going under a board without looking, either knows the board by heart or has been told not to look, and the reason stood between the two, and he had no standing to step up to either one. He had seen the same order of reaching in a yard shoulder turning before a yard gaze and in a clerk’s hand going to a slate before his eyes went to a stall, and he never made the two into one rule; this hand under this board would stay where it had been put.

The widow’s mark hung where it hung at her throat, a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and she did not touch it. A shelf was not an opening. An afternoon was not an opening. The mark was touched before the opening of a kiln and at no other hour, and there had been no opening here, only a row of loaves gone through with the lips and a finger under a board and come away without a word. She turned from the shelf and told the fire nothing further, and the fire gave its four low pulls and kept the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for a hundred and forty-one days.

The boy lay with the cold bracket at his ear and the brown light going off the boards. The shelf above him kept its two gaps and its charcoal marks beneath, and the hand had come away without a word, and in the other room the crust lay stiff in the fold with its empty place beside it still unnamed. The two rooms of the house did not touch. His small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water behind the wall and came down against the fold, and one hundred and forty-one went on with nobody’s thumb upon it.

— Day 142 —

Reed 23, Y.H. 813 (Thirdday)

Before the first grey the yard in Low Kiln had the look of a pot stood out overnight: the same shape in the same place on the ground, with the warmth gone out of it and a skin drawn over the top. Sella Vail came down from under the kiln-house roof with the basket held level against her hip and the fold tucked in with her thumb, and gave the instruction to the wool before she gave it to the morning — Keep it off his ears; it had come all the way down in the dark and would go all the way back up — and then to the wind that got in under the eaves and went looking for the cracks, and to nothing that sat in a basket. It was a carried pot and she carried it as one, with the weight on the forearm nearest the wall and the basket square, so that nothing slopped and nothing in it was asked what it wanted. The boy lay with his cheek to the wool and the small wet sound coming and going as his head wandered and was not set right. The wool had ridden back from the small bare joint of his wrist, and the cold had got in there in its known order, wrist before temple, temple before cheek, and he had stopped being surprised by the order some weeks ago and had not stopped wanting it to be different. One hundred and forty-two days old. Four months and twenty-two days. The numbers lay edge to edge with all the others, exact, complete and unspent, and there was no thumb in the district that would have known what to do with them.

Three or four men came up out of the river road and across the Clay Yard with their collars high against the early air. They did not come as a group comes to a door in daylight, with a hand raised and a word said; they came the way water comes at a stone, each one arriving a little behind the one before, and the last of them put his hand to the door-lip before his eyes went to the yard. It was a small thing and the boy saw it and kept it on the tray where such things lay: the hand to the record before the person, whether the record was a slate, a cord, the underside of a shelf or the edge of a door held a finger’s width open. He did not lay it across Berr’s twice-wiped thumb or the soft-cuffed clerk’s slate or the finger that went beneath the loaf shelf. Hands went first in this district because the district kept its accounts in places a face could not reach; it was a habit and not a doctrine, and he had no standing to enter anybody’s hand anywhere.

The men went low-voiced into the room off the shed, and the door of it kept its finger’s width of open as it had kept it on other mornings, as though a door left so could still be counted as shut. Sella carried past at the half-pace. She turned no head toward the door. She gave the house no name for the men and did not say the room was cold, and the low voices went on behind the finger of open without arriving at the basket as anything that could be repeated. A thing not named is not therefore a thing not kept; she kept it by not naming it, the way she kept the two lines in the corner by not sweeping them, and the household went on around the keeping as a yard goes around a stone.

Jorra Vail came through the finger’s-width door of the Vail wheel-shed before the men’s voices had gone out of the air. The dust was ground as small as meal and worked into both forearms past the bent little finger and under the nails, grey and fine, and it was not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel. He held his forearms a little away from his coat as he came through, so the dust should not brush off on the door, and kept the dusty forearms and the clean ones on one tray the way a potter keeps two slips that must never be mixed. He asked neither which slate had been broken nor whether it had been a birth-slate, because asking either would have made the dust into an entry, and the dust was carried where it was carried and named nothing. Under the lowest rack at the yard’s edge the broken birth-slates lay where they had lain, gone around and entered by nobody. A grey powder on a working forearm is a record as exact as any slate, and this one told what had been handled and not what it had cost, and the boy put it on the same place as the carrying arm and left it there.

He spoke to the wheel before he spoke to anyone, before Sella, before the pot he took off the bench, and the words went to the wheel as a man’s words go to an animal he has worked beside and has no need to flatter. “It doesn’t care whose hands,” he said. “It cares if they’re steady.” To a pot, low, with no welcome in the voice and no unkindness either, he said, “You’ll do.” The pot went on the wheel. He knew the full note that was in it before it left his hands — knew it three turns ahead, as he had known it on other mornings — and kept the knowing behind his teeth, with a kiln-master come up from High Kiln standing not two paces off in wet clay and tallow and the sweetish edge of lead glaze on his coat. The kiln-master said the pot was singing before its time, and that a pot that sings early generally sings itself out early, and Jorra said nothing at all. He did not say it because saying it would call the note early and make something of it, and he had spent sixteen years watching men make something of notes. The boy put the withheld knowing on the tray of near-full answers, advanced and not paid, and did not add it to the hearings. He had six of the full note on a place of their own and no seventh; a knowing kept behind a man’s teeth is not a sound, and he did not promote it into the empty place to keep the row tidy.

Sella had not finished telling him the fire was low. “The fire’s low, Jorra, the fire’s — ” The words were still going toward the pot when his bent finger went into the cold iron of the damper ahead of his hand, found the tongue by heart, moved it a hair’s breadth and left it standing where he had put it. The iron took the cold out of the fingertip and gave a little of it back in reverse, the way a clamp gives back to the hand that sets it. The household straightened around his silence the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted: Sella shut her mouth on the instruction and gave the end of it to the pot, the broom found its line again, and the kiln-master went on talking about early singing to a man who had already answered him with his hands. Nobody said the room off the shed was cold. Nobody said the men had come in with their collars high. The door kept its finger’s width of open, and the low voices went on behind it, or stopped and left the space as a shape in the air, and from the basket one was the same as the other.

The boy read the iron again as a man reads an instrument he has used and put down a thousand times. It was one piece of metal. Its bite held a vessel whole, and its bite could take the hand off a man, and there was no word in Grathic that carried both mercies in one breath; the house had one word for the holding and another for the taking and kept them on two shelves, as it kept two slips from mixing. He had set such a bite on a forearm in a country whose rooms he would never have described to these people, and had watched the Warm back up behind it like meltwater standing against a jam of ice, and had known exactly what he was stopping and what it would cost if he left it a moment too long. That knowledge was a tool and a clamp was a clamp; it was not a doctrine, and he would not have asked it to be one. Here it was one iron with two bites, filed as an object worth nothing and joined to no kiln. He did not carry it to the shut counting room mentioned over buckets in the yard; he did not carry the shut room to the iron. The two tellings stood on their own tray — breath through the nose, breath in the throat, both saying not the war’s hand — and this morning’s men went into a room with a door a finger’s width open and gave no third telling to anyone. The shut room of the two tellings and the room off this shed stood where they stood, and neither had been made the other. He kept them apart by hand, the way another man keeps three slips from mixing, and the keeping was the whole of the work and could not be entered anywhere.

Tarn took the broom only after it had been given its instruction. Sella gave it to the broom before she gave it to him — Round it. Go around — and he took it up as a man takes up a tool already named, saying “Yes, house” to the thaw early and late, agreeing so thoroughly that no argument could find a foot, and keeping the late because a late thaw puts off the paying, though the paying had stood in the ruts since the water went. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line at the Vail corner, and no scrubbing lifted it; the pale clip-ring at his left wrist, in place since Y.H. 806, held what light there was and gave it back. He moved quickly, as he always moved, and stopped moving altogether when anybody told him he had done well, which nobody had done this morning and which he had arranged his morning to avoid.

His seventh year stood nearer its end than it had ever stood, and was held. It was a record in muscle, complete and closed, and worth nothing anyone could pay; it was the same record the cutter’s arm kept and the same record every arm in the yard kept, and not one of them could be entered on a slate. The boy lay with his cheek to the fold and read the lifting from the low angle: the hand to the bundle first, the eyes to the girl after, the cost nowhere at all. He had learned that order before he could hold up his own head and had never once been given standing to enter it anywhere.

A woman come up from the beds with dry mud worked into the creases of both wrists asked what the cutters’ hands would be paid when the tend was out. She stood a moment with the question in her mouth and then shut two fingers out of a fist as she turned away, the way a woman closes a thing she has been carrying and will not set down where anyone can see it. A slate would be found and a figure made if a hand came up in a month or a year. No hand had come up. The boy did not ask which hand, or whose, or what the figure would have been. He had no standing to ask and no word in Grathic that would have carried the question, which was perhaps the same thing.

The last bundles had gone downriver in the morning and come back on shoulders in the afternoon, and the afternoon had set them down in the ruts and left them there while the light went out of the bank. He had kept it through a hundred and forty-three days in a body that could not have lifted a single object from the towel, and the keeping of the things apart was the only work in the room that no slate would enter.
