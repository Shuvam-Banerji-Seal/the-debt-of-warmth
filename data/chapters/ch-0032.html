— Day 135 —

Reed 16, Sixthday, Y.H. 813

The reed month went into its last third on a morning the colour of wet wool, with the sky the grey of the reeds below it and no line drawn between the two. Low Kiln came up into the light the way a pot comes up from under a bench — the shadow first, the rim after it, the whole belly last — and the yard received it without remark. Sella Vail carried the boy out at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, going wide around the stones and the laid-down pans and anything set out to dry, and gave the instruction to the wool and to the river wind and never to the basket. Keep it off his ears; it had come down with them and would go back up with them, and a basket set down was a pot set down and left. The little-door burn on the back of her hand closed on the wicker’s edge and then on nothing. Woodsmoke went before her down the yard and wet wool went with her, and under both the faint sour mineral tang of the Ash that had gathered in the corners of this house for two years and would not be swept. He was one hundred and thirty-five days old, four months and fifteen days, and nobody in the yard said so, and nobody under any roof in the district knew there was a number to say.

She set him down at the edge of the work where no slate lay, as she had set him down every morning of the cutting, and stood a half-pace off with the burn shut on nothing. The basket sat in the thin shade at the rack’s end with the wool up around his ears and the grey coming through at his cheek. A woman at a tub lifted her head as they passed, saw a basket and the woman carrying it, and went back to her work without appearing to have seen either. That was the whole of the welcome the yard had for them and the whole of the harm it meant them; he had long since stopped looking for more in it. The instruction went to the wool and the river wind again, low and practical, and went nowhere near the basket. It had never occurred to anybody under this roof to give the basket an instruction, and a thing with no instruction was a thing nobody had taken responsibility for, and so it was left where it was set.

The reeds stood in the ruts where the barley had stood a month before, in grey ranks going down toward the reed beds, the broken ends green under the frost and the tips pale as old bone. Nobody counted them. They came in on the shoulders and in the crooks of arms, a going-downriver at first light and a coming-back at dusk, and a bundle standing in the mud was judged by the arm that had carried it and by nothing set on a slate. A cutter going down past the racks shifted his load from one shoulder to the other and said to a girl with a creel who had asked him the weight of it, “Lift it. Your arm’ll tell you what went into it, and that’s the whole of what it knows.” He went on with the bundle, and the girl lifted it, and the arm under it gave her its answer and kept the rest. The girl shifted the bundle on her hip and asked what it would cost, and the cutter, already a dozen paces down with another armful, said over his shoulder, “It doesn’t know cost. Ask it how heavy it is.” The girl did not ask again. By noon the bundles would stand in the ruts where the barley had stood, each one a shape nobody had numbered, and the arms that had brought them would go back downriver for more. A judge could have set a figure on the whole day’s cutting in the time it takes to lift a pot; no judge was set here, and the reeds went on standing for the arm that had carried them and for nobody else. The boy, carried at the edge of the work where no slate lay, watched the shoulders go down the yard and come back up it and noted the order of them without deciding to: the hand went to the load before the eyes went to the road, and the road got no more of a look than the load got a name.

A woman working the strips at the drying racks straightened from her work with dry mud worked into the creases of her wrists and asked the cutter what the hands would be paid now the reed had gone into its last third. It was a question about a figure made to sound like a question about the weather, and the yard heard it as both. The cutter did not stop. “The weather and the river,” he said. “Ask them. They’ll tell you when they’ve had enough.” The woman kept her eyes on his retreating shoulders a moment longer than the shoulders required, and then went back to her strips with the answer standing where he had left it. No figure went with her. Nothing had been entered. She shut two fingers out of a fist as she went back to her strips, the way a woman shuts a count she will not say aloud, and the gesture went down into the mud with her and came up nothing. The cutter had given her a direction because a direction was the only answer the yard kept in stock; a figure would have had to be made, and a made figure wants a maker, and the maker would have had to stand where the hands were and be named. Nobody in the ruts wanted that standing. The boy watched the two fingers close and noted them on a place of their own, beside the shoulders and the load and the road, and did not make a sum of the three. The reed would be cut because the reed was there and the river was low enough to cut it, and when the hands had had enough they would stop, and the stopping would not be written down either. Account-day stood four days off at Reed 20, and nobody in the ruts had yet said the date.

the Clay Yard went around its own absences the way water goes around a stone it cannot move. Sella carried him past the clean dark line where the Ash ridge had turned, and past the new finer hairline of grey that had gathered beside it and been called the thaw, with a care that looked like not seeing either one. She did not sweep them. A line left where it was was still a line kept, and the house said the grey would go when it went, and he had learned by now that a thing a house said would go was a thing a house had decided not to carry out. At the racks she went wide around the space beneath the lowest rack where the broken birth-slates lay, as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and entered it no more than she had entered it on any of the hundred and thirty-four days before. Then they came to the edge of the worked strips, and there was the other place.

It was not a hole and it was not a mark. It was a space in the returning line where, on Seed 15, a hand had gone in with the seed and on Seed 27 had not come out with the rest. Sella went around it with a look and a wide step and no number, the way the yard had gone around it every morning since, and the boy felt the half-pace change by the width of a hand as she shifted the basket away from it. No name went with the step. No cause, no body, no figure, no payer. If a hand came up out of the ground in a month or a year a slate would be found and a figure made to stand at the foot of it; until then the space would go on being gone around, and the month would go on standing short. A cost with no figure was still a cost, and the arm that had carried the seed in that morning had known the weight of it and knew nothing of what it had cost.

He set the going-around on the place where it already lay, beside and never among the shelf’s two gaps and the crust in the fold two rooms away, and drew no line through them. A saying of the loaf-shelves held that two gaps stay two gaps until a finger lays them together and an account then wants a name, and a neighbour had once laid a planting and a child’s appetite under one cause and gone off with her pot without seeing she had joined anything. The keeping of the places apart was invisible, exact, and was the only work in this yard that nobody had asked him to do.

He thought about the arm that carried him as a man might think about an instrument he had once known by the wear on its handle. She had carried him out every morning of his life and would carry him back at dusk, and the arm had kept its record in the muscle — the reach to the wicker, the settling of the weight, the half-pace kept from every open slate, the width of the step around the place where the hand had gone in. The record was exact. It told what had been carried and how it had been carried and where the carrier had chosen to go around. It told nothing of what the carrying cost. There was no nerve in the muscle that could enter a figure, and no Court thumb would ever read the writing of it, and the arm would have given the same account if it had been carrying a pot or a sack of clay or his own small useless weight. He had spent a hundred and thirty-five days learning the difference between a record and a price, and the arm kept its record on a place where nothing could be entered against it: complete, faithful, worth nothing anyone could pay. He could have drawn the arm the way a man draws a field before the instrument comes down — the reach, the pressure, the direction of the give, the place where the tissue had been taught to do the same thing a thousand times and now did it without being told. What he could not draw was the cost, because the cost had never been put into the muscle in the first place. It lived in a different room: in a woman counting under her breath toward no opening, in the grey at her temple, in the half-beat her hand rested longer than the setting needed before it went away. That room was not this one and he had no standing to enter it. He kept the arm’s record and the other room on separate places, as he kept everything, and the keeping was the only honest work available to a child who could not hold a cup.

The order of reaching was the same here as it had been in every room he had ever been carried through. The hand went to the record before the hand went to the person. It had gone to the wool and the pot and the fire and the broom before it came to him, and here it went to the load and the line and the space in the returning line before it came to the child in the basket. He noted the order as a man notes a sequence he knows the cost of and cannot touch, without standing to change it and without a word in Grathic to ask for a different one. A man who could once reduce a shoulder in the dark could not now close two inches of his hand on the rim of the basket two paces off. The competence was in the noticing and the helplessness was in the hands, and the two sat side by side and did not become one.

Beyond the racks and the worked strips the Ilm went on with its note of water over water, a low fifth thing that the yard had never counted among the full note and never would. The river wind brought it up to the ear under the fold and took it away again, and the boy’s head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far. Nobody under this roof had been given an instruction about the sound, and nobody in the yard had been given an instruction about the thread at his mouth, and a basket set down was a pot set down and left. Morning went into midday over the racks while he lay at the edge of the work and listened. The note went on coming up out of the water below the reed beds and going off again on the wind, a low thing with no figure in it and no payer named, the fifth thing that was never counted among the others. His head went toward it and made the small wet sound and came back, and the hand outside the wool went up an inch and came down, as it had gone up and down on every morning since the month turned. Reach and note and count stayed in three places and none of them became one.

His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note and came down against the fold. He could not reach the bundle going down the yard, or the pot, or the space beneath the lowest rack, or the place in the returning line where the hand had gone in and not come out. One hundred and thirty-five days old, four months and fifteen days, he kept the count exact and unspent and spoken by nobody. The woman at the racks went back to her strips with the weather in her mouth. The reeds stood grey in the ruts beyond the racks, uncounted, and the arm that carried them knew what it had carried and not what it had cost.

At midday she carried the boy in off the yard and under the roof of the Vail kiln-house in Low Kiln, through the door that kept its finger’s width of open and did not shut behind her, and the light changed from the grey of the ruts to the brown of a room kept out of the wind. Sella Vail gave the instruction to the wool before she gave it to anything else — keep it off his ears, it was the river wind — and to the pot, and to the broom standing where the broom stood, and to the fire under its blind ash, and never once to the basket. The basket was a pot set down, and a pot set down was left where it was put. Woodsmoke went ahead of her into the room and wet wool went with her, and under both the faint sour mineral tang of the Ash that had lived in the corners of this house for two years and would not be swept.

She set him low and a little back, near the bench leg, at the angle at which the underside of a board becomes a roof. It was an angle she had chosen every day of his life without knowing she had chosen it; from the basket he saw what a man standing saw only the top of. the loaf shelf ran above him with its row kept the way a house keeps its days badly — loaves pushed along, the hard heels of them going toward the wall, and spaces where a loaf’s width should have stood. There were two of those spaces. The old one sat at the row’s end and had been there longer than the boy had been under the roof; the second was a palm’s breadth that had come in on the night of Seed 6 with no scrape, no crumb, no print and no word, and had been going around the house’s mouth ever since as a thing nobody named. The front edge of the board was at his eye level and its underside opened above him like a low roof seen from beneath, the grain running the length of the room with the dark of the day held in it.

The cold iron of the bench bracket was at his cheek. He had been set so near it that the cold came through the wool and did not give, and he lay with the small bare joint of his cheek against it and the grey light going over the top of the shelf where his eyes could not follow. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and nobody under this roof had been given an instruction about the sound. The thread at his mouth had frozen on the walk in and would stay frozen until somebody was told to wipe it, which nobody had been.

Above him she stood with her back to the basket and moved her lips along the row. It was not a counting that any ear could take. The lips went along the loaves and stopped at the spaces and went on, the way a woman’s hand goes over a bare board and closes on the loaf beyond it. She glanced once at the clay jar standing cold under its glaze on the wall shelf, and gave no figure. The jar had stood in the house since he was a month old and he had watched her glance at it for a hundred and thirty-five days and never once watched her touch it. Whatever the jar held was held there without a number, and the glance was the whole of the house’s account of it. Then she pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carried the little-door burn, the grey streak at her temple showing pale in the light off the coals — the streak she said had come in the winter Garl Vail died and would not discuss beyond that — and told the fire something low that was an instruction and not an account. She told the fire it was the thaw.

The dry warm smell of the shelf — bread and dust and the clay that had been carried past it a thousand times — lay over the room. For one breath it was crossed by another smell, sharper, mineral, a little bitter: charcoal, the charred stick drawn across the underside of the board and left there. It came from under the shelf where the marks lay, where nobody thought to look for a keeping, and it went as quickly as it came, leaving the bread smell where it had been.

She did not look down at the board. That was the first thing he noted, because he was lying at the angle where the board’s underside was all there was to look at, and her face was turned the other way toward the row and the jar and the wall. Without a word she reached a hand behind her and down, and the little-door burn on the back of it closed on the wood’s front lip and opened again as the fingers went under. He saw the knuckle set against the grain and the thumb come up, the way a hand goes under a shelf to feel for what it knows is there, not to find out what is there. The finger went under far enough for the knuckle to set. It stayed a breath out and came back. The burn closed on nothing as the hand withdrew, and she did not go back to the shelf.

From the low angle he saw the hand go under and come back and saw nothing of what the marks said. He had seen them before, from this same angle on the first Account-day of Reed: short strokes in groups a finger’s breadth apart, some pressed hard enough to leave a smooth track where the stick had dug, some the colour of old smoke and gone almost into the grain, faintest toward the row’s end as if the hand that made them had gone away from the task or grown tired of pressing. They did not make Grathic words. No slate had been set under them to catch what they meant. He did not count the strokes now. He did not press a thumb where her thumb had been, or lay the underside of the board beside the two gaps above it, or lay either of them beside the one stiff brown-edged crust lying two inches from his ear in the room the shelf did not touch. He did not make a cause of the habit. A hand that goes under a board without looking either knows the board by heart or has been told not to look, and the reason stood between the two and was not entered. The care of the hiding told more than the writing would, and the writing was not his to read, and so he kept the care and the marks on a place of their own and left both where they lay.

A saying of the loaf-shelves held that two gaps stay two gaps until a finger lays them together and an account then wants a name. The saying stood in the room with the two gaps and went unfulfilled, as it had gone unfulfilled every afternoon since the palm’s breadth came in on the night of Seed 6. Neighbours had laid the old space and the empty place at a child’s ear under one cause in a single breath and gone off with their pots without seeing they had joined two rooms; he had heard the sayings and kept each on its own place, and had not made any of them his own. Now there was a third place under the shelf, and a finger had been under it and come away without a word, and still nothing touched. The keeping was exact and invisible and was the only work in this house that nobody had asked him to do.

He knew the weight of a paper turned face-down on a table because what was written beneath it was meant for one reader and not the room, and he knew that the turning of it mattered more than the words. That was a knowing without a hand to spend it on. Here the hand went under the board without the paper, without the table, without so much as a look to see whether anyone was watching the shelf. A second account kept beneath the first, in a material no Court thumb could spell, on a board a child lay under and a woman stood over with her back turned. He filed the hand beneath the board on the tray where the other record-first hands stood — the midwife reaching for the slate before the child, the thane wiping his thumb before he touched nothing, the clerk setting his figure down before he looked at the face, the woman at the bare board taking her loaf — and did not let it become a cause. Five likenesses in a row will stand up and call themselves a doctrine if nobody holds them apart, and a doctrine is an account that has found a payer. Here there was no payer, no figure, no reading and no finger in the district laid the marks below the shelf across the gaps above it, so he held them apart, which was what a man does with instruments on a towel before he loses the use of his hands.

A neighbour come up from the strips with dry mud worked into the creases of her wrists and water standing in the creases let herself in behind Sella and stood with a wet pot against her hip. She did not look into the basket; baskets were not looked into. “What’ll the row want before the tend’s out?” she asked, and asked it of the room, or of the board, or of Sella’s back, and not of any person standing there. It was a question about a figure wearing the coat of a question about the weather, and the house heard it as both.

Sella did not turn from the shelf. “Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there,” she said. “The house has said it.”

The woman shifted the pot from one hip to the other, and the water in the creases of her wrists went dark again where her sleeve rubbed them. “The pot doesn’t answer.”

“No,” Sella said. “But it’s more use than me.”

That was the whole of it. The woman carried the question away level, the pot held steady against her so nothing slopped, and went back down toward the strips with the dry mud in her wrists and the unanswered question in her mouth, and neither of them entered anything. A cost asked in the form of a direction had been answered with a direction, and the answer had gone home with her and left no figure standing in the room. The boy watched her go and set her question on the place where the yard’s other unanswered questions stood — the reeds judged by the carrying arm, the hands that went in by the look, the weather, the river — and did not make a sum of them. Account-day stood four days off at Reed 20 and nobody in this room had said the date.

The widow’s mark hung at Sella’s throat where it hung, a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the kiln scratched into it, and she did not touch it. A shelf and an afternoon were not openings, and no opening had come under this roof in a hundred and thirty-five days. The clean dark line where the Ash ridge had turned lay along the skirting board with the new finer hairline of grey beside it, and neither had been swept, and the house called both the thaw and said the grey would go when it went. She told the fire it was the thaw, turned the coals with the back of a broken pot, fed them, and stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand counting under her breath toward no opening, while the grey streak at her temple showed pale and the little-door burn shut on the pot’s edge and then on nothing.

Above him the shelf kept its two gaps — the old space at the row’s end and the palm’s breadth that had come in on the night of Seed 6 — and beneath it the charcoal marks lay pressed into the grain where the finger had been and come away without a word. The room that held the shelf and the room that held the crust and its empty place were two rooms, and neither touched the other. He lay at the low angle with the cold iron at his cheek and his hand outside the wool going up an inch toward the note of water through the finger’s width of door and coming down against the fold. One hundred and thirty-five days old, four months and fifteen days, he kept the count exact and unspent and spoken by nobody, and the marks under the board waited for a hand that was not his and a day that had not come.

Reed 17, Seventhday, Y.H. 813

Before first grey they came up the Clay Yard in ones and twos, three or four of them, their collars high against a wind that had not yet made up its mind which way it would blow. Sella Vail carried the boy out at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, and the dark shapes went ahead of her and turned in at the shadow of the Vail wheel-shed without a word that travelled. She gave the house no name for them. She did not say that the back room was cold.

The room off the shed kept its finger’s width of open, as it had kept it through every day of his life, and the light came through that thin seam and lay on the flagstones like a thread laid down and not taken up. A door left a finger open is a door somebody means to come back through without the trouble of opening it properly, and the boy, who had once held the corner of a chart down with the flat of a knife while another hand went in underneath, read the small architecture of the gap and gave it no use. Men went in. Men came out. What they carried in went across the threshold in low voices and came back out as dust on a thrower’s forearms, and nothing in the yard was entered.

The collars were high not against the weather but against being known, which is a different use for a collar and one the boy had learned to read on men who went into rooms where a slate lay face-down. He watched them from the level of the basket and saw what the half-pace was for: it kept a carrying arm from coming within reach of a hand that might want to know what it had carried. A low voice is a voice that has decided who may hear it before it decides what it will say, and the house gave the men the whole of its attention in the only way it had — by going on with its work and giving them no name. Sella’s little-door burn closed on the wicker’s edge and then on nothing as she passed, and her head did not turn. Whatever the room held, it held it in the dark behind a seam a finger would pass through, and the boy set the seam on the tray where the other kept things stood, beside the charcoal under the shelf and the cord inside the cutter’s coat, three materials no Court thumb had been asked to spell. He did not let them touch.

The instruction went to the wool and to the river wind and never to the basket. Keep it off his ears; it had come down with them before the light and would go back up with them at dusk, and a basket set down was a pot set down and left. Woodsmoke went before her and wet wool went with her, and under both the faint sour mineral tang of the Ash that had lived in the corners of this house for two years and would not be swept. She carried him past the finger’s width of door without turning her head toward it, at the half-pace, going wide around a pan set out to dry and a slate propped where a child might knock it, and if she knew the men had come she had made nothing of their coming that a Court thumb could read.

Jorra Vail came through the open door of the shed while she was still giving the instruction to the wind. Fine grey slate dust was ground small as meal into both forearms past the bend of his little finger and under the nails, and it was not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel. He kept the dusty forearms and the clean ones on one tray, as a man keeps two states of one morning apart on a counter where nothing is labelled, and he asked neither which slate had been broken nor whether it had been a birth-slate. The questions stood where they had stood for years, and the boy did not put them into words for him. He spoke to the wheel before he spoke to anyone. “It doesn’t care whose hands,” he said. “It cares if they’re steady.” Then, to a pot turning under his hands with the grey working into the creases of them, “You’ll do.” There was no welcome in the voice and no unkindness either, only the flat note of a man telling a vessel what it already was.

He knew the full note of that pot before it left the wheel. He had known it three turns before, in the moment when the clay was still settling into its shoulder and the note had not yet been given to the air, and he would not say so in front of a kiln-master come up from High Kiln, who stood at the shed door with the sweetish edge of lead glaze on his coat and Ash on his cuffs and said the thing was singing before its time. The kiln-master stopped with his mouth open on the next part of it, the way a man stops when what he has said has not been taken up. “You’ll want a thinner lip on that,” he said, and then, because Jorra gave him no answer, “A pot that sings early generally sings itself out early.” He drew his thumb across his own cuff, where the sweet glaze had come off on the cloth, and looked at the wheel and not at the basket, which was how a man looked at a basket when he had decided it held no account worth entering. Jorra put one hand back to the clay and said nothing. He let the man have the seeing of it and kept the knowing, and the knowing went on a place of its own in the boy’s file, one of the near-full answers that were advanced and never paid, a vessel’s note held behind the teeth because a man who said it early would have something made of it. The kiln-master went back up toward the terrace with his coat carrying the sweet smell and his remark spending itself on the boards, and the wheel went on turning under hands that did not report what they knew.

Sella had not finished telling him the fire was low. “The fire’s low, Jorra. It wants—” The end of it never arrived at him. Before she could set the instruction down where it belonged, his bent finger went into the cold iron of the damper, ahead of his hand the way it always went, and moved the tongue a hair’s breadth and left it standing. The household straightened around his silence the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted off it. Cold iron takes the heat out of a finger faster than water takes it out of a stone, and the bent finger had gone in ahead of the hand because a thrower learns early which part of him can afford the lesson. It came away with the tip gone white for a breath and pink again after, and the boy read the small colour change as an account paid in full and entered nowhere: the tongue moved a hair’s breadth, the flame found a new way through, and some pot in the house would be warmer by the width of a hair by midday. There was no charge and no payer named; a house that says the house where another woman says I can move a damper without telling anybody what it cost the finger that moved it. Nobody gave the finger a word. She shut her mouth on the sentence and gave the end of it to the pot, and the pot, which had not been asked, received the instruction as pots do, without answer and without thanks.

The boy watched the finger come away from the iron. He had watched it a hundred mornings and the iron was not a new thing to him, but he read it again as he read everything that would not stay read: one piece of cold with two bites in it. The bite that closes on a vessel and holds the whole heat in, and the bite that closes on a hand and can take the hand off a man, the same iron doing both according to where it is set and who has the use of it. There was no word in Grathic that held the two mercies at once. He had held such a bite in his own hands in another country and known it by one use only, as an instrument that stops a thing from bleeding out; here the same iron had two bites and the kiln put a boy’s hand into it before breakfast, and neither the woman nor the man who set it there called either bite by a name. He filed the double bite on the tray where objects lay, exact, complete and worth nothing, joined to no kiln and to no man’s silence, and refused, as he refused every morning, to let it stand up and call itself a doctrine.

Beside it, on the same tray and not touching it, lay the two tellings of the counting room shut in the war with the men inside dead of the breath, one carried eighteen leagues east with a date, the second carried eighteen leagues east with a bag and a date, both taken by the yard as history and neither taken into this house. No third telling had come. The men who had gone low into the room off the shed had not supplied one; they had supplied dust and quiet and the finger’s width of door. The boy kept the two tellings where they lay and did not lay them across the iron, though the word for a room shut with the damper closed and a room with a finger’s width of open would have been the same word if he had had a tongue for it and a verdict to spend.

Out in the yard the thaw was being argued in two directions at once. A woman come up from the strips with dry mud in the creases of her wrists wanted it early and said the reeds would rot where they stood; a man with a barrow wanted it late and said nothing good had ever come up out of water. Tarn said “Yes, house” to the first and “Yes, house” to the second, agreeing so entirely that the argument had nowhere left to put its foot, and went on wanting the late one, because a late thaw puts off the paying. The thaw went around the yard as a direction and not a date, and he kept his own date where the house could not check it.

Before the broom he went to the sill and set the small knife at the very edge of it, and told the empty yard where it was, as he had told it every morning of the boy’s life: “It’s back. You can check.” The yard gave no answer and he did not need one; the telling was the account and not the hearing of it. Sella carried him down off the square and below the sill to the outer edge of the fish-trade shade where the stalls thinned out and no Court slate had ever reached, and set the basket at the very end of it where no slate lay. She gave the instruction to the wool and to the running water and never to the basket, and stood a half-pace off. The river wind came up to meet the woodsmoke going off her coat, and under the boards the Ilm went over its own stones with the low note that he had kept as a fifth thing since he was six days old. Salt stood white where the flood had touched the posts and gone away, and pale eel slime had dried in long grey strokes on the boards and on the sleeves of the women working them.

Below the shade, at the tubs, a woman had wrapped a child’s foot in three turns of wool. The foot stayed dark. It was the cold to the woman who had wrapped it and weather to the yard, and the two facts went past each other as they had gone past each other every morning of his life and never met. A neighbour come down with a pail said the water would go when it would go, that it was weather and not a thing a person could carry into a house, and the woman with the wool did not answer her, because there was no answer that would have put the two in the same sentence. The boy read the foot the way he had once read such a thing in a room with white light and no smoke in it: the extremity going before the core, the face putting on its almost-smile that was not peace, the afterdrop waiting on the far side of a warming nobody would perform. He knew the whole order of it. The knowing worked perfectly and bought nothing. There was no figure in any slate that would have named what had fastened there, no cause to write across the water and the wool, no cure he could have carried two paces even if a hand had been put into his to carry it, and no word in the language of the house that would have let him tell the woman what he knew. He did not lay the dark foot beside the story he had heard from other mouths of a child under this roof who had cooled while everybody was kind. A story from a yard and a foot below a sill were two things in two places, and he had spent a hundred and thirty-seven days learning the cost of drawing the line. The foot stayed dark inside its three turns of wool, and the yard went on calling the water weather.

Mira Silt worked the eels over the board next down with pale slime dried in long grey strokes on her sleeves and river silt dried pale in the hollow of her neck, her head held a little to the right where the top of her left ear had been, her small smile kept on her face the way a door is kept on the latch. Her board lay beside Orra’s knot-string and did not touch it. A weir woman come down to the outer boards asked whether the run would be thin now the reed hands were out at the beds. Mira flicked a string of eel-bones against her thumb while she decided whether to lie, moved two from hand to hand, put one away, and answered the question and then her own. “Thin? Who’s to say thin? It comes when it comes. Ask the river.” She said something low about a boat that had come up on the flood and nothing about the hand that had not come out of the strips, and the smile did not move on its latch.

A buyer in a coat with the river still in its hem named a figure for two pair. Mira’s thumb went under her apron to the grey wool cord and stayed there longer than the figure required, while she decided what the figure was worth to the woman who had said it. “Two marks the pair and two short. You’ve said it twice and I’ve counted it twice. There’s nothing in the sentence that wants an answer.” The buyer took the eels and went. Shifting a creel showed the second cord twisted twice beneath the grey — the one never carried upriver to Nine Pots, never lifted at a door — and then the apron came down over it. Neither knot was counted by any thumb but her own, and the knot for the thin child upriver was not touched. The word that would have named the double turn stayed where it had stayed since the year of the flower, unspoken on these boards and unspoken everywhere else he had ever heard it. He read the shape without the name and did not supply the one thing that would have turned a shape into an account.

Her not-looking was a keeping set on a place of its own. She did not look into the basket and had not looked into it all morning, and that not-looking was the opposite of the pricing face a buyer turns across a market and the opposite of the thane’s not-looking up at the Cross, and he had no word for any of the three. The three stood on separate trays and did not touch. Below the north bridge at the out-of-line landing the soft-cuffed clerk was still set for a measure nobody called, his cuff grey from washing and grey again by midday, his slate held to his forearm with the writing turned where nobody else could read it, his hand going to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls. The market sold its reeds and its eels over the top of him as water goes over a stone that lies where a stone lies; nobody stepped around him and nobody asked what he was set there for, and nobody had taken him off since the month turned, or if somebody had, somebody had put him back in the dark. The boy kept the clerk’s order of reaching on its own place beside the midwife’s, the thane’s, the wheel-keeper’s and the woman’s at the shelf, and did not make a doctrine of them, because a doctrine is an account that has found a payer and no payer had been named to any of these hands.

The arithmetic of the market and the arithmetic of a cold foot ran on separate trays and had never been laid across one another. A figure read aloud was not the figure paid; a body read exactly was still a body nobody would warm. The boy set the two facts where he set all the others, edge to edge and not touching, the way a man lays instruments on a towel and knows each by the wear on its handle without ever mistaking one handle for another. The four greens stayed four and the five shapes stayed five, and no fifth green had come to a mouth this day to make the count come out even; the word forty lay unspoken with the readable double turn beside it, its name withheld and its knot uncounted; the loan that could hear was still uncalled and the seventh year had not given.

None of it could be spent. He was the only adult under any roof in the district and the least use in any of them: a man who had once laid out instruments on a towel and known every one by the wear of its handle, now a child who could not close two inches of his hand on the rim of the basket that held him. The carrying arm went on keeping its exact account of what it held and saying nothing of what it cost. Berr’s finger had gone over a figure and left it where it was; the stall-keeper’s palm had taken two marks short without a count; Mira’s thumb had gone under her apron to a cord no Court thumb would spell; Orra’s last knot kept its double turn with its name unspoken; the reed-cutter’s cord had gone up the boards inside a coat drawn close over it; the clerk’s hand had gone to his slate before his eyes went to the stalls; and below the sill a child’s foot stayed dark inside three turns of wool while the yard called the water weather. Each was exact, each was complete, and not one of them had a payer who could be named. He did not join them. Five likenesses in a row will stand up and call themselves a doctrine if no hand holds them apart, and a doctrine is an account that has found a payer; he had kept his finger off all of them for a hundred and thirty-seven days and would keep it off now, the way a man keeps his finger off a wound he has decided not to open.

The light went along the rim of a bowl on the pot board and came off it, and the market went on selling over the clerk and over the missing step without making a fall of it. River wind came up to meet the woodsmoke. The note of water went over water and under the boards where no slate lay, a fifth low thing never counted among the four, and the foot below the sill stayed dark in its three turns of wool, the afterdrop waiting on the far side of a warming nobody would perform. One hundred and thirty-seven days old, four months and seventeen days, exact and unspent and told by nobody. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water and came down against the fold without reaching it, and the count went on going.

The going of the light had come down off the square and into the clay road before she lifted the basket. Sella Vail carried the boy at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, woodsmoke going before her and the river wind coming up to meet it, and gave the instruction to the wool and to the wind and never to the basket. Keep it off his ears. It had come all the way down with them and it would go all the way back up. In Low Kiln the doors were shutting and the ash-coloured light lay along the ruts with the mud dried pale in the hollows of it; a pan set out to dry on a flat stone caught the last of it and held it, and a trough by the well’s edge had a skin of ice that had formed since morning and would be gone by morning and was called weather by every woman who stepped around it. She went around the pan and the trough as she went around everything a person might knock over, and the half-beat in her walk was the same half-beat she had kept in the square when the market sold its reeds over the clerk and over the missing step. Nothing in the going of the light had changed the pace.

The carrying arm kept its account in muscle, registering every lift of the day from the Weir up to the shade and back again, what had been held and how long and how the weight had shifted at the crossing; it was exact and complete and told nothing of what any of it had cost. He had learned that in the last third of the reed month, and the knowledge was worth nothing he could hand to anybody. Under the wool his small hand outside the fold went up an inch toward the note of water that came up from the river below and came down again, not toward the bundle or the pot or any of the places gone around, but toward the direction of the water, which was the only direction he had. The count went on going without anybody’s thumb upon it.

At the lowest rack she went around the space beneath it as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. The broken birth-slates lay where they had lain through the planting and through the reed cutting, gone around and entered by nobody, and the space kept them the way a closed hand keeps what it will not show. A woman taking in a strip of reed lifted her elbow to let the basket pass and did not look at the place under the board. The boy was carried past at the half-pace and saw only the dark under the lowest rail and the pale frost-bitten ends of the reeds beyond. He had learned in a hundred and thirty-seven days that a place gone around is a place kept, and that a thing kept in the ground under a rack is not a thing entered on a slate; the two are different orders of keeping and he had no standing to lay them together. He did not lay them together.

Under the roof of the Vail kiln-house she set him on his bed and not in the Debt Loft, lifting him out of the basket without looking into it and setting him down as one sets a pot down where it will not be slopped. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without once looking at his face, and the thread of drool at his mouth froze where it lay, because nobody under this roof or any roof in the district had been given an instruction about the thread and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. Her hand rested a half-beat longer than the setting needed before it went away, and that half-beat was the whole of what she gave him that could not have been given by somebody who said what it was. She did not say it and he did not ask, not having the words and not having the breath. The one brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold two inches from his ear with the empty place beside it unnamed and no third, and it had gone from warm to cold to past the smelling of anything and nobody had lifted it or called it by a name. The shelf kept its two gaps in the other room and the two rooms did not touch.

She told the fire it was the thaw, and turned the coals with the back of a broken pot so that one small place among them came through with the heat and the rest lay under blind Ash. “The house will keep it,” she said, where another woman would have said I, and stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand counting something under her breath toward no opening, her lips moving the way they had moved along the loaf row at midday when the two gaps stood side by side and nobody gave them a figure. She pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn, the grey streak at her temple showing pale off the coals, and did not look toward the bed. She set the pot where it could be found in the dark, rim out and handle toward the reaching hand, and the handle stood two inches beyond the hand that would have reached for it and would not have closed on it. The widow’s mark hung where it hung on its cord against her throat and she did not touch it, because a dusk is not an opening and a shelf, a strip, a rack, a basket and a bed are not openings. She gave no explanation of the count she kept and went to her own bed.

In the small hours he turned out his file the way a man lays instruments on a towel before he begins, each thing in its place and nothing laid across anything else. He had kept six hearings of the full note: five from four sources — a cup across a birth room, a pot under the wheel-shed wall, the district’s slate twice at the Cross, a man giving at the Drawing — and the sixth, the small flat knock out of a woman as the men lifted her, filed beside the five and never among them. No seventh had come in a hundred and thirty-seven days and he had not invented one to fill the place where a seventh would stand if there were sevenths to stand. The told bell stood beside the heard bell, four, four and a gap in each, and the two neither agreed nor disagreed, being two objects in two places with nobody crossing himself at either. Three near-full answers stood on their own tray, advanced and not paid: the cup, the pot, a rack pot lifted a hand’s breadth too high and set down again, each answered so nearly that the want under the note could be felt, and none of them filled.

The interval tray held its older intervals, Stone 13 and Stone 17 and Stone 25, with the half-beat behind a standing finger at the damper, the stroke that went on over the flagstones without the check, and the breath a man drew at the top of a reach and did not spend. And now a fifth stopping lay among them beside the first four, an object among objects: the sentence about an ear under this roof being worth more than the debt that holds it, brought all the way this day to the word that would call the loan and stopped there with no figure and no name, the breath drawn at the top of the reach and not spent. Five likenesses lay on that tray and he held them apart, for a man who lets five likenesses stand up together will find they have stood up and called themselves a doctrine by morning, and a doctrine is an account that has found a payer. No payer had been named to any of these five. The fifth stopping was an interval and not a payment, an object and not a rule, a thing that had been stopped and not a thing that had been settled. The loan that could hear was still uncalled and the seventh year had not given. The damper’s double bite was filed as an object joined to no kiln, the bite that holds a vessel whole and the bite that can take the hand off a man, with no word in Grathic for both mercies and no standing to improve either. The two tellings of the shut counting room stood side by side with no third, breath through the nose and breath in the throat, both saying not the war’s hand, and the room off the shed kept its finger’s width of open and gave up nothing.

The charcoal marks under the board lay on a place of their own, short clusters of strokes pressed hard and faded nearly into the grain, and the finger that had gone beneath the front edge at midday had come away without a word and told him nothing of what the marks said; he did not lay the underside beside the two gaps above it or beside the crust in the fold, and left the reason of the hand where it stood, between knowing the board by heart and being told not to look. The cold foot below the sill stayed the cold to the woman who had wrapped it and weather to the yard, uncured, uncaused, unnamed, with the afterdrop waiting on the far side of a warming nobody would perform. Below the north bridge at the out-of-line landing the soft-cuffed clerk stayed set for a measure nobody called, his hand going to his slate before his eyes went to the stalls; nobody had taken him off since the month turned, or somebody had put him back in the dark. Every object was in its place and nothing was laid across anything else. It was the whole of his inventory and it was worth nothing. He was the only adult under this roof and the least use in it: a man who had once laid out instruments on a towel and known every one by the wear on its handle, now a child who could not close two inches of his hand on the rim of the basket two paces off, with a tray of records he could not lift to any table and no table that would have taken them. He had spent a hundred and thirty-seven days learning that a likeness is not a cause and that five stoppings do not make a payment, and the keeping of the things apart was the only honest work in the room and the one no slate would ever enter. Exact, complete, worth nothing.

Through the finger’s-width door came the river’s note of water over water, beyond the reed beds and the Weir on the Ilm, a fifth low thing never counted among the full note; through the floor under blind ash the carried fire took its coal and gave back four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for a hundred and thirty-seven days; overhead the boards kept four nails, four, and a fifth that was a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts in three places, and none of them became one. A man who lays three counts together and calls them one account has made a cause out of a room, and there was no room under this roof for another cause.

Cold arrived in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, the wool riding back from the small bare joint so the cold found the bare place and went under the fold where nobody had been given an instruction to put a hand. One hundred and thirty-seven days old, four months and seventeen days, he was told by nobody, and if somebody had said the number aloud nobody under this roof would have known what it counted, and he would not have known either what it counted, only that it was his and exact and unspent. The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down against the fold. It went on being exact in the dark while the house kept its three counts in three places. In the next room the row of loaves kept its two gaps and under the shelf the charcoal waited where it had been put, pressed hard and faded nearly into the grain, for a hand not his and a day that had not come.
