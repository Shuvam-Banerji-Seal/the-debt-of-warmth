The Fire Takes in Fours

Days 363–365 · Stone 4–6, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, Sill

The river goes under a skin it did not lay on and will not lift off. The kiln takes in fours and keeps its fifth where no slate reaches. The hand goes to the clay at the throat before it goes to the fire, and nobody calls it a payment.

— A firing-yard saying of Low Kiln, at the first firing of Stone

— Day 363 —

Stone 4, Fourthday, Y.H. 813

The hand outside the wool lay back against the fold on answer three hundred as a man lays down a tool where the next hand will find it without looking, and the room went on around it the way a room goes on around a thing put away for the night. The boy lay on the bed at the low angle of the loaf shelf under the Vail kiln-house roof, in Low Kiln, Sill, Grath, in the small hour before light, and the dark had a weight and a smell: wet wool, tallow smoke, clay dust gone cold, and under them the faint sour mineral tang of the ridge along the skirting board that had not been swept in two years and would not be swept on a morning that was no opening. The file lay open on the towel at the head of the bed with every gap left gap, the last line ending where three hundred honest answers ended and no fourth hundred standing up to be counted. Through the floor the carried fire kept four low pulls with the silence where the fifth should have stood, its payer unnamed three hundred and sixty-three days; through the finger’s-width door the river kept its note of water over water, a low thing over a lower thing, muffled now under its winter skin, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming. Above him the boards kept four nails on the near one, four on the next and a thumb-width knot where a fifth might have been driven and was not. Three counts in three places, and none rose into another, for agreeing any two would want a payer put under both and none stood; the bell’s gap stayed in its square and was not folded into them.

Three hundred and sixty-two had stood exact behind the day; three hundred and sixty-three stood now, one more in a row nobody counted aloud, not round, not a threshold, only a day. Six months and one hundred and eighty-two days had become one hundred and eighty-three, and nobody under the roof said so. He had long ago stopped expecting to be told what the number counted, which was not the same as not wanting it. The fire’s fifth was unpaid the same number with its payer unnamed; Garl Vail’s name had not been spoken under this roof the same number of days, and the salt-stiff coat kept his broader shape on its peg behind the door without anybody’s giving it a word. Stone had come on in the dark beyond the door four mornings back and had not been made to mean anything since; this was its fourth day, and it kept its place the way a pot keeps its place in a cupboard, carrying two handles and none the worse for them.

The surgeon took the body in its old order, as a field is taken before work, by the small signs first because the small signs do not lie and the large ones are mostly weather. One stiff leathery brown-edged barley crust lay two inches from the ear, gone harder and browner through the long cold, its corner standing a hair off the wool, its empty place unnamed and no third coming in the night to make the row come out even. The thread of drool at the mouth had frozen in the dry kiln heat and was unwiped, because nobody under any roof has ever been given an instruction about the thread and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. Cold had stopped at cheek, temple and the small bare joint of the wrist where the wool rode back, as at the end of a line written out for it with neither an order to go farther nor an order to go away. The Afterdrop waited beyond all three, past a warming nobody in this house would perform.

He knew the composed face that was not composure. He knew a cold patient cannot clot and will seem better before he is worse, and must be warmed from the outside and slowly, never hurried, never rubbed, never set too near the fire; he knew the order in which a body takes heat back, the small joint first and the core last, and the order in which it gives it up, the reverse, which comes sooner than anybody likes, and that pink returning to a face is the surface still cold and not the core. He knew it exactly and it was worth nothing at two inches of air. Underneath the knowing the infant wanted the crust lifted the two inches to his mouth, the thread wiped and a hand that would close on the pot handle set where the fingers could reach it. Both were true, the second was louder, neither won, and the quarrel between them was the work. The body humiliated him in the exactness and not in any grand way: drool frozen, a head that could not be held up, a crust gone leathery past the point where any child would eat it, the pot handle two inches beyond the reaching hand, a hand that could not lift itself to its own chin. He had once reduced a thousand shoulders in the dark and he could not keep a fold of wool on his ear. The joke lived in the exactness, cost him nothing and was not funny to anybody else.

Sella Vail came to the hearth before she came to the basket, fire before basket, as she had come at every opening of a day since she took the house. “It’s the thaw,” she told the coals, and turned them with the back of a broken pot, the pot’s back going in under the blind ash and lifting the red edge where the heat had been kept. “The house will keep it,” she said, where another woman would have said I, and stood a moment with her hands still, counting a small intake, a hold and a letting-go toward no opening, giving neither gap a figure. Her finger lay on top of the loaf shelf so the charcoal tally underneath stayed unread; her lips moved along a two-gapped row and figured neither gap. Her eye went once to the clay jar standing cold under lead glaze, and her hand shut half over nothing, the little-door burn on the back of her left hand closing on air; the cord of the widow’s mark at her throat was oiled where a working thumb had touched it ten thousand times, and the mark hung where it hung and was not touched, because a morning and a hearth are not openings, and six openings in six months are six and not a rule, the sixth held at the kiln mouth on the second of Ash without a name and no seventh made due. The hand goes to the mark before the kiln and at no other hour, and the first firing of Stone stood one morning ahead as a date named to nobody.

At the pot board she lifted the twice-mended pot with her thumb first where the wire crossed the crack, held the weight a half-beat before she let it come on the handle, and left the hairline showing between old-brass staple and tighter wire. “Leave the crack showing. People trust a thing they can see has been handled,” she told the pot. “If it’s worth mending it’s worth mending twice. The second time is the real one,” she told the fire, and both sayings went to the pot and the fire and never to a person, and neither was improved in the keeping and neither was wasted. Repair stayed apart from restoration, for a restored thing hides the hand that handled it and a mended thing leaves that hand showing; a pot with its crack showing is a pot a man can trust with his warmth, and a pot made whole again is a pot that has had the hand that mended it taken out of the story. She turned the dressed grain under wool with a touch lighter than a woman would use on a sleeping child, her fingers going in and coming out without closing, no figure lifted off it and no handful. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without a look at his face. “Keep it off his ears,” she told the wool. “It’s the river wind.” Then she set the pot rim two inches beyond the reaching hand with a half-beat at the setting and no look toward the bed. The half-beat was the distance between intention and arrest, bought at nothing, not a gift, for a gift wants a giver and nobody gave; the instruction went to the wool and the river wind and the fire she would bank against their coming back, and never to the basket. The boy, who was part of the load and not the speaker of it, kept the order of the morning exactly.

The edges came near to touching and the finger went between; a finding filled to make a row come out even is a finding forged. The Ash 5 joining, the Ash 17 direction and the Ash 23 direction stood each on a place of its own with a finger between every pair, still gathering and unspent, and no ownerless voice came over the boards to make a sixth telling of the burned slate, a third telling of the shut room or a sixth asking of the row. No sixth hearing, seventh opening, sixth held object, sixth telling, third shut-room telling, sixth asking, fifth shaping, tenth hand or tenth stopping was supplied to make a row come out even.

Three counts in three places did not become one: the carried fire’s four low pulls with its fifth unpaid and its payer unnamed under the floor, the boards’ four nails and four and a thumb-width knot overhead, and the river’s water now under its skin through the finger’s-width door, a low thing over a lower thing, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, never numbered among the hearings. Agreeing any two would want a payer put under both and none stood, so the three stayed where they were and the bell’s gap stayed in its square. Attention was the one thing no institution in Grath had thought to tax, and it was the whole of what he had spent, and it cost nobody anything and bought nothing that could be entered on a slate. The surgeon took his small clean satisfaction in a field taken as found and left as found, and it cost him nothing, which was how he knew it was not the same as peace. Underneath it the infant went on wanting the crust lifted, the thread wiped and a hand on the pot handle, and the want kept the satisfaction from settling.

The first firing of Stone stood one morning ahead as a date named to nobody, and the boy’s first birthday on Stone 7 stood three mornings beyond that and the Hearth Drawing on Stone 12 further still; none was brought into a day that had not earned it and none was named to him under any roof. Neither of the district’s two habits of naming its months was set right or left in so many words — the older women by the weather, the slatemen by the number of Account-days — and the month carried both names the way a pot carries two handles. The deep-winter month in which he was born held, and nothing in this morning’s quiet crossed under the roof as sign, lesson, mending, cause, payment or payer. The promise under the wheel-shed was neither given nor withdrawn, and he’s not Vail stayed sealed and was not a hand’s business on this morning. The far-off hand that would one day lay the district’s private keepings together was still far off and was not his.

In the small hours the hand outside the wool came up an inch toward the river’s note under the finger’s-width door, the way a hand goes up in a field it has learned not to disturb. It did not reach, and the inch was not a reach; most of what a hand does in a field is wait, and he had once taken a hand into a cavity slowly, with the whole of the attention, and knew the difference between the moving and the waiting. It took a small intake from the room whose floor does not touch his, held the interval whole on the note’s own finishing under the ice, let the intake go, and came straight down and laid itself back against the fold as a man puts away a tool where the next hand will find it without looking. The note had done before the hand came down, so the letting-go was neither early nor stayed and the hand did not stay; the note’s own finishing ended the interval and not any decision of the hand. The intake was not a breath kept for warmth, the interval not a stopping of the river, the note not his.

He filed it as answer three hundred and one: exact, complete, worth nothing at two inches of air, a restraint and not a payment borrowing no house count and supplying no payer, laid across none of the nine arithmetics, the bell’s gap, the covered grain, the handful, the thumb’s resting, the held breath at the sill, the staying at the inch, the early letting-go, the sixth opening, the pot-crack resting, the river’s winter skin, the ninth hand, the Ash 5 joining, the Ash 17 direction, the Ash 23 direction, the fourth shaping, the strip in the sleeve nor his own kept gaps. Three hundred and one honest answers stood on the sixth place, each an object and not a power, and not one stood up and called itself a doctrine. The hand lay back against the fold on answer three hundred and one; the file lay open on the towel at the head of the bed with every gap left gap; the pot rim stood two inches beyond the reaching hand with the hairline of the first break showing between old-brass staple and tighter wire; one stiff leathery brown-edged barley crust lay two inches from his ear with its empty place unnamed and no third, the thread frozen and unwiped; and through the finger’s-width door the river went low under its winter skin, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming. Stone kept its fourth day without a boundary drawn and the deep-winter month held.

The morning went its length as a banked coal goes, giving its heat by degrees that nobody marked, and gave way to the afternoon without a boundary drawn. In the afternoon Sella Vail lifted the boy down from under the Vail kiln-house roof in Low Kiln, Sill, Grath, and took the basket level in both hands at the half-pace she keeps from every open slate, the pace of a woman carrying a pot filled that must not be slopped. The instruction went to the wool, to the river wind, and to the fire she would bank against their coming back, never to the basket. “Keep it off his ears,” she told the wool. “It’s the river wind.” Under the fold his hand lay back against the crust as a tool lies where the next hand will find it, and the crust’s corner stood a hair off the wool with its empty place unnamed and no third. The kiln stood cold behind its wall and would stand cold through this day and open on the morrow; she had banked the coals under blind ash before she lifted him, with the back of a broken pot, and said nothing of the hour or of what the hour was for. The yard smelled of clay dust gone dry in the cold, woodsmoke, wet wool, and under them the sour mineral tang of the ridge along the skirting board that had not been swept in two years and would not be swept on an afternoon that was no opening.

He took the going-around as a field is taken before work, by the small signs first because the small signs do not lie and the large ones are mostly weather. The woman carrying him was careful with the house, and he was part of the load, and that was the whole of his place in it: a thing carried level, with the whole of her attention on the level and none of it on the person under the wool. He wanted more than to be part of the load and could not ask for it, and the wanting was quieter than the crust at his ear and did not win. The surgeon took his small clean satisfaction in a field taken as found and left as found, and it cost him nothing, which was how he knew it was not the same as peace. At the pot board she lifted the twice-mended pot with her thumb first where the wire crossed the crack, held the weight a half-beat before she let it come on the handle, and left the hairline showing between old-brass staple and tighter wire. “Leave the crack showing. People trust a thing they can see has been handled,” she told the pot. “If it’s worth mending it’s worth mending twice. The second time is the real one,” she told the fire. Both sayings went to the pot and the fire and never to a person, improved in the keeping and very likely not as good as the man had said them, and his name went unspoken while they went on being said; repair stayed apart from restoration, for a restored thing hides the hand that handled it and a mended thing leaves that hand showing. She turned the dressed grain under wool with that lighter touch and no figure and no handful, then set the pot rim two inches beyond the reaching hand with a half-beat at the setting and no look toward the bed. The half-beat was the distance between intention and arrest, bought at nothing and not a gift, for a gift wants a giver standing and none stood; he had once known a surgeon who could not resist closing that distance in a patient and had learned, by closing it, what it cost. The rim stayed where it was set. The hand could not close two inches on it and did not try.

The body took itself in the surgeon’s old order, and the old order did not flatter it. One stiff leathery brown-edged barley crust lay two inches from his ear, gone harder and browner through the long cold, its corner standing a hair off the wool, its empty place beside it unnamed and no third come to make the row come out even. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the dry kiln heat and was unwiped, because nobody under any roof had ever been given an instruction about the thread and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. Cold had stopped at cheek, temple and the small bare joint of the wrist where the wool rode back, as though it had come to the end of a line written out for it with neither an order to go farther nor an order to go away. The Afterdrop waited past all three, past a warming nobody in this house would perform. He knew the composed face that was not composure, knew a cold patient cannot clot and will seem better before he is worse, knew the order in which a body takes heat back, the small joint first and the core last, and the order in which it gives it up, the reverse, which comes sooner than anybody likes, and that pink returning to a face is the surface still cold and not the core. He had once taken a core temperature with a glass under a tongue and read the number off it in a white room; now he could not lift a crust two inches to his own mouth. The infant wanted the crust lifted, the thread wiped and a hand that would close on the pot handle set where the fingers could reach it. Both were true, the second was louder, neither won, and the quarrel between them was the work.

In the small hours the surgeon took his small clean satisfaction in a field taken as found and left as found, and it cost him nothing, which is how he knew it was not the same as peace. Under the Vail kiln-house roof in Low Kiln, Sill, Grath, the room held the small sounds by which a field is read before the light and after it: the dry rasp of a coal settling under its ash at the hearth, the small tick of cooling clay somewhere along the loaf shelf, the river’s note low under its winter skin through the finger’s-width door, and over all the roof’s own silence, which is not the absence of sound but the sound of a house holding itself level. Three hundred and sixty-four had stood exact behind the day and three hundred and sixty-five stood now, one more in a row nobody counted aloud, round to the old register and a threshold nobody crossed; six months and one hundred and eighty-four days had become six months and one hundred and eighty-five, told by nobody under any roof. He kept the number because keeping was what he had, and he had long since stopped expecting anybody to say what a number counted, which was not the same thing as not wanting it. The house kept its days in loaves, in glances, in the space where a loaf had stood and in the shape a room took when a weight was lifted out of it, and no line was drawn for a foot to cross and no foot crossed one.

The carried fire kept four low pulls with the silence where the fifth should have stood, its payer unnamed three hundred and sixty-five days. The name of the man whose salt-stiff coat kept his broader shape on its peg behind the door had not been spoken under this roof the same number. Two counts stood exact and unspent and nobody joined them, for agreeing any two would want a payer put under both and none stood.

No sixth hearing, seventh opening, sixth held object, sixth telling, third shut-room telling, sixth asking, fifth shaping, tenth hand or tenth stopping was invented to make a row come out even, and the far-off hand that would one day lay the district’s private keepings together was still far off and was not his.

Underneath the exactness the infant went on wanting the crust lifted the two inches to his mouth, the thread wiped and a hand that would close on the pot handle; both were true, the second was louder, neither won, the two registers quarrelling without verdict in the ordinary small hours of the last day, and the argument was the work.

In the small hours the hand outside the wool came up an inch toward the river’s note under the finger’s-width door, the way a hand goes up in a field it has learned not to disturb. Most of what a hand does in a field is wait, and he had once taken a hand into a cavity slowly, with the whole of the attention, and knew the difference between the moving and the waiting. The hand did not reach, and the inch was not a reach. It took a small intake from the room whose floor does not touch his, held the interval whole on the note’s own finishing under the ice, let the intake go, and came straight down and laid itself back against the fold as a man puts away a tool where the next hand will find it without looking. The intake was not a breath kept for warmth, the interval not a stopping of the river, the note not his. Stone came on in the deep of winter, and on the fifth morning Sella Vail went to the hearth before the basket, told the coals it was the thaw and banked her fire against their coming back. She carried the boy down at the half-pace she keeps from every open slate and set him beside the kiln mouth, level and out of the draught, where the heat came from the flank and not full in the face. Then, her hands gone still, she put her left hand to the widow’s mark at her throat, held it one breath, let go, lifted the clay lid with the back of her hand and tipped the carried coal under a broken pot.
