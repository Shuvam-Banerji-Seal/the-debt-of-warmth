The Sentence Finished

Days 160–162 · Tallow 11–13, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln and the Weir, Sill

A hand held back from the iron is still a hand that knows where the iron stands. The kiln takes four pulls and leaves the fifth where nobody looks; the river counts water over water and enters nothing. A sentence allowed to finish is a sentence nobody can be made to give back.

— A saying of the Clay Yard, spoken at the second working of Tallow

— Day 160 —

Tallow 11, Y.H. 813 (Firstday)

Before first grey the room held the shape of the night pressed into it: the hollow of the bed, the crease of the fold, the small warm place where a head had lain and was lying still. One stiff brown-edged crust lay two inches from his ear in the fold, gone leathery through the dry weeks, and the empty place beside it had no name and kept no third company. the boy lay on his back under the Vail roof, and the thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the night and kept its line, because nobody under any roof had been given an instruction about the thread. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the low note of water going over water beyond the finger’s-width door and came down against the fold. He was one hundred and sixty days old and five months and ten days, and nobody under that roof said so, and nobody under that roof would have known what to do with the saying if a man had put the words into their mouths. The adult who had once known the weight of a body on a table and the exact order of its failing watched the room through a child’s eyes and could not lift his own head; the child who lived in those eyes was cold and wanted the breast and the crust, and did not know that either had ever been refused him. The house kept its days in loaves pushed along a board, in a glance at a clay jar, in a space at the end of a row that no woman called a number. His count had neither rim nor handle and would not have sat among them. It stood where it stood. It went on.

Beyond the wall the Clay Yard was coming up into the hot dry smell of clay giving up its water. That was the first smell of the day, hot clay under tallow, and the first sound was not a bell but the kiln breathing in fours with a gap, four low pulls and a silence where a fifth should have been, a breath the yard had long ago stopped listening for. In his bed he could not see the mouth of it, but he knew the shape of the heat behind the brick the way a man knows the shape of a thing from the sound it makes when it is shut away: kept, and counted, and not to be gone into. Above him the boards kept four nails, four, and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb. He had counted them in the dark and would count them again. Three counts in three places — the river, the fire, the boards — and none of them became one. A fourth likeness would make a cause, and a cause would want a payer. No finger of his drew the line.

❦

Sella Vail came in from the yard with the basket on her hip, the little-door burn closing on the wicker handle and opening again, and she did not look into the basket. She gave the instruction to the wool and the fire and the pot and the broom before she gave it to anybody, and never gave it to the basket, which was a vessel filled and carried and had no instruction of its own to answer. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. “It’s the river wind.” To Tarn in the doorway she said the house wanted the yard swept before the sun came on the flagstones — not I want it, not you sweep it — and Tarn said “Yes, house” as though the sentence had been finished for him before he was born. She kept the half-pace from every open slate on the way across the room, and there was no slate in the room, and she kept it all the same.

At her throat the widow’s mark hung where it had hung since the first firing of the month, a thumb-length of clay under its glaze with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and her hand did not go to it. A shelf was not an opening. A morning was not an opening. A well was not an opening. The hand went to the mark before the kiln and at no other hour, and there had been no opening under this roof since the month came up, and there was none this morning. Her eye went once to the clay jar that stood cold under its glaze against the wall, the way a hand goes to an instrument before a case is opened, and went away, and she did not touch the jar and did not go back to it.

At the hearth she told the fire it was the thaw, turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and said, “The house will keep it,” where another woman would have said I. She stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand, counting under her breath toward no opening, her lips moving on a row that stood in some other room of her head; then she pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn, the grey streak at her temple pale off the coals, tipped the carried coal under the back of the pot and set the pot rim out with its handle standing two inches beyond the reaching hand. The fire took the coal the way a mouth takes food given to it, without thanks and without question, and answered four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been. It had missed the fifth for a hundred and sixty days and no payer had been named to the silence. She did not look toward the bed. She did not touch the mark, because a hearth was not a door and a morning was not an opening.

❦

the Clay Yard had come up in the night into the hot dry smell of clay giving up its water, and the women went around what they would not enter as they had gone around it every morning since the kilns were brought up for the year’s last working season. There was the clean dark line in the Vail corner where the Ash ridge had turned, and beside it the new finer hairline of grey called the thaw, and neither had been swept. There was the space beneath the lowest rack where the broken birth-slates lay, and the yard went around it as wide as a woman goes around a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. And there was the empty place in the returning line of the worked strips where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out: a woman looked once at the space between two stooping backs, took a wide step and said nothing, and another took the step behind her without looking at all. No number came out of the looking.

“That’s where it was,” the first woman said low, to the ground rather than to the girl beside her, and set her foot down beyond the space. The girl went on with her hands in the slip. The name, the body, the cause, the figure and the payment had not come out of that place in all the days since, and the boy held the absence on a place of its own and did not lay it across the loaf shelf’s two gaps, the empty place at his ear, or the round bare ring on the wall where a pan had stood out to dry through Reed. A fourth likeness would make a cause and a cause would want a payer; no finger of his drew the line.

At the well the the trough stood bare and dark under its little roof, with the skin of ice that had covered it through the lean weeks gone these days and not revived, and the water in it was called weather to nobody in particular. One woman dipped a palm and tasted and said it was different, and another said Different from what, and a third said weather, and the argument ended not in a winner but in nobody left to carry it, the change standing as a shape with no name and no cause entered in no slate. Sella passed with the basket and gave the instruction to the running water and the wool and never to the basket, and kept the half-pace from every open slate, and went around the trough as wide as she went around the rack space, and gave no explanation of the turn to the basket. A basket set down was a pot set down, and a pot set down was left where it stood.

❦

Tarn took the broom only after it had been given its instruction, never before; he held it a moment with the small tool he had put into his pocket when he thought nobody looked, and put the tool back before anybody could check whether it had ever been away. To a woman who wanted the thaw early and a man who wanted it late he said, “Yes, house,” to both, agreeing so thoroughly that the argument had nowhere to put a foot, while wanting the late one in the private part of his head because a late thaw puts off the paying. His seventh year stood nearer its end than it had ever stood and was still held, six counted in the worn thumb-mark where the house could check and the seventh at the lip of the sill where it could not, the one entry in this house which the house had never checked. The small knife lay at the very edge of its sill and the unforged collector sentence lay beside it, and the breath from Seed 16 was still unspent, standing as a small interval at the top of each reach where it should have gone in and had not. His chest had been tight since the dark. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line and no scrubbing lifted it, and the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back.

His stroke went on over the flagstones past the shallow bright patch without even the half-check, the stones keeping the place where it had stopped, and went over the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector without once turning along it. No collector came. Nine stoppings stood where they had stood and no tenth stood up before the seventh year had been earned; a tenth stood up now would be a debt invented to make the nine into a bill. The sentence about an ear under this roof being worth more than the debt that holds it stayed where he kept it, unsaid. The loan that could hear was uncalled. He said nothing of any of it, and Sella, within hearing, gave the end of her instruction to the pot and asked nothing, and the two silences under the one roof stayed unjoined.

❦

Mid-morning came up under the roof and the boy was carried through the door again in the basket, and he read the firing as he read an operating theatre he could not enter. Heat was kept and counted behind a screen of brick, and there was a gate with two bites, the bite that holds a vessel whole and the bite that can take the hand off a man, with no word in Grathic that would hold both mercies in one breath. He had once stood with a towel in his hands in a room where heat was kept behind a screen, and had watched a man stand at the gate and not go in, and a cost was paid there by a man whose name was not spoken. The grammar of it was older than this house: the name taken out of the sentence, the hand out of the field, the cost out of the account. Here the cost went up in four low pulls and a silence and was named by nobody for a hundred and sixty days. He filed the iron as an object joined to no kiln, beside the two shut-room tellings and the told bell standing beside the heard bell, and did not let it stand up as a rule. A rule wants a payer, and there was no payer named to any of it.

Near the basket’s rim the air was warm from the hearth and cold from the door at the same time, and the note of water going over water came through the finger’s-width door and went under the warmth and stayed. The adult who had once laid a chart closed over its finding and gone away leaving the neat margin showing watched the house keep its accounts in loaves and glances and unnamed spaces, and knew that nothing under this roof had changed by the figure that had gone up the clay road. The child’s body was carried under a board and was cold, and wanted the crust two inches from his ear and the pot handle standing beyond the reaching hand and the number spoken by somebody who would have known what a number was, and got none of them and could not ask for any. Cold arrived in its known order, cheek, temple, the small bare joint at the wrist where the wool rode back, and stopped there as if it had reached the end of its instruction. Want came up small and importunate and was not answered. Both were true and the second was louder.

His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water through the finger’s-width door and came down against the fold with one hundred and sixty exact, complete and unspent in the dark. The fire missed its fifth under the floor and its payer was unnamed for a hundred and sixty days, and the charcoal waited under the shelf for a hand that was not his and a day that has not come.

❦

The afternoon came into the kiln-house sideways, through the finger’s-width door and the slack cracks of the oiled paper, and lay on the boards in thin bright strips that stirred when the door stirred and went nowhere. Sella Vail carried the boy in with the basket on her hip and set him low near the bench leg, at the angle where the underside of the loaf shelf becomes a roof, and did not look into the basket. She gave the instruction to the wool and the pot and the broom before she gave it to anybody, and the basket got none of it. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. “It’s the river wind.” The window was shut and there was no wind in the room, but the saying had been said to the wool every day of his life and the wool had never once answered it.

From the basket’s low angle the underside of the board was a ceiling with its own weather: a dry smell of old bread and clay dust, a shadow the length of a hand, the faint sweet edge of the thaw coming in from the yard where the new grey lay beside the clean dark line. Above him the house kept its arithmetic the way it kept everything, badly and without variation. Loaves went along the shelf and were pushed into a row. Her eye went once to the clay jar that stood cold under its glaze against the wall, the way a hand goes to an instrument before a case is opened, and went away. There was a space at the end of the row that nobody called a number; a woman’s hand would pass over it, find the hard heel of the next loaf, and go on with her work. A shelf was not an event. An absence on a shelf was ordinary work. His own count had neither rim nor handle and would not have sat among them. It stood where it stood. One hundred and sixty, exact, complete and unspent, and nobody under this roof had told him what a number was, and nobody under this roof would have known what to do with the telling if a man had put the words into their mouths.

❦

A neighbour came up from the strips with a wet pot held in both hands, water dark at her cuffs and a little of it running down her wrist to her elbow, and stopped in the doorway before she had decided what she was asking. The pot was a common pot with a chip at the lip, and she held it level, the way a woman holds a thing that has slopped once and will not slop again. “What does a firing cost,” she said, “set down there?”

She said it to the board, or to the room, or to the back of Sella’s head; it was not a sentence addressed to a person, and so it did not require a person’s answer. Sella did not turn toward the basket. Her little-door burn closed on nothing as her hand came away from the pot she had been wiping, and opened again when the hand opened.

“Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there,” she said. “The house has said it.”

The neighbour stood a moment with the question still in her mouth, having had it taken out of her hands and handed back in a form that would not carry. Then she carried it away level, pot and all, so that nothing slopped, and went back down the yard with wet cuffs and an unanswered sentence that had changed shape without her noticing. Sella went on wiping. The burn shut when the cloth went down and opened when it came up, two straight sides and a lintel and a threshold doing their ordinary work. An answer that gives no figure is still an answer, and a house that says it once has said it for the year.

❦

She went to the shelf with her back to the basket and stood at it a while, moving her lips along the row. The boy could see the back of her skirt and the broad fall of her shoulders and the coal-black hair held at the temple by the grey streak, and could not see her face. From his angle the front edge of the board hung over him like the lip of a low roof, and under that lip, where nobody thought to look because nobody stood where he stood, the charcoal lay in short clusters against the grain: some pressed hard enough to have left a shine, some faded nearly into the wood, a keeping put where a keeping is least expected and therefore most likely to last. He had seen the marks before and had not read them. He did not read them now.

Her hand went beneath the front edge.

It went under without a look and without a word, the fingers going exactly where they were going, and came away with nothing in them. She did not go back to the shelf after. She did not glance down at the basket to see whether the movement had been seen, and gave no explanation of it to the wool, the pot, the fire or anybody. A hand that goes under without looking either knows the board by heart or has been told not to look, and the reason stood between the two, with room for both and no way in. He kept the movement on a place of its own, the way he kept a map folded before he knew the country: the hand, the edge, the coming-away, nothing read, nothing copied, nothing joined to the two gaps above it or to the one crust in the other room. A keeping put where nobody expects to be read tells more by the care of its hiding than the writing would. He made no cause of the habit. There was no cause to make that would not want a payer.

The shelf kept its two unnamed gaps, the old space at the row’s end and the palm’s breadth that had come in on the night of Seed 6 with no scrape, crumb, print or word. No third came into it that afternoon. There was a saying of the loaf-shelves of Low Kiln that two gaps stay two gaps until a finger lays them together, and that an account then wants a name, and no finger in Low Kiln had laid these together. In the other room one stiff brown-edged crust lay two inches from his ear in the fold, gone leathery in the dry weeks, with its empty place beside it still unnamed and no third beside it either. The round bare ring on the wall where a shallow clay pan had stood out to dry through Reed kept the shape of its absence on a tray of its own. It was not laid across the shelf’s two gaps or the crust’s empty place or the empty place in the returning line of the worked strips where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out. It was not laid across the sixth green as a colour joined to no ear, or across the foundling as a shape with no sale. A fourth likeness would make a cause, and a cause would want a payer, and no finger of his drew the line. He had learned early in this house, and in a better-lit room that he carried behind his eyes without ever naming, that three likenesses in a row will stand up and call themselves a doctrine if no hand holds them apart. He held them apart.

The light went out of the afternoon and came back lower, through the door in one bright strip that ended at the bench leg and did not reach him. Sella lifted him out of the basket with the little-door burn closing on the wicker and opening again, and carried him into the other room. She set him on the bed under the roof and not in the Debt Loft, base first and weight settled, and put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without once looking at his face. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the dry heat and kept its line. She left it where it was. Nobody under any roof had been given an instruction about the thread, and a basket set down was a pot set down, and a pot set down was left where it stood.

Her hand rested a half-beat longer than the setting needed before it went away. It was the only thing in the room that admitted anything, and it admitted nothing that could be entered: no figure, no name, no payer, no opening. If it was love, it had been given no instruction and kept no slate, and he had no word in Grathic that would hold it without turning it into a debt. He did not try to make one. He lay with the crust two inches from his ear and let the half-beat go where half-beats go.

At the hearth she told the fire it was the thaw, and turned the coals with the back of a broken pot. “The house will keep it,” she said, where another woman would have said I. She stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand, counting under her breath toward no opening, her lips moving on a row that stood in some other room of her head; then she pushed the coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn, the grey streak at her temple pale off the coals, tipped the carried coal under the back of the pot and set the pot rim out with its handle standing two inches beyond the reaching hand. She did not look toward the bed. The widow’s mark hung where it had hung since the first firing of the month, a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and her hand did not go to it. A dusk was not an opening. A hearth was not a door. The hand went to the mark before the kiln and at no other hour, and there had been no opening under this roof since the month came up, and there was none at the going of this light.

The coals took what was given without thanks and without question, and answered in their own grammar: four low pulls, and the silence where the fifth should have been. The fire had missed the fifth for a hundred and sixty days and no payer had been named to the silence. The two rooms did not touch. Through the finger’s-width door came the river’s note of water going over water; through the floor came the fire’s four low pulls and its gap. He lay within reach of both and could enter neither.

In the small hours he turned out his file the way a man lays instruments on a towel before a case begins, each thing going into its own hollow and nothing resting across anything else. Six hearings of the full note lay beside and never among — five from four sources, and the sixth, the small flat knock out of a woman as the men lifted, from a person — with no seventh and none invented to fill the empty place. Three near-full answers stood on their own tray, advanced and not paid: a pot under the wheel-shed wall, a rack pot lifted a hand’s breadth too high and set down again, and Jorra Vail’s withheld knowing of a Vail pot’s full note, among the answers and not among the hearings. The older intervals lay at Stone 13, Stone 17 and Stone 25 with the half-beat behind a standing finger, the unchecking stroke, his own held breath from a market morning. On the widened tray lay nine stoppings — Reed 5, 8, 11, 14, 17, 20, 23, 26 and 29 — objects and not a rule, intervals and not payments, no payer named to any, held apart by hand because a doctrine is an account that has found a payer. A tenth had not stood up out of the nine. The seventh year was earned by no man under this roof and had not given, and the loan that could hear was uncalled. The damper lay beside them as one piece of iron with two bites joined to no kiln, the holding and the taking with no Grathic word for both mercies; the two shut-room tellings stood side by side with no third; the told bell stood beside the heard bell.

Three counts in three places did not become one: the river’s note of water over water through the finger’s-width door, the fire’s four pulls and its gap through the floor, the boards’ four nails, four, and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of the thumb overhead. He had counted them in the dark and would count them again. A fourth likeness would make a cause, and a cause would want a payer. He kept the three where they were, and the pan’s gone place where it was, and the charcoal where it was, and did not lay a finger on any of them.

Cold arrived in its known order, cheek, temple, the small bare joint at the wrist where the wool rode back, and stopped there as if it had reached the end of its instruction. Want came up small and importunate for the crust two inches from his ear, and for the number spoken by somebody who would have known what a number was, and got neither. Both were true and the first was louder in the body, and the second was the one he kept. One hundred and sixty stood exact, complete and unspent. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water through the finger’s-width door and came down against the fold. The fire missed its fifth under the floor with its payer unnamed for a hundred and sixty days, and the charcoal waited under the shelf for a hand that was not his and a day that has not come.

— Day 161 —

Tallow 12, Y.H. 813 (Secondday)

Before first grey the Clay Yard lay under a sky the colour of wet ash, and the pots on the racks stood with their bellies full of the dark, each one holding its small private cold until somebody put a hand to it. The boy came out from under the Vail roof in the crook of Sella Vail’s arm, basket on hip, with the wool tucked at his ears and the instruction already given to the wool and not to him: Keep it off his ears. It’s the river wind. The door stood a finger’s width open, as it had stood a finger’s width open for three years, and the low note of water going over water came through that finger’s width, went to the wall, and stayed there, a fifth low thing never counted among the hearings.

Three men came up the yard before the light, and a fourth behind them. They came with their collars high against the sweetish edge that was on the wind — High Kiln breathing over the roofs, lead glaze gone metallic on a wrong wind — and went low-voiced toward the finger’s-width-open door of the back room off the wheel-shed, and did not look at the basket. Sella carried past them at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, turned no head toward the door, gave Low Kiln no name for the men, and did not say that the room was cold. Her little-door burn closed on the wicker and opened again. A basket set down was a pot set down, and a pot set down was left where it stood, and nothing that had been said to the wool needed saying to a basket. She said the house where another woman would have said I, and the sentence about the men went nowhere at all.

The boy kept the two tellings on a tray of their own. A man come downriver from Greybank had said it twice, on Ilmen 26 and again on Silt 25, of a counting room shut in the last war with the damper closed and the men inside dead of the breath, eighteen leagues east; each time he said it as a curiosity with a date, and each time the yard took it for history and went back to its pots. Two tellings stood side by side with no third. The back room off the wheel-shed was a door that kept its finger’s width of open and men who went low into it, and no telling at all had been made of that; he laid no finger of his between the room and the story. A likeness laid down by hand was a likeness held. A likeness left to lie was a cause waiting for a payer.

❦

Jorra Vail came through from the shed with the day’s dust on him. It was fine grey slate dust ground small as meal, worked into both forearms past the bent little finger and under the nails, and it was not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel: a thing ground small as meal had got that way by being broken, while a thing mixed was put together on purpose, and the boy kept the two states of one pair of forearms on one tray without asking which slate had been broken or whether it had been a birth-slate. The dust went into the creases and stayed there, the colour of a swept floor seen in a bad light, and the bent finger kept its bend whatever was on it, pointing the way a hinge points whether the gate is open or shut. The broken birth-slates lay under the lowest rack, where the yard went around them as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it and entered them into nothing. He wanted the two questions the way a man wants an instrument that is not on the towel, and kept them where questions can last for years.

He spoke to the wheel before he spoke to anyone. “It doesn’t care whose hands,” he said. “It cares if they’re steady.” He said it to the wheel the way another man says a thing to an animal he means to work, with no welcome in it and no unkindness either, and the wheel gave back nothing. He set a pot on the bench and turned it a quarter, and told it, “You’ll do.” The boy knew the full note that pot would give before it left the wheel, and the knowing went where such knowing always went in this house: onto the near-full tray beside the hearings and never among them, advanced and not paid, a thing known and not given, as a man knows a finding before he sets his name to the chart and leaves the margin clean. He had no word in Grathic for the knowing that would not turn it into an account, and he did not make one.

❦

A kiln-master came up from High Kiln on the wrong wind before the pot had left the bench, with Ash pale on his cuffs and that sweetish edge at the corner of his mouth. He stood a moment with his hands behind him, looking at the pot the way a man looks at a thing he has come to put a price on. “That one’s singing before its time,” he said. “A pot that sings early generally sings itself out early.”

Jorra did not look at him and did not look at the boy. He put his hand back on the wheel. “It’s a Vail pot,” he said.

“I didn’t say it wasn’t.”

“You said it early.”

The kiln-master let the sentence carry away down the wind and went off toward the racks with his cuffs pale and his bag at his side, and no pot was lost in the yard that morning and nothing was made of the saying. The boy kept the withheld knowing where it belonged. A knowing held back is not a hearing given; six hearings stood at six from four sources with a sixth from a person, and no seventh came to stand among them and none was invented to fill the empty place. He did not call the kiln-master a fool or the wheel-keeper a liar. Both men had said what their places required, and the finding stood on its own tray with nobody’s name on it.

❦

The damper was cold. It had been cold since the coals were banked and would stay cold until the feeding, and Jorra went to it the way a man goes to a thing he knows by the feel of it in the dark, with the bent finger leading as it had led every morning for three years. Sella stood with her back to the basket and her hand on the back of the broken pot that kept the carried fire under its belly, and she was telling him what the house told him every morning.

“The fire’s low,” she said. “It wants—”

And there it stopped. The rest of the sentence did not come. It stood in the room with nobody to carry it, the way a spoon stands in a dish that has been set down.

On every morning of the boy’s life the bent finger had gone into the iron before that sentence was finished. He knew the order of it as he knew the order of the kiln’s four: the finger going in ahead of the hand, the tongue of the iron moving a hair’s breadth, the end of her instruction arriving at the pot instead of at the man, and the household straightening around his silence the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted. An obedience that was also a correction neither of them would have named. A hand that knows where the iron stands even when it is held back from the iron. He had watched the lowering of the man’s voice for three years the way the whole household had, as a weather signal, and had learned to read a lowered voice and a waiting hand as signs of what the day would do.

This morning the finger went to the cold iron and stopped.

It went as far as the iron and no further. The cold took the heat out of the fingertip — cold iron does that, takes and does not give back until a hand has stayed a while and the iron has consented to give — and the finger lay on it without moving, and the man stood over it with his weight on the balls of his feet the way he stood at the wheel when a pot was going crooked and he wanted it to come right by his not touching it. He did not look at Sella. He did not look at the basket. He did not move the tongue. The room went on around the waiting: the kiln breathing behind the wall, the river going over water beyond the door, Sella’s hand on the broken pot with the little-door burn shutting and opening, shutting and opening, on the wood.

Sella’s hand stayed where it was. She had begun a sentence and the sentence was hers to finish, and for the first time under this roof nobody took it off her before she had.

“It wants feeding,” she said.

Only then did the tongue of the iron move a hair’s breadth, and stand where it had been moved.

The household straightened around a sentence that had been allowed to finish as it had straightened around his silences for three years. No one said a word about the waiting. No one looked into the basket to see whether the waiting had been seen. No explanation was given to the wool, the pot, the fire, or anybody. The pot that had been singing before its time went on standing on the bench with its note still in it, known and withheld and standing.

The boy put the fraction on a place of its own. It was a change held and not spoken: not a reconciliation, not a hearing, not a payment, with no payer named to it and no slate entered. The last of the reeds had gone downriver before the boy did, and the work of them had stopped being a bundle and become a direction. The boy felt it in the arm that carried him: not the reeds themselves, which were out of sight now, but the going down and the coming back, the two turns of the wrist that had made a morning’s labour into a road the body remembered the way a dam remembered the weight of the water it had held and let go. He had once closed a chart over a finding and left the neat margin showing; he knew the shape of an answer that had had its question taken out of it when he met one, and he met one every morning under this roof.

The Weir lay one league upriver of Sill, and the boy had been carried the length of the road often enough that the place had the wrong familiarity of a room entered in the dark: the plank walk on its posts, the flat blessing stone where the hearth-maids blessed the traps, the lower boards where the eels were worked, and a landing set a hand’s breadth out of line below the north bridge. A basket set down was a pot set down, and a pot set down was left where it stood.

The reed-cutter came along the boards with the season finished and no bundle left to set down. On the flat stone he straightened the short length of wool-grey cord gone dark with the oil of a working thumb, turned it once so the knots stood, tied one low with his teeth and a pull of the thumb, bit the end off, and put it back inside his coat with the flat of the hand, neither folded nor shown. “You can ask the cord,” he said.

“And who’ll answer me?” she said.

On the loaf shelf the row kept its two unnamed gaps and the charcoal under its front edge waited unread for a hand that was not his and a day that had not come. One stiff brown-edged crust lay two inches from his ear with its empty place beside it still unnamed and no third; the shelf’s two gaps were not one gap twice and were not the crust’s gap and were not the pan’s gone place, and he set no finger between any of them. Ash was worked into the creases of a hand two rooms away and a new hairline of grey had gathered in the corner beside the old dark line, finer than the old and swept by nobody; a thing ground small as meal had got there by being broken, while a thing mixed was put together on purpose, and he kept the two on separate trays as he kept everything. The river’s note of water over water, the fire’s four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, and the boards’ four nails, four, and a knot the width of a thumb were three counts in three places and did not become one. The fire’s payer had no name under any roof for a hundred and sixty-two days.

One hundred and sixty-two stood exact, complete and unspent as his hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water through the finger’s-width door and came down against the fold. He had stopped expecting to be told what the number was, which was not the same as not wanting it. Cold arrived in its known order, cheek, temple, the small bare joint at the wrist where the wool rode back, and stopped there as if it had reached the end of its instruction. Want came up small and importunate, for the crust two inches from his ear and for the number spoken by somebody who would have known what a number was, and got neither; both were true and the second was the one he kept. Through the wall the fire missed its fifth under the floor, its payer unnamed for a hundred and sixty-two days. The sentence that had been allowed to finish stood where it had been left. The charcoal waited under the shelf for a hand that was not his and a day that had not come. His hand went up an inch toward the river’s note and came down against the fold, and the count went on.

The Chronicle of Chapter 39

After the first Account-day the firing month went back to its work. The boy was one hundred and sixty days old on the first morning and one hundred and sixty-two on the last, and nobody said so. The kiln breathed in fours with a gap; the fire missed its fifth with its payer unnamed, and the river kept its note. Sella Vail gave her instruction to wool, fire, pot and broom, never to the basket, kept her half-pace and left the widow’s mark where it hung: a morning, a shelf and a dusk are not openings. The yard went around the clean dark line, the thaw, the broken birth-slates and the place where a hand went in on Seed 15 and did not come out. At the shelf she sent a wet-pot neighbour’s cost to the pot, then reached a finger beneath the front edge where the charcoal lay, without looking, and brought it away unread.

On the second morning three or four men went low into the back room and got no name. Jorra Vail came through with slate dust ground small as meal on both forearms, told a pot it would do, and withheld its note from a kiln-master. Then, for the first time under that roof, his bent finger went to the cold damper and stopped. Sella had begun The fire’s low. It wants---; the hand waited while she finished It wants feeding, then moved the tongue a hair’s breadth. No one spoke. The boy filed the fraction on a place of its own --- a change held and not spoken, not a reconciliation, not a hearing. Tarn’s stroke went on over the flagstones without the half-check; nine stoppings stood and the loan stayed uncalled.

On the last day the finished reeds had become a direction counted in the carrying arm, which keeps the weight and not the cost. Downriver at the Weir the reed-cutter tied one knot with his teeth and hid his cord; Orra Weir gave the ninth of Ilmen where a figure was asked, and Mira Silt answered a thin run with Ask the river, her second twist twice beneath the creel. A child’s foot stayed dark in three turns of wool; the reading of the cold bought nothing at two paces. The soft-cuffed clerk stayed set for a measure nobody called.

At the going of the light she carried him home, going wide around the pan’s gone place, the trough and the rack space. She set him on his bed and not in the Debt Loft, told the fire it was the thaw and The house will keep it, and set the pot handle two inches beyond the reaching hand. In the small hours his file was exact and worthless: six hearings with no seventh, nine stoppings with no payer. One hundred and sixty-two stood exact, complete and unspent. The fire missed its fifth; the charcoal waited for a hand that was not his and a day that has not come.
