A Figure Held Edge-On

Days 153–159 · Tallow 4–10, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, the Weir and Sill

At the first counting of the firing month the crier holds the slate edge-on to his own forearm and reads a figure the goers-by cannot see. An arm come down from the Weir knows the weight of what it carried and not what it cost; a hand going to a slate knows the figure before it knows the face. The yard keeps what it keeps in wood, cord and wool, and a thing set where nobody looks is a thing nobody can be made to give back.

— A saying of the moot-post, spoken at the first Account-day of Tallow

— Day 153 —

Tallow 4, Y.H. 813 (Fourthday)

Before first grey the room held the shape of the night in it: the hollow of the bed, the crease of the fold, the small warm place where a head had lain and was lying still, and the crust two inches from his ear with its brown edge gone leathery in the dry heat. the boy lay on his back under the Vail roof and knew the hour by no clock the house kept, only by the order of the cold, which had come cheek, then temple, then the small bare joint at the wrist where the wool had ridden back, and had stopped there as if it had reached the end of its instruction. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the night and nobody had been given an instruction about the thread, so it kept its line. Beyond the finger’s-width door the river went over itself with the low note of water over water that had been there every night of his life, and his hand outside the wool went up an inch toward it and came down against the fold. He was one hundred and fifty-three days old and five months and three days, and nobody under that roof said so, and nobody under that roof would have known what to do with the saying of it if somebody had put the words into their mouths. The house kept its days in loaves pushed along a board, in a glance at a clay jar, in a space at the end of a row that no woman called a number. His count had no rim and no handle and no woman passing with a pot would have set it down. It stood where it stood. It went on.

He laid the night’s file away the way a man puts instruments back into a case, without opening it to add anything and without taking anything out to keep the empties company. Six hearings of the full note stayed six with the seventh place left as empty as it had been found; three near-full answers stayed on their own tray; the nine stoppings stayed objects with no payer named to any and no tenth stood up among them. He had learned that an empty hollow is a thing in its own right and not a place waiting to be filled, and that a man who sets an instrument into one because the hollow is there has already decided what the case is. The file lay down. Nothing in it had grown a hand during the night.

The first smell of the day was hot clay under tallow, coming through the finger’s-width door before any sound of the yard; it went under the wool and along his cheek and was gone, leaving a dry mineral edge in the mouth. The first sound was not a bell. There would be no bell before Tallow 10 and no market before it, and the Cross kept its silence the way a slate keeps a figure that no hand has been set upon. The sound was the kiln’s breath behind the wall, four low draws and a gap where the fifth should have been, regular as a sleeper breathing through a partition and no more addressed to him than that. Under the floor the carried fire answered in the same figure, and through the door the river kept its fifth low note, and he set the three on three places and did not lay one across the other. Two likenesses in a row are the beginning of a rule, and a rule wants a foot at the bottom of it with a name under the foot. He kept his finger between them.

Sella Vail’s the widow’s mark hung where it had hung since the first firing of the month, a thumb-length of Low Kiln clay under its glaze with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and it was not touched in this room. Three days had gone since the hand went to it before the kiln mouth, held one breath and let go, and the rule had kept itself every hour since with nobody to praise it and nobody to see it broken. A shelf was not an opening. A well was not an opening. A morning under a roof with a basket set down in it was not an opening. The mark kept its place; the house kept its count in loaves and glances; the boy kept his in a number that no mouth under the roof would have known how to take from him if he had held it out.

Sella came in from the yard with the basket on her hip, the little-door burn closing on the wicker handle and opening again, and she did not look into the basket. She gave the instruction to the wool and the pot and the fire and the broom before she gave it to anybody and never gave it to the basket, which was a vessel filled and carried and had no instruction of its own to answer. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. “It’s the river wind.” To Tarn in the doorway she said the house wanted the yard swept before the sun came on the flagstones — not I want it, not you sweep it — and Tarn said “Yes, house” as though the sentence had been finished for him.

At the hearth she told the fire it was the thaw, turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and said, “The house will keep it,” where another woman would have said I. She stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand, counting under her breath toward no opening, her lips moving on a row that stood in some other room of her head; then she pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn, the grey streak at her temple showing pale off the coals, tipped the carried coal under the back of the pot and set the pot rim out with its handle standing two inches beyond the reaching hand. The fire answered four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been. It had missed the fifth for a hundred and fifty-three days and no payer had been named to the silence. She did not look toward the bed, and her hand did not go to the widow’s mark, because a morning was not an opening and a hearth was not a door.

Then she lifted him out of the basket without looking into it and carried him out under the roof, base first and weight settled, the way a pot is lifted that must not be slopped. He felt the turn in the shoulder that carried him and the small outward swing at the threshold and the settling back, and he kept the half-pace from every open slate because she kept it, though there had been no open slate within reach of the basket all morning. A woman with a wet pot asked what the firing would cost set down there; Sella sent her to the pot with “Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there. The house has said it,” and carried the unanswered question past at the half-pace with nothing slopped from it.

the Clay Yard had come up in the night into the hot dry smell of clay giving up its water, and the women went around what they would not enter as they had gone around it every morning since the kilns were brought up. There was the clean dark line in the Vail corner where the Ash ridge had turned, and beside it the new finer hairline of grey that had gathered through the lean weeks and was called the thaw, and neither had been swept. There was the space beneath the lowest rack where the broken birth-slates lay, and the yard went around it as wide as a woman goes around a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. And there was the empty place in the returning line of the worked strips where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out: a woman looked once at the space between two stooping backs, took a wide step and said nothing, and another took the step behind her without looking at all. No number came out of the looking. No name, no body, no cause, no figure and no payment had come out of it in all the days since, and the boy held the absence on a place of its own and did not lay it across the loaf shelf’s two gaps or the empty place at his ear or the round bare ring on the wall where a pan had stood out to dry through Reed. A fourth likeness would make a cause and a cause would want a payer; no finger of his drew the line.

At the well the trough stood bare and dark under its little roof, the skin of ice that had lain on it like a skim of fat set aside in a dish gone these three days and not revived, and a woman letting down the bucket called the water weather to nobody in particular. The weather had heard worse and answered nothing. On the wall the clay jar stood cold under its glaze; Sella’s eye went to it once, as a hand goes to an instrument before the case is opened, and went away, and the jar was not touched. He had learned the order of reaching — the hand to the record before the hand to the person — and had seen it performed by a midwife laying slates before turning to a fold, a thane passing a finger over a figure before looking at the crowd, a clerk with hand to slate before eye to stalls, a reed-cutter straightening his cord before lifting his trap, a woman reaching a finger beneath a board before her eyes went to the row, and Sella’s glance at the jar before anything else. Not one of the hands explained the grammar it was keeping. He set the grammar on its own place and made no rule of it, because a rule wants a payer and no payer had been named to any of them.

Tarn took the broom only after it had been given its instruction, never before; he held it a moment with the small tool he had put into his pocket when he thought nobody looked, and said, “Yes, house. I’ll sweep it. I swept it this morning. I’ll sweep it again.” His seventh year stood one tend from its end and still held, six counted in the worn thumb-mark where the house could check and the seventh at the lip of the sill where it could not, the knife set back at the very edge with the unforged collector sentence beside it, the breath from Seed 16 unspent with its small interval standing at the top of each reach. His chest had been tight since the dark. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line and no scrubbing lifted it, and the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back. His stroke went on over the flagstones past the shallow bright patch without even the half-check, the stones keeping the place where it had stopped, and he did not turn his head along the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector. No collector came. The nine stoppings stood where they had stood and no tenth stood up among them before the seventh year had been earned; a tenth stood up now would be a debt invented to make the nine into a bill.

The kiln’s breath came through the wall again in fours with a gap, and the kiln mouth lay two paces off with its heat kept and counted behind the screen of its own brick. He read the opening as a thing done and gone past the way a door is gone past once a man is through it: a theatre in which the danger is kept behind a screen, and the hands that know where the screen stands are the hands that must not go behind it. Somewhere in the brick was a gate with two bites, the bite that holds the heat and the bite that can take the hand off a man, and the heat was counted while the cost was paid by somebody whose name was not spoken under any roof. He knew the order in which a man goes when the breath takes him wrong — head first, the skin doing what the vessels under it do, the almost-smile that is not peace, the core holding on after the extremities had given out — and the knowing was exact and worth nothing with the heat two paces off. Nobody made a lesson of it. The yard went on with the work.

At the going of the light Sella carried him up the clay road at the half-pace, woodsmoke going before her and the river wind coming up to meet it, the two passing on the stair without speaking as they had passed every morning and every evening of his life. Halfway up she went around the place on the wall where a pan had stood out to dry through Reed, at the well she went around the trough now without its skin of ice, and at the yard she went wide around the space beneath the lowest rack, and gave no explanation of any of the turns to the basket. Under the roof she set him on the bed and not in the Debt Loft, put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without once looking at his face, and left the thread of drool frozen and unwiped, her hand resting a half-beat longer than the setting needed before it went away.

At the hearth she told the fire it was the thaw, turned the coals with the back of a broken pot, said “The house will keep it,” stood a moment counting under her breath toward no opening, pushed the coal-black hair back with the burn-wrist, and set the pot rim out with its handle two inches beyond the reaching hand. She did not look toward the bed. She did not touch the widow’s mark, because a dusk is not an opening. The fire answered four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for a hundred and fifty-three days. The two rooms did not touch.

In the small hours he turned the file over once and laid it back without adding to it. One hundred and fifty-three stood where no mouth under that roof would have known how to set it down, exact, complete and unspent, five months and three days; want came up small and importunate for the crust two inches from his ear and for the number spoken by somebody who would have known what a number was, and got neither. The crust kept its empty place beside it and no third came. The round bare ring on the wall, the shelf’s two gaps, the field’s empty place and the crust’s empty place stayed each on its own place and none was laid across another. Through the finger’s-width door came the river’s note of water over water, a fifth low thing never counted among the hearings; through the floor the fire missed its fifth; overhead the boards kept four nails, four, and a fifth that was a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts in three places and none became one. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note and came down against the fold with one hundred and fifty-three exact, complete and unspent in the dark. The month had gone one day further into itself without an entry, and the count went on.

— Day 154 —

Tallow 5, Y.H. 813 (Fifthday)

Before first grey the yard had not yet decided what sort of day it would be, and the men who came up the clay road had the look of men who had already decided for it. Three or four of them went low-voiced with their collars high against the river wind, and turned in at the door off the Vail wheel-shed that kept its finger’s width of open, going down into the back room the way a man goes into a room he has entered before and expects to enter again. No woman under the roof said who they were. Sella Vail carried the boy past at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, the little-door burn closing on the wicker handle and opening again, and she turned no head toward the door, and gave the house no name for the men, and did not say the room was cold. The instruction went to the wool and the pot and the fire before it went to anybody, and never to the basket. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. “It’s the river wind.”

He was one hundred and fifty-four days old and five months and four days, and nobody under any roof said so, and nobody under any roof would have known what to do with the saying of it if a man had put the words into their mouths. The house kept its days in loaves pushed along a board and in a glance at a clay jar and in a space at the row’s end that no woman called a number. His count had no rim and no handle and no woman passing with a pot would have set it down. It stood where it stood. It went on.

There were two tellings of a counting room shut in the war with the men inside dead of the breath, and both had come downriver as a curiosity with a date, and the yard had taken them for history both times. A third voice had once only mentioned the room, which is not the same thing as telling it, and he had kept the difference as carefully as he kept the rest. This morning no voice told it again. The two tellings lay side by side where they had been laid and did not touch, and he did not set a third beside them to keep them company. A row of likenesses will stand up and call itself a rule if a man leaves it long enough alone, and a rule wants a payer at its foot; he had learned to keep his finger between them.

❦

Sella set the basket low at the edge of the wheel-shed work where no slate lay, and the boy lay at the angle at which the underside of a shelf becomes a roof. The hot dry smell of clay giving up its water came up off the yard and got under the nails and stayed there; under it was the fainter warmth of tallow, and under that again, on a wind that came the wrong way off High Kiln, the sweetish edge of lead glaze that put a false sweetness in the mouth and made a man think of fruit where there was no fruit. Dust lay ground small as meal on the sill and on the backs of hands and in the white of a knuckle when a fist opened. He did not turn his head toward the wind. A smell was a smell and not a hearing, and what a wrong wind carried was not the same thing as what a man said when he chose to say it.

Jorra Vail came through from the shed with the day’s fine grey slate dust ground small as meal into both forearms past the bent little finger and under the nails, not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel. The dust lay in the creases of his wrists and in the white of a knuckle when a fist opened, finer than slip and drier, and the boy set the two on two places on one tray and did not let one stand for the other. A thing ground is a thing that got there by being broken. A thing mixed is a thing somebody put together on purpose. He had learned to tell the two before he could have said why, and the telling, exact and complete, stood in the basket and was worth nothing. Jorra asked neither which slate had been broken nor whether it had been a birth-slate, and kept the dusty forearms and the clean ones on one tray without a word passing between the two states. Under the lowest rack the broken birth-slates lay where they lay, gone around as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and no woman reached under them and no man entered them into anything.

“It doesn’t care whose hands. It cares if they’re steady,” he told the wheel, in the flat voice that went to the tool before it went to the clay and to the clay before a person. He told a pot, “You’ll do.” He knew the full note of it before it left the wheel — knew the pitch it would give under a knuckle when it had cooled and been set down — and he did not say so when a kiln-master come up from High Kiln on the bad wind, with Ash the colour of the ridge on his cuffs, said the pot was singing before its time and that a pot which sings early generally sings itself out early. Jorra said nothing. The kiln-master went off with his sentence whole and the pot went on turning, and the knowing went on the tray of answers that had come near to full and had not been paid, and not among the hearings. There were six hearings of the full note, five from four sources and the sixth, the small flat knock out of a woman as the men lifted her, from a person, and there was no seventh and none invented to fill the place where none was owed. A knowing held back is not a hearing given; he set the two on separate hollows and kept his finger between them. The room came level again in the space of a breath, with nothing added to it and nothing taken away.

He had watched the withholding the way a man watches a hand keep pressure in a cavity that will not hold pressure on its own. The pot’s note was in the pot before ever a knuckle went to it, and Jorra knew the pitch the way a man knows the weight of a thing he has lifted a thousand times, in the muscle and not in a figure; but a knowing spoken before a kiln-master becomes a thing the kiln-master can enter, and the man who says it early will be told it is early and made to answer for the saying. So the knowing stayed where it was put, advanced and not paid, on the tray with the other answers that had come near to full. He did not grudge it its place. An answer that has not been spoken cannot be used against the man who kept it, and an answer that cannot be used is worth exactly what he could do with it, which was nothing.

Before Sella had finished telling him the fire was low, Jorra’s bent finger went into the cold iron of the damper, moved the tongue a hair’s breadth, and left it standing. The cold took the warmth out of the fingertip as iron takes it, without haste and without malice. The end of her sentence arrived at the pot instead of at him, and the household straightened around his silence the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted off it: Sella’s hands went on to the next pot, Tarn’s head stayed down over his work, and nothing in the room turned to look at what had been done. It was an obedience and it was a correction neither of them would name. He read the iron as one piece with two bites, the bite that holds a vessel whole and the bite that can take the hand off a man, with no word in Grathic that would hold both mercies in one breath, and filed it as an object worth nothing, joined to no kiln. A clamp is a clamp and a gate is a gate; a man who calls the one the other already wants an account, and an account wants a payer at its foot. There was no payer named to this iron and no name spoken over it under this roof.

Tarn took the broom only after it had been given its instruction, with the small knife set back at the very edge of its sill where six could be counted and the seventh could not, the unforged sentence lying beside it and the breath from Seed 16 unspent with its small interval at the top of each reach. His chest had been tight since the dark. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line, and the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back. His stroke went on over the flagstones past the shallow bright patch without even the half-check, the stones keeping the place where it had stopped, and he did not turn his head along the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector. No collector came. He did not ask about the room and Jorra did not ask about the ear, and the two silences stood in one room unjoined, the loan that could hear still uncalled and the seventh year still held. The stroke that went on when it might have checked was another interval on the tray, an object and not a payment, the place in the stone kept as a thing nobody would enter; he laid it beside the standing finger and laid no finger across the two.

At midday Sella carried him back under the roof and set him on the bed and not in the Debt Loft, going halfway around the place on the wall where a shallow clay pan had stood out to dry through Reed and was not standing now, tilting shoulder and basket so nothing knocked, and giving no explanation of the turn to the basket. The round bare ring where the pan had been kept its shape on a place of its own. It was not laid across the shelf’s two gaps or the crust’s empty place or the field’s empty place where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out; no finger of his drew the line, because a fourth likeness would make a cause and a cause would want a payer, and none had been named to any of them.

She stood with her back to the basket and moved her lips along the loaf shelf, where the row kept its two unnamed gaps and the space at the end that nobody called a number, and glanced once at the clay jar cold under its glaze. Her finger did not go beneath the front edge today. The charcoal tally lay where it lay under the board, unread and uncounted, and a hand that either knew the row by heart or had been told not to look stayed where it was. In the other room one stiff brown-edged crust lay two inches from his ear with its empty place beside it, unnamed, and no third crust came into the fold. The shelf’s two gaps, the pan’s gone ring, the crust’s empty place and the field’s empty place stayed each on its own tray, and he kept them there by care rather than by discovery, the way a man lays instruments out on a towel with nothing resting across anything else.

The kiln’s breath came through the wall in fours with a gap, and under the floor the carried fire answered in the same figure, its payer unnamed for a hundred and fifty-four days; through the finger’s-width door came the river’s note of water over water, a fifth low thing never counted among the hearings; overhead the boards kept four nails, four, and a fifth that was a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts in three places, and none became one. He laid his file away as he had laid it out, with six hearings beside and never among, three near-full answers advanced and not paid, nine stoppings with no payer named to any and no tenth stood up, the damper’s double bite joined to no kiln, the two shut-room tellings with no third, the told bell beside the heard bell. One hundred and fifty-four stood exact, complete and unspent. The adult count and the child’s body argued as they had argued every day of his life, the one dry and exact and given to keeping, the other immediate and cold and impatient for the crust; both were true and the second was louder, and neither had a hand to enter anything. Want came up small and importunate for the crust two inches from his ear and for the number spoken by somebody who would have known what a number was, and got neither. The round empty ring on the wall kept the shape of its absence on a tray of its own while, through the wall, the fire answered four low pulls and a silence with no payer named, and his hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down against the fold.

— Day 155 —

Tallow 6, Y.H. 813 (Sixthday)

The last of the reed work came downriver in the morning as a direction and not a bundle. There were no more grey ranks of cut reed standing in the beds behind the yard; there was only the going down and the coming back, and the going down lived in the arm that carried it before it lived in a cart or on a slate. Sella Vail carried the boy along the river road at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, the little-door burn closing on the wicker handle and opening again, and a woman come up from the beds with empty hands said to nobody in particular that the hands would want paying now the reeds were out of them. She was answered with the going down and the coming back, and with the river, and with the weather, and went on up the road with the question still in her mouth and no entry anywhere. A cost asked as a direction is answered with a direction. It leaves no figure and no payer and no place to put either. He lay at the angle at which the basket’s rim becomes a wall and heard the note of water going over water under the bank, a fifth low thing never counted among the hearings. An arm that has carried a load all morning knows the weight of what it carried and knows nothing of what it cost; the muscle keeps the one and has no nerve that can enter the other. He was one hundred and fifty-five days old and five months and five days, and nobody on the river road said so, and nobody on the river road would have known what to do with the saying of it if a man had put the words into their mouths. The house kept its days in loaves pushed along a board and the yard kept them in the weight an arm remembers. His count had neither rim nor handle and went on standing where it stood.

At the Weir she set the basket at the edge of the work where no slate lay, going around a slate propped against the bank with the basket tilted at the half-pace, and gave the instruction to the wool and to the running water before she gave it to any person, and never gave it to the basket. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. “It’s the river wind.” The river wind went along the the plank walk on its posts and came up between the boards where the traps stood with their mouths upriver, and salt stood white on the posts where the flood had touched them, and the whole place smelled of cold fish and river silt and tallow smoke. Below the sill the water said its low note over the stones and was called weather by everybody who had cause to pass it and by nobody who had cause to look at it. She stood a half-pace from the nearest open slate and did not look into the basket.

❦

The reed-cutter came along the boards with the last of the season on his shoulder and the short length of wool or reed-fibre cord looped inside his coat, gone grey with the oil of a working thumb. He had no bundle to set down, for the work had finished and become a direction, but on the flat blessing stone he straightened the cord along his knee and turned it once so that the knots stood. They stood in a row that no eye but his had cause to read. He tied one low with his teeth and a pull of the thumb, bit the end off, and put the cord back inside his coat with the flat of the hand, neither folded nor shown. A weir woman at the rails asked what it held.

“You can ask the cord,” he said.

“And who’ll answer me?” she said.

“No more can the Court,” he said, without anger and without a smile, and went up the boards to lift a trap. His hand had gone to the cord before it went to the trap and before it went to the woman; he had not counted a knot where anyone could hear him, and no figure came out of the cord into the morning. The boy could not have said how many knots there were. He had seen the straightening and the turn and the tie and the putting away, and the order of them stood as a shape and not as a number, a tally carried in wool and teeth and thumb that told what had been gathered and not what the gathering had been paid. Three times in a row such a hand would have made a grammar, and a grammar wants an account at its foot; he kept this one on the stone where it had happened and did not carry it up the boards after the man.

Below the stone Orra Weir stood at the lower boards with her board on its side and a grey cord at her waist, turning the board with the wrist that had turned it ten thousand times. Her hands were like old rope; the pale eel scars lay uncovered across both forearms, salt was dried white at her wrists, and the grey streak showed first through the black hair when she moved her head. When she was tired the nasal half-song of the Fen came into her voice, flat and low, and it had come into it this morning as it came into it every morning now, without any words that would have made a song of it. She pulled one knot down with her thumb and set it among the others with a small flat knock — the knock of a thing laid where it belongs, not the small flat knock of a vessel cracked all the way through, and the boy set the two sounds on places that did not touch.

A woman at the rails, with the river in her hem and salt at her wrists older than the day’s wetting, asked what the levy would take now the hands were out of the reeds.

“The ninth of Ilmen,” Orra said. “The collector’s boat came round the bend on the flood and had six oars. One man stood at the stern and never touched them.”

“That isn’t the levy,” the woman said.

“It’s a date. The river takes what the Court doesn’t. You can shout at either one. I’ve tried.” She said it slow, and the half-song went under the words as it went under everything she said when she was tired, and then she went back to the knots.

A date laid where a figure had been asked is an answer with the question taken out of it, the way a chart is closed over its finding and the neat margin left showing. The boy set the ninth of Ilmen and the boat with its six oars on the place where she had laid them, and the man standing at the stern with his hands off the oars on a place of his own beside them, and wrote no cause across either. Orra’s thumb went to the cord again as the sentence ended, and he could not tell whether that was a lie or only the habit of counting, and made no cause of that either. The last knot on the cord kept its double turn, tail tucked, tied left over right with the small extra weight of a thumb pressed in; it was legible and complete and wanted nothing from him. The word that would have named it stayed where it had stayed since the year of the flower. The cord went back upriver to the Eel House, where no clerk could spell it.

Below the sill, at the outer boards of the fish-trade shade, Mira Silt worked the eels with pale slime dried in long grey strokes on her sleeves and silt dried pale in the hollow of her neck. She held her head a little to the right where the top of her left ear had been, and kept her small smile on her face the way another woman keeps a door on the latch: it could be shut without anybody seeing the movement. A weir woman come down the boards with a pail asked whether the run would be thin now the last of the reeds were out of the water.

“Thin?” Mira said. “Who’s to say thin? It comes when it comes. Ask the river.” She flicked an eel-bone against her thumb while she decided whether to lie, moved two from hand to hand, and put one away when the deciding was done. “I’ll tell you what I know and then it’ll be yours to carry. Don’t say I gave it you.”

It was not a slate propped at the rack end to dry and not one of the birth-slates under the lowest rack; it was a runner’s slate, carried as a runner carries a thing that is not hers and is answerable to somebody else. Hessa Rale of Slate Row had walked the road between the Cross and Greybank often enough to have stopped measuring it, and she came at the pace of a person who has made a bargain with distance and keeps it without thinking. Her coat was shortened at the skirt for the road’s mud, and the mud had dried on it in a pale scurf which she had not brushed off, because a runner’s coat is a tool and nobody scours a tool for its own sake. Her hands were the hands of a woman who carries and does not open. The fingers closed on the slate’s edge with a working looseness, the thumb lay along the grain of the frame, and nothing pressed where the writing was. She held the slate edge-on to her own forearm so that the face of it turned toward her sleeve and the writing looked at nobody. A strap crossed her chest, and a second shorter strap had been let out to take the weight when the road was long.

Her hand went to the slate before her eyes went to the people in the yard. The small flat correction came first, a settler of the strap and a turn of the wrist to bring the edge round, and only then did her face take in the women at the trough, the racks, the kiln mouth, the basket at the half-pace against the wall. A woman nearest her said the morrow was the Account-day and asked what the levy would take now that the kilns had been brought up and hands were in the month.

“I don’t read it,” Hessa said. “I carry it. There’s a difference, and the road doesn’t care which.”

The woman wanted more than that and said so with her hands on her hips.

“Cross to Greybank,” Hessa said. “Reading goes up before noon. I’ve got a road to walk.” And she set the edge of the slate back against her forearm and went on through the yard, leaving the question standing where it had been set down, the way a man leaves a pot where there is no hand to carry it.

The boy watched her go and filed the order of reaching as an eighth such hand-shape in the district, and put it on a place of its own. He had learned that grammar before he had learned the order of words: the hand to the record before the hand to the person. It was not Marra’s pause between strokes at the edge of a flare light, not the neat dark hand that set a name down before anybody looked at a face, not the clerk at the Court’s table, not the soft cuff set at the out-of-line landing for a measure nobody called, not the soft hands at the fringe of Slate Row, not Berr’s twice-wiped thumb, not the finger that went beneath the loaf shelf in the other house without a word and without looking. Eight hands in one district performing one grammar and not one of them explaining it, each kept on its own place with nothing laid across anything else. He did not let the number of them trouble him into a rule. A rule wants a payer, and no payer had been named to any of them.

She was a runner and not a reader of anyone’s private keeping. No figure came off her slate in the yard. She did not open it and nobody asked her to open it, and the edge-on was not a hiding of something from herself but the manner of carrying a slate so the writing is not rubbed and no goer-by takes a figure off it without a hand to put there. The boy set that distinction beside the knowing withheld from a kiln-master and beside the hand under the board, and kept all three apart. Beyond the racks her coat went down the clay road in the direction of Greybank with the writing turned to her sleeve, and the figure on it remained what figures on carried slates remained in this district: a thing somebody else had set down and nobody here had read.

Under the Vail kiln-house roof in the afternoon the house went on in its own grammar, as a house will when a month is gathering interest and there is no instruction about any of it. Tarn took the broom only after it had been given its instruction and said Yes, house to a woman who wanted the thaw early and to a woman who wanted it late, agreeing so thoroughly with both that no argument could find a foot, while going on wanting the late because a late thaw puts off the paying. He had the small knife at the very edge of its sill with six counted in the worn thumb-mark where the house could check and the seventh where it could not, the one entry in this house which the house had never checked. Garl Vail’s unforged collector sentence lay beside it. The breath from Seed 16 was still unspent, standing as a small interval at the top of each reach where it should have gone in and had not; the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was under the roof and gave it back. His seventh year stood nearer its end than it had ever stood and had not given. No tenth stopping stood up out of the nine to make a bill of them, and no collector came up the road, and the loan that could hear was not called.

Jorra Vail came through from the Vail wheel-shed with the day’s fine grey slate dust ground small as meal into both forearms past the bent little finger and under the nails, not bone-coloured slip, keeping dusty forearms and clean ones on one tray. He said a word to the wheel and another to a pot and asked neither of them which slate had been broken or whether it had been a birth-slate, and no man came up on the wrong wind today to say that a pot was singing before its time, so the saying was not repeated under the roof. Before Sella Vail had finished telling him the fire was low his bent finger went into the cold iron of the damper, moved the tongue a hair’s breadth and left it standing, and the household straightened around his silence the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted. He did not look toward the basket. Tarn did not ask about the room and Jorra did not ask about the ear, and the two silences stayed where they lay under one roof without a word put between them. The boy knew the order in which a body takes heat back and the order in which a vessel gives it up, and that one piece of iron had two bites in it, the holding and the taking, with no Grathic word for both mercies; and the knowing bought him nothing with the heat two paces off and his hand where it was.

At the loaf shelf Sella moved her lips along a row that stood with two gaps and gave nobody a figure, and glanced once at the clay jar that stood cold under its glaze against the wall, and did not touch it and did not go back to it. The charcoal lay under the front edge where it lay and waited unread. No third gap came. In the bed one stiff brown-edged crust lay two inches from his ear with the empty place beside it still unnamed and no third, and the two rooms did not touch. Through the finger’s-width door came the river’s note of water over water, a fifth low thing never counted among the hearings; through the floor the carried fire gave four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been; overhead the boards kept four nails, four, and a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts in three places and none of them became one. The adult who had once stood with a towel in his hands in a room where heat was kept behind a screen watched the numbers keep their separate places; the child’s body was carried under a board and was cold and wanted the crust and the warmth that never came within two paces of the reaching hand. Both were true and the second was louder.

At the going of the light she carried him up the clay road at the half-pace, woodsmoke going before her and the river wind coming up to meet it, the two passing on the stair as they had passed every evening of his life. She went around the place on the wall where the pan had stood out to dry through Reed and was not standing now, around the bare dark trough at the well and wide around the space beneath the lowest rack, and gave no explanation of any of the turns to the basket. She set him on the bed and not in the Debt Loft, base first and weight settled, put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without once looking at his face, left the thread of drool frozen and unwiped because nobody under any roof had been given an instruction about the thread, and rested her hand a half-beat longer than the setting needed before it went away. At the hearth she told the fire it was the thaw, turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and said, “The house will keep it,” where another woman would have said I. She stood a moment counting under her breath toward no opening, pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn, the grey streak pale off the coals, set the pot rim out with its handle standing two inches beyond the reaching hand and did not look toward the bed. She did not touch the widow’s mark, because a dusk is not an opening and a hearth is not a door, and the hand goes to the mark before the kiln and at no other hour. She went to her own bed without explaining the count.

The unknown number had gone from a man’s fist up the clay road and had arrived nowhere; ahead of it a narrow slate had gone edge-on up the road toward Greybank with the writing turned to a runner’s sleeve and no figure read off it under any roof in Sill. The pot handle stood two inches beyond the reaching hand. The fire gave four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for a hundred and fifty-eight days. In the dark one hundred and fifty-eight stood exact, complete and unspent, and the boy’s hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water through the finger’s-width door and came down against the fold.

Tallow 10, Y.H. 813 (Tenthday)

The tenth day of Tallow came up over the Cross grey and close, with the river wind coming under the eaves of the square and the smell of hot clay coming down on the same breath from High Kiln. It was the first Account-day of the firing month and the market had come with it the way a cart comes with its load, the pigs already over the weighing board before the light was properly in the boards, and the potters setting bowls out on the pot board with the dust still in the hollows of the rims. The boy lay in the basket in the finger’s width of shade at the foot of the moot-post, his cheek against cold iron and one crust’s worth of tallow and wet wool in his nose, and the crowd leaned over the reading block above him with their backs to the sun that had not yet come. He was one hundred and fifty-nine days old, five months and nine days, and nobody in the square had any reason to say so.

Sella Vail set the basket in the shade where no slate lay and stood a half-pace off from every open slate in the square. She gave the instruction to the wool at his chin and to the river wind coming up under the eaves, and gave none of it to the basket. A woman near the pigs asked what the firing would cost now that the kilns had hands in them, and Sella sent her to the pot with Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there. The house has said it, and carried the question away level in her own hands so that nothing slopped. The boy watched the half-pace keep its width while the crowd came and went around it, and noted, as he had noted every market of his life, that a woman could stand all morning in a square without once being asked to answer for the basket at her feet.

Berr Clay came up short off the clay road. He had the red wool cord of his office folded square under his coat instead of worn across it, and the green of tasted lead glaze stood at one corner of his mouth; under the hot clay was the smell of nothing at all, which is the most expensive smell in Grath. His hands were large and clean. He wiped his thumb twice on his apron before he touched nothing, once for the slate and once for the figure that would be on it, and held a narrow slate unopened to his forearm with the writing turned where the goers-by could not see it. Then he passed a finger over the figure and let his eyes rest on it a breath longer than the writing required, the check of a man setting down a dish he is already sure will not slop.

“One in twelve, as the notice says,” he said low into the goers-by.

The crowd leaned in because the voice was low and there was nothing else to do with their bodies. He said the levy was called and said it was not called by him. A man with a number shut in his fist asked what the firing would cost set down there, and Berr said a number went into a slate and not a bundle, and that if the man’s house wanted it again there would be a slate at the watch. Your house, never I. He took himself out of his own sentence as neatly as a hand goes out of a field before the dressing goes on, leaving the field open and clean and nobody’s. The figure went from the man’s fist to his mouth to the shoulder of somebody going up toward High Kiln and arrived nowhere. The printed one in twelve stood where it stood and nobody challenged it. Berr went off up the clay road with the narrow slate under his arm and the box locked in his house behind him and the true subtraction inside, believing he had done the district a kindness.

Eight pigs went over the weighing board while the reading was still in the air, driven across by two men with poles, and a Sill assessor put the figures on a slate before anybody could ask which eight he meant. His hand went to the slate before his eyes went to the rail. At the square’s edge people brought numbers to a heap of turnips and left them standing side by side without making them agree, which is the district’s whole arithmetic done in the open air. A stall-keeper with meal ground into the creases of her forearms sold a loaf and took the mark into her palm without counting it where anyone could hear. The boy watched the hand go to the mark and then to the loaf: record first, person after, cost wherever it could be set down without a name. He had once set a figure down on a chart while a body lay open under a sheet, and the chart had been exact and the body had not been saved, and nobody had made the two agree. He did not call the market a lesson. A lesson wants a payer, and the morning was only doing what it always did.

The bell went four, four and a gap, and nobody crossed himself. The heard bell stood beside the told bell from the Long Night, two lots of four with the same silence where the fifth should have been, and the boy kept them on separate places without putting a hand across either. A figure read aloud was not a figure paid; a chart can say one thing while a room has gone and done another. He had once held a towel in a room where heat was kept behind a screen and had watched a man stand at the gate and not go in, and the grammar of this square was older than the square: the name taken out of the sentence, the hand out of the field, the cost out of the account. Above him Berr’s twice-wiped thumb and the finger laid on a figure went onto a place of their own among the record-first hands, not joined to the midwife’s reaching hand, the clerk behind the Court’s table, the woman who kept the fire, the cutter with his cord, or the runner with the slate. Nine hands in one district performing one grammar and not one of them explaining it. A rule wants a payer, and no payer had been named to any of them.

Eight hands, one grammar, no joining. He kept a finger between them because likenesses standing in a row will call themselves a doctrine, and a doctrine is an account that has found a payer. No payer had been named to the eighth.

Below the north bridge, at the out-of-line landing, the soft-cuffed clerk was still set for a measure nobody had called. His cuff was grey from washing and grey again by midday. He held his slate to his forearm with the writing turned inward, and his hand went to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls, every time, entering the figure before he looked at the man who had given it. Nobody stepped around him and nobody had taken him off since the month turned. The market sold its pots and pigs and eels over the top of him as water goes over a stone that lies where a stone lies, and a girl with a creel took the landing sideways, caught the rail and went on selling without looking at her feet. No fall was made of it. The boy put the clerk’s order of reaching on its own place beside the others and did not join it to the runner’s; two records going inward were two records going inward, and the resemblance was a shape and not a cause.

He kept the shape of the set clerk the way he kept the shape of a clamp left standing on a vessel: not what it did, but where it stood and how long it had been standing there. Nobody had set this one down in front of him and nobody had taken it away. A measure set down before anybody called it is a measure set down ahead of something, and ahead of something is a direction, and a direction is an answer to a question nobody in the square had asked aloud. He kept it as a shape and made no rule of it. By midday the cuff had gone grey a second time from washing and grey again from the work, the eels went on being sold over the top of him, and the boy’s hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the stalls and came down against the fold with one hundred and fifty-nine still exact and unspent.

Sella did not shift the basket out of the shade and did not look into it. At the going of the light she took up the half-pace and carried him home up the clay road, woodsmoke going before her and the river wind coming up to meet it, the two passing on the stair as they had passed every evening of his life. She said nothing about what had been read over him. The kiln breathed behind the wall in fours with a gap, and in Low Kiln the fires were being banked for the night under broken pots, four low pulls apiece and a silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for a hundred and fifty-nine days.
