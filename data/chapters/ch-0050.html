The Seventh Year at the Lip

Days 201–203 · Ember 22–24, Y.H. 813 · The Vail kiln-house, Low Kiln

The last tend keeps six where the house can count and one where it cannot; the river goes on with its note and does not stop for the hand that follows it.

— A saying of the drying racks in the kiln month

— Day 201 —

Ember 22, Y.H. 813 (Secondday)

The hand outside the wool lay where the fold had taken it, still and small and unasked. In Low Kiln, under the Vail kiln-house roof, the small hours went on around it and the house went on with its ordinary keeping, and nobody drew a boundary under the dark or under whatever had stood before it. The kiln month had entered its last tend, and the tend had opened the way every tend opens — the same cold on the same sill, the same coal banked under the same pot — so that a man looking for a beginning would have found nothing to mark one with. He had been two hundred days old in the dark and was two hundred and one days old in the morning, six months and twenty-one days, and nobody under any roof said either number. He had stopped expecting to be told, which was not the same thing as not wanting it; the wanting had gone down into the body and lived there as the body’s own business, alongside the hunger and the cold. The first smell was hot clay under tallow and wet wool under that, and beneath all of it the sour Ash breath of the unswept corner, which the house called the thaw and had called it so for a year without once sweeping it. The first sound was the kiln drawing in fours with a gap behind it, a brick breath taken and held and let go and then the little silence where a fifth pull should have stood — not a bell. Bells belonged to readings and to the district’s arithmetic; this was a kiln doing what a kiln does, and the boy had learned to tell the two apart before he had learned to hold up his head.

Two hundred stood in him exact, complete and unspent, the ledger carried out of the dark and not yet opened; the morning would make it two hundred and one and nobody under any roof would say either number. No hand had come to spend it. One leathery brown-edged crust lay two inches from his ear in the fold, stiff enough in the dry heat of the kiln month to keep its own shape against the wool, and its empty place lay beside it without a name, and no third came to stand where the second had been. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and stayed frozen, a small bright line nobody had been told to wipe, for a basket set down is a pot set down and left, and a pot set down and given no instruction about its thread is a pot that keeps its thread. Cold had come as far as his cheek and his temple and the bare joint at his wrist where the wool rode back, and had stopped there, as if it too had been given an instruction and had come to the end of it; the afterdrop waited on the far side of a warming nobody in this house would perform. The surgeon’s register in him took the night as a man takes a chart held at the wrong end: heat going off the high air and staying in the fold, a thread left where nobody had been told to wipe it, cold that stopped at a joint and went no further, and a small hand outside the wool with an inch of possible motion in it, filed separately from all of it, exact, complete and worth nothing at two paces. The file was carried in the mornings and laid out only in the small hours, the way a man lays instruments on a towel when the case is shut and the work is done; there was no towel under this roof and no case and no instruments, but the order of the laying was the whole of the discipline, and he would not have it done in the daylight where a glance could fall on it and call it a habit. Six hearings stood beside and never among, the sixth out of a person and not a source; three near-full answers stood advanced and unpaid; nine stoppings stood as nine objects with no tenth before the seventh year was earned; the told bell lay beside the heard and the told figure beside the paid. None of it was laid out now. It went with him under the wool as a ledger goes in a coat, exact, complete and with no hand to spend it. He wanted the crust, and wanted the pot handle two inches beyond his hand, and wanted a number spoken by somebody who would have known what a number counted; he got none of the three, and the wanting of them went on without hope in it and without end.

Under the floor the carried fire gave four low pulls and kept the silence where the fifth should have closed, its payer without a name for two hundred and one days. Through the finger’s-width door the river kept its note of water over water, a fifth low thing that had never been counted among the hearings and had never been asked to stand up and be one; it went on with its note the way water goes on over water, with no regard for the ear under the roof or for the hand lying still against the fold. Overhead the boards kept four nails, four on the next board, and a knot the width of a thumb where the fifth nail should have been. Three counts in three places: four pulls and a gap under the floor, a fifth low note through the door, four nails and a knot in the boards above his head. They did not become one. A man who wanted them to agree would have had to put a payer under all three and no payer stood to any of them, and the boy kept his hand between them such hand as he had. He had learned early that a row of like things left standing long enough will call itself an account, and an account will want a figure, and a figure will want somebody to have paid it; he had spent two hundred and one days refusing to supply one, which was not a power and not a conclusion but only the order in which he laid the things down. The fire went on missing its fifth, the river went on keeping its fifth, the boards went on keeping their four and their knot, and the morning came up over Low Kiln without reconciling any of them.

Sella Vail came in from the Clay Yard with the day’s dust held in her apron and gave the instruction before she gave it to anybody, and it went to the wool, the fire, the pot, the broom, the running water and the river wind, and never to the basket. The basket was a pot that had been filled and set down, and a pot set down with no instruction given to it is a pot left where it stands. She kept her half-pace from every open slate as she came under the roof, the same half-pace she had kept going up in the sun and would keep coming back in the dark, so that no slate could take a figure off her passing and no figure could take a name off the child in the basket. At the loaf shelf she stood a moment with her back to the basket and moved her lips along a row that stood with two unnamed gaps, the old space at the row’s end and the palm’s breadth that had come in on the night of Seed 6, and gave nobody a figure. Her eye went once to the clay jar cold under its glaze on the shelf by the wall, and her hand started toward it and shut half over nothing without a touch, the little-door burn closing on air as it closed on whatever came within reach of it, and then the hand went back to the row. She pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carried the burn, so that the grey streak at her temple showed pale off the coals, and did not discuss the streak or the winter it had come in. The household straightened around the quiet of her as cloth straightens when a weight is lifted; nobody had been told what the grey was and nobody asked.

At the hearth she told the fire it was the thaw, and turned the coals with the back of a broken pot, and said The house will keep it where another woman would have said I. Then she stood with the back of the pot in her hand and counted under her breath toward no opening — the small intake, the hold, the letting go — and the count arrived nowhere, as it had arrived nowhere on every other morning of the boy’s life, and the house kept its warmth without once saying who had paid for it. She fed him barley gruel from the house’s own dish, two fingers behind his ear, with the spoon wiped on her sleeve and not on his mouth; the little-door burn shut on the dish’s edge and opened again empty, and the corner of the mouth she had fed went on carrying its line of gruel, which she did not wipe. She set the pot rim out with its handle two inches beyond the reaching hand, and let her hand rest a half-beat longer at the setting than the setting needed, and did not look toward the bed. She never looked toward the bed at the setting. The two inches between the handle and the hand were not a distance anybody in this house had been asked to measure, and the hand lying outside the wool on the fold did not close them. the widow’s mark hung on its cord at her throat where it had hung since the first firing, touched once before the kiln on Ember 1, held one breath and let go, and at no shelf, no hearth, no well, no rack, no basket and no bed since. A morning was not an opening and a shelf was not an opening; there had been no opening since before the kiln on Ember 1, and nobody under any roof had praised what the one opening had mended or seen what it had broken.

A neighbour came to the threshold with dry mud-scurf on her hem, the sort of scurf that comes off a yard that has been walked dry and not yet watered, and stood with her hands tucked into her apron. “What will the firing want now the last tend’s in?” she asked, and asked it of no one in particular, which in this district is the way a cost-question is asked when the asker does not want to be the one who owns it. Sella did not turn from the pot. “Ask the pot,” she said. “Ask the weather. The pot knows what it wants and the weather knows what it’ll take, and the house has said it.” The neighbour stood a moment with the question still in her mouth, the way a woman stands with a cup she has not been given a place to set; then she took the answer away level, with nothing priced in it, nothing refused and nothing entered, and went off down the step with her dry hem and her unspent figure. She had not been told a number and had not been denied one; she had been sent to the pot and the weather, and a cost sent in that direction arrives nowhere and stays nobody’s. The surgeon’s register in him marked the shape of it without approving it: a cost asked as a direction is an answer from which no payer can be read, and a household that keeps its warmth by never pricing anything has a way of staying warm that no slate can enter. He had spent years in a room where every cost was entered before the dressing went on and every hand was counted at the end, and here the whole district did its arithmetic in loaves and glances and went on warm without once writing down who had paid. It was a method and it worked, and he did not call it honest and did not call it a lie; he kept it on its own place, as he kept everything, with his hand between it and the next thing. Up the clay road and out of hearing, Berr Clay’s box had gone locked toward High Kiln with the true subtraction inside it, and Hessa Rale’s narrow slate lay unread at Greybank with the writing turned to her sleeve; the printed figure stood at one in twelve and had not been challenged under any roof, and a figure read twice over a basket was still not the figure paid. None of it came through the finger’s-width door or under the Vail roof, and the boy did not lay the neighbour’s level going-away beside any of it.

Before first grey, three or four men came up the yard with their collars high and went low into the finger’s-width-open the back room off the Vail wheel-shed, and the house gave them no name and did not say the room was cold. The boy heard the small dry sounds of their going — a boot set down carefully on the stone outside the shed, the brush of a sleeve against the jamb, the low placing of voices in a room where voices must be placed quietly — and heard them as a man hears a door shut in a room he cannot enter: the sound had a direction and no interior. The door kept its finger’s width of open after them. Two tellings of a counting room shut in the war, with the men inside dead of the breath, had come into this district in other years, one on Ilmen 26 and one on Silt 25, and a third voice at the bucket-round had only mentioned such a room; none had been joined to this room and no third telling came now. The men went in and the morning went on, and no word under the roof said what they were or why they had come before the light.

Back under the roof the boy lay with the fold at his ear and the crust two inches from it, and put the question to his hand. He did not put it aloud; there was no need and no ear in the house to hear a question if he had had the voice to make one. The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water through the finger’s-width door, went up as it had gone up on other mornings and in other small hours, and came down. It came down at once, with no holding in it at all, down against the fold before the small intake at the top of a breath could have been counted and before any hold had begun. The asking was not always answered; this was one of the mornings when it was not, and there was no failure in the refusal and no success in the asking, only the inch that had gone up and the inch that had come down. He filed it on a place of its own beside the others: beside the three held changes on their three places, the small dry scrape from the Debt Loft on a fourth, and the pale line at the gum of the man carried out on a fifth. It was not a hearing and not a seventh, not a stopping and not a payment, not one of the three held changes, not a key, not a power and not a rule; no payer was named to it and nothing was stood up out of it to call itself a sign. He still could not close the two inches to the pot handle, lift his chin the two inches to the crust, or speak a number that anybody under any roof had been asked to believe. The body humiliated him and moved by an inch in the same motion, and both were true, and the inch was worth exactly what an inch was worth in a district that kept its accounts in loaves and glances and the space where a loaf should have stood. The market did not get to read it. The hand came down against the fold, the fold took it without a word, and two hundred and one stood exact in the wool, complete and unspent, spoken by nobody under any roof. The carried fire missed its fifth, the river kept its fifth, the boards kept their four and their knot, and the last tend had opened as an ordinary morning with no boundary drawn under anything. The river went on with its note and did not stop for the inch that had gone up and come down without riding it for so much as the length of a breath.

The afternoon thinned without ending. Heat went off the high air first, where there was nothing to hold it, and came down the wall by degrees, and left the top face of the shelf, and stayed longest in the low places — under the shelf’s underside, behind the pot, in the fold of wool where the boy lay at the angle the board makes into a roof. That roof stopped at the edge of his reach. The heat came down its underside in a slow slant, thin as a finger laid along a board, and stopped at the same edge and lay there, going no further, as if it too had been given an instruction and had come to the end of it. He had felt a line hold that way in a body under his own hands, warmth climbing to a landmark and stopping at the landmark, the edge exact and not argued with. He did not make a finding of it. A finding was a thing a man could enter, and nothing under this roof was entered.

She moved her lips along the row without sound, the way a woman reads a line she knows by heart and does not need to see, and the row stood with two unnamed gaps: the old space at the row’s end, and the palm’s breadth that had come in on the night of Seed 6. She gave nobody a figure. Her eye went once to the clay jar cold under its glaze on the shelf by the wall, and her hand started toward it and shut half over nothing without a touch, the little-door burn closing on air the way it closes on whatever comes within reach of it, and then the hand went back to the row. She pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carried the burn, so that the grey streak at her temple showed pale in the low light, and did not discuss the streak or the winter it had come in. The instruction went to the fire and not to the boy, as all instructions in this house went to the wool, the pot, the broom, the running water and the river wind, and never to the basket; the basket was a pot that had been filled and set down, and a pot set down with no instruction given to it is a pot left where it stands. Then she stood with the back of the pot in her hand and counted under her breath toward no opening — the small intake, the hold, the letting go — and the count arrived nowhere, as it had arrived nowhere on every other afternoon of the boy’s life, and the house kept its warmth without once saying who had paid for it. She set the pot rim out with its handle two inches beyond the reaching hand and let her hand rest a half-beat longer at the setting than the setting needed, and did not look toward the bed. She never looked toward the bed at the setting. The two inches were not a distance anybody under this roof had been asked to measure, and the hand lying outside the wool on the fold did not close them. The widow’s mark hung on its cord at her throat where it had hung since the first firing, touched once before the kiln on Ember 1, held one breath, let go, and at no shelf, hearth, well, rack, basket, yard, afternoon or dusk since. An afternoon was not an opening and a shelf was not an opening; there had been no opening since before the kiln on Ember 1, and nobody under any roof had praised what the one opening had mended or seen what it had broken.

At the kindling the small knife lay at the very edge of its sill where a man could put a thumb and find it where it should be. Six years stood in the worn thumb-mark, in the place where the house could check; the seventh stood at the lip, where no thumb of the house had gone. Garl Vail’s unforged collector sentence lay beside it — the one about a man sent for a collector who turned back at the frozen ford — kept where the seventh year was kept and entered in nothing. Tarn came up with the broom and took it only after the broom had been given its instruction, as a man takes up a tool already named. He told the wood what he told it at every going and coming of the light. Six counted. The next I don’t count. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line, and no scrubbing lifted it; the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back. His chest had been tight since the dark, and at the top of the next reach the breath from Seed 16 stood unspent, with its small interval showing where a breath should have gone in and had not.

He said Yes, house to those who wanted the thaw early and to those who wanted it late, agreed so entirely that no argument could find a foot, and went on wanting the late one because a late thaw puts off the paying, though the paying had stood in the ruts since the water went. His stroke went over the flagstones and past the shallow bright patch without the half-check, and the stones kept the stopped place where the movement had once waited and was not given it again. Then he swept the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector, over and over, without once turning along it; no collector came up the road and no collector went down it. At the hearth, with the coals banked and the knife set back where it could be checked, the sentence about an ear under this roof being worth more than the debt that holds it came up to the word that would call the loan and stood there with no figure and no name. He did not say it. He drew the breath he should have spent, did not spend it, did not call the loan, and stood no tenth stopping up before the seventh year was earned. The seventh year stood nearer its end than it had ever stood and had not given; nine stoppings stood as nine objects on their tray, with no tenth among them and no payer named to any, and the loan that could hear was still uncalled.

Through the finger’s-width door of the Vail wheel-shed the wheel went on turning, and Jorra Vail was at it with his bent little finger leading. Three or four men had gone low into the back room before first grey and the house gave them no name and did not say the room was cold. Two tellings of a counting room shut in the war stood where they had always stood, one with a date and one with a date, and no third came now to join them to the door off the shed; the boy kept the two apart and supplied nothing from anything he had heard under this roof. He came through with fine grey slate dust ground small as meal into both forearms past the bend of the little finger and under the nails, not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel; he parted dust from slip the way a surgeon parts clot from vessel and kept dusty forearms and clean ones on one tray, asking neither which slate had been broken nor whether it was a birth-slate. The broken birth-slates lay under the lowest rack where the yard went around them and entered them into nothing. He spoke to the wheel before he spoke to anyone. It doesn’t care whose hands. It cares if they’re steady. He told a pot You’ll do in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either, and knew the full note the pot would give three turns before it left his hands. A man come up on a wrong wind said that a pot singing before its time generally sings itself out early; Jorra kept the knowing back and gave him nothing, and the man took the silence for the silence of a man at work and went off up the yard with his saying, which stayed a saying about a pot and was not made true as a lesson. No pot was lost.

When Sella’s voice went low and unfinished at the fire — The fire’s low. It wants— — his bent finger went into the cold iron of the damper ahead of his hand and held there until the sentence reached feeding and the end was given to the pot. Then the tongue moved a hair’s breadth and the finger was left standing in the iron, cold taking the cold out of the fingertip. It was a remembered repetition, the same holding as on other afternoons, not a change made again, not paired with the other three places; nobody spoke of the waiting and no payer was named to it. He read the iron as one piece with two bites, the holding and the taking, and there was no word in Grathic that would carry both mercies in one mouthful; it was joined to no kiln and entered in no slate, a clamp a clamp and not a doctrine. He did not look toward the basket or the bed, did not ask about the ear while Tarn did not ask about the room, and he’s not Vail stayed sealed as tightly as a lid nobody had tried to lift in three years. The promise of the boy’s name to the men who burn slates was neither given nor withdrawn.

At the going of the light she carried him home up the clay road at the half-pace, with the instruction going to the wool and the river wind and never to the basket: keep it off his ears, it’s the river wind. Woodsmoke went before her in long low bars with the last of the sun in it; the river wind came behind with the taste of water over water, and the two passed without taking each other’s colour. The arm that had carried him two hundred and one mornings kept the height of the handle, the give of the shoulder, the small turn of the wrist at a step’s end — a record in muscle that remembered weight and not cost, and would not have known a figure if one had been set in front of it. She went wide around the pan’s gone round ring, the bare dark trough whose water was called weather, the rack space with its broken birth-slates, the field’s empty place and the place the carriers crossed, each with a look and a wide step and no number. The yard made room for these absences the way a body makes room for an old injury, by going around it and never testing it.

Under the roof she set him on the bed and not in the Debt Loft, put the fold right without looking at his face and left the thread at his mouth frozen and unwiped. The two rooms did not touch. Through the finger’s-width door the river kept its fifth low note; under the floor the carried fire gave four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer without a name for two hundred and one days; overhead the boards kept four nails, four, and a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts in three places that did not become one, because a man who wanted them to agree would have had to put a payer under all three and no payer stood to any of them.

In the small hours he turned out the file the way a man lays instruments on a towel — no towel, no instruments and no case, but the order of the laying the whole of the discipline — and found it exact, complete and worth nothing. Six hearings of the full note stood beside and never among: five from four sources, and the sixth, the small flat knock out of a person as the men lifted her, filed where a thing out of a person must go and never among the sources. No seventh had come in two hundred and one days and none was invented to fill the empty place, for an empty place kept empty is a finding and a finding filled to make a row come out even is a finding forged. Three near-full answers stood advanced and unpaid. The told bell lay beside the heard, four, four and a gap with nobody crossing himself; the told figure lay beside the paid, one in twelve read twice over a basket and still not the figure paid, the box gone up the road locked with the true subtraction inside and the runner’s slate unread at Greybank with the writing to her sleeve. There was no hand in this house to spend any of it. A reading of a body at two paces is exact, complete and worth nothing without a hand to carry it; he had made such readings for years and the finding had never once walked out of a room by itself. The file lay under a roof the market could not enter, and whatever was kept there stayed kept.

The crust’s gap, the shelf’s two gaps, the pan’s gone round ring and the field’s empty place lay on trays that did not touch — no third gap, no fourth likeness made a cause, no finger of his drawing the line between them. He kept the six places where they lay and would not let them stand up together and call themselves a rule, because a rule is an account that has found a payer and no payer stands to any of them. The fold took the hand without a word. The river went on with its note and did not stop for the inch, and two hundred and one stood exact in the wool, complete and unspent, spoken by nobody, and the count went forward into Ember 23.

— Day 202 —

Ember 23, Y.H. 813 (Thirdday)

The dark under the Vail kiln-house roof had the same shape it had worn the night before, the shape of a pot kept under ash: the heat down in the low places, the cold at the edges, and no hand anywhere to say which of the two had been given an instruction. In Low Kiln the kiln month’s last working week went on into its third day without a boundary drawn under the second, and the boy came into his two hundred and second morning at six months and twenty-two days, and nobody under any roof said either number. He had stopped expecting to be told. The wanting was still there, but it had gone down into the body with the hunger and sat there without a word, the way a tool put aside at dusk sits where it was set until somebody reaches for it in the morning; the tool does not ask to be reached for, and the wanting did not ask to be counted.

The first smell was hot clay under tallow and wet wool under that, and beneath all of it the sour mineral breath of the unswept corner, which the house called the thaw and had called it so for a year without lifting a broom to it. The first sound was the kiln drawing in fours with a gap behind it: a brick breath taken, held, let go, and then the little silence where a fifth pull should have stood. Not a bell. Bells belonged to readings and to the district’s arithmetic; this was a kiln doing what a kiln does, and the boy had learned to tell the two apart before he had learned to hold up his head. Ash had gathered in the corner in a clean dark line with a finer hair of grey beside it, and the two lay together without either becoming the other, as the day’s two rooms lay together without touching.

Under the floor the carried fire gave four low pulls and kept the silence where the fifth should have closed, its payer without a name for two hundred and two days. Through the finger’s-width door the river kept its note of water over water, a fifth low thing that had never been counted among the hearings and had never been asked to stand up and be one; it went on with its note the way water goes on over water, with no regard for the ear under the roof or for the hand lying still against the fold. Overhead the boards kept four nails, four on the next board, and a knot the width of a thumb where the fifth nail should have been. Three counts in three places. They did not become one. A man who wanted them to agree would have had to put a payer under all three, and no payer stood to any of them; he had spent two hundred and two days refusing to supply one, which was not a power and not a conclusion but only the order in which he laid the things down. The crust lay leathery two inches from his ear with its empty place beside it unnamed and no third, the thread of drool frozen and unwiped because nobody under this roof had been given an instruction about the thread, cold stopped at cheek, temple and the small bare joint at the wrist where the wool rode back, and the afterdrop waited on the far side of a warming nobody in this house would perform.

Before first grey a wrong wind came up off High Kiln and brought the sweet edge of lead glaze with it — a thin sugary taste at the back of the throat, the sweetness that sits under a thing gone wrong rather than on top of it. It came over the yard, went under the door, touched the fold where the boy lay, and went out again on the same wind, leaving nothing behind it that any hand could take up. The district called such a wind a direction when it came and called it nothing at all when it went; he had learned to read the direction of a thing before he read its meaning, because the meaning was usually supplied by somebody with a slate and a reason. He read this one as a direction and entered it nowhere.

The saying about a pot singing before its time had come up the yard on such a wind on other mornings and would go out on such a wind again. A man who said it meant no more by it than a man means who says a wet thing will cost more in the thaw: it was a saying about a pot, and the pot in question had gone on being a pot. He did not make the saying true of a person, because a saying made true of a person is a cause with a payer standing behind it, and no payer stood here. The sweet edge went out on the wind it had come on and the yard went on with its work, and nothing ailed anybody that the yard was prepared to name.
