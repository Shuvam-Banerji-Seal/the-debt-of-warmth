Chapter

8

The Five Cold Days

Days 25–30 · Kindling–Ilmen 1, Y.H. 812–813 · The Vail kiln-house, Low Kiln, Sill

One fire goes from door to door and every house says it kept it. The grey is burned in the morning and called gone. The name nobody spoke at the Remembering is the only thing that stays put.

— A saying of the Low Kiln yards, Sill

— Day 25 —

Kindling, Y.H. 812

Before the light had cleared the eaves the clay dish came up the Clay Yard with a coal under ash, and nobody said where it had been lit or what it had cost to carry it. It went from hearth to hearth the way a loaf goes round a table when there are more mouths than crusts and everybody must be seen to have had a share. At the first door a woman touched her own fire to it and gave it on. At the next a man held a coal in his cupped fingers, put it under the ash, and did not look at the hand that took the dish away. “Keep it buried,” a woman said, and the words went down the yard ahead of the dish, practical and unkind and ordinary, the sort of thing said to a pot and not to a person. The yard was giving its fire in thimblefuls, each house a little of what it kept, so that what came up the yard was not any one hearth’s flame but all of them gathered into the space a thumb could cover. It was Kindling, the first of the Five, and the year’s fire was being carried through Low Kiln in the dark. Cold ash clung to the rim of the dish, the same grey as the Ash ridge under the skirting board, and he had learned by now not to join two things because they had a colour in common. Woodsmoke lay low under the eaves. The thatch had a skin of frost on it, and the wind off the Slip Brook came under the doors with the smell of wet wool and tallow.

Sella Vail carried Corin to the threshold in the basket with the fold at his chin, twenty-five days old and going where he was taken. She set him on the sill, where the door stood a finger’s width open and the smoke went out through the gap instead of up, and went to the kiln. From where he lay he could see the boards of the floor and the lower half of the yard and nothing at all of the hands that brought the dish the last few paces. He heard the ash in it shift, a dry small sound like a cough stifled, and felt the stir of the woman who handed it over and the woman who took it, two bodies turning in the dark with a thing between them that neither would name. He could not see into the dish. He could only know that it held something the yard had gone to the trouble of gathering, and that the trouble was ordinary enough that nobody had made a word of it. The fire had come into the house from along the yard, from a door he had never been carried through, from hands that had not once thought to tell him what they were carrying. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound it made when it went too far, and nobody wiped his mouth. Cold came in at cheek, then temple, then wrist, in the order he knew and could not argue with, and the child in him wanted the breast and the crust and the surgeon in him noted the order and made nothing of it.

Before she opened the kiln her hand went to the widow’s mark at her throat, the thumb’s length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it. She held it the space of one breath and let it go. The little-door burn across the back of her hand opened on the iron as she reached for the lid and shut on nothing when the lid was up. She tipped the dish and the coal went in under the fire and the fire took it without a sound, the way a mouth takes a thing it has been waiting for. The dish went back out of the door with darker ash in it than had come up, and nobody looked at the difference. The rule of that hand was exact as a stitch: before the opening and at no other hour, not at a bank, not at a reading, not at a dusk. He had watched it at every opening he had been carried to, and the order never changed — hand to throat, hand to lid, hand to fire — which meant the thing it guarded had not changed either and would not while the house stood. A thing kept that exactly had a reason, and the reason was a thing the house could not say in front of a man from Slate Row. He had no word to ask it with and no standing to ask it even if he had.

The light came up off the fire and went along the wall and into the basket, and he counted it because counting was the one thing the body he was in had left him. Four, and four, and a place where a fifth would be if there were a fifth. Not a gap this time — a place, an empty shelf where a thing could have stood — and he set it on the tray beside the interval of the pot turned toward on the bench that had given nothing, and kept the two trays from touching. The bench’s interval lay on one tray, the half-heard note past the door on another, the cracked pot in the yard on a third; the empty shelf went beside the first because it was nearest in shape, not because it was the same thing. He had learned that nearness was not sameness in a better-lit room and the lesson had never once been convenient. It was not a hearing. The ear that held five hearings of the full note from four sources added no sixth, and there was no hand in the basket to make one with even if a sixth had been there to be made. A place where a fifth would be was an absence with the shape of a presence, and absences were the only things in this house that could be kept without anybody entering them. There was no word in Grathic for the empty shelf, and if there had been he could not have said it.

He had known heat moved from vessel to vessel once, in a room with white light and a hand of his own that did not shake, and he had known who paid for the warmth and what it was for. That room was gone and the hand with it. Here the hand that carried the coal belonged to a neighbour whose name he would not be told, the vessel was a dish of clay, and nobody under the roof had a word for what had been carried or a thought to spend on where it had been before it came through the door. He wanted to ask who kept the yard warm enough to give fire away at the end of a Long Cold, and he wanted it with the whole of a man’s curiosity and had the throat of a thing that could only gurgle to ask it with. He was the only person in the house who knew that warmth had been moved, and the only one with less standing than the dish to ask who had paid to move it. The household took the gift as it took the weather or the bread: it was there, it was ordinary, it would be there in the morning. Nobody entered its origin and nobody entered its payer. The joke of it had been old in the other life and was new again in this one, and it was funny in the way a wound is funny when you have stopped expecting it to heal.

Somewhere out in the yard a door shut, and then another, and a woman came back into the room with her fist closed around nothing and did not open it. The first measure of the year had been taken while he lay on the threshold with the fold at his chin, and no figure for it was spoken under the roof. She set the shut fist down on the bench as though it were a cup and went to the tubs, and the room went on with its work as though nothing at all had been carried past the door. He learned it the way he learned most of what the house knew — from the order of a door closing and a fist shut, and from the way a mouth thought better of its sentence when it came near the basket. He could not spend what he had not been told, could not correct it, could not hand it back. The fist stayed shut through the pot and the fire, and the measure went where it had been entered. A number that never reached him was still a number, and the district had already entered it somewhere he would never be taken.

The work went on around the basket as though a fire had not come into the house at all. Jorra Vail came in from the Vail wheel-shed with slip dried to the colour of old bone up both forearms and the bent little finger of his left hand leading the rest of him, and told a pot on the bench that the shoulder was thick and he would thin it. “You’ll do,” he said to another pot, in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either, and told the wheel it did not care whose hands were on it, only if they were steady, and did not say the word anyone could use against him for the back room, which kept its cold the way it had kept it all through Stone. He went to the damper before Sella had finished telling the fire it was low, going in with the bent finger ahead of his hand and leaving the end of her sentence where it was. The wheel-shed smelled of damp clay and tallow and the sweet edge of glaze that came up on a bad wind from High Kiln. The full note was in a Vail pot before it left the wheel and he would not have said so in front of a kiln-master, who would have called it early and made something of it; the boy knew this about him the way he knew the bent finger, and kept both where questions can last for years. He could not reach the wheel and had no way to ask why Jorra never looked toward the bed.

Tarn came down from the Debt Loft with Ash in the creases of his knuckles that no scrubbing lifted and the pale clip-ring plain at his left wrist, and set the small bench knife on its sill with the care of a man putting down a thing he can be asked about. “It’s on the sill,” he said to the yard, which had nobody in it but the basket. “You can check.” He looked at the kindling stack where four bundles stood and four and a space where a fifth should have been, and took his eyes off it as a man takes his eyes off a woman’s face when she has been crying. “Six counted. The next I don’t count.” Six years of the seven were behind him and counted every one; the seventh he held back, the one entry in this house the house could not check. “Yes, house,” he said to the ground, and swept where he had already swept, because an argument has nowhere to put its foot when a man agrees with everything. Behind the door Garl Vail’s coat kept its salt shape on its peg and his seat in the shed kept its hollow on the left, and the ridge along the skirting board ran to the corner and turned it without a break, unswept and not to be swept until the Last Day. The charred edge of a slate lay in the kiln ash black on one side and eaten into the shape of a strip of bark, swept around for a year and never picked up. Garl’s proverb went to the fire and the pot and never to the basket: leave the crack showing; people trust a thing they can see has been handled.

At the bank Sella fed him barley gruel thinned with milk from the house’s own dish, holding his head with two fingers behind the ear and wiping the spoon on her sleeve between the dish and his mouth. The gruel was thin and warm and went down the way warmth goes down in a body that was learning the cold; he took it because it was given and not because he could ask for it. “The house keeps it,” she said, which was as near as she ever came to saying she had kept it herself; and to the fire, “Feed it before you feed yourself,” in Garl’s voice improved by twenty years of use, and then to the wool, not to the basket: keep it off his ears, it’s the river wind. The wind came off the Ilm, which still held under its ice, and there was no occasion to look at the river and no way to reach it. In the fold at his side the two crusts lay where they had been put and neither was eaten, lifted or named — one stiff since Stone 15, brown-edged and gone past smelling of anything, the other still giving a faint breath of barley smoke when the wind turned. She put the fold right at his ear without looking at his face and did not wipe the drool at his mouth. A gift that could not be answered was a gift that could not be refused, and this one had been given twice, once by a woman who would not say she knew him and once by a house that would not say it had taken him in for anything but the keeping.

At dusk she banked the coals with the back of a broken pot and did not touch the mark, because a bank was not an opening and the rule held as exactly at the end of the day as it had before the lid that morning. The little-door burn shut on nothing all the way from the fire to the bed. Nobody had spoken to the basket all day; the door had been shut a finger’s width against the draught and the day had gone over the yard with the dish and the fist and the knife on its sill, and not one person under the roof had said a word to the thing lying in the fold. The coat kept its salt shape, the wheel-seat its hollow, the ridge its unbroken corner, and the debt against a firing of tiles lay where it lay, neither paid nor settled nor done with. The year’s first measure sat in a fist that had gone back to its work and the fire sat under ash that would be there in the morning, and neither had been spoken of to him. The kiln took its breath under the roof. Four, and four, and a gap.

He lay on his bed with the two crusts in the fold at his side and the new fire somewhere in the ash under the pot, and his hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth and came down on the fold. The surgeon who had once reduced a shoulder in the dark could not lift a crust to his own mouth. The coals hissed once and held. The fire kept four, and four, and a gap.

— Day 26 —

Weighing, Y.H. 812

The clean voice came across the yard ahead of the man who owned it, over the wet boards and the frost lying on the thatch, and it carried nothing of the yard in it. No shouting, no pig noise, no argument about the weather. It went on at the level of a spoon set down on a table, and the yard leaned toward it the way a yard leans toward any man who has a slate and the law behind his hand. Sella Vail carried Corin down in the basket with the fold at his chin, twenty-six days old and going where he was taken, and she kept her half-pace from every open slate the whole way to the fringe of the Cross, as if the slates were sleeping animals and a near step would wake them.

She set him where the reading carried and no slate lay open, at the outer edge of the boards where Slate Row begins to be spoken of as a direction, and turned the basket with her knee so his face was toward the voice and not toward the writing. Her little-door burn shut on nothing as the weight came off her hand. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool, not to the basket; “it’s the river wind.” She did not look toward his face. She never did, on a day when a voice had a slate behind it. The looking was a kind of entering, and the house had decided long ago what it would and would not enter. It was Weighing, the second of the Five, and the year’s measures were being read aloud in a voice that had been washed and mended and made to fit a room.

He had heard the like before, in a market, in a man’s low proverbs with the green of tasted glaze at his mouth. What came over the boards now was the same grammar wearing a temple’s clothes. “The measure was banked,” the officer said, and went on without a breath for the yard to take it in: “the account stands as entered; the return was recorded; nothing is lost that we have kept.” The sentences were clean of the man who spoke them. He had taken himself out of his own sayings as neatly as a surgeon takes a hand out of a field, leaving the work done and no hand in it. It was not the shouting of a market and not the proverb of a kiln-master. It was a reading, and a reading does not need anybody to agree with it.

The surgeon in him had once sat across a table from a man who reported a death in just such a voice, with the note turned down and the chart closed over the thing it had omitted, and had heard a room accept it because the voice was level and the handwriting neat. The memory came in the old tense and went out again; there was no break, no white light over the boards, only the wet wool at his ear and a man’s clean sentences arriving one after another. He had performed that report himself, once, with a signature at the end of it that made a thing done look like a thing decided. Berr Clay had spoken the district’s grammar at the Cross through a tend of Account-days and the boy had learned to hear the man disappear inside it. Here the disappearance had been trained. It had a uniform, a cadence and a room behind it, and it did not need to lower its voice to make two hundred people lean in, because the leaning had been done for it years ago and entered somewhere.

Cold came at cheek, then temple, then wrist, in the order he knew and could not argue with. The drool at his mouth went cold and stayed. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound it made when it went too far, and nobody put it right while the reading was on. He counted the officer’s sentences because counting was the one faculty the body he was in had left intact: four measures named, four named, and a place where a closing line should have stood and did not. He set the missing line on the tray with the bench’s interval, because it was nearest in shape, and kept it from the five hearings of the full note as he kept all intervals from hearings. The count stayed five from four sources. A voice could be clean and still leave a space; he had known that in a better-lit room, where the space had been the only honest part of the report.

Then the voice altered, not in tone but in what it was made to carry. A whole return was set down in the air over the boards for a person who was not in the yard. The officer did not raise his voice to announce it; he had no need to, because a whole return is a thing a room receives without being told to receive it. A figure stood in the sentence, and the name of the person it belonged to stood beside the figure, and the yard took both in the way a dry board takes rain, without comment and without giving anything back. The boy heard the figure and could not spend it. He could not correct it, could not hand it back, could not ask whose it was or what had been taken to pay for it. It was somebody else’s wholeness, read out over a basket that had no name of its own entered anywhere he could reach. The name that came with it was a name the household would not repeat after the reading, and he knew this before anybody under the roof had opened a mouth, because he had learned the shape of a name that gets gone around by not being said.

The officer finished the sentence and left a space at the end of it, the space that in the Vail kiln was the missing breath between four and four. The yard went on with its own business around that space. Women shifted on their feet; a man coughed into his hand; a gate somewhere at the back of the Cross went on its hinge and stopped. Nobody filled the space. The boy filled it the way he filled the kiln’s gap, not with a sound but with the counting of its length, one, two, three, and a place where the fourth would stand if the fourth had been given. He had counted the space after the bell on Stone 30 when nobody crossed himself; he had counted the space on the bench where a pot had been set down and given nothing; he had counted the space where a name should have stood under a roof where names were gone around by not being said. This space went on the same tray with those, among the gaps and the intervals, and not among the hearings. It was not a sixth hearing of the flat note. It was the silence after a name, which is a different object and must be kept in a different place, or a man ends by writing a cause where he has only noticed that two absences have the same shape. Five hearings from four sources. The count did not move.

At the edge of the reading, where the boards gave onto the path toward High Kiln, Berr Clay stood with the red wool cord of his office folded under his coat instead of worn across it, as a man folds a thing he has done with for the day. The green of tasted glaze showed at one corner of his mouth. His hands were large and clean, and he wiped his thumb twice on his apron before he touched the slate in his hand, the way he wiped it at the moot before he read a figure to two hundred people who had to lean in to hear him. He said nothing at all to anybody. He did not look toward the basket. He passed a finger over his slate, as if to make sure the figure he had put there was still where he had left it, and went off in the direction of his own house with his face arranged in the patient expression of a man who has done the district a kindness and expects no thanks for it. Whatever he kept locked at home he left locked there, and the boy had no way to reach it and no word with which to ask. He filed the clean hands and the folded cord on a tray of their own, unjoined to the voice and unjoined to the clerk’s soft cuff at the market’s edge, because a man who reads a figure and a man who enters one may be the same man or two men, and the boy had learned the cost of deciding such things from a better-lit room.

The household did not repeat the name. Sella stood a half-pace off with her burn shut on nothing and her hands busy at the fold, and when a neighbour came back from the reading and said something low about a whole return having been announced, she answered with the weather and the wind off the Ilm, which still held under its ice. The name went off the yard with the reading and was not picked up again. Garl Vail’s proverb went to a pot and the fire and not to the basket: leave the crack showing; people trust a thing they can see has been handled. The coat kept its salt shape behind the door of the Vail kiln-house, the wheel-seat its hollow on the left, and the ridge along the skirting board its corner turning without a break, unswept and left for the Last Day. Nobody said whose return had been read out. Nobody said who was absent. The boy lay with the name-shaped silence in his mouth the way other men carry a bad taste, and counted it, and would not let it join anything.

The reading did not stay in the yard. When the officer had done with the figures he went with them through a door, and the voice went in ahead of him, and the boy heard the last of it from where he lay on the outer boards. He was not carried through that door. He learned the Court’s room the way he had learned most of the rooms in this country — by a voice going in through its door and by what other people had said of it when they thought no basket was near. It had a bench by the door, from which the slate was read; a pot set on the bench; a draught under the door going along the floor to the corner, felt at the ankles before the cold was felt anywhere else. Those things had been described to the yard on Stone 19 by a neighbour just come back from the room, and the description had lain in the boy’s private file ever since with no cause written on it. Now the voice going through the doorway gave the room its own sound, and with the sound came the other thing the neighbour had reported: a place in the figures where a line should stand and does not, the same every time. He set the room down in its own place beside the birth room in House Silt, beside the Vail kiln-house, beside the death-house two doors down where the ridge was a finger’s width thicker. Four rooms. The same bench, the same pot, the same draught going to the corner, the same place where a line should stand. He kept the four likenesses in four separate places, because a likeness laid on a likeness will grow a cause whether or not anyone put one there, and he had made that mistake once in a better-lit room and carried the correction for the rest of a life. He wrote no cause. He only set the room beside the others and left it there, the way a man lays four tools in a row and does not yet decide what they are for.

Sella lifted the basket off the boards in the late afternoon and carried him up the yard with woodsmoke and river wind caught under the fold. Nobody had spoken to the basket all day. At the door she did not go to the kiln, and because there was no opening there was no touch of the widow’s mark; the hand that had touched it before the first light of Kindling kept its rule as exactly on Weighing as it had on any Account-day, and the little-door burn shut on nothing all the way from the yard to the bed. She put the fold right at his ear without looking at his face, and gave the instruction to the fire and the wool, and said nothing about a whole return read out for somebody who was not there. In the fold at his side the two crusts lay where they had been put — one stiff since Stone 15 and gone past smelling of anything, the other still giving a faint breath of barley smoke when the wind turned — and neither was eaten, lifted or named, and no third crust was added. She fed him barley gruel from the house’s own dish with the spoon wiped on her sleeve and said “the house” where another woman would say I, and the warmth went in entered nowhere.

Tarn came down from the Debt Loft with Ash in the creases of his knuckles and the small bench knife back on its sill where it could be checked. “It’s on the sill,” he said to the yard, which had nobody in it but the basket. “You can check.” He looked at the kindling stack where four bundles stood and four and a space where a fifth should have been, and took his eyes off it. “Six counted. The next I don’t count.” Six years of the seven were behind him and counted every one; the seventh he held back, the one entry in this house the house could not check. “Yes, house,” he said to the ground, and swept where he had already swept. Behind him the wheel-seat kept its hollow and the salt coat its shape, and the proverb went to the pot and the fire and never to the basket.

The silence after the name went off the yard and came under the roof with the boy and lay down beside him on the bed. He counted it the way he counted the kiln’s missing breath, and filed it with the intervals and not with the hearings, and the five hearings from four sources stayed five. The officer’s clean sentences had left no figure he could spend and no name he could hand back, and the room behind the voice had the bench, the pot, the draught and the gap that the other three rooms had, and he had written no cause in any of them. At the bank Sella turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and did not touch the mark, because a bank was not an opening. The fire kept four, and four, and a gap. He lay with the two crusts in the fold and his hand outside the wool going up an inch toward the warmth and coming down, and nobody under the roof had said whose return had been announced, or who had been the person not in the room.

— Day 27 —

the Long Night, Y.H. 812

The short day came in under a sky the colour of a banked coal, and nothing was carried up the Clay Yard in a clay dish. The fire that had come into the house on Kindling lay where it had been put, under the back of a broken pot, and the coals kept their own counsel. Corin woke as he had gone to sleep, carried, with the fold at his chin and the two crusts at his side — one stiff past the smell of anything, one still giving a thin breath of barley smoke when the wind turned. Twenty-seven days old and going where he was taken. He could not ask the name of the day; he had learned the names of days the way a man learns a room, by the draught under its door and the order in which the people in it moved. This one was the Long Night, the third of the Five, and the house had already begun to arrange itself around the absence of a flame.

Under the Vail kiln-house roof the work went on in the ordinary order, which is the order that needs no words. Sella Vail dried what had been wetted and mended what had been torn, and her hands counted when they had nothing to hold — spoons, nails, the breaths of the child in the basket — because a hand that keeps a fire keeps the account of small things. The kiln was not opened that day, and the hand at her throat stayed where it was. She had touched the widow’s mark before the opening on Kindling and would touch it again only before the next; a day that did not ask the kiln to open asked nothing of the mark. Her little-door burn shut on nothing when her hands were empty and shut on cloth when they were full, and she gave the instruction to the wool and not to the basket: “Keep it off his ears. It’s the river wind.”

Tarn swept the yard before the light cleared the sill and swept it again when the light had, the same work twice and entered nowhere. The small knife went back to its sill and he told the yard where it was, unasked, the way another man states the weather. Six years of the seven were behind him and counted; the seventh he held back, the one entry in this house the house could not check. His broom checked half a stroke at the place on the bench where a pot had once given nothing, and went on. He did not turn his head. He had turned it a degree once, on a day when a vessel went past the door, and had said nothing then either, and the boy under the roof had kept that silence as carefully as he kept his own.

In the Vail wheel-shed Jorra Vail worked in slip the colour of old bone, dried to that colour up both forearms, the bent little finger of his left hand going in ahead of him at the damper. He had moved it before Sella had finished telling him the fire was low and had not explained why a low fire needed the damper moved first. He spoke to a pot and not to a person. “You’ll do.” The wheel did not care whose hands were on it, only if they were steady; he had said it so often the saying had worn smooth as the seat worn hollow on the left by twenty years of another man’s leaning. Of the room off the shed he said nothing, and the door of it kept its finger’s width of open, and the boy filed the not-saying away with the other absences that had shapes.

Cold set in early. The oiled paper lifted at the windows and the grey skin on the Slip Brook held under the wind without breaking, and women at the drying racks pulled their sleeves down over their knuckles and called it weather. It came at the child in its known order: cheek, then temple, then wrist. The surgeon in him noted how a room cools from the edges inward, boards and sill first, the middle holding longest, and how a small body goes at the extremities before the core will admit to it; the child in him wanted the breast and wanted the crust that was given, and wanted them with a louder voice than any measurement. The two registers quarrelled without a word, as they had quarrelled every cold day of his twenty-seven, and neither want was answered. He had known a room once in which a man pulled from cold water was wrapped too quickly and worsened before he settled, and had learned there to distrust the moment of rescue; here the rescue was a fold put right at his ear and a spoon wiped on a sleeve, and the worsening came on schedule, cheek then temple then wrist, with no one in the room thinking to call it anything but night. The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth of the fold and came down. It was a reach he could not make into a reach, and he had long ago stopped being surprised by the difference. The drool at his mouth went cold and stayed, and nobody wiped it, because a basket at the edge of a board is a pot set down and left.

❦

At dusk Sella damped the fire and did not relight it. She turned the coals with the back of the broken pot and said to the pot what another house might have said to a person: “Feed the fire before you feed yourself. It’ll still be there in the morning.” It was a saying that had belonged to Garl Vail and had improved in the keeping, and it went to the pot now, and not to the basket. The coat that had been his kept a broader man’s shape on its peg in the dark, and the wheel-seat in the shed kept its hollow, and the ridge of the Ash along the skirting board ran to the corner and turned it without a break, unswept and left for the Last Day. The house sat down in the dark the way a family sits down at a table, in its own places, with the fire out and nothing between the voices but air.

Alda Reed of House Reed was among them, with her grey wool shawl and her hands scrubbed to the quick; she smelled of soap and warm milk and of the wish to be somewhere else. She told the story of a child who had come back thin out of the Return, thin as a slipped knot, who had known warmth before he knew a face. “He knew the stone before he knew his mother’s hands,” she said. “He grew worse on the third day. So did the one before him. Stone seven, the account went up on the eighth, before the paper went out.” She went on giving dates after the question had stopped: a cooper’s seventh in Mud End, a potter’s third child in Stonemouth, the exact day of a birth in a house nobody at the fire would ever enter. Where the story should have opened into wonder she put a date down flat, the way she laid a slate flat on a bench, and covered the thing underneath it. The hearth-maid’s dates stood where wonder should have stood, and went on standing after there was no wonder left to ask about.

Tarn told his in the flat words he used for facts that had no feeling in them. “You can’t return a man to himself,” he said to the floor. “They tried with my mother. She came back with somebody else’s hands.” He did not say what had happened to the hands before they were hers; he did not need to, because everyone at the fire had heard the same shape of story and knew which part of it nobody finished. The boy listened to the space after the sentence the way he listened to the kiln between its breaths. A man had come back with another man’s hands, and the hands had gone on being counted as his mother’s because a slate had said so, and the space where a true account should have stood was left lying in the middle of the room for anybody to step around.

The boy lay with the voices going over him and counted the dark instead of the flames. There was no fire to count; the flame that had come in on Kindling was under ash and giving nothing to the room. So he counted what the telling left: the space between a question and its answer, the place where a name should have stood and did not, the point where a story stopped short and went on with dates instead. Three gaps.

A woman near the hearth had asked of the thin child, “And what did he call his mother?” and the answer had come two breaths later, and those two breaths lay in the dark between the question and the name that did not come with it. Someone else had begun a sentence with a man’s name in it and had left the name out and gone on to the weather, and that left a place the size of a word where the name should have stood. A third voice had been telling of a stone warmed for a cot and had stopped short at the part where the stone did not hold, and the story had gone on with a date instead of the thing that happened. Three gaps, all of them made by people who were kind and had not noticed they had made them.

He set all three on the tray with the bench’s interval, the half-heard note past the door and the pot that had cracked in the yard, and kept them from the five hearings of the full note from four sources. An interval was not a hearing; a gap in a story was the same kind of object as a gap in a vessel’s breath, and both were things left lying where someone had stopped. The count of hearings stayed five. It had stayed five through a market and a reading and a carrying of fire, and no sixth had been added by anybody in this room, and there was no hand in it that could add one.

In one telling of the year the bell came back. A woman whose man had been taken in a levy said it had gone four, four and a gap over the Cross, longer than any bell hung there, and that nobody had crossed himself when it did. The boy set that bell beside the bell he had heard under the moot-post’s thin shade and kept the two in two places. They had the same shape: four, four and a space where the closing stroke should have stood. A likeness laid on a likeness will grow a cause whether or not anyone put one there, and he had made that mistake once in a better-lit room and carried the correction for the rest of a life. The told bell stayed a told bell. The heard bell stayed a heard bell. The gap in each of them stayed a gap, and the two gaps did not touch.

He had once sat at a kitchen table in the life before this one while an older man told a story about a man who had died badly, and had noticed even then, with a fork in his hand and no reason to notice, that the story kept a space where the dead man’s name should have been. He had not known what the space was. He knew now what shape it was, and knew that shaping it and naming it were two different acts, and that only one of them was his to do. The old life came in that far and no farther: a table, a fork, a voice gone out years ago, and then the wet wool at his ear and the dark under the Vail roof and the two crusts where they had been put.

Tarn said in the dark that six were counted and the next he did not count, and set the knife where it could be checked. “It’s on the sill,” he said. “You can check.” The seventh year was the one entry in this house that the house could not check, and he held it back as he had held it back every night of the term, and the dark took the sentence without answering it. Jorra said nothing about the room off the shed. He shifted once where he sat, and the damper was where it should be, and he did not say it was cold. Garl’s proverb went to the pot again, not to the basket: leave the crack showing; people trust a thing they can see has been handled. The coat held the shape of a broader man in the dark, and no one took it down, and no one said the name that would have gone with it.

The Long Night ended without fire. It ended the way it had begun, with work in the front of it and voices at the back, and with the boy twenty-seven days old and carried everywhere. The house sat in the dark with the fire out and the boy counting the space between one voice and the next, cold come at his cheek and wrist, the two crusts lying where they had been put, neither eaten nor lifted nor named. the Remembering was tomorrow, and the dark kept its own account and gave no name away.

— Day 28 —

the Remembering, Y.H. 812

The names went along the Clay Yard before the light had cleared the sill, carried from door to door the way a coal is carried from one hearth to the next, each house taking the sound into its own mouth and giving it on a little changed. Corin heard them pass over him and did not know which of the sounds were names. They came in the same low register as the rest of the yard — the rasp of a broom, the slap of a loaf set down, the small flat knock of a pot gone wrong — and they left the same spaces behind them. A sound. A space. A sound. A space. He counted the spaces because there was nothing else to count that morning, as he had counted the nails in the boards and the breaths of the kiln and the steps of the woman who carried him. He thought at first that it was the yard breathing. Only later, when the last name had gone up the hill and the quiet lay back over the roofs like a fold of wool put down, did he understand what the district had been doing on the Remembering, the fourth of the Five: it had been saying the dead, one house to the next, and the spaces were where it stopped for breath.

Sella Vail had carried him to the threshold and no farther. She held him there with the fold at his chin, her little-door burn shut on nothing, her hip turned so that the morning came at him sideways and the wool took half of what the yard said. She did not set him where a slate lay open, and she did not tell him why she had brought him this far and no farther. The hand at her throat did not go to the widow’s mark. There had been no opening of the kiln, and a remembering was not an opening; the rule held as exactly on a named day as it held on a market’s Account-day. She gave the instruction to the wool. “Keep it off his ears. It’s the river wind.” She did not say whose wind, or whose river, or whose ears; and when a name went by that she knew, and the house next door took it up and gave it on, she neither repeated it nor stopped it nor looked toward the basket to see whether he had heard it. She said “the house” where another woman would have said “I”, and of the names she said nothing at all.

Under the Vail kiln-house roof the day went on by not saying one thing. The coat that had been Garl Vail’s kept the broader man’s shape on its peg behind the door, salt still stiff in the creases of the shoulders, and nobody took it down to carry it past him. In the Vail wheel-shed the seat kept its hollow on the left, worn by twenty years of a man’s leaning, and it stayed hollow. Along the skirting board the Ash ran grey to the corner and turned it without a break, as it had turned it every morning of the boy’s life and every morning for a year before, unswept and left for the Last Day. And the proverb went to the fire and to the pot and never to the basket: feed the fire before you feed yourself; it’ll still be there in the morning. The house remembered by keeping the objects and withholding the word. A man could be four things at once and not be named by any of them.

She went around the name the way she went around Ash left in a corner. It was a careful circle, and there was no word for the thing circled. When she spoke to the fire she said feed it and that’ll hold, and the sentence stopped where the name would have stood and went on with the pot. When Jorra Vail came in from the shed she told him the fire was low and did not add the rest, and he moved the damper and did not ask what the rest had been. When she spoke to the wool she spoke of the river wind. Three times in one morning the house came up to the edge of a word and went around it, and the boy lying at the edge of the boards watched her do it with the attention he had once given a surgeon crossing a field he did not want to cross: he knew the shape of the avoidance without knowing the thing avoided. Her hands were never still. They counted spoons, nails, the breaths of the child in the basket. They did not count the name. The lead glaze at her throat kept its own green under her collar, touched at no hour that morning, and the little-door burn closed on cloth and on nothing and gave neither away.

The boy counted the silence where the name should have stood. He did not decide to. It came the way the counting came in that house, unbidden and exact: the space between a woman’s sentence and the word she did not put into it had the same shape as the space between a kiln’s fourth breath and its fifth. He had been keeping such spaces on a tray of their own since the birth room. There was the pot turned toward on the bench that had given nothing when it was set down; there was the half-heard note that had gone past the door on a day when a vessel was carried; there was the pot that had cracked two paces from his head and had been called a pot gone wrong; there was the gap in the told bell and the gap in the heard bell, kept in two places; and now there was this — a place the size of a word where the house’s own dead man should have stood. He set it with the others and not with the hearings. It was a gap, not a hearing. Of the full note there had been five hearings in his ear from four sources, and the fifth of those had come out of a dying man under a cold iron tube, and nobody in this room had added a sixth.

Five. From four sources. No sixth.

Alda Reed of House Reed came under the roof in the middle of the morning with the birth-slates under her arm and soap and warm milk on her, the smell of a room where somebody else’s child had just been fed. She laid the slates writing-down on the bench before she turned toward the fold, as she had laid them on every other morning she had come, and only then put her hand to the child. From the birth-flower shrine down the yard a woman had spoken one of the morning’s names aloud and asked whose it was; the question came through the door and stopped at the bench. Alda did not look up from the slate she was straightening.

“Stone seven,” she said. “The account went up on the eighth, before the paper went out.”

It was a date where a name should have stood, and it was the same date she had given at the end of nineteen steps to a woman who had asked for her child. Her hand went once inside her bodice to the little tin on its cord and came away without opening it. The cord showed for a moment at the edge of her shawl, a grey line with a small weight at the end of it, and was gone. She pressed the fold flat at his shoulder and kept her hand there longer than a fold required, long enough for the child’s warmth to come up into her palm and for the surgeon in him to note the exact pressure of a woman who wanted something and would not ask. Then the wool rode back at his wrist and the red edge of the birth-flower showed, a branch of it running from the third rib down the forearm and stopping short of the hand; and Alda crossed two fingers at the corner of it before she knew she had done it. She went on giving dates after there was no question left to answer: a cooper’s seventh in Mud End, a potter’s third child in Stonemouth, the exact day of a birth in a house nobody at the bench would enter.

He had once stood in a better-lit room and watched a hand go past a note turned face-down on a chart, and had learned there what a covering looks like when the person doing it believes it is care. He knew the shape of Alda’s two fingers and the shape of the date laid over them, and knew they were the same gesture made by two hands that had never met. He could hand neither back. He could not have put a name to the gesture in Grathic even if a mouth had been given him, and there was no word in this house for the thing a woman did when she covered what she would not say. He kept the sign on one place of the tray and the date on another, and did not let them touch. A likeness laid on a likeness will grow a cause whether or not anybody put one there.

In the old register a hand went into a coat pocket and found wool where a small lit rectangle with a name had lain. The hand had been there before, on a night of orange light on wet snow, and the name under the light had not been looked at then either. It had not been looked at now. There had been a promise about a boat on cold water, made across nine summers to a woman who had organised her life around not waiting, and the boat had never been bought; the promise had gone on being carried without the boat, which is a way of carrying a thing that has no handle. The shape of it came and went in the register where such shapes were kept, untagged, with no name looked at and nothing crossing back that a child’s hand could hold. The hand that reached was a man’s hand and the pocket was a coat’s pocket and the wool was the wool at his own ear, and the three did not join. He came back to the fold the way a man comes back from a room he has no business in, with nothing in his fingers and the same small exactness of loss.

— Day 29 —

the Last Day, Y.H. 812

The bread went into the oven before the light had cleared the sill, and the house took the heat the way a vessel takes water that is poured too fast — carefully, and with more of it going than staying. Corin was carried close enough to feel it, in the basket at Sella Vail’s hip under the Vail kiln-house roof, and the warmth came to him in levels: first the oven mouth when she drew the lid, then the standing heat in the room, then the thin edge of it that reached the fold at his chin and went no further. He had spent every day of his life in rooms where heat was held in something and counted as it went. A man who had once put both hands inside a stranger’s chest to keep a failing heat from spilling had no hand now for the loaves going in beside him. The bread was the last bread of the year. They baked it as they baked every loaf, without a word about the year.

The loaves came out set a little apart from one another, brown-edged and set firm, and Sella moved her lips over them as she set them on the rack. It was a counting that kept no figure. Three here, two there, one turned with its brown edge up and looked at twice, the same arithmetic she had been moving along the shelf for a tend and more, so that no number need ever be said aloud where a slate might be near. The smell of barley smoke went into the wet wool and stayed there. The boy lay with the fold at his ear and let the heat come and go off the loaves in small waves, each wave weaker than the one before, and thought of the way a room cools after the fire is banked and before it settles. He knew the order of that cooling the way other men know the order of their own stairs. It did not help him. There was still no hand in the house that would take him the first pace toward the rack.

In the fold the two crusts lay where they had been put. The older was stiff past the smell of anything, brown-edged and cold since Stone 15; the newer still gave a thin breath of barley smoke when the wind turned off the brook, though it had been given on Stone 28 and had no warmth left in it for a week. Neither had been lifted. Neither had been named. No third crust came into the fold on the Last Day, though bread was going out of the oven in armfuls and the house was full of the smell of it. He understood the rule by which that happened without being told: a gift that cannot be answered cannot be refused, and a gift that cannot be refused is best not spoken of, because a thing spoken of becomes a thing a person might have to answer. So the loaves went on the rack and the crusts stayed in the fold, and the two records of the same barley lay a hand’s breadth apart and were never put together.

Bread. Two crusts. No third.

Mira Silt came up from the eels in the middle of the morning with pale slime dried on her sleeves and silt in the creases of her neck, her head held a little to the right against the cold in the place where the top of her left ear had been. She flicked an eel-bone against her thumb while she said it, a small grey thing from the pocket of her apron, and put it away when the deciding was done. The grey went in the morning, before the light had cleared the sill. It warmed the boards by degrees and missed one breath in every four.

Four, four, and a gap. The kindling’s empty fifth lay on its own tray, separate from the fire’s interval, and he did not let the two touch.

Outside, the yard was already speaking of Slate Row the way it spoke of the river or the north gate — a direction, not a place anybody meant to go this morning. It was the Opening of the Slates. The new year’s accounts were being opened somewhere beyond the row of houses, in a room with a bench and a pot and a draught along the floor, and the talk came back over the boards as a shape rather than a report. One in twelve stood printed where it had stood last year and nobody in the yard challenged it; the true subtraction that would have put a figure on what a house had actually lost was spoken by nobody at all, and went past the basket the way a fist goes past a child, shut and unopenable. He had learned by now that a figure heard in the yard was not a figure he could spend, correct or hand back. The Five were behind them. The year had opened without anybody coming to the kiln-house to do it, and the district had entered a new twelve months on its slates before the boy under the fold had been entered on any of them.

❦

He was thirty days old. A new number had been written at the head of the district’s account and the sun had come up over the same yard, and the only thing that crossed from one to the other whole was the count nobody had thought to enter. It went on exactly as it had gone on in the old year: the kiln’s four, four and a gap; the kindling’s fifth that was not there; the bell’s gap as it had been told in the dark; the missing note on the bench that had been a silence and not a hearing; the ridge that was no longer in the corner; the two crusts lying in the fold; the five pauses lying side by side in a row and not joined. A year was a change of handwriting on the same account. He had believed that once, in a life where the accounts had been kept in binders and the handwriting had changed every January, and the belief had survived the crossing without carrying any of the binders with it.

The intervals stayed on their own tray. The pot turned toward on the bench that had given nothing; the note that had gone past the door and been too far to confirm; the pot that had cracked two paces from his head and been called a pot gone wrong. The silence where a name should have stood lay with them and was not a hearing. Of the full note there had been five hearings from four sources and no sixth, and the Drawing collector’s ordinary hand with no pause in its own place stayed filed apart from the five sightings of the neat dark hand, and the charred slate lay in the kiln ash exactly where it had lain, black on one side and eaten into the shape of a strip of bark. He kept all of it in its several places because a likeness laid on a likeness will grow a cause whether or not anybody put one there. He had made that mistake once in a better-lit room and had carried the cost of it for the rest of a life. He could no more join the five pauses now than he could lift a hand to his own chin. He did not know Grathic, could not have written a figure on a slate if one had been put into his palm, and was carried everywhere in a basket that was not his.

❦

Work turned toward the thaw in Low Kiln, and toward the slip pits, though the Ilm still held under its ice and had not moved for anybody’s argument. Jorra Vail came in from the Vail wheel-shed with bone-coloured slip dried to the colour of old bone up both forearms, the bent little finger of his left hand going in ahead of him at the damper, which he moved before Sella had finished telling him the fire was low. He said to the wheel what he never said to a person.

“The wheel doesn’t care whose hands. It cares if they’re steady.”

To a pot set down on the bench he said, “You’ll do,” in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either, and went back to the shed without once looking toward the bed where the boy lay. There had been no slate dust on his forearms since the Last Day, and the boy kept the clean forearms and the dusty ones in two places, as he kept everything a face refused to report. The yard had begun to call the cold a warm spell and the warm spell the weather, the word that lets a person go home without owning a day of it; a woman come up from the pits said the thaw would be early, and a man who had looked at the ice said it was as sound as stone, and by noon both had agreed it was weather. Down below the sill the river lay under its skin of ice and did not stir.

Tarn swept before the light was properly up, with grey Ash worked into the creases of his knuckles that no scrubbing lifted, and set the small knife back on its sill and told the empty yard where it was, unasked.

“I put it back. You can check.”

Six years of the seven were counted; the seventh he held back again on the first morning of the new year, the one entry in this house that the house could not check. He had wanted a late thaw in Stone because a late thaw put off the paying, and now the year had turned and the paying was a year nearer and he went on sweeping as though it were the same count he had swept to yesterday. His broom checked half a stroke at the place by the bench where no note had come and went on. He did not look toward the basket. He had not said what he heard on any of the five days, and the boy kept the two silences under the one roof side by side where they had lain all year, an equality with no word on either side of it.

Garl Vail’s coat kept its salt shape on its peg behind the door and nobody took it down on the first morning of the year. In the shed the seat kept its hollow on the left, worn by twenty years of a man’s leaning, and stayed hollow; the ridge under the skirting board was gone from the corner now, burned and called dealt with, and the house was not less shaped by the absence of it. His proverb went to the pot and to the fire and never to the basket: leave the crack showing; people trust a thing they can see has been handled. The crack had been showing all year in a grey line along the wall, and now the line was gone and the proverb went on being said to the pot, as though a thing left in plain sight could not be made to disappear by a woman with a pail of water.

Sella put the fold right at his ear without looking at his face, the little-door burn on the back of her hand closing first on nothing and then on the wool. She fed him barley gruel thinned with milk from the house’s own dish, the spoon wiped on her sleeve and two fingers behind his ear holding his head where it wanted to go, and said the house where another woman would have said I. “Keep it off his ears. It’s the river wind,” she told the wool; and to the fire, “Feed it. It’ll still be here in the morning.” She said nothing about where the warmth in the dish had come from or who had paid for it, and had not once in thirty days said the word for the thing she kept. In the fold the two crusts lay where they had been put. One had been stiff since Stone 15 and one still gave a thin breath of barley smoke when the wind turned off the yard, though the wind had turned a hundred times since the smoke left it. Neither had been lifted. Neither had been named. No third crust came into the fold on the first morning of the year, though there was bread in the house and the oven had been hot five days ago.

❦

Nothing had been joined. Five hearings of the flat note from four sources and no sixth; no word in Grathic for the sound, and no hand to tap a vessel and ask it to answer. The intervals and the silence where a name should have stood were kept apart on their tray. Five pauses lay side by side in a row, and the Drawing collector’s ordinary hand with no pause in it lay in its own place, and the charred slate lay in the kiln ash where the broom had gone around it for a year. He was thirty days old, cracked, low-capacity, unranked, below Thin, with twelve to fifteen embers at best and a flaw nobody had named. He was mute and carried everywhere, could not roll, stand, read a slate, lift a hand to his own chin or feed himself; cold came to cheek and temple and wrist in the order he knew, and the wet thread at his mouth was not wiped, and the blanket would not stay on.

He lay on his bed under the Vail roof with the fold at his ear and the two crusts in it, and the fire behind him took its breath in fours and missed one. The count went on into a year the district had already entered on its slates, unentered, untaxed, worth nothing to anybody in the house and not for sale in any market of it. Outside, the yard called the weather what it called the weather, and under the ice the river did not move. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth and came down again.

The Chronicle of Chapter 8

On Kindling, before first light, a clay dish came up the Clay Yard with a coal under ash, passed from hearth to hearth. Sella Vail touched the widow’s mark, held it one breath, tipped the coal into the kiln, and touched it at no other hour. Corin, twenty-five days old at the threshold, counted the firelight: four, and four, and a place where a fifth would be. A collector took the year’s first measure; no figure reached the roof.

On Weighing an officer read the measures in a clean voice, the Cross’s grammar in a temple’s clothes. A whole return was announced for someone absent; a name was read that the household would not repeat, and the boy filed the silence after it among the gaps. Berr Clay wiped his thumb and said nothing. The Court’s room came as a voice through a doorway: bench, pot, draught and missing line. He set it beside three other rooms and wrote no cause.

The Long Night came with no fire after dusk. The house sat in the dark telling of the Return: a child who knew warmth before a face, a man returned with somebody else’s hands, a hearth-maid giving dates where wonder should stand. In one telling the bell went four, four and a gap, and he kept the told bell beside the heard bell without letting them touch. Cold came at cheek, temple and wrist; neither the breast nor the crust was given. Tarn held his seventh year back.

On the Remembering names passed door to door, but under the Vail roof Garl’s name was not spoken; Sella went around it as around ash left in a corner. Alda Reed touched her tin unopened, crossed two fingers at the birth-flower without knowing it, and covered the sign with a date. In the old register a hand found wool where a lit rectangle with a name had lain; the name went unlooked-at. The charred slate lay in the kiln ash.

On the Last Day bread was baked, and no third crust came into the fold. Mira Silt answered a question with a question and her own answer, touched the grey cord under her apron at a figure, and showed a second cord twisted twice beneath it; the knots went uncounted. Sella would not let the ash be swept early, measured both ridges with one thumb and left them where they lay, then banked the coals without touching the mark.

In the morning the grey was burned and called dealt with. No opening came, so the mark stayed untouched and the Kindling fire still lay under ash. Slate Row a direction, one in twelve printed and unchallenged, and the district entered a new year without coming to the kiln. At thirty days old he carried his count into it: five hearings from four sources and no sixth, the empty fifth, the unspoken name, five pauses unjoined. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth and came down.
