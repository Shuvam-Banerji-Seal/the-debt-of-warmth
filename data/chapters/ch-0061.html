The Row Stood Where It Stood

Days 234–236 · Grain 25–27, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, Sill

Three askings do not make a witness. The weather answers the first, the price of barley the second; the third goes up the terrace while the row stands where it stood. A witness stands somewhere and can be asked, and nobody stood.

— A saying of the Clay Yard, told in the last tend of Grain

— Day 234 —

Grain 25, Fifthday, Y.H. 813

The hand lay against the fold where it had come down on the river’s next low note, and the boy set the coordination down where a man sets a tool on a bench at the end of the work. It was not watched. It was not named. It was not asked to stand up and say what it was. He had once reduced a shoulder in the dark by feel alone and now could not close the two inches to the pot handle, and both were true and neither was a sign; the two rhythms held one against the other across a wall that does not touch went onto the sixth place with the other answers, an object laid with objects, exact, complete, worth nothing. The room that warmed him and the room he could not enter kept the old wall between them. Fire went on in the one and river in the other, and neither stopped for the ear under the roof.

He was two hundred and thirty-four days old and six months and fifty-four days, and nobody in Sarn, Sill or Grath said either number in his hearing or over his head. The count had gone forward in the dark from two hundred and thirty-three to two hundred and thirty-four with no hand to enter it and no voice to say so, exact, complete, unspent. Hot clay came first under the Vail kiln-house roof, hot clay under tallow and wet wool under that, and under all three the sour Ash breath of the unswept corner the house called the thaw. The day’s first sound was not a bell. Behind the wall the kiln drew in four low pulls and stopped where the fifth should have stood, and the stopping stood where it stood with nobody under any roof to put a name to what stood in it.

He used the three counts the way a man uses three instruments that will not read against one another. Under the floor the carried fire gave four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer without a name for two hundred and thirty-four days. Through the finger’s-width door the river kept its note of water over water, a fifth low thing never counted among the hearings. Overhead the boards kept four nails on the near board, four on the next, and a thumb-width knot where the fifth nail should have stood. Fire under the floor, river through the door, nails overhead: three counts in three places, and none rose into another. A man who wanted them to agree would have to put a payer under all three, and no payer stood to any of them, so he kept them on three trays and let the trays lie where they lay. The surgeon’s register took the three in at a glance and filed them apart, the way he had once filed three wounds from one blow that had no single instrument behind them.

Sella Vail had gone to her own bed before the small hours and left the coals to keep themselves, and there was no breathing number anywhere in the house. The two rooms did not touch. In the room that was not his the fire kept its own time under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, and in the room that was not hers the river kept its low note through the gap, and between them a wall stood that had never been given a door. Nothing in his room held a count for him to borrow. He had learned to keep a rhythm without the sound, as a man keeps the step of a road he has walked a thousand times, but he borrowed nothing from the room that does not touch his and did not try. The dark went on being the dark, and the cold went on stopping where it had stopped, and he lay with the fold at his ear and listened to three things that would not become one.

Nothing from the old life broke through into the small hours. No voice came with its grammar of a different country, no lit rectangle with a name on it, no file in a white room; the surgeon was present only as a way of noticing, and the noticing was enough. He parted a colour from a hearing the way he had once parted clot from vessel, and a shape from a seventh of a note, and the figure read over a covered basket from the figure paid, and let each lie on the place it had earned. It was a dry and unsentimental business, and it cost him something that he could not name and did not try to name. He had a joke about it that he did not laugh at, because a laugh costs more than a man in his position can spare. The child in him wanted the crust and the adult kept the trays, and the two went on arguing without a word between them.

The body had its own exactness and did not care what he made of it. One stiff leathery brown-edged barley crust lay two inches from his ear; the empty place beside it kept its name to itself and there was no third to make the row come out even. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the dry heat and was unwiped, because nobody under any roof had ever been given an instruction about the thread, and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. Cold had stopped at cheek, at temple, at the small bare joint of the wrist where the wool rode back, as though it had come to the end of a sentence written out for it and had neither an order to go further nor an order to leave. The afterdrop waited on the far side of a warming nobody in this house would perform. He knew the afterdrop when he saw it. He wanted the crust. Both were true and the second was the louder, and it had been the louder for two hundred and thirty-four days.

He turned out the file as a man lays instruments on a towel and found it exact, complete and worth nothing. There was no towel, no instruments, no case, but the order of the laying was the whole of the discipline and he had it still. Six hearings of the full note stood beside and never among: five from four sources, a cup across a birth room, a pot in a shed through a finger’s width of door, the district’s slate twice at the Cross, and the sixth, the small flat knock out of a woman as the men lifted her, from a person, filed where a thing out of a person must go and never among the sources. No seventh had come in all these days and none was invented to fill the empty place, for a finding kept empty is a finding and a finding filled to make a row come out even is a finding forged. Three near-full answers stood on their own tray, advanced and unpaid: a pot under a shed wall that had answered nearly full with air under the note and a want in it, a rack pot lifted a hand’s breadth too high and set down again, and a knowing a wheel-keeper kept back from a man come up on a wrong wind.

Beside the near-full answers stood three shapings of the pot saying, all sayings and no rule. A pot that sings before its time mostly sings itself out early. The pot that sang early last month had not been heard from since. A pot gone quiet on every row come the thaw. None had been promoted into the hearings, no cause had stood up, no lesson had been made, no name had been given to what ailed the man carried out on Day 188, and no pot had been lost in the yard. The burned-slate word stood at four on a place of its own, and the two tellings of the counting room shut in the war stood at two, with a hand between the three sayings and the tellings and no fifth and no third. The told bell lay beside the heard, four, four and a gap with nobody crossing himself; the told figure lay beside the paid, one in twelve read low twice over a covered basket and still not the figure paid, while the true subtraction stayed locked in a box going up the road toward High Kiln and answered nothing for the man carried out on Day 188.

Nine stoppings stood with no tenth before the seventh year at its very end and still ungiven. The Seed 16 breath was unspent and the loan uncalled. Three held changes stood on three places, the loft-sill scrape on a fourth, the carried man’s pale line on a fifth and the held inch with its answers on a sixth. Beside the hearings but never among them the seventh green was a colour and the seventh shape was a shape, gathered at a market and carried unjoined through the working mornings since, with no eighth of either, no name spoken over either, no loaf, no slate and no sale. The dressed grain lay covered near the hearth with no figure over it, counted in glances and handfuls and by the carrying arm’s weight that knew weight and not cost, entered on no slate. Grain’s second arithmetic stood changed and unread under this roof, and a row of seven walked-around things in the Clay Yard was still seven and not an account until somebody found a payer to stand under it, and none did.

On a seventh place of its own, with a finger between it and the hearings, the sayings, the tellings and the count, the question stood with two askings and no witness. A voice with no owner, no room, no hand at the kiln and no cause had asked over the boards which row the pot had gone quiet on; the weather had answered and the price of barley had answered after it, and the voice had gone off up the terrace while the row stood where it stood. He had opened the question as a surgeon opens a wound and found no witness at the bottom. Two askings were not one witness; a witness stands somewhere and can be asked, and none did and none could. The question was not a fourth shaping of the pot saying, not a hearing, not a seventh of the full note, not a fifth telling of the slate, not a cause, not a name and not a fifth witness. It was a seed kept on its own tray for a far-off day when somebody might stand somewhere and be asked.

Sella came to the fire before she came to the basket and before she gave anything to the basket, which had never been given an instruction and had no need of one. She gave the instruction to the wool, the fire, the pot, the broom, the running water and the river wind, and to no basket. She told the fire it was the thaw. She turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and fed them, and said The house will keep it where another woman would have said I. Then she stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand counting under her breath toward no opening — small intake, hold, letting go — and the count went nowhere that any slate could read. The fire answered the telling of the thaw with four low pulls behind the wall and the silence where the fifth should have stood, and she went on feeding it as though it had said nothing at all. She pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carried the little-door burn so that the grey streak at her temple showed pale off the coals; the burn closed on nothing when the hand had nothing to hold, and opened again as she reached for the pot.

At the loaf shelf she kept her back to the basket and moved her lips along a row standing with two unnamed gaps, and gave nobody a figure. Her finger lay on top of the shelf’s front edge, not beneath it, so the charcoal tally stayed unread, uncounted and uncopied. Her eye went once to the clay jar cold under its glaze on the shelf by the wall, and her hand shut half over nothing without a touch. The widow’s mark hung where it hung on its cord at her throat, touched once before the kiln on Ember 1 and at no hour since; there had been no opening in fifty-four days, because a morning, a shelf and a basket are not openings. She set the pot rim out with its handle two inches beyond the reaching hand and let her hand rest a half-beat longer at the setting than the setting required, and did not look toward the bed.

A woman with a pot hitched against her hip came to the threshold and stood there a moment before she asked, the way people stand before they put a number into the air. “What’ll the storing take,” she said, “now it’s under wool? A body can’t tell by looking at a fold.”

“Ask the pot,” Sella said.

“The pot doesn’t keep a figure.”

“The weather knows the rest.” She said it flat, and went on turning the coals, and there was an end of it. The woman shifted the pot from hip to hip and went off down the step with her question still in her mouth, and nothing slopped, nothing was priced, refused or entered. The figure was a direction and the direction arrived nowhere. The hand was asked nothing by any of this, and the file waited where it had been laid.

The dressed grain lay under its fold near the hearth, turned and looked at for damp and gathered back by the handful, changed in state and given no figure. It had come off the worked strips east of Low Kiln and had been beaten out on the flagstones and winnowed so the chaff went up on the river wind; the carrying arm remembered the weight of it and not the cost, and the arm’s record went into no slate. Out in the yard, when the half-pace took them through it, the seven walked-around places would get a look and a wide step and no number, and seven things standing in a row would still be seven, because a row is not an account until somebody finds a payer to stand under it. No payer came up the clay road on Grain 25 and none went down it.

He put the first question to the hand while the morning was still young, the first way of this chapter. The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the river’s low note through the finger’s-width door under conditions that had sometimes answered, held to the small intake only, and came down at once before the letting-go. It was an answer and it was a miss and it was both without shame, filed on the sixth place with no payer named. He did not ask the hand to extend itself by a hair’s breadth beyond the inch, because a man who asks a thing to reach will make an account of the reaching and want somebody to stand under it, and nobody stood. The hand came down and lay against the fold while the fire went on in the room that warmed him and the river in the room he could not enter, neither stopping for the ear. Forty-three answers stood there now, each an object and not a power, exact, complete and worth nothing at two inches of air.

Grain 30, the third and last Account-day and market, stood five mornings ahead and was not brought into a day that had not earned it. The ownerless question kept its two askings over the boards and was not given a third in a morning that had not asked it; that was held for the afternoon of a day still to come. Nothing had been joined, named, opened, called, spent or ended. The fire went on in the room that warmed him and the river went on in the room he could not enter, and between the two the count went forward exact and unspent into a day that had earned its heading and would not say it.

The afternoon thinned without ending. It did not go out the way a lamp goes out; it wore away the way the edge of a thumb-mark wears, a little at a time, so that a man who looked up from his work could not have said when the light had turned, only that it had turned and the day was thinner for it. Through the finger’s-width door the river kept its note of water over water and did not alter it for the light. Heat came under the low angle where the boy lay, taking its route behind the pot first and into the fold afterwards, as though it knew the room by heart and had walked it a thousand times. It settled there. It went no further.

He had been set at the angle where the underside of the loaf shelf becomes a roof, and from there the room above him was all board and shadow and the bright edge of a pot rim now and then. He could not lift his head to see the shelf’s row, and did not try, because a man who tries to lift a head that will not lift becomes a man arguing with his own body, and there is nothing to be gained in an argument that the body always wins. The crust lay two inches from his ear, stiff and leathery and brown at the edges, the last warmth that had crossed House Silt before he was taken, gone cold now and keeping the shape of what it had been. The empty place beside it kept its name to itself. No third came to make the row come out even, and he did not want one, because a third crust laid beside two absences would be a kindness with a figure on it, and a kindness with a figure on it is the beginning of an account.

The thread at his mouth had frozen in the dry heat and was unwiped. Nobody under any roof had ever been given an instruction about the thread. A basket set down is a pot set down and left, and the house had left it with the same care it left the pot: set down, covered, not looked into. Cold had stopped at his cheek, at his temple, at the small bare joint of the wrist where the wool rode back, as though it had come to the end of a line written out for it and had neither an order to go further nor an order to leave. The afterdrop waited on the far side of all three, past a warming nobody in this house would perform. He knew the afterdrop when he saw it. He wanted the crust. Both were true, and in the afternoon the second was louder than it had been in the small hours, because a child’s hunger does not keep the hours an adult keeps, and the adult’s register, which had been exact all morning, went on being exact while the child in him went on being hungry. The two argued without a word between them, and had argued so for two hundred and thirty-four days.

He did not turn out the file in the afternoon. That was work for the small hours, when the house was quiet and a man could lay his instruments on a towel without somebody coming to sweep the towel away. He left the trays where they lay: the hearings beside and never among, the near-full answers, the sayings, the tellings, the stoppings, the held inch with its answers. Each had its place and a finger between places, and the finger was the important thing, because a man who lets two things touch will find by morning that they have stood up together and called themselves a cause. No cause had stood up under this roof. Nothing had been paid.

Garl Vail was kept in objects and not in a story. The salt-stiff coat hung on its peg behind the door with the crease at the left shoulder that no brushing would take out, and it held a broader man’s shape the way a pot holds the shape of the hand that threw it long after the hand has gone to other work. In the shed the wheel-seat kept its twenty-year lean to the left and the hollow that went with it, and Sella Vail would not let anyone fill the hollow, though she let her son sit in it; the distinction had stood in the house for a year and had never been explained, and the boy had long since stopped expecting an explanation. In the corner of the fire room the clean dark line lay where the Ash ridge had turned, and beside it the new finer hairline of grey that the house called the thaw lay finer still, and neither had been swept. A line left where it is is still a line kept. The proverb about leaving the crack showing had gone years ago to a pot with no crack in it, and was said now over a corner where the repair had been rubbed out, a saying that had outlived the thing it was true about and cost more for the outliving.

The debt against a firing of tiles lay where it lay, circled without a name and worse for being uncalled, because a man cannot pay a debt nobody will name and cannot settle it and cannot be done with it. It went around the house the way a woman goes around Ash in a corner: a careful circle and no word for the thing circled. His name had not been spoken under this roof for two hundred and thirty-four days, and no lesson had been made of any of it, and no collector had come up the clay road on Grain 25 and none had gone down it. In the district the arithmetic had changed twice and could not be changed back, and a man might have thought the change had something to do with the uncalled debt if he had wanted a cause badly enough to stand under it; the boy did not want one. He left the two on separate trays, with the true subtraction locked in a box going up toward High Kiln and answering nothing for the man carried out on Day 188. Two hundred and thirty-four stood exact, complete, unspent, and six months and fifty-four days had been told by nobody under any roof in Sarn, Sill or Grath. The fire’s fifth had gone unpaid the same number of days, and the fifth had not been invented to make the row come out even.

At the kindling Tarn took the broom only after it had been given its instruction, as a man takes up a tool already named and handed to him, and took it up as an instrument. “Yes, house,” he said, and told the wood “Six counted. The next I don’t count.” His chest had been tight since the dark. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line, and no scrubbing lifted it; the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back without appearing to know that it held any. He set the small knife at the very edge of its sill, six counted in the worn thumb-mark where the house could check and the seventh at the lip where it could not, and Garl’s unforged collector sentence lay beside it where it had lain for six years: the man sent for a collector who turned back at the frozen ford, a sentence with no name in it and never one, though the man who turned back had been Garl himself.

The other sentence came to his teeth as he reached, the one about an ear under this roof being worth more than the debt that held it. It went all the way to the word that would call the loan and stopped there with no figure and no name. It was a remembered stopping and not a new one. Nine stoppings stood with no tenth before the seventh year at its very end and still ungiven, and the remembered stopping added nothing to the nine and took nothing from them, an object on the tray and not a payment and not a rule. He drew the breath that should have gone in at the top of the reach and did not spend it; the Seed 16 breath stood where it had stood since the planting, a small interval at the top of each reach, unspent. The loan that could hear stayed uncalled, and no collector came up the clay road on Grain 25 and none went down it. He had said I owe six so often that the words had worn smooth as the thumb-mark, and he had never once said the word that would make the seventh a number the house could enter. Somewhere above the shed the loft settled with a small dry sound, and it came to him as a direction and nothing more: up, and to the left, and no further. The warm stone Sella had given him in the winter Garl died lay in its unmarked pot in the Debt Loft, two years gone and cold long since, and he had stopped trying to reconcile it with the debt, which is not the same thing as forgiving it. His stroke went over the flagstones past the shallow bright patch without the half-check and went on over the collector’s road without once turning along it.

At the edge of the Vail wheel-shed Jorra Vail came through with the day’s meal-fine grey slate dust ground small as meal past the bent little finger and under the nails, white in the creases where a fist had opened, and not bone-coloured slip. He kept dusty forearms and clean ones on one tray and parted them as a surgeon parts clot from vessel, asking neither which slate had been broken nor whether it was a birth-slate. Three or more men had gone low into the finger’s-width-open the back room before first grey and got no name from him, and he did not say the room was cold. The broken birth-slates lay under the lowest rack where the yard went around them and entered them into nothing.

He spoke to the wheel before he spoke to anyone. “It doesn’t care whose hands. It cares if they’re steady,” he said, and told a pot “You’ll do” in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either. He knew the the full note that the pot would give three turns before it left his hands, knew it with air under it and a want in it, and withheld the knowing. It went to the near-full tray beside the hearings and never among them, and no fourth near-full answer stood up to join the three. The three sayings stayed three sayings, and The burned-slate word stood at four, a direction over the boards from Slate Row arrived nowhere, and the two tellings of the counting room shut in the war stood at two with no third, a hand between them and the three sayings. No fifth telling came and no fourth shaping of the pot saying stood up to call itself a cause, and no name was given to what ailed the man carried out on Day 188.

He grew quieter whenever the burned-slate word went around, and the household straightened around the quiet without an order, the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted off it; the quiet went before him into the shed the way a cold air goes before a door. He’s not Vail stayed sealed as tightly as the hour it was spoken, and the promise of the boy’s name to the men who burn slates was neither given nor withdrawn. He did not look toward the bed or the basket, and Tarn did not ask about the room, and the two silences stayed unjoined under the one roof.

When Sella’s voice went low and unfinished at the hearth — “The fire’s low. It wants—” — his bent finger went into the cold iron of the damper ahead of her sentence, ahead of his hand and ahead of the word. The iron took the cold out of the fingertip and gave nothing back. He held until the sentence reached the pot and the end of it was given there, then moved the tongue a hair’s breadth and left the finger standing in the iron. It was a remembered repetition, not a change made again, and it lay where the old fraction lay, joined to no kiln and entered in no slate. He read the iron as one piece with two bites, the holding and the taking, with no word in Grathic that would carry both mercies in one mouthful; a clamp was a clamp and was not asked to mean more.

At the kindling Tarn took the broom only after it was instructed and told the wood that six were counted and the next he did not count, the knife at the lip beside Garl’s unforged collector sentence. The ear-sentence stopped at the word that would call the loan; nine stoppings stood, the Seed 16 breath was unspent, and no collector came on Grain 25, 26 or 27. Jorra Vail parted dust from slip, withheld the note he knew three turns ahead, kept the burned-slate word at four and the shut-room tellings at two, and held his bent finger in the damper until Sella’s sentence reached the pot.

In the second afternoon the ownerless voice came a third time over the boards: which row had the pot gone quiet on? A wrong wind off High Kiln answered first with the sweet edge of lead glaze, the price of barley after it, and the voice went up the terrace while the row stood. He opened it as a surgeon opens a wound --- what it asked, where it stood, what it wanted, who would stand under the answer to pay --- and found no witness at the bottom. Three askings are not one witness. He set the third telling on the seventh place beside the first two, a finger between each pair and between them and the sayings, tellings, hearings and count.

Six times the hand answered, the forty-third through the forty-eighth: the fifth a miss entered without shame, the last a two-rhythm coordination held across the wall --- through one of Sella’s counted breaths, past the silence after her count, and down on the river’s next low note. Each was filed on the sixth place with no payer named. At the close the file lay open on the towel, complete and worth nothing; nothing had been joined, named, opened, called, spent or ended.
