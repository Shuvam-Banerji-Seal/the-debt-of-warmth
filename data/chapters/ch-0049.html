A Figure Twice Read

Days 198–200 · Ember 19–21, Y.H. 813 · The Vail kiln-house, Low Kiln

A slate is read for the moot and again for those who come late, and the figure is the same both times. The pot does not care which reading you counted, and the hand that cannot reach the handle is written in neither.

— A saying of the pot board in Low Kiln, in the kiln month

— Day 198 —

Ember 19, Y.H. 813 (Ninthday)

The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water through the finger’s-width door and came down. It had gone up in the dark and come down in the dark, and the dark had not changed its mind about anything. He lay on the bed under the roof with the fold against his cheek and one leathery brown-edged crust two inches from his ear, its empty place beside it still unnamed and no third, and the thread of drool at his mouth frozen where the small hours had left it, unwiped, because nobody under any roof had been given an instruction about the thread and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. The first smell of the day was hot clay under tallow and wet wool under that, and beneath all of it the sour mineral breath of the unswept corner, which the house called the thaw and had called it so for a year without sweeping it; the first sound was the kiln drawing in fours with a gap behind it, not a bell. Cold had come to cheek and temple and the small bare joint at the wrist where the wool rode back, and had stopped there as if it had reached the end of its own instruction. The afterdrop waited on the far side of a warming nobody in this house would perform. The boy had been a hundred and ninety-seven days old in the dark and was a hundred and ninety-eight days old in the morning, six months and eighteen days, and nobody under any roof said either number. He had stopped expecting to be told, which was not the same as not wanting it.

❦

Under the floor the carried fire gave four low pulls and kept the silence where the fifth should have closed, its payer without a name for a hundred and ninety-eight days. Through the finger’s-width door the river kept its fifth low note of water over water, a low thing heard beside the others and never numbered among them; overhead the boards kept four nails, four, and a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts in three places, and none rose up and became the others, because a man who wanted them to agree would have had to put a payer under all three and no payer stood to any of them. The man who had once read a chart by its neat margin and known the finding was worth nothing without a hand to carry it read these three the same way and let them lie where they lay. A count that finds a payer becomes an account, and an account becomes a rule with a hand to spend it; there was no hand here and no payer, so the count went on exact, complete and unspent, and the morning came up over Low Kiln without drawing a boundary around anything. He wanted the crust, wanted the breast, wanted the pot handle two inches beyond his hand and a number spoken by somebody who would have known what a number counted, and got none of them, and had long since learned to want them without making a petition of the wanting.

The body he had been given kept its own poor account. It could not roll, could not lift its chin two inches to the crust, could not close the two inches to the pot handle, could not hold up its own head through a whole spoonful; it drooled and was fed by a sleeve that was not its own, and was carried, dressed and discussed in the third person by a woman who said the boy where another woman would have said my son. He had once reduced a shoulder in the dark and known which rib would tear the lung, and now the whole of his field was one bare joint at a wrist where the wool had ridden back. The old life did not break into the room; it stood behind him like a man waiting outside a door with a chart in his hand, and the chart said nothing he could use. Both things were true at once, and the younger of them was louder, being hungry.

Sella Vail gave the instruction before she gave it to anybody. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool, “it’s the river wind,” and to the fire, “It’s the thaw,” and to the pot, “Don’t set that near the door. You’ll pay for it in the thaw.” She said the house where another woman would have said I, carried the basket at a half-pace from every open slate, and gave no instruction whatever to the basket, which went out under a fold and came back under the same fold without a word spoken over it, as a pot filled is carried that must not be slopped. At the hearth she turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and told the fire it was the thaw, and the fire took the coal under its blind ash and answered four low pulls with the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for a hundred and ninety-eight days. “The house will keep it,” she said, where another woman would have said I, and stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand counting under her breath toward no opening: the small intake at the top, the hold, the letting go. No opening stood at the end of the count and she had not looked for one, for a woman who counts toward a thing she does not want to find will keep the count exact and let it arrive nowhere.

Her eye went once to the clay jar cold under its glaze on the wall shelf, her hand starting toward it and shutting half over nothing without touching it — the little-door burn closing on the air and opening again — and she left the jar where it stood, fetched from three doors down in another month for a reason nobody had told her to give. She pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the burn so the grey streak at her temple showed pale off the coals; it had come in the winter Garl Vail died, and she would say no more than that. The widow’s mark hung where it hung on its cord at her throat; it had been touched once before the kiln on Ember 1, held one breath, let go, and at no hour since, for a morning and a shelf are not openings and there had been no opening since before the kiln on Ember 1. Then she set the pot rim out on the board with its handle standing two inches beyond the reaching hand, and her hand rested a half-beat longer at the setting than the setting needed before it went away. She did not look toward the bed at the setting. She never looked toward the bed at the setting. She fed him barley gruel from the house’s own dish, the spoon wiped on her sleeve and not on his mouth, two fingers behind the ear holding his head the way one holds a thing that will not hold itself, and said nothing about where the warmth in the dish had come from or who had paid for it. The boy watched her hand arrive at the pot before her eyes arrived at the room, and put that order on a place of its own where he kept such orders, without making a row of them and without letting the row stand up and call itself an account. She wiped the spoon on her sleeve and did not wipe his mouth, and did not wipe the thread at the corner of his lip, because the thread had not been given an instruction and neither had she, and the two omissions lay in two places that did not touch. He watched the burn close on the dish and open again, and wanted the crust, and wanted somebody to say what a day counted, and got neither, and did not turn the wanting into a petition.

Before first grey had fairly gone she carried him out into the Clay Yard. Woodsmoke lay in the yard in long low bars and the river wind came behind it with the taste of water over water, and the two passed without taking each other’s colour, as a clean hand passes a stained one on a stair and neither leaves a mark. At the yard well the trough stood bare and dark and its water was called weather; the yard went wide around the space beneath the lowest rack of the drying racks where the broken birth-slates lay, and around the clean dark line and the new finer grey beside it, with a look and a wide step and no number. At the threshold a neighbour with dry mud-scurf on her hem stopped them and asked what the firing would want now that the second reading stood a day off.

“Ask the pot,” Sella said. “The house has said it.”

“It’s the firing,” the woman said. “It’ll want something.”

“Ask the weather.”

“Then somebody’s carrying it,” the woman said. “That’s what it comes to, with another reading a day off and nobody saying what it wants.”

“The house will keep it,” Sella said, and turned the pot a half-inch on the board with two fingers, as if turning the pot might settle the question where answering it would not.

“Our lot kept it last year.”

“Ask the pot. It knows more than I do.”

The woman stood a moment with her question still in her mouth, and set her pot down, and looked at the pot, which kept nothing and told nothing, and took the question away level, with nothing priced, refused or entered, and went back to her work without appearing to have seen the basket. The figure that had gone up the road in a locked box stayed up the road and was not brought back under this roof; the printed one in twelve stood where it had been read, unchallenged, and a figure read aloud was not the figure paid. The sweet edge of lead glaze came up off High Kiln on a wrong wind and went out on the same wind, a direction answered with the pot and the weather, not a second edge paid so soon after the carrying-out; the sickness was not named in Grathic, no collector was called, and no lesson was made of the glaze. At the edge of the clay road she turned him toward home. The arm that had carried him a hundred and ninety-eight mornings kept the height of the handle, the give of the shoulder, the turn of the wrist at a step’s end — a record in muscle that remembered weight and not cost — and went around the pan’s gone round ring with a look and a wide step and no number, and did not ask the yard for a figure. Under the wall where the shallow clay pan had stood the dust kept a round ring with no scrape, no chip and no word, and she went around it as she went around the trough and the rack space and the place the carriers had crossed. Each got a look and a wide step and no number. The boy kept the ring on its own tray, not laid across the shelf’s two gaps or the empty place at his ear, because three absences left standing long enough will call themselves a cause and a cause wants a payer. No third gap came and no fourth likeness was made a cause, and no finger of his drew the line.

Back under the roof the warmth had gone off the high air first and had settled in the low places: under the loaf shelf, behind the pot, in the fold of wool where he lay with the crust two inches from his ear. The underside of the shelf made a roof that stopped at the edge of his reach, and the heat came down that roof in a slow slant and stopped at the same place, as if it too had been given an instruction and had come to the end of it. Nothing crossed that edge. The thread of drool had not been wiped in the yard and was not wiped now. He watched the slant end where his fingers ended, and thought, without arriving at anything he could have told somebody, that a house which could stop heat at an edge and stop cold at a wrist and stop a question at a pot had found the same answer three times and would not have called it an answer at all.

At the note of water over water through the finger’s-width door he put the question to his own hand. The hand outside the wool went up an inch and he asked it to stay. It came down before the counted breath had finished — a shorter holding, the body keeping its own time, an inch let go early and no failure in it, for an inch held briefly is no more a reaching than an inch held long — and the fold took it without a word. The small intake at the top, the hold, the letting go: the rhythm went on in the other room without him and did not stop to notice whether an inch of a boy’s hand had stayed with it. That was the whole of it, and there was nothing to be proud of in it — not a reach, not a stronger hand, not a sign that anything under this roof had been entered, only a brief agreement between a hand and a breath in a room he could not enter. He filed it on a place of its own beside the others and did not lay it across them: not a hearing, not a seventh, not a stopping, not a payment, not one of the three held changes, not a key, not a power and not a rule. No payer was named and no doctrine stood up out of it. The man who had once kept pressure in a cavity with both hands until the vessel took was keeping nothing now but one inch of a small soft hand, and the keeping cost him nothing and bought him nothing. He still could not close the two inches to the pot handle, lift his chin to the crust or speak a number; the body humiliated him and moved by an inch in the same motion, and both were true. The asking had been answered and then answered differently, and the answer was worth nothing and nobody saw it. Ember’s second Account-day and market stood one day off, and the market would not get to read the inch.

One hundred and ninety-eight stood exact in the dark of the wool, complete and unspent, spoken by nobody under any roof. The carried fire had had no payer for a hundred and ninety-eight days; the charcoal under the shelf was unread by a hand that was not hers and for a day that had not come; the mark had been touched once before the kiln and at no other hour; the warm stone lay back in its unmarked clay pot in the Debt Loft, two years gone and cold and unreconciled by not trying, which is not forgiving; the Seed 16 breath was unspent at the top of every reach and the loan that could hear was uncalled; the cost of the man carried out while the month was young was entered nowhere. The river kept its fifth low note, the fire missed its fifth pull under the floor, and the boards overhead kept four nails, four and a knot the width of a thumb, three counts in three places and none rising up and becoming the others, because a man who wanted them to agree would have had to put a payer under all three and no payer stood to any of them. Nothing had been joined, named, opened, called, spent or ended. The count went forward.

The afternoon thinned without ending. Heat went off the high air first, lifted as if by a hand from the upper boards and set down lower, and it stayed longest in the low places: under the loaf shelf, behind the pot, in the fold of wool where the boy lay with one leathery crust two inches from his ear. The underside of the shelf made a roof that stopped at the edge of his reach, and the heat came down that roof in a slow slant and stopped at the same place, as though it too had been given an instruction and had come to the end of it. Nothing crossed that edge. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the dark and no instruction had come for it in the afternoon, and it stayed where it had been put, a small stiff line he could neither feel nor lift.

He lay under the roof of the shelf under the roof of the house, two roofs, and neither reached past the small bare joint at the wrist where the wool had ridden back. Cold had come that far in the small hours and had gone no farther. Between the cold and the warmth there was the width of a joint, and that was the whole of his country. A man who had once stood at the edge of a field and decided which bodies would be carried and which left where they lay now measured a kingdom by the exposure of one wrist. He did not make a grievance of it. The body’s humiliations were exact and ordinary, and the surgeon’s register took them down without comment: cheek, temple, wrist, the afterdrop waiting on a warming nobody in this house would perform.

Sella Vail stood at the shelf with her back to the basket and moved her lips along a row. It was not a song and not a count that anyone could have taken down. The loaf at the end stood where a loaf had stood; the old space at the row’s end kept its place through the kiln month; and a palm’s breadth of bare board had come in on the night of Seed 6 and had not been filled since. Two gaps stood in the row and she gave neither a figure. A figure spoken over a shelf would travel; a figure kept behind the teeth stayed where it was put, which was a thing a woman could do with a gap as with a pot — set it down and leave it standing. Her lips went along the row and came back to the same place twice, the way her hand went to the same place twice when she counted fuel, and she gave nobody the thing she counted.

Her eye went once to the clay jar cold under its glaze on the wall shelf. Her hand started toward it and shut half over nothing without touching it, the little-door burn closing on the air and opening again. The jar had been fetched from three doors down in another month and set there for a reason nobody had told her to give. Its glaze was cold and she left it so. Her finger, going to the shelf’s front edge, stayed on top of the board and did not go beneath. Beneath that edge lay the charcoal tally, put there by a hand that was not hers, unread, uncounted, uncopied by anyone under this roof; and the beat that had stayed under the board on Day 166 stood where it had been left, on a place of its own, not stood up again this afternoon and not paired with anything. She straightened the board with the flat of her palm and turned from the shelf. The two gaps stayed in the other room, where a shelf’s absences do not touch the one crust lying two inches from a boy’s ear.

At the hearth she told the fire it was the thaw. She said it to the fire and not to the room and not to the basket, and turned the coals with the back of a broken pot, the pot’s back going in under the ash and lifting and letting the heat come up through the gaps. “The house will keep it,” she said, where another woman would have said I. The fire took the coal under its blind ash and answered four low pulls with the silence where the fifth should have closed, its payer without a name for a hundred and ninety-eight days. She did not supply one. A man who wanted the four to agree with the fifth would have had to put somebody under them, and no payer stood to the fire’s gap.

Then she stood a moment with the back of the broken pot in her hand and counted under her breath toward no opening. The small intake at the top, the hold, the letting go. The count went out of her and arrived nowhere, and this was the whole of the house’s method for staying warm: a cost asked as a direction, an answer given to the pot and the weather, nothing entered. From the low angle of the shelf he knew the shape of the count by the pause in her hands, the way a man learns the rhythm of another man’s breathing in the next bed. It was not his breath. It was not his fire. There was a wall between the hearth and the bed, and the wall held.

She pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the burn and the grey streak at her temple showed pale off the coals; it had come in the winter Garl Vail died, and she would say no more than that. The widow’s mark hung where it hung on its cord at her throat, touched once before the kiln on Ember 1 and at no shelf, fire, basket, afternoon or dusk since, because an afternoon is not an opening. Then she set the pot rim out on the board with its handle standing two inches beyond the reaching hand, and her hand rested a half-beat longer at the setting than the setting needed before it went away. Two inches was the whole distance: not a great gulf and not crossable. He could no more close it than lift his chin to the crust or speak the number that stood in his head. She did not look toward the bed at the setting. She never looked toward the bed at the setting, and the room that held the pot and the room that held the boy went on not touching each other, as they had touched in no hour of his life.

At the kindling the small knife lay at the very edge of its sill, six years standing in a worn thumb-mark where the house could check them and the seventh at the lip where it could not — the one entry in this house that no house could enter. Beside it lay the unforged sentence of Garl, the one about the man sent for a collector who turned back at the frozen ford. It lay there as a tool lies that has never been beaten into shape, neither a proverb for the yard nor a charge against a man, and nobody under the roof had forged it in all the boy’s days.

Tarn took the broom only after the broom had been given its instruction. A tool that had not been spoken to was a tool with no work in it, and a clay-bound hand who took up a tool before its work was named was a hand that would be entered for it; so his fingers closed on the handle only when Sella had given the broom its word. “Yes, house,” he said, and told the kindling what it had been told so often that the words had worn smooth as the thumb-mark: “Six counted. The next I don’t count.” He said it in the tone of a man stating the weather, a statement of fact that invited somebody else to laugh so that he would not have to.

A neighbour wanted the thaw early; another, coming off the clay road with dry scurf of mud on her hem, wanted it late. “Yes, house,” he said to each, agreed so entirely that no argument could find a foot between them, and went on wanting the late one, because a late thaw puts off the paying, though the paying had stood in the ruts since the water went. His broom went over the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector, over and over, without once turning to look along it. No collector came up or went down the clay road that afternoon. He swept the length of it as a man sweeps a step he does not mean to descend, and kept his eyes on the bristles.

His chest had been tight since the dark. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line at the corner, and no scrubbing lifted it out; the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back. The sentence about an ear under this roof being worth more than the debt that held it came up to his teeth as he worked. It went all the way to the word that would call the loan, and stopped there with no figure in it and no name. He drew the breath he should have spent at the top of the reach — the breath that had stood unspent since Seed 16 with its small interval where the going-in should have gone — and did not spend it. Nine stoppings stood where they had been set, objects and not a rule, intervals and not payments; no tenth stood up among them before the seventh year was earned, the seventh year stood nearer its end than it had ever stood and had not given, and the loan that could hear was not called. His stroke went on over the flagstones and past the shallow bright patch kept there from another day without the half-check, the stones holding the place where the stroke had once stopped and nobody entering it, and he did not look toward the bed or ask about the room.

In the edge of the shed, where a finger’s width of glass let the afternoon in thin, Jorra Vail spoke to the wheel before he spoke to anyone. “It doesn’t care whose hands,” he said. “It cares if they’re steady.” The wheel turned on. The three or four men who had gone low into the finger’s-width-open back room before first grey got no name from him, and he did not say the room was cold; the two shut-room tellings stayed two with no third. He kept the dusty forearms and the clean ones on one tray of his attention, the grey slip dust ground small as meal past the bend of the little finger and under the nails, white in the creases where a fist had opened, not bone-coloured slip; and he asked neither which slate had been broken nor whether it was a birth-slate. The broken birth-slates lay beneath the lowest rack and were entered by nobody. To a pot coming round under his hands he said, “You’ll do,” in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either. He knew the full note that pot would give three turns before it left his hands and withheld the knowing from a man come up on a wrong wind who stood a moment at the shed’s edge with the sweet edge of lead glaze at one mouth-corner; the man went off up the yard with his silence taken for the silence of a man at work, and his saying about a pot singing before its time stayed a saying about a pot and was not made true as a lesson. No pot was lost. The knowing went to the near-full tray beside the hearings and never among them, advanced and not paid.

When Sella’s voice went low and unfinished at the hearth — “The fire’s low. It wants—” — his bent finger went into the cold iron of the damper ahead of his hand and held there. He held it while she finished the sentence with feeding and gave its end to the pot; then the tongue moved a hair’s breadth and the finger was left standing in the iron. It was a remembered repetition, the same holding he had made on older days, not a change made again, not paired with the beat under the shelf or the half-check on the stones and not stood up with either into a rule. The iron was one piece with two bites, the bite that holds a vessel whole and the bite that can take the hand off a man, and there was no word in Grathic that would carry both mercies in one mouthful. He joined it to no kiln and entered it in no slate. The household straightened around his silence the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted, and nobody spoke of the waiting. He’s not Vail stayed sealed in whatever room he kept it, and the promise of the boy’s name to the men who burn slates was neither given nor withdrawn.

At the going of the light she carried him home up the clay road. The instruction went to the wool and to the river wind before it went anywhere near a person, and never to the basket: “Keep it off his ears,” she said, and the wind went on doing what it had been told and the basket was carried without a word. Woodsmoke lay in long low bars before her, laid one above another across the falling air, and the river wind came behind her with the taste of water over water. The two passed without taking each other’s colour, the smoke going up and the water going over, and she walked between them at her half-pace as a woman walks between two things she has been given no instruction to join.

The arm that had carried him a hundred and ninety-eight mornings kept its record without a word said over it. The height of the handle, the give of the shoulder at a step’s end, the small turn of the wrist that set the basket level again — these were held in the muscle the way a surgeon’s hands hold the feel of a cavity they have packed, a record of weight and not of cost. The arm knew what it had lifted and had never been told what the lifting was worth. It did not arrive anywhere. It went on carrying.

She went wide around the pan’s gone round ring on the wall’s low place, where nothing had stood for a month and no scrape, crumb, print or chip had come; around the trough at the yard well, bare and dark with its water called weather; around the space beneath the lowest rack of the drying racks, where the broken birth-slates lay and were entered by nobody; around the empty place in the returning line where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out; and around the place the carriers had crossed with a man breathing and the pale line at his gum. Each got a look and a wide step and no number. It was a way of walking a yard that has learned not to step where a thing is missing, the way a woman goes around a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it; and she gave the yard no name for any of the places, because a name is a handle and a handle is the beginning of a cost. Three absences left standing long enough would call themselves a cause and a cause would want a payer, and no third gap came and no fourth likeness was made a cause and no finger of his drew the line.

Under the roof she set him on his bed and not in the Debt Loft. She put the fold right at his ear without looking at his face, left the thread frozen, and set the pot rim out with its handle two inches beyond the reaching hand, her hand resting a half-beat longer at the setting than the setting needed. The widow’s mark hung where it hung; a dusk was not an opening and there had been no opening since before the kiln on Ember 1. She told the fire it was the thaw and banked the coals with the back of a broken pot, and the carried fire answered four low pulls with the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for a hundred and ninety-eight days. Through the finger’s-width door the river kept its fifth low note of water over water, and overhead the boards kept four nails, four, and a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts in three places and none rose up and became the others, because a man who wanted them to agree would have had to put a payer under all three and no payer stood to any of them. The two rooms did not touch: hers held a pot, a fire, a woman counting under her breath toward nothing entered, and his held a boy who could not close two inches and a number nobody had spoken. The wall between them kept both.

In the small hours he turned out the file the way a man lays instruments on a towel, with no towel and no instruments and no case, but the whole order of the laying. Six hearings of the full note stood beside and never among: five from four sources and the sixth out of a person as the men lifted her, filed where a thing out of a person must go and never with the sources. No seventh came and none was invented to fill the empty place, for an empty place kept empty is a finding and a finding filled to make a row come out even is a finding forged. Three near-full answers stood on their own tray, advanced and unpaid; the told bell lay beside the heard, the told figure beside the paid. Nine stoppings lay as objects and not a rule. The Seed 16 breath stood unspent at the top of every reach, and the loan that could hear was uncalled.

Three held changes stood on three places — the fraction at the iron, the beat under the shelf, the half-check on the stones — and no hand of his laid them across one another. The small dry scrape of a pot’s foot set down on loft stone stood on a fourth place; the pale line at a gum, read at two paces and worth nothing at two paces, stood on a fifth; the held inch, asked and answered and answered differently in the afternoon, stood on a sixth. The crust’s gap, the shelf’s two gaps, the pan’s gone ring and the field’s empty place lay on trays that did not touch, with no third gap come, no fourth likeness made a cause and no finger of his drawn across them. The stone lay cold in its unmarked pot in the loft, two years gone and unreconciled by not trying, which is not forgiving; the charcoal under the shelf was unread; the cost of the man carried out stood entered nowhere.

The figure that would be read at the Cross on the morrow was not entered in any of it. A figure read aloud is not the figure paid, and the printed one in twelve stood where it had been read, unchallenged, while the true subtraction went up the road in a locked box and was not brought back under this roof. The read figure lay beside the paid and never became one; it was a thing said over a crowd, and the crowd went back to its pigs, and nothing under this house had been entered by it. He set each thing down in its own hollow and nothing rested across anything else, for a rule is an account that has found a payer and no payer stood to any of them. He took the file up again in the same order, exact, complete and worth nothing. The hand stayed down against the fold, and the count went on.

— Day 199 —

Ember 20, Y.H. 813 (Tenthday)

The district had two names for its months and neither of them was wrong. Tallow was the firing month, Ember the kiln month, and a man who used both names in one breath was not corrected, because a correction is the first small entry in an account and there was no account in a pair of names. On the twentieth of Ember the clay road stood pale in the sun with the dust of a dry week, and Sella Vail kept the same half-pace from every open slate that she had kept on the first Account-day of the month, walking the same road the same distance to the same square with the same basket under her arm. The instruction went to the wool and to the river wind before it went to anything that might have answered it. “Keep it off his ears,” she said. She did not say it to the basket. The basket was carried without a word, the way a pot is carried that has been filled and must not be slopped, and the boy inside it came into his hundred and ninety-ninth day with nobody under any roof saying so, six months and nineteen days, told by nobody, the count going on in the dark of the wool where no slate in Sarn could reach it.

At the Cross she set the basket in the moot-post’s finger’s width of shade where no slate lay, the iron at his cheek cold enough to be a thing and not a promise, and she stood half a pace off, which was the distance at which a woman could be said to be near a thing without having spoken to it. She gave the wool its instruction again and gave the wind its instruction, and gave the basket no reason at all. Her hand went to the cord at her throat and stopped short of the widow’s mark; a reading was not an opening, and the mark had been touched once before the kiln on Ember 1 and at no shelf, well, road, basket or market since. Her other hand, the one with the little-door burn, shut half over nothing and opened again. She did not look into the basket. A woman who looks into a basket at a reading has begun to ask it a question, and a question asked of a basket goes where all such questions go in this district: to the pot, to the weather, to nobody who could be entered for the answer.

He lay at the low angle of the shade with the fold against his cheek and one leathery crust two inches from his ear, its empty place beside it still without a name and no third, the thread at his mouth frozen from the morning and unwiped because no instruction had ever come for the thread. Above him the square went on making its ordinary noise. Two hundred people leaned to the reading block with their backs to the sun, and eight pigs went over the wet boards with the mud throwing up in small dark fans, and a man with a goad kept his eyes on the pigs and not on the people, because a man who watches the crowd at a weighing is a man who wants to be entered for the pigs. The boards had been wetted to make the weights true, or so the saying went, and the water on them was called weather as the water in the bare trough was called weather, and neither was ever entered.

He had once stood at the edge of a field and decided which bodies would be carried and which would be left where they lay, and the crowd at a reading was a field of a different kind with the same order in it. He read the backs before he read the faces: the man with his collar held high and the cough he would not spend, the woman with clay dried pale in the creases of her wrists standing where the wind did not reach her, the child with the goad who was hungry and would be fed after the pigs. Nobody in the square knew that a finding was being taken off them in silence and entered nowhere, which was the only kind of finding he could still take. It was worth nothing. He took it anyway, the way he had once taken a blood pressure from a man already dying and written it on a chart nobody would read.

The bell went four, four and a gap. Nobody crossed himself at it. The boy had heard the bell of the Long Night told to him in a clean voice in a room he had never entered, and now he heard this bell with his own ear in a square he could not leave, and he kept the two beside each other and never laid one across the other, the heard beside the told as the told figure would presently lie beside the paid. The gap after the second four was not a fifth stroke and was not meant to be one. It was a place where a stroke had not come, and a place where a stroke had not come is not a stroke. Under a roof he knew well, the carried fire would be drawing in fours and leaving the silence where the fifth should have closed, its payer without a name for a hundred and ninety-nine days; overhead in that room the boards kept four nails, four, and a knot the width of a thumb. Here at his ear the river kept its fifth low note of water over water, a fifth thing that had never been counted among the hearings. Three counts in three places, and none rose up and became the others, because a man who wanted them to agree would have to put a payer under all three and no payer stood to any of them. He did not make a lesson of the gap. A lesson wants a teacher, and a teacher wants a payer.

Berr Clay came up short off the clay road a little before the top of the morning, the red wool cord of his office folded square under his coat instead of worn across it, and the green of tasted lead glaze at one corner of his mouth where the habit of the old kiln-masters had left it. He smelled of hot clay and, under that, of nothing at all, which is the most expensive smell in Grath. His hands were large and clean. He wiped his thumb twice on his apron before he touched nothing, once for the slate and once for the figure that would be on it, and held a narrow slate unopened to his forearm with the writing turned toward his sleeve. A finger went over the figure. His eyes rested on it a breath longer than the writing required, the way a surgeon’s eye rests on a finding before the chart is closed over it, and the finding stays what it was and the chart is neat. Sella, who had known his wife, read the green at his mouth as nerves; the masters read it as thoroughness. The boy, who had been a surgeon and was now a basket, read it as a habit with a taste in it and put it where a habit goes, not where a cause goes.

The printed figure stood where it had been read, unchallenged, and a figure read aloud was not the figure paid. There was no shout at the reading and no hand raised against the slate. The pigs went on over the boards. Someone at the turnips put a figure down beside another figure and left them standing side by side without making them agree, which is the whole art of a market, and the boy took the shape of it in without entering it anywhere.

No figure came off the slate. At the market’s edge a small shape in cast-offs worn like a uniform stood with silt dried pale in the creases of its wrists and a gap where a front tooth had been. No sound came out of it that any clerk could have taken down. No loaf passed anywhere within reach of the basket, no slate came near it, no sale was paid. From the low angle of the basket he saw the order of the hands before he saw what the hands were for. Berr’s twice-wiped thumb and his finger passed over the figure before Berr’s eyes took the crowd. Hessa’s hand found the slate before her eyes took the yard. The soft-cuffed clerk’s hand found his slate before his eyes took the stalls. Each order lay on a place of its own, and the two records going inward stayed two records going inward, the runner’s hand carrying and the clerk’s hand entering, with nothing laid across either. A row of such hands left standing long enough would call itself an account, and an account would go looking for a payer, and the boy knew better than to leave them standing that way. He had no hand to lay them out with and no slate to enter them on; he kept them by looking, which is a keeping that costs nothing and is worth nothing, and went on keeping it.

The crowd went back to the pigs. The bell’s gap stood where it had been and nobody crossed himself at it. Berr’s box went up the clay road locked with the true subtraction inside. Hessa’s slate went after it edge-on, the writing to her sleeve and no figure coming off it. The printed one in twelve stood where it had been read, unchallenged, and the figure read was not the figure paid, and the count in the wool went forward without a word spoken over it.

The basket was carried as a pot is carried that has been filled and must not be slopped, at the half-pace she had kept going up in the sun and would keep coming back in the dark, and the boy inside it came down the road with the market behind him and nobody under any roof asking what the figure had counted. The road had dried pale in the week’s heat. The ruts held the day’s dust in their shallow troughs, and the dust rose behind her step and settled again without ever being entered, as the district’s arithmetic had risen that morning and settled where it had been spoken.

Woodsmoke went before her in long low bars, laid one above another across the falling air, and the river wind came behind her with the taste of water over water. The two passed without taking each other’s colour, the smoke going up and the water going over, and she walked between them at her half-pace as a woman walks between two things she has been given no instruction to join. The arm that carried him had carried him a hundred and ninety-nine mornings. It kept the height of the handle, the give of the shoulder, the small turn of the wrist at the end of a step, a record in muscle that remembered weight and not cost. Had somebody asked that arm what it had carried, the arm could not have told them; it could only have gone on carrying. That was the house’s whole way of knowing a thing, by the shape of it in the hand and never by what it was worth.

She went wide around the pan’s gone round ring on the low place of the wall, where nothing had stood for a month and no scrape, crumb, print or chip had come; around the trough at the yard well, bare and dark, its water called weather; around the space beneath the lowest rack of the drying racks, where the broken birth-slates lay and were entered by nobody; around the empty place in the returning line where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out; and around the place the carriers had crossed with a man breathing and the pale line at his gum. Each got a look and a wide step and no number. It was the way a woman goes around a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and she gave the yard no name for any of the places, because a name is a handle and a handle is the beginning of a cost.

A neighbour come up from the racks with slip dried pale in the creases of her wrists stopped at the yard’s edge with a question already half out of her. What would the firing want, now the figure had gone up twice? Sella did not turn toward the basket. “Ask the pot,” she said. “Ask the weather. The house has said it.” The woman took the pot and the weather away as an answer and went off level with her empty hands, nothing priced, nothing refused, nothing slopped and nothing entered. The boy felt the question go past him and arrive nowhere, and kept the shape of its travelling where such shapes go, joined to no rule. The district’s figure had gone into the air over two hundred people and could not now be got back into a fist; under this roof a cost asked as a direction came to rest on a pot and went no further. It was a method for staying warm, and it had kept this house warm a long while without once saying who paid.

Three or four men had gone low into that room before first grey and he gave the house no name for them and did not say the room was cold. He kept the dusty forearms and the clean ones on one tray of his attention and asked neither which slate had been broken nor whether it was a birth-slate; the broken birth-slates lay under the lowest rack and were entered by nobody. The two shut-room tellings stayed two with no third, and the boy did not lay them across the dust or the damper or anything else standing near them.

“It doesn’t care whose hands,” Jorra said to the wheel before he said anything to a person. “It cares if they’re steady.” To the pot on the wheel he said, “You’ll do,” in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either. The man took the silence for the silence of a man at work and went off up the yard with his saying, which stayed a saying about a pot and was not made true as a lesson, and no pot was lost. Hot clay came before the grey, under the tallow and under that the wet wool, and under all of it the sour mineral breath of the unswept corner. The boy took the smells in their order as a man takes a pulse before a history, one thing arriving and then the next and the last thing staying longest, and Low Kiln came awake around him in that order too. It was the kiln month’s last working week and the district still called Tallow the firing month and Ember the kiln month, and corrected neither, as it had all year; a boundary drawn anywhere would have wanted a figure written on it, and there was no figure under this roof that anybody had been asked to write. Behind the wall the kiln drew in fours with the gap where a fifth pull should have closed, not a bell, and through the finger’s-width door the river kept its fifth low note of water over water, and above the bed the boards kept four nails, four, and a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts in three places, and none of them rose up and became the others, because a man who wanted them to agree would have had to put a payer under all three and no payer stood to any of them.

He was two hundred days old and six months and twenty days, and nobody under any roof said so. The number stood in the wool where the others had stood, exact, complete and unspent, and he had stopped expecting it to be spoken, which was not the same thing as not wanting it. Under the floor the carried fire gave four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have closed; its payer had gone two hundred days without a name. The one brown-edged crust lay leathery two inches from his ear with its empty place beside it unnamed and no third, the thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and been left unwiped, because nobody under any roof had been given an instruction about the thread, and cold had come as far as his cheek, his temple and the bare joint at his wrist where the wool rode back and stopped there as if it had reached the end of its own instruction. Sella Vail gave the morning its instruction before she gave it to anybody. It went to the wool, the fire, the pot, the broom and the running water, and never to the basket; the basket was carried as a pot is carried that has been filled and must not be slopped, and a pot that has been given no instruction is a pot that has been set down and left. Her eye went once to the clay jar cold under its glaze and her hand started toward it and shut half over nothing without a touch, the little-door burn closing on air as it closed on whatever came within reach of it, and then the hand went back to the work.

The carried fire missed its fifth under the floor, unnamed for two hundred days; the river kept its note of water over water, the boards four nails, four and a thumb-width knot. Three counts in three places, none became the others, for agreeing them would have wanted a payer and none stood to any.

Sella Vail gave the instruction to the wool, fire, pot, broom and running water, never the basket, and said the house where another woman would say I. She fed him from the house’s own dish, set the pot handle two inches beyond his reach, moved her lips along a two-gapped loaf row without a figure, and kept her finger from beneath the charcoal. Cost-questions went to the pot and the weather. The widow’s mark went untouched; there had been no opening since Ember 1. She carried him home around the pan’s gone ring and the yard’s absences, giving each a wide step and no number.

On the twentieth of Ember the district’s arithmetic changed again. Two hundred leaned to the block; the bell went four, four and a gap with nobody crossing. Berr Clay wiped his thumb twice, passed a finger over the slate and read One in twelve, as the notice says low into the crowd --- the figure read twice over the basket, once for the moot and once for those who came late. He called the levy, denied it with your house, never I, and went off with the box locked and the subtraction inside. Hessa’s slate went up the road unread; the clerk stayed set for an uncalled measure. It did not answer the man carried out breathing: no figure, no cause, his pale line where it was.

Tarn told the kindling six counted and the next he did not count, brought the ear-sentence to the word that would call the loan and stopped; nine stoppings stood with no tenth before the seventh year was earned. Jorra parted dust from slip, withheld a note known three turns ahead from a wrong wind, and held his bent finger in the damper until the sentence reached the pot --- remembered, not made again.

Four times he questioned his hand: down before the breath was done, down at once, held for the small intake, and in the small hours held through a whole breath at her fire, coming down with the breath. He filed each on its own place, not a hearing, seventh, stopping, payment, key, power or rule, no payer named. He still could not close two inches to the pot handle, lift the crust or speak a number. The body humiliated him and moved by an inch, and the inch was worth nothing. Two hundred stood exact in the wool, unspent, spoken by nobody.
