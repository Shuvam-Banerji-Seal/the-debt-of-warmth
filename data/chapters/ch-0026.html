The Hand That Does Not Come Back

Days 116–119 · Seed 27–30, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, Sill

You count them going in. You don’t count them coming out. What comes back is a row, and a place the yard goes around.

— A saying of the strip-keepers, Low Kiln

— Day 116 —

Seed 27, Y.H. 813

The going of the light came down over the worked strips east of Low Kiln the way chaff comes down in a still room, slowly, and settling on whatever has been left standing. The seed stood up in the rows and the turned brown ground kept the shape of every back that had bent over it since Seed 15, the heel-marks along the drills, the little dark pits where a fist had let seed go, the pale dust of the broken clods. The last hands were coming out of the strips now, shaking the dirt from their fists the way a man shakes water from a rag and gets most of it off, and the yard took them in the way it had taken everything this month: in glances and handfuls and the space where a man’s arm should have been. No figure went with them. A woman at the head of the second strip lifted two fingers with chaff worked deep into the creases and held them up a moment for the woman beside her to see, and did not say what they stood for, and shut them again. A man with a barrow came up behind her with the wooden rim of it against his hip, opened his mouth on the number of stooped backs the row had held, shaped the word with his teeth, and thought better of it. He shut his mouth the way the woman had shut her fist. The barrow went on up the yard with nothing in it that a slate could have read.

“Seed’s in,” the woman with the chaff said. She said it to the ground, because the ground was what had been done to and the ground was what would answer in the end. “The rest is weather.” She rubbed the two fingers together and the chaff came away in little dry flakes and went down onto the turned earth, and she closed the fingers into a fist again and went to the next hole as though nothing had been held in them at all. Her sleeve was rolled above the elbow and the skin there was the colour of the dry slip in the yard, with a pale line across the wrist where a rope had once been and a scab the size of a pea on the knuckle. The woman beside her looked at the fist, looked at the row, and went on with her own holes. Nobody asked what the two fingers had been. In this district a thing was counted when it was in the pot and not before, and a woman holding up two fingers in a field was a woman counting nothing any clerk would want, and the chaff went on falling out of her crease into the brown ground as she worked.

The man with the barrow stopped where the strip broke into the yard and set it down on its legs. The rim had been rubbed smooth by hands that had pushed it through four plantings and the wood had gone the colour of old bone where the hips went; a crack ran along the under-edge and had been stuffed once with clay and gone dry again. He stood a moment with his mouth still half open on the number, looking along the row as though the row might supply the word if he waited long enough. It did not. The seed stood up in its small green and gave back nothing that would have served. He shut his mouth, spat once into the dirt for the having of it, and took the barrow up again by the handles. A girl at the rack end called to somebody about a pot and got no answer, and he did not turn, because the question had been given to the pot and not to him, which was the way questions were given in this yard. The barrow’s legs lifted out of the soft ground with a small sucking sound and he went off up the yard with his back to the strips.

That was the whole of the counting: two fingers, a half-shaped word, a glance along a row. The district had kept its figure for the planting the way a woman keeps a coal under ash, by covering it and going on with the day. It had wanted hands in the month of Seed and hands had gone in, and the strips had been worked and the seed was up, and the figure for what it had cost had gone from the strip to a man’s teeth and back again and arrived nowhere. Weather would have the last word, the yard said, and the weather had not spoken yet, and a body got used to going on without the word because there was nothing else to do. Sella Vail had carried the boy to the very edge of the work and stood a half-pace from every open slate, the basket held level the way a woman carries a bowl filled to the lip and will not let it slop. She did not look into the basket. Beyond the fold the rows went on with their small green under the going light, and the river wind came off the Ilm with the sour edge of mud in it, and she gave the instruction to the wool and to the wind and never to the basket.

The hands came out of the last strip and the line of them shortened toward the yard gate, and there was one place in the line that the yard went around. It went around it the way it went around other places: the clean dark line where Ash had turned under the corner of the Vail kiln-house, the space beneath the lowest rack that a pail of hot water might have stood under if anybody had set a pail of hot water there. A woman with a pot came up the strip’s edge and stepped wide of the space without looking at what she was stepping wide of, and a man following her stepped wide of it too because the woman had, and a girl with a broom went around it with the bristles lifted and set them down again beyond it. Nobody said what the space was. Nobody called it an accident or sent for anybody or put a figure against it. One of the hands that had gone in on Seed 15 had not come out with the rest. Nobody said so. It had been a place in the line in the morning and it was a place in the line at dusk and the going of the light found it still gone around, exact and unentered, held the way a coal is held under ash.

“Mind there,” the woman with the pot said to the girl with the broom, and the girl minded, stepping the wider way and setting her bristles down beyond it. The words were ordinary words about a place in the ground and did not say which place or why, and the woman went off with her pot under her arm and did not look back at what she had gone around. A man come up from the bends stopped with his foot already set toward the space, took it back and set it down on the other side of it, and went on, and the two men beside him went on too without asking him why he had changed his foot. If a hand were lifted out of the ground in a month or a year, a slate would be found for it and a figure would be made to stand at the foot of it; if no hand came up, the space would go on being gone around and the month would go on standing short without a figure. The yard kept the place the way it kept the clean dark line and the space under the rack, by going wide and entering nothing and calling it nothing.

The boy read the going-around the way he had read the shut fist at the well three days before: a chart closed with a neat line drawn over a note nobody had opened. Exact, complete, worth nothing. He had once stood at the foot of a bed and drawn such a line over a man already beyond the room and gone on to the next, and the neatness of the line had been the whole of the harm; here the line was drawn by feet stepping wide and the note beneath it was left where it lay. He set the field’s empty place on a place of its own. He did not lay it beside the shelf’s two gaps in the next room or the one stiff crust in the fold or the empty place at his ear, because a likeness laid on a likeness in this district grew a cause as surely as silt gathers behind a weir, and a fourth would have made the cause with a clean hand at the foot of it. The clean hand was the one object in this world he had spent two lives declining to become. He kept his own hand out of the field.

“Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. “It’s the river wind.” She said it twice, the way a woman tells a pot a thing and then tells it again because the pot has not moved. To the wind she gave the rest of it, and to the basket she gave nothing at all. When the woman with the pot asked whose boy it was, Sella said “The house’s,” and went on with the fold, so that the answer stood where an answer given to a pot would stand and went no further. Her little-door burn shut on nothing as her hand passed the basket’s rim and opened on the handle when she took the weight again. The grey streak at her temple showed pale in the dying light off the river, a single pale thread in a head of coal-black that she said had come in the winter Garl died and would not discuss further. She kept her half-pace from every open slate and did not look into the basket toward the strips.

Cold arrived in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist. The wool rode back from the bare joint so the cold found the place it had come for, and the river wind went under the fold at his ear and found the other place. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and nobody put it right, because the instruction had gone to the wool and not to the wandering. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours under the roof and had not been wiped, because nobody under that roof had been given an instruction about the thread and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. He wanted the stiff brown-edged crust two inches from his ear in the fold, and the breast, and the bucket’s lip two paces off where a bucket stood with a cold iron handle on it, and the number of his days said aloud by somebody who would have known what a number of days counted when they said it. He got none of them and could ask for none of them. He was a man who had once held pressure in a cavity with both hands while a student counted the pulse out loud, and he could not close two inches on a fold that smelled of wet wool and nothing else. Both facts were true, and the second was the one that was loud here, as it had been every day since the stone at his belly went cold.

He watched the order of the yard as he had watched it in a better-lit room: the record before the person, the instruction before the hand, the going-around before the look. The woman’s two fingers had gone up before her face had; the man’s mouth had shaped the figure before his eyes had gone to the barrow; the girl’s broom had gone around the space before anybody had said there was a space to go around. It was the same order he had kept on the other side of his life, where a chart was laid open before a family was told and a name was written before a body was carried out. Here nobody was being carried out. The order went on all the same, exact and worthless, the way instruments laid out on a towel are exact and worthless until a hand takes one up, and no hand in Low Kiln was going to take this one up today.

His small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the worked strips and came down. One hundred and sixteen stood where one hundred and fifteen had stood under the roof in the small hours, exact, unspent and spoken by nobody in the district, while the seed stood up in the last tend and the line came out of it with one place gone around. He had long ago stopped expecting to be told and had not stopped wanting it, and the two went on together the way a coal goes on under blind ash, giving back nothing a man could see and keeping its heat all the same.

The yard turned away from the strips into the dusk. The man with the barrow went off up the road with his mouth shut and the rubbed rim against his hip; the woman with the chaff went home with the two fingers closed in a fist nobody would ask about; the girl’s broom went around the space and left it where it was. Sella turned the basket back toward the kiln-house with the level held and the fold at his ear, going around the space as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and did not look into the basket toward the strips. Behind them the empty place in the line stood where the yard had left it, gone around and entered by nobody, and the going light went off the worked ground and left the rows standing on their own small green. Nothing was named, nothing was counted, nothing was paid, and the place went on being kept.

Back under the Vail kiln-house roof the going of the light came down through the oiled paper in thin grey squares, and the house took the boy in the way a cup takes water poured against its inside: carefully, and with a little left over that nobody entered. Sella Vail set the basket down where no slate lay and gave the bucket its instruction before she gave it to anybody.

“Round the lip,” she said to the bucket. “No slops on the ash.” To the broom she gave the next: “Go around. It cost enough to come in; it will cost more to take out.” To the pot she gave the one about the door, and to the fire the one about its own cold mouth, and to the basket she gave nothing at all, the way a woman sets a bowl filled to the lip on a table and does not speak to it in case it should slop. The words went to the things and the things took them; the words did not go to the basket, and the basket did not expect them. She said *the house* where another woman would have said I, and nothing in the room turned its head at that, because the house had been saying it for three years and the boy had heard it since before he could hear anything else.

She went around the space beneath the lowest rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and kept her half-pace from every open slate between the door and the bench. The river wind came under the eaves off the Ilm with the sour edge of mud in it, and she gave the instruction to the wool and to the wind and never to the basket: keep it off his ears, it is the river wind. His small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth of the fire and came down. The fire was two paces off and the hand had as much chance of reaching it as it had of reaching the year before last. Both facts were true, and the second was the one that was loud here.

The wool had ridden back from the bare joint so the cold found the place it had come for, and the wind under the eaves went under the fold at his ear and found the other place. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and nobody put it right, because the instruction had gone to the wool and not to the wandering. He wanted the stiff brown-edged crust two inches from his ear and the breast and the bucket’s lip two paces off with its cold iron handle, and the number of his days said aloud by somebody who would have known what a number of days counted when they said it. the loaf shelf stood in the next room, and the house kept its days there badly: loaves pushed along a board, a glance at the clay jar on the wall, and a space at the row’s end nobody called a figure. A neighbour came in with a pot under her arm, come up from the worked strips where the seed stood up and the hands had come out with one place gone around. There was chaff dried in the creases of the fist that held the pot’s handle, and mud on the skirt-hem where she had come across the low ground, and she set the pot down on the bench with the care of a woman who has carried it a long way and means to carry it away again. Then she reached past the bare board before her eyes found the basket, the way a hand goes to a well-lip before the face goes to the water, opened for the half-beat it takes to find what is not there, and closed on the hard heel of a loaf beyond. The half-beat was small enough to be nothing. It was the same smallness as the shut fist in the field and the two fingers with chaff worked into the creases, exact, complete and worth nothing.

“The end place came in with the planting,” she said to the board, or to her pot; it was not said to the boy and did not want an answer from him. “And this one is the price of a wet thing set down wrong.” She shaped the second saying with her mouth as she said it, the way a woman shapes a thing she has said before and expects to say again, and put the loaf under her arm with the pot and went off without once seeing that she had laid two bare boards under one cause, or that the two boards were one absence, or that there was a third empty place in a room her feet had never entered. The saying went out with her through the door and left the shelf standing with two gaps and no figure over either.

Sella moved her lips along the loaf row without a figure, the way a woman counts what she will not say, and did not look toward the basket while she did it. The loaves went along the board in the shape of the days to Kindling, and her lips came back to the same place twice and went on, and the shape stood where a figure would have stood if a figure had been wanted. She had heard the neighbour’s two sayings and heard them as two sayings and not one absence, and the private roll she kept stayed where it was kept and was not opened for a neighbour with a pot.

The boy set the shelf’s two gaps on a place of their own, and the one stiff brown-edged crust in the fold with its unnamed empty place beside it on another, and the neighbour’s one-breath joining on a third. Three places that did not touch. He allowed no third gap and no fourth likeness; a likeness laid on a likeness grew a cause as surely as silt gathers behind a weir unless a hand held them apart, and the clean hand at the foot of a cause was the one object he had spent two lives declining to become. He kept his own hand out of it. That the neighbour’s hand had gone to the board before her eyes had gone to him was the order of the house laid bare — the record before the person, the pot before the child — and it was the truest thing said under this roof today and the least spendable. He had learned in a better-lit room what a neat line drawn over a note does to the note, and he did not draw one here.

Once, and only once, Sella’s glance went to the clay jar on the wall shelf, cold under its glaze, and went back to the loaves. Her hand did not go to the widow’s mark at her throat, because a shelf is not an opening and she had been given no instruction to open anything. The clean dark line where Ash had turned under the corner lay where it lay, and the new finer grey hairline beside it lay where it lay, and neither was swept; the house called both the thaw and the thaw would go when it went. No opening had come under this roof in all his days, and the roll that would have entered one stayed unshown.

At the going of the light she carried him to his bed under the roof and not to the Debt Loft, and set him down the way a woman sets down a pot she has carried a long way and cannot bring herself to let go of. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without once looking at his face, and left the thread of drool at his mouth frozen and unwiped, because nobody under this roof had been given an instruction about the thread and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. Her hands were never still; when they had nothing to do they counted, and here they counted the breaths at his ear and entered the count nowhere. The hand rested a half-beat longer than the setting needed before it went away. The half-beat was the whole of what the house would allow itself, and it went out with her hand.

She told the fire it was the thaw, and turned the coals with the back of a broken pot, and said “The house will keep it” where another woman would have said I. She stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand counting something under her breath toward no opening, her lips moving the way they had moved along the loaf row, and pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn. The grey streak at her temple showed pale in the dying light off the coals, a single pale thread in a head of coal-black that she said had come in the winter Garl died and would not discuss further. She set the pot where it could be found in the dark and did not look toward the bed. The little-door burn closed on nothing as the hand came away.

The boy turned out the private file as a man lays instruments on a towel, and found every object where he had left it. Six hearings of the full note stood beside and never among, five from four sources and the sixth, the small flat knock out of a woman as the men lifted, from a person; no seventh had come and he had not invented one. Three near-full answers stood advanced and not paid on their own tray, and three intervals on another, and beside the intervals both the half-beat of a stroke checked and the stroke that went on without it, two objects and not a rule. The told bell lay beside the heard bell in two places that did not touch. The word forty lay unspoken with the readable double turn beside it and the weight of a thumb a little apart from both, and he had not said it and no thumb had counted the knot. Four low things stood in four places with the river’s note of water over water a fifth, and the two tellings of the shut counting room lay joined to no kiln, and the double bite of the damper lay filed as an object joined to no firing. The held breath from Seed 16 lay where it had lain, unspent, and the collector sentence nobody forged lay beside it, and the loan that could hear was still uncalled. The figure the yard had not given for the hands that went into the strips lay on a place of its own, a chart closed with a neat line over a note nobody had opened.

To these he added what the day had given: the place in the returning line that the yard went around with a look and no figure, and the neighbour’s one-breath joining of two bare boards. He set them on places of their own and laid no finger across either. He did not lay the field’s gap beside the shelf’s two, or the neighbour’s saying beside the crust in the fold, or any of them beside the word forty; a line drawn through the places would have made a cause with a clean hand at the foot of it, and the clean hand was the thing he had spent two lives declining to become. The file lay complete and exact, and there was nobody under the roof who could have read a word of it. His exactness was the only arithmetic in the district and the more exact it grew the more it isolated him, and he went on keeping it because there was nothing else to keep.

The carried fire answered four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, warming the boards by degrees under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, its payer unnamed for a hundred and sixteen days. It took its coal and gave back its four and kept the fifth where it had always kept it, and the house said the thaw and went on. Every warmth in this room had a payer somewhere, and no slate in the district carried the name.

The door stood a finger’s width open. Through the gap came the river going over itself with the low note of water over water, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming; through the floor came the kiln’s four, four and a gap. The two counts did not meet. The three counts did not become one. Above him the boards kept four nails, four, and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb, and the boy lay with the count where the count had always been.

His small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down. One hundred and sixteen stood where one hundred and fifteen had stood in the small hours, exact, unspent and spoken by nobody in the district, while the river went over water beyond the finger’s-width gap and the fire missed its fifth breath under the floor and the two counts did not meet. The fold smelled of wet wool and tallow and nothing else, the crust lay stiff two inches from his ear with the empty place beside it unnamed, and the thread at his mouth froze again in the dark and was not wiped, because nobody had been given an instruction about the thread.

Before first grey the men came up the Clay Yard with their collars high, and the boy was carried out to the edge of the light at the Vail wheel-shed and set down where no slate lay, the way a woman sets a pot at the edge of a table and not in its middle. They went in low-voiced to the room off the wheel-shed, which stood a finger’s width open and did not shut, and the boy did not go in and no name was taken down. Jorra Vail came out through that finger’s width of door ahead of them and gave the house no name for them and did not say the room was cold. He had learned to carry an undeclared thing the way a potter carries a cracked vessel: level, and with the hand under it. The low voices went on behind him and came to nothing in the yard, and the yard went on without them, the way a floor goes on under a spilled bucket that somebody will wipe on the morrow. Collars held high are a way of giving a face to nobody, and a face given to nobody is a face no slate can take; the boy had known men who did this in a better-lit room, and he knew the shape of it here without needing the words for it in Grathic.

Sella Vail had given the instruction to the wool before she gave it to anybody: keep it off his ears, it is the river wind. The wind came under the eaves off the Ilm with the sour edge of mud in it, went under the fold at his ear and found the place it had come for. Cold arrived in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist. The wool had ridden back from the bare joint in the night and the cold found that place too. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and nobody put it right, because the instruction had gone to the wool and not to the wandering. He counted the boots going past the basket without meaning to, the way he had once counted a pulse before putting a hand into a wound; four pairs, and a pause, and another. The count arrived nowhere, because there was nowhere in this district to put a count of men. The men did not look at the basket. A thing that does not look at a basket has already decided what a basket is worth, and the boy had spent a hundred and seventeen days being worth what the district decided, which was nothing that could be entered.

Jorra came through with fine grey slate dust ground small as meal into both forearms past the bent little finger — not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel, which dried to the colour of old bone and stayed on the outside of a man. This had been worked inward, into the creases, the way grit works into a knee or a splinter into the flesh beside a nail. The boy kept the dusty forearms and the clean ones of the other mornings on one tray, two states of one man’s morning, and asked neither which slate had been broken nor whether it was a birth-slate. Those were the boy’s two questions and he could not ask either, so they stayed where questions are allowed to last for years, which in this house was anywhere and forever. The broken birth-slates lay under the lowest board of the racks, gone around as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, entered by nobody, and the space above them went around the basket as the basket went around the space. A district can go around a thing for three years and call the going-around care; the boy had learned to go around it too, and to call nothing at all.

He spoke to the wheel before he spoke to anyone. “It doesn’t care whose hands,” he said to it, in the flat voice he kept for the tools and the pots and never for the basket. “It cares if they’re steady.” The wheel gave back its low note, the note of a thing that has turned long enough not to mind whose hands turn it. Garl’s seat kept its twenty-year lean to the left and Jorra put his weight into it the way a man puts weight into a hollow he has never managed to fill. To a pot coming up under his hands he said “You’ll do,” in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either, and he knew the full note of a Vail pot before it left the wheel — knew it the way a man knows a vessel by the feel of it under his hand before he puts iron to it — and would not say so before a kiln-master come up from High Kiln, who would call it early and make something of it. A man who knows a thing early and says it early spends the knowing; a man who keeps it keeps the one piece of the house nobody can take off him. The boy knew that he knew and kept it on a place of its own and did not lay it beside the dust, because a likeness laid on a likeness grows a cause unless a hand holds them apart.

The kiln-master went along the row of finished pots with his hands behind his back and said nothing that a slate could carry away, and Jorra let him, and turned the wheel a half-revolution and said to the pot under his breath that it would do. Under the shed roof the air held damp clay and tallow and the sweetish edge of lead glaze carried up from High Kiln on a bad wind, and under that, faintly, the sour mineral tang that came up out of the corner of the house where Ash had lain two years and would lie until somebody burned it and called it dealt with. The boy lay at the edge of the light with the small wet sound coming and going at his mouth and wanted the pot’s note, and wanted the breast, and wanted the number of his days said aloud by somebody who would have known what a number of days counted when they said it. He got the note. The other two he had learned not to expect, which is not the same thing as stopping wanting them.

Sella had not finished telling Jorra the fire was low. “It’s low,” she said, and the rest of the instruction was still in her mouth — the part about feeding it before anybody fed themselves, Garl’s proverb that had gone to a pot with no crack long ago and cost the more now that there was nobody to say it in his own voice. Jorra’s bent finger went into the cold iron of the damper ahead of her sentence. The iron took the warmth out of the fingertip and kept it, giving a little back as it warmed; it is the nature of iron to take first and return what it has no use for. He moved the tongue of iron a hair’s breadth and left it standing there. The end of her instruction arrived at the pot instead of at him, and the household straightened around his silence the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted: Sella’s hand finished its half-circle with the broken pot, Tarn’s broom found its line, the wheel went on turning, and the low voices in the room off the shed went lower. Nobody had ordered the straightening and nobody had observed it. Obedience that arrives ahead of the instruction is obedience and a correction at once, and both of them went past the basket without a word.

The boy read the iron once more as he had read it every time the finger had gone into it, and kept the reading longer this time because the thing had been done before he was told it was being done. It was the bite that holds a vessel whole and it was the bite that can take the hand off a man. One piece of iron, two mercies, and no word in Grathic that held both. He had learned in a better-lit room what a clamp does when it is left too long on a limb, and what happens when it is taken off too soon, and here the two were the same object with the same name, and nobody under the roof would have known what he was looking at. He filed the reading as an object worth nothing, joined to no kiln, and set it beside the other object on the same tray without drawing a line between them. The two tellings of the shut counting room lay on their own tray and had not been brought across; nothing under this roof was going to bring them across today either. The truest thing in the room was the least spendable thing in it, and it lay where no Court thumb would ever read it.

Tarn took the broom only after Sella had given it its instruction. She gave it to the broom first — go around, it cost enough to come in, it will cost more to take out — and only then let the broom be taken, and Tarn took it up as a man takes up a tool already named, saying “Yes, house” to an early thaw and a late one in the same breath, agreeing so entirely that an argument had nowhere to put its foot, keeping the late because a late thaw puts off the paying though the paying had stood in the ruts since the water went. His seventh year was pressed to the very edge of the sill and still held. The small bench knife lay at the edge with six years standing in a mark his thumb had worn almost flat and the seventh nearly gone, the one entry in this house the house could not check, and to the kindling he said “Six counted. The next I don’t count.” The unforged collector sentence stayed where he kept the seventh year, and neither was entered. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line and no scrubbing lifted it; the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back.

His chest had been tight since the dark and the breath he had decided not to spend on Seed 16 was still unspent. There was a small interval at the top of each reach where the breath should have gone in: the broom went up, the interval opened, the breath did not come, the broom came down. He had been sent once for a collector in the Long Cold and come back at the frozen ford and told the house it had been done, and he kept that sentence where he kept the year and never looked along the road that went down toward the Fishstairs. A loan that can hear is a loan that can be called, and the sentence that would call it had not been said under this roof in six years and would not be said today.

Sella set the basket just inside the finger’s-width-open door of House Silt, at the edge of the light and away from every place a slate might lie, and gave the instruction to the wool and never to the basket. She opened no slate. The errand that had carried her three doors stayed an errand to a room and did not turn into a walk to Slate Row, though most of what this room held would have read better there. She stood a half-pace off with her burn shut on nothing. In the back room a neighbour scrubbed at a board with lye water and did not look up at the writing, the tin or the fold pressed too long; a low song came up through the floor with its words flattened out of sense by work. Above the abandoned board-bed the round pewter tin kept its pale fold at the seam and its thumb-bright rub on the lid, unopened.

Alda Reed came short, as a woman comes who has been called out of a room where somebody else has just been fed: soap-and-milk gone cold under river silt, lye sour under the soap, her nails scrubbed to the quick with clay still under them. She had her birth-slates under her arm and a narrow slate of her own in the fold of her grey shawl. She crossed to the bench and laid the writing-down flat before she turned toward the fold. It was the order of a hand that reaches for the record before the person, and it did not vary; he had watched it from a basket at a market’s edge and from a bed and had never once seen her reverse it. He had watched a senior man lay his hand on a chart before he looked at the patient under it, and learned there what the order meant: the record was the thing that would still be there when the person was carried out. There was no word in Grathic for that order and he had no voice to spend on it, and she performed it again now in a room where the person was not going to be carried out at all.

She pressed the wool flat at his shoulder a breath longer than a fold requires. The red edge of the birth-flower showed where the wool had ridden back at his wrist, a branch of it running from the third rib down the forearm and stopping short of the hand, and she crossed two fingers at the corner of it before she knew she had done it, the sign made in front of a basket and covered before it could be read. She covered it with the date she had given the woman: Stone seven. The account went up on the eighth, before the paper went out. It stood now over a woman more mornings dead than any living person keeps count of, uncollected, and no living person could take it off the sign.

“Warm and breathing,” Alda said. “That’s all I’ve set down.”

It was exact. It was not true. He had no way to tell her so and no standing if he had, and she did not wait for an answer; she went on with dates after the question had stopped, because dates are a handle on a thing too large to carry: a cooper’s seventh in Mud End, a potter’s third child in Stonemouth, the last high water, the exact day of a birth in a house nobody in this room would ever enter. The dates came faster than dates had any business coming, and the room arranged itself around them the way a market arranges itself around a reading it has not understood.

Her hand went once into her bodice to the little tin on its cord and came away without opening it. The grey line of the cord showed for a moment at the edge of her shawl, a small weight at the end of it, and was gone. On her own narrow slate she wrote what she did not leave lying about, in the second hand no Court physician had ever seen, and turned it writing-down into her shawl toward a floorboard in a house the boy could not name: six pages in that hand, all unread, the sixth written weeks ago and no seventh written today, and the account not paid again. Below the pewter tin the fold of grey wool held only the hollow where the small pale river stone had once lain, and nobody in the house said either thing was gone.

Two private records in two materials stood apart in one room and were carried to no door: the tin above the bed with its pale fold and thumb-bright rub, and the turned slate going toward the floorboard. Mira’s cord and her second twist lay on another board in another part of the town, and Orra’s knots lay beside it without touching, and the three records stood in three places that did not touch. The boy set them where they stood and wrote no cause across the space between them. He could not read Grathic, could not ask which house held the floorboard, could not hand the gesture back to the woman who made it. A date laid over a sign is a cloth laid over a thing nobody wants looked at, and a cloth is not a cure and not an answer and not an opening.

Alda went out as she had come in, short, with the birth-slates under her arm, and Sella asked nothing about the writing or the tin or the fold pressed too long. A half-pace and a turned slate stood between the two women for the whole of the visit, which was the arrangement, and neither had put a word into it in three years. Sella lifted the basket level and carried him back out at the half-pace from every open slate, going around the space under the lowest rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and did not look into the basket on the way.

A likeness laid on a likeness grows a cause the way silt gathers behind a weir if no hand holds them apart, and the clean hand at the foot of a cause was the one object he had spent two lives declining to become. The account stood advanced and not paid, toward a far-off day when it might want a name, and no name came today.

Below the sill a child’s foot stayed dark inside three turns of wool. It was the cold to the woman who had wrapped it and weather to the yard, and the two facts went past each other as they had gone past each other every morning of his life and never met. The extremity went before the core; the face put on its almost-smile that was not peace; the afterdrop waited on the far side of a warming nobody would perform. He knew the whole of it the way he had once known it under the lamps of a trauma bay, and the reading worked perfectly and bought nothing: no silt-leach, no cause, no cure, no word for it in the language of the house, no name to carry to any door, and no way to close two inches of his own hand on a fold. It was the most exact thing in the room and the least spendable. He had heard tell, from other mouths and never from hers, that the woman under this roof had once sat three nights with a child of her own who cooled while everybody was kind; he had not laid that story beside the dark foot, and he did not lay it there now. A story from a yard and a foot below a sill were two things in two places, and he had spent a hundred and eighteen days learning the cost of drawing the line.

She set the pot where it could be found in the dark and did not look toward the bed. The fold lay where she had put it, one stiff brown-edged crust in it and the empty place beside it still without a name, and the thread at his mouth had frozen again in the going light and nobody under the roof had been given an instruction about the thread. Every warmth in this house had a payer somewhere and no slate in the district carried the name.

The fire’s four, four and a gap came up through the floor and the river’s note of water over water came through the finger’s-width door, and the two counts in two places did not meet, and the three counts did not become one. His small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note and came down. One hundred and eighteen stood where one hundred and seventeen had stood in the small hours, exact, unspent and spoken by nobody who would have known what a number of days counted when they said it. Seed 30, Tenthday, Y.H. 813, Account-day and the last market of Seed

The last market of Seed came up over the Cross with a thin light the colour of skimmed slip, the kind that shows everything and warms nothing. Sella Vail carried the boy down from Low Kiln at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, going around the space beneath the lowest rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and set the basket in the moot-post’s finger’s width of shade where no slate lay. The instruction went to the wool. “Keep it off his ears. It’s the river wind.” It went into the wool and into the wind off the Ilm and never into the basket, and she stood her half-pace off with the little-door burn closing first on the handle and then on nothing. Cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, the wool riding back from the bare joint so the cold found the place it had come for. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and nobody at the moot had been given an instruction about that sound. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had wiped it. He was a man who could have told you by the feel of the back of a hand on a brow how far a body had gone, and he could not close two inches on the rim of the basket that held him. Both things were true. The second was the one that was loud.

Across the square the reading block stood with the day’s mud drying on it in the shape of boots. Two hundred people leaned toward it in the way a crowd leans when a man is about to speak low, because if he speaks low they must come close and being close is itself a kind of having been told. Eight pigs went over the weighing board with the assessor’s hand on the slate before his eyes went to the rail and his mouth went afterward, entering them before anybody could ask which eight he meant. Beyond the north road the worked strips stood with the seed up, and one place in the line was still gone around with a look and no figure. The hands had come out of it the day before. One of the hands that had gone in on Seed 15 had not come out with the rest, and the yard had gone around the space the way a woman goes around Ash left in a corner, and gone around it again this morning on the way to the reading. No body had come up. No cause had been said. No name had been given the space and no figure set at the foot of it. The planting month ended on this day and Reed came on the morrow, and the space went into the new month still held and still unentered.

Berr Clay came up to the block short, off the north road, with the red wool cord of his office folded under his coat instead of worn across it and the green of tasted glaze at one corner of his mouth. He smelled of hot clay and, under it, nothing at all, which was the most expensive smell in Grath. His hands were large and clean, the hands of a man who had once carried clay and had then stopped carrying it and kept the carrying in the way he held things. He wiped his thumb twice on his apron before he touched anything, once for the slate and once for the figure that would be on it, and held a narrow slate unopened against his forearm while he passed a finger over what was written and let his eyes rest on it a breath longer than the writing required. It was the check of a man setting down a dish already sure it will not slop, a small careful passing of the thumb that cost nothing and would be entered nowhere. The boy set the twice-wiped thumb on a place of its own. He had watched a midwife reach for a slate before a child, a neighbour reach for a bare board before she looked at him, a clerk behind a Court table put hand to paper before eye to face, a man price a basket without looking into it, Jorra speak to the wheel before the pots, Sella give the instruction to the broom before anybody, Alda lay her writing-down on the bench before the fold. Berr’s thumb made another shape on another place, and the boy laid no finger across any of them, because a likeness laid on a likeness grows a cause as surely as silt gathers behind a weir, and the clean hand at the foot of a cause was the one object he had spent two lives declining to become.

Berr read low, and the crowd leaned in.

“One in twelve, as the notice says.”

He said it into the top of the two hundred the way a man says a thing to a pot, without raising his voice and without looking to see who had heard. The notice had been printed through two bad winters and a war of deductions that he called, when pressed, a disagreement about arithmetic; a man could stand under a printed figure all morning and never think to ask whose arithmetic it agreed with. “The levy is called,” he said. “It is not called by me.”

A man come up from the strips brought a number up out of a fist and held it a moment in the open palm for the yard to see, the way a man shows a caught thing before he decides whether to keep it. “What did the planting cost,” he said, “now the hands have been in it?”

Berr did not look at the palm. He passed his finger over the figure on his slate again, the way a surgeon goes back to the chart before he goes back to the patient, and the boy knew the order before the man’s mouth had finished closing.

“A number goes into a slate, not into a strip,” Berr said. “If your house wants it again, there’ll be a slate at the watch.”

Your house. Never I. He took himself out of his own sentence as neatly as a surgeon takes a hand out of a field before the dressing goes on, leaving the field open and clean and nobody’s, and the man with the number stood with his palm still half open and nowhere to put it. He shut the fist and put it back into his sleeve, and the number went from the fist to Berr’s mouth to the shoulder of a man going up toward High Kiln and arrived nowhere. That was the district’s whole arithmetic, done in the open air over worked ground: a figure read aloud that was not the figure paid, a figure put into a slate that was not a figure put into a strip, a figure withheld in the very act of planting withheld like any other figure. The true subtraction sat in a locked box in a house up the road and had not been shown to anybody. The figure that went into the slate did not know the hand that had not come back out of the strip. It did not know there had been a hand. It did not know there had been a strip.

The boy kept Berr’s twice-wiped thumb and his finger passing over the figure on a tray of their own and did not join them to any other record-first hand. A figure read over the top of a basket was a figure read over the top of a basket, and the basket had stood in the moot-post’s shade since morning with a child in it who had no name any slate wanted. Nobody had looked in. Nobody had entered him. The crowd leaned a little closer at the reading and went back to its pigs when the reading was done, and the moot went on being a moot with a space in it shaped like the hollow of a wheel-seat that nobody would fill.

The sentence went around the square the way a bucket goes around a yard of thirsty people, passed from a fist to a mouth to a shoulder, and nobody thought to ask who was carrying it or where it had been filled. A woman with a shawl over her head said something about the thaw and the late rains, and Berr answered her with an instruction about the notice, which was how he apologised, and she went away with the answer as though she had been given one. The boy had learned the shape of that grammar in the better-lit room of his other life: the speaker taken out of the sentence, the figure left standing in it, the cost carried by nobody. He had once let a chart go by with a note turned down under his own signature, and the shape of it had stayed with him for two lives since. Here the chart was a narrow slate held unopened against a forearm, and the signer was a man wiping his thumb clean, and the not-reading was done in the open air before two hundred people who leaned in to hear it.

The bell went four, four and a gap, longer than a church bell, and nobody crossed himself. The boy kept the heard bell on a place of its own beside the told bell of the Long Night, two counts in two places that did not touch, because a bell heard in a market and a bell told in the dark in a house with no fire are the same four strokes and the same missing stroke and not the same thing. Below the north bridge at the Fishstairs the soft-cuffed clerk was still set at the out-of-line landing with his cuff grey from washing and his slate held to his forearm for a measure nobody had called, his hand going to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls. The market sold its eels over the top of him as though a man with a slate were a thing a landing always had. The boy kept the grey of the washed cuff on one place and the slate held at the edge on another. A man who washed his own cuff had a hunger or a habit; a man who held a slate where nobody else could see the figure had a use. The two readings stood edge to edge and did not touch, and he made no rule of either, because a measure set down before anybody called it was a shape and not yet a cause, and the day it wanted a name would come from another direction and another mouth. The steps came back four, four and a landing a hand’s breadth out of line where a step should have been, and a whole market walked over it carefully without making a fall of it: a girl with a creel took it sideways and caught the rail and went on selling, a man with a pot stepped over and did not look at what he had stepped over, and nobody called it an accident. The boy set the out-of-line landing beside the missing fifth stroke and laid no finger across them. A landing out of line and a bell with a gap were two things in two places, and the market walked over the first and went on with the second the way it went on with weather.
