Hands for the Strips

Days 104–106 · Seed 15–17, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, Sill

The seed goes in by the fist and the hands go in by the look. Ask either what it cost and it will tell you the weather.

— A planting saying of Low Kiln

— Day 104 —

Seed 15, Y.H. 813

The brown skin of the worked strips of the barley fields east of Low Kiln had kept the boot-print since the water went, drying into a crust that took a heel and gave it back slowly, and on the fifteenth of Seed the hands the strips had been said to want went into them at last. They went in stooped, a row of backs along the turned ground, and the seed came out of fists in handfuls that nobody called aloud. Brooms went around what the sweep had left — around the stone the flood had set in the second strip, around the boot-print, around the place where the brown broke into the new finer grey the yard called the thaw — and the bristles worked over the crust without once crossing the lines. The boy was carried out through the Clay Yard and set at the edge of the work where he could see it and not be part of it, one hundred and four days old, three months and fourteen days, and nobody in the field had a figure for the hands, the strips or the seed. The work was done with a glance and a count of handfuls, and no number was spoken anywhere.

For a month the thing before him had been a direction, a place a man pointed at from the yard with his chin, and now it was worked ground with bodies in it, and the small lifting of that arrived before he could check it. The thing promised had come; he let himself feel it for the length of one breath. Then the cold came after, as it came after every warming in this district: the hands were here and the number was nowhere, and the only exact thing in the field was a boy who could not lift a hand to his own chin.

❦

A woman at the head of the first strip let the seed out of her fist a little at a time, and would not say what the fist held. She had counted it into the fist from a basket and let it out into the brown ground and said nothing over it, and when the woman next to her asked how much went to the strip she said the weather would have the last word, which was an answer that cost her nothing and could be entered nowhere. Chaff worked into the creases of her fingers as she went, pale dust in the lines of a hand that knew the seed by feel and not by count; when the fist was empty she closed it again on nothing and went to the next hole with the chaff still in its creases, and the basket was not lifted for a figure.

He read the emptied fist as a chart closed with a neat line and a signature. He had once stood over twenty bodies in the space of ninety seconds and decided, with a part of him that did not argue, which of them would be carried out, which would be left where they lay, and which would be given a mark and set aside for a man with more time; the sorting was a handful placed in a row and the row signed at the foot so that no one afterward could say the decision had not been made, and it had never once felt clean. That was the part of him that read the woman’s fist now — the same dry instrument brought to a seed-strip — and the reading came exact and complete and worth nothing. It told him the shape of a count that had been made and shut and would not be said. It could not tell him the count, and if it could he would have had no tongue for it and no hand to write it, and the woman had gone to the next hole with the chaff in her creases.

He watched the order of her reaching: to the basket, then the fist, then the ground, and only at the end a glance back at the basket to see what was left. It was the order he had seen in a midwife going to a slate before a child, in a clerk going to a slate before a face, in a neighbour who reached for a shelf before she looked at him. Here it ended in a basket and a strip and nobody the worse for it, and he kept the order on a place of its own and wrote no cause across the distance between the hands. The river wind came off the Ilm and lifted the loose hair at the nape of his neck; cold arrived in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, and the wool rode back from the wrist so the cold found the bare place. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had been told to wipe it, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left, and nobody had given an instruction about the thread.

❦

A man stood at the head of the second strip with his hand on the wooden edge of a barrow and opened his mouth on the number of hands the work would want before it was done. He shaped it. The figure was in his teeth and the yard was leaning toward him to hear it, and then he looked at the strip — at the turned brown ground, at the backs stooped over it, at the broom going around the stone — and thought better of it, and shut his mouth, and put his weight back on the barrow. The figure went nowhere. It had come up out of the ground into a man’s mouth and gone back into the ground without a slate in the field to take it, and he went off along the strip with the barrow, his hand still on the wooden edge, and did not once look toward the boy.

He knew the shape of such a thing. It was the shape of a number that goes from a fist to a mouth to a shoulder and arrives nowhere, except that this one had gone from the strip to a man’s teeth and back to the strip, and nothing in the field had changed its figure because a man had nearly said it. He set the near-thing on the place where he set the others and did not join it to the woman’s fist or to the barrow or to the direction he had carried for a month. A field that withholds its number in the very act of planting is no different from a market that spends a whole morning inside an account without once addressing the thing it counts, and there was Grathic for the weather and for the thaw and none for this. The two sayings he had heard a month ago — hands wanted by the morrow, the weather having the last word — had stood then on two places, and he left them standing now and did not add the near-number to them and call it an answer. Three likenesses in a row will make a man who wants a figure go home satisfied with the thing he has made, and the only hand here was small and outside the wool.

❦

Sella Vail had given the bucket its instruction before anybody had asked and the broom its instruction before the broom was taken up, and she had given no instruction at all to the basket. “Go around,” she told the broom, the way another woman might have told it to sweep. “It cost enough to come in. It’ll cost more to take out.” Only after the broom had been taken up did she tell the women where to put their feet. When one of them asked whose hands these were, she said the house had sent them, and did not say I. She kept her half-pace from every open slate in the yard, and went around the space under the lowest rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and entered it at no point in the morning. The widow’s mark hung where it hung at her throat and was touched at no hour, because a strip, a rack and a yard are not openings and no opening had come.

When a woman asked how many days stood between this morning and the next tend, Sella moved her lips along the loaf row, pushed a loaf back to where it had been, and came to the same place twice, and gave nobody a figure. The house would pay in loaves later; the house had always paid in loaves later, and nobody had said who had laid the loaves there. He watched her go around the slate and around the space under the rack at the same half-pace she kept from every open slate, and saw that the keeping and the planting were one act done in two materials, with a glance for the broom and a glance for the ground and no figure in either. She did not look into the basket and did not look toward him, and she said the house where another woman would have said I, and the field went on with its hands.

❦

Berr Clay came down off the north road at midday with the red wool cord of his office folded under his coat instead of worn across it, and the green of tasted glaze at one corner of his mouth, and hot clay over the smell of nothing at all, which is the most expensive smell in Grath. His hands were large and clean. He wiped his thumb twice on his apron before touching nothing, and held a narrow slate unopened against his forearm, passing a finger over the figure written on it and letting his eyes rest a breath longer than the writing required. “One in twelve, as the notice says,” he said low into the top of the work, and the nearest women leaned in because they had to, and went back to their holes. He said the levy was called and that it was not called by him.

To a man who brought a number up out of a fist and asked what the planting would cost now that hands had gone into it, Berr said a number goes into a slate, not into a strip; if the house wants it again, there’ll be a slate at the watch. He said your house and not my account, and took himself out of his own sentence as neatly as a surgeon takes a hand out of a field before the dressing goes on. The man with the number in his fist carried it off again up the road and arrived nowhere with it, and Berr never once looked into the basket and went off toward High Kiln with the box in his house still locked and the true subtraction inside, believing he had done the district a kindness.

He kept the twice-wiped thumb on a place of its own. He had heard the order it came in — thumb to apron, thumb to apron, finger to figure, speaker out of sentence — and he did not join it to the woman’s fist or the man’s barrow or the slate at the watch; he kept such hands together on the tray where such hands lay and wrote no cause across them. The figure Berr had read aloud was not the figure Berr had paid, and the figure Berr had paid sat in a locked box in a house up the road, and the strip had not changed its figure because a man had looked at it. Nobody in the field crossed himself at any of it, and the brooms went on around the stone.

❦

He set the direction he had carried for a month beside the number the yard would not give, and held them on two places. He had held them there through the waiting and he held them there now, with the hands in the ground and the seed in the brown skin and not one figure spoken over any of it. The holding was the whole of the work and the work produced nothing, and the nothing was the exact price of keeping the two places clean. He was one hundred and four days old and nobody there knew it, and nobody there would have known what to count if they had; there was no word in Grathic that would have carried the saying.

His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the worked strip and came down. He wanted the brown-edged crust two inches from his ear under the roof, and the breast, and the bucket’s lip, and the number of his days said aloud by somebody who would know what it counted, and he could reach none of them; the wanting was no louder now than the counting. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and the cold held at cheek and temple and wrist. The woman’s fist closed on the seed with chaff in its creases and the number of hands went nowhere, and the man went off with the barrow and the brooms went around the stone, and the boy was carried at the edge with the direction and the number still two things on two places. Nothing was joined. Nothing was paid. Nothing was named. The count stood at one hundred and four, unspent, and the field went on with its hands.

❦

The afternoon came into the kiln-house sideways, through the door that stood a finger’s width open, and lay along the floor in a thin strip of light that stopped short of the shelf. The board of the loaf shelf was bare where it had not been, and the bareness had the colour wood takes after it has held bread long enough to remember the shape of it. A neighbour came in with a dented pot against her hip and reached past the bare board without looking at it first, and her hand went to the board before her eyes went anywhere, the way a hand goes to a chart before the eyes go to a face. She opened the reach for the half-beat it takes to find what is not there, closed on the loaf beyond it, and only then let her eyes come down to the basket where the boy lay. Her thumb left a pale print on the bare wood and went on with the loaf; the print did not stay the shape of anything.

“The old place at the end came in with the planting,” she said, and set the loaf in her pot. She said it to the board, or to the pot, and not to anybody standing there. “A row comes up short. A body gets used to it.”

To Sella Vail, who had come in behind her with a broom against her shoulder, the neighbour said what the planting would cost now that hands had gone into the strips. Sella moved her lips along the loaf row, pushed a loaf back to where it had been, came to the same place twice, and said the house had said it and the woman should ask the pot. She gave nobody a figure. The neighbour took her loaf and went with her pot, and the half-beat stayed where it had been, on the board, with nobody to enter it.

She went out with the pot held against her hip, and the dent in it caught the light and went dull as she turned; there was no slate on her and no cord at her throat, only a woman taking a loaf home in a pot, and the boy set her going on the tray where other hands had gone before her and wrote no cause across it. Three days ago a breath had held two absences; today one of them had been said alone. He did not supply the one she had left out. It would have been easy — a child’s empty place and a shelf’s empty place laid side by side in the mind, with a line drawn through them and a clean hand at the foot to sign it — and he had declined that hand for a hundred and four days with nobody in the house knowing he had been offered it.

He had learned to read the half-beat before the hand closed on what came after it. It was neither a long pause nor a short one; it was the pause a hand makes when it expects a thing and finds the place of a thing instead, and he had seen it in a midwife reaching for a slate before she reached for a child, in a clerk going to a record before he went to a face, in a woman at a well putting her hand out to keep a child from water. Here it ended in a loaf and a pot and a woman going out with both, and he kept the order on a place of its own. The hand had gone to the board before the eyes had gone to him, and that was the whole of it. There was no cause in it and he wrote none.

In the next room the fold lay on the bed with one brown-edged crust in it, stiff and gone past smelling of anything, and an empty place beside it nobody had named. Between the shelf’s two gaps and the fold’s one gap there was a wall, and the wall existed in his keeping and nowhere else. He set the shelf’s absences on one place and the fold’s absence on another and held them there with the only hand available, which was small and outside the wool. A likeness laid on a likeness grows a cause unless a hand holds them apart; he had learned what three likenesses in a row will do to a man who wants an answer, and he had spent his whole life declining to be the clean hand at the foot of the line.

❦

Sella had given the broom its instruction before she gave it to anybody and now she stood over the clean dark line where the old Ash ridge had turned its corner, and over the new finer grey beside it, with the broom in her hand and the bristles resting on the boards. She swept neither. The new grey lay hair-thin along the skirting, finer than the old line and the colour of a pot washed out into water; the house called it the thaw and said it would go when it goes. Outside, the yard had gone around what the water left and called the brown skin dry enough to bear a boot; here the two lines lay along the skirting with a clean board between them, and the broom stood against the wall in the hours when no hand was on it so that nobody would take it up by the handle and make an accident of sweeping. The boy had learned what the word did before he had learned what it was said over — it let a person go home warm without owning the day — and he kept the grey and the dark line on two places and did not let one become the other. He had seen a woman in this house lay one thumb in a ridge here and the same thumb in a ridge two doors down and carry the knowledge back without saying which was which; he had seen it and had not asked her what she meant by it, because a thumb laid in Ash is an entry in a hand no Court would read. The little-door burn on the back of her left hand closed on the broom handle and then on nothing, and nobody in the room asked what had closed it. The space under the lowest rack went around as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and the broken birth-slates beneath it were entered by nobody. He had no tongue for the question of which slate had been broken and no hand to point with, and beyond that he had decided not to want the answer, because a question that cannot be put becomes a place, and he had places enough.

Her hands were never still; when they had nothing to do they counted spoons, nails, the breaths of a sleeping child, and when the counting had nowhere to go the burn closed on the empty air over a slate and opened again. He had watched it close on a cup handle and on the well-rope and on nothing at all, and he had never once seen it close on the mark. The clay jar on the wall shelf stood cold under its glaze, turned to the little light that came through the door; she glanced at it once and said nothing over it, the way a man glances at a lock he has decided not to try. He had watched that glance for a hundred and four days and did not know what the jar held or why it stood on the wall shelf and not on the bench with the pots. The widow’s mark hung where it hung at her throat, a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it; people noticed the burn first and the mark last, which was the order she preferred, and she did not touch it now. A shelf was not an opening. A strip was not an opening. A bed was not an opening. She had lost a husband to the one thing in this house that was an opening and had never once described it that way herself; she touched the mark before the kiln was opened and at no other hour, and no opening had come in a hundred and four days.

“Feed first,” she told the fire, which was her husband’s saying said to a fire and not to a person, and there was no I in it and no figure. She turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and told the fire it was the thaw, and the fire took the coal and gave back four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, as it had given on every day since he came under this roof. Its payer had gone unnamed for a hundred and four days. The house went on warm on an account nobody would own, and the boy set the missing fifth beside the four as an object and not as a hunger. The warmth came and nobody said whose coal it was; in another district a man might have set a figure beside a gift and called it paid, but here a gift that could not be answered could not be refused either, and the house kept both facts where it kept the clay jar — on a shelf, within sight, never opened, never explained.

❦

At the going of the light she carried him up the Clay Yard at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, going around the space under the lowest rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. She had given the basket no instruction; the instruction had gone to the wool and the pot and the fire, and the basket was carried like a pot filled that must not be slopped. The yard smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and, under them, the faint sour mineral tang that came up from the unswept corner. Under the Vail kiln-house roof she set him on his bed and not in the Debt Loft, and put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without looking at his face.

He was one hundred and four days old, three months and fourteen days, and nobody under the roof knew it and nobody under the roof would have known what to count if they had; there was no word in Grathic that would have carried the saying. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far. Cold arrived in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, and the wool rode back from the wrist so the cold found the bare place. She did not pull the wool down, because the instruction had gone to the wool and not to the wrist; the cold found the bare place and went on finding it, and he had learned the order of it the way a man learns the order of an instrument tray laid out in the dark — what comes first, what comes after, what will be wanted before it is asked for. The crust was two inches from his ear and the bucket’s lip two paces off, and neither was an opening, so neither was given. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had wiped it, because nobody had been given an instruction about the thread and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. He wanted the crust and could not lift a hand to it; the wanting was quiet now and the counting went on all the same.

In the fold one brown-edged crust lay stiff with its empty place unnamed and no third; in the next room the shelf kept its two gaps. He held them in two rooms that did not touch, and the holding produced nothing and was the whole of the work. A wall is not a thing a boy can build with a hand outside a fold of wool, and he had built one all the same out of not-looking and out of instructions given to pots and never to baskets.

❦

The door stood a finger’s width open. Through the gap came the river’s low muttered note from the Ilm, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, water going over water in a voice the district had no word for and called the thaw. Through the floor came the kiln’s breath, four, four and a gap under blind ash and the back of a broken pot. The two sounds came in two separate places and had come so for a hundred and four days, and he kept them so; a fifth sound laid beside four would make a count, and a count in this house wanted a payer nobody would name. The note came nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, which was a fact about water and not about anything else, and he kept it as a fact and not as a voice; the district had made one word do the work of the many it had not got around to naming. Four low pulls from the floor and a low turning from the door, and between them the width of a room and a wall that did not join them; he had lain awake on the first night he spent under this roof and heard the two in two places, and he heard them in two places now, and a hundred and four days had added nothing to them but the count.

The boards overhead kept four nails and four and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts did not meet. He had counted the nails on the first night he was laid under this roof and he counted them now, and the count was the only arithmetic in the house that stayed still while loaves went along a shelf and glances went around a yard and the days went badly kept.

His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down. He wanted the crust two inches from his ear, and the breast, and the bucket’s lip, and the number of his days said aloud by somebody who would know what it counted, and he could reach none of them and could ask for none. The neighbour’s half-beat lay on the board in the other room, and the old space at the row’s end lay beside it, and the fold’s crust lay stiff under the roof, and none of the three touched. Nothing was joined. Nothing was paid. Nothing was named. The count stood at one hundred and four, unspent, and the river went on over itself in the dark under the roof.

Before first grey the men came in. They came up the yard from the road side with their collars held high and their feet set down carefully on the trodden ash, and they went into the room off the shed that stood a finger’s width open, and did not shut it behind them. There had been a frost in the night and the boards had tightened with it; the door kept its width the way a door keeps a habit, and a thin seam of cold came through it and went along the floor toward the wheel. The boy was carried in in the basket under the fold of wool and set at the edge of the work where he could see the door and not be part of what went through it. Voices came out of the room, low and few, the way a man speaks when he has laid a hand over a thing before he says its name. They did not carry. He had learned to hear the shape of a saying without the words of it, and he set this shape on a place of its own, where other shapes of the same kind lay and none of them had been entered.

Jorra Vail gave the house no name for them. When a woman at the rack end asked who had come in before light, he did not say the men and did not say the room, and he did not say the room was cold. He said nothing at all, and went on with what his hands were doing, and the yard went on around him the way cloth goes around a weight that has not moved. The boy watched the order of it: the door a finger’s width open, the man’s back turned to it, the forearms going to the wheel. He had seen that order in a midwife going to a slate before a child and in a clerk going to a record before a face, and here it ended in a silence that cost nobody a word and could be entered nowhere. He wrote no cause across it. Three likenesses in a row will make a man who wants an answer go home satisfied with the thing he has made, and the only hand here was small and outside the wool.

He came through the finger’s-width-open door with fine grey slate dust worked into both forearms past the bend of his little finger. It was not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel. Bone-coloured slip dried to the colour of an old bone and sat on the hair of the arm where a thrower had dipped; this went finer, into the creases and under the bent finger where a hand does not wash itself clean without knowing it has been at something. On other mornings the forearms had come out pale and dry and the boy had kept those clean ones and these dusty ones on one tray as two states of one man’s two mornings. He asked nothing of either state. He had no tongue for the question of which slate had been broken and no hand to point with, and beyond that he had decided not to want the answer, because a question that cannot be put becomes a place, and he had places enough. The broken birth-slates lay under the lowest rack, gone around as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it and entered by nobody through a hundred and four mornings and this one.

He spoke to the wheel before he spoke to anyone. “It doesn’t care whose hands,” he said, in the flat voice he kept for tools and pots. “It cares if they’re steady.” The wheel gave its low note under the words and went on turning, and the shed smelled of damp clay and tallow and, carried up from High Kiln on a bad wind, the sweetish lead edge that sat at the back of the mouth and did not go away with water. The boy’s head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far. Cold arrived in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, and the wool rode back from the wrist so the cold found the bare place. Nobody pulled it down; the instruction had gone to the wool and not to the wrist.

A pot came up under his hands and he looked at it the way a man looks at a thing he has already judged and is only now asking it to confirm. “You’ll do,” he said, with no welcome in the sentence and no unkindness either. He knew the full note of a Vail pot before it left the wheel — knew it in the hand before the knuckle went over the rim and before any pot had been set down for anybody else to hear — and he would not say so before a kiln-master from High Kiln, who would call it early and make something of it. The boy knew this about him as he knew the bend of the finger and the dust in its creases, and kept both where questions can last for years. The full note was a fact of the vessel and not a saying about it; a man who could hear it in his hands before the world heard it under a knuckle had either a gift or a trick of attention, and in this district either one would be made to mean something the man had not said. He kept the knowing on its own place and did not offer it to the kiln-master who was not in the room.

Sella Vail had not finished telling him the fire was low. “The house keeps it low overnight. Feed it before — ” The bent finger went into the cold iron of the damper ahead of the sentence, going in ahead of his hand the way it always went, and the iron took the cold out of his fingertip and left the fingertip warmer than it had been before it touched. He moved it. The end of her instruction arrived at the pot instead of at him, and the household straightened around his silence the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted. He had not answered her with a word and had not looked at her. Her little-door burn shut on the empty air over the bench where her hand had been and opened again on nothing, and nobody in the shed asked what had closed it.

The boy watched the order of the reaching and kept it: her mouth on the instruction, his finger on the iron, the sentence going to the pot and the pot taking it. It was an obedience and it was a correction, and neither of them had given the other a word with which to call it. Under the same roof another ear kept a sound of its own and said nothing about that either, and the two silences lay side by side without touching, as they had lain since the boy came under this roof. He did not join them. A household that straightens around a silence is a household that has heard the silence and agreed what it means, and there was no Grathic for that agreement either, any more than there was a word for the two faces turned the same way that meant a keeping and a pricing. He set the straightening on the place where he set such things and wrote no cause across it.

He read the damper once more while the iron cooled back to the temperature of the room. It was any iron that stops a thing from running: the bite that holds a vessel whole and the bite that can take the hand off a man were one bite and one piece of iron, and no word in Grathic held both mercies at once. He had held such iron himself in a room he no longer stood in, closing it on a vessel that would have emptied a man in four minutes and closing it just as readily on a limb that had to come away, and the two acts had used the same pressure from the same hand and the same dry counting of what would be lost. Here the house called the iron a holding and never a taking, and had called it so for a hundred and five mornings. He filed the reading as an object on a tray, exact and complete and worth nothing, joined to no kiln. The two tellings of the shut counting room lay on another place, breath through the nose against breath made in the throat, saying not the war’s hand, and he did not bring them across to this iron and did not let a third telling come.

The room off the shed kept its finger’s width of open through the morning and no one went through it that the boy could see and no name was given to anyone who had. At midday the yard went around the space under the lowest rack with a broom and a glance and no figure spoken; the clean dark line where the old Ash ridge had turned its corner and the new finer grey beside it lay unswept and were called the thaw that would go when it went. The widow’s mark hung where it hung at her throat and was touched at no hour, because a shed, a strip, a shelf and a rack are not openings and no opening had come. She glanced once at the clay jar on the wall shelf, cold under its glaze, and said nothing over it, and gave the instruction to the fire and the pot and never to the basket. The boy had watched that glance for a hundred and five days and did not know what the jar held or why it stood on the wall shelf and not on the bench with the pots, and he had stopped wanting to know in the way a man stops reaching for an instrument that is not on the tray.

He agreed so entirely that no argument could find a foot. That was the whole of his defence and the whole of his debt: a man who assents to everything has given nothing away, because there is no side left for a slate to find him on. The boy had watched surgeons do the same thing in a room where two men wanted opposite ends of a patient and the chart had to come out clean; the trick was to agree with both and let the hand do what the hand had already decided. And yet Tarn wanted the late. He checked the rest of what he carried as the same man might check a tray of instruments after a long case: by touch, in order, knowing before he looked which ones were missing.

Six hearings of the full note. Five from four sources — a cup across a birth room, a pot in the Vail wheel-shed through a wall, the district’s own slate twice at the Cross, a man giving at the Drawing — and the sixth, the small flat knock out of a woman as the men lifted her, from a person, filed beside the five and never among them. No seventh. A pot lifted a hand’s breadth too high at the well had come up to the rim of a note and gone back down; the half-beat after the knock was a different object from the knock and lay on the interval tray where it had been put. Three near-full answers stood on their own tray, advanced and not paid. Three intervals lay on another. The word forty was a saying on a tray of its own, unspoken in three days, with the readable double turn of a second cord twisted twice beneath a grey one beside it and the weight of a thumb on wicker kept apart from both. Four low things kept their four places, and the river’s note of water over water was a fifth thing on a place of its own and not a hearing. The two tellings of the shut counting room, breath through the nose against breath made in the throat, both saying not the war’s hand, stayed joined to no kiln with no third. The double bite of the damper, the iron that holds a vessel whole and can take the hand off a man with no word in Grathic for both mercies, lay filed as an object and joined to no kiln. The loan that can hear was still a loan that could be called, and the sentence that would call it had not been said under this roof.

He added, without entering them, two women keeping two records in wool and knots on boards that did not touch, a clerk set at a landing for a measure nobody had called, a soft-cuffed hand going to a slate before its eyes went to a stall. Nothing had moved. Nothing had been paid. Nothing had been joined.

The keeping cost him nothing and earned him nothing, which was its exact distinction. A man who keeps a record in a ledger is paid in the authority of the ledger; a man who keeps one in the head of a child who cannot speak is paid in the child’s own silence, and the silence is not even his to spend. He had spent a hundred and six days becoming the only exact instrument in a house that kept its arithmetic in loaves and glances, and the instrument had no handle and no use and no hand to hold it.

He was the only adult in the room and the least use in it. A man turns out instruments on a towel because he means to use them. He had turned out a whole district’s kept things on the towel of his attention and there was no operation to perform, no field to open, no hand to carry any of it to a door. The file was exact, complete, and worth nothing. The count went on.

At the going of the light under the Vail kiln-house roof Sella told the fire it was the thaw. She did not say whose thaw, and the fire was the better for being addressed first. She turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and fed it, and the fire took the coal and gave back four low pulls with the silence where the fifth should have been, the way it had given them for a hundred and six days. Its payer had gone unnamed for a hundred and six days. A man might have asked who paid for the coal that warmed the boards by degrees. There was no man under this roof who would have answered him, and the boy had stopped asking questions nobody in the room could hear.

She said the house where another woman would have said I, and did not look toward the bed.

On the loaf shelf in the next room the row kept its two unnamed gaps, the old space at the end that had come in with the planting and the new palm’s breadth that had come in the night of Seed 6 with no scrape, crumb, print or word. In the fold one brown-edged crust lay stiff with its empty place beside it unnamed and no third. No collector had come for the woman in the brook and the morning had come all the same. The omission stood where it had been left, unentered and unnamed, and bread went on being broken over it the way bread is broken over any table. He did not call it a heresy. A heresy is a thing with a name and somebody at the foot of it to answer; this was only a thing not done, and the not-doing had gone on long enough to have the shape of a custom. Somewhere an account wanted a collector and nobody had sent one, and the morning had come anyway, with nobody to answer for it. Warmth could be left where it fell and the morning would come over it; the boy carried that plain fact on a place of its own and did not make a doctrine of it. A doctrine would have wanted a name, and a name would have wanted somebody to answer for the morning.

The clean dark line where Ash had turned its corner lay where it had always lain, and the new, finer hairline of grey lay beside it unswept and was called the thaw that would go when it goes. The space under the lowest rack had been gone around as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and the broken birth-slates beneath it were entered by nobody. No opening had come, so the widow’s mark hung at her throat where it hung, untouched at a shelf, a strip, a basket, a road, a door and a bed. The private roll stayed unshown.

The door stood a finger’s width open. The river’s low muttered note came through the gap from the Ilm, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, and through the floor came the kiln’s breath, four, four and a gap under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, its payer unnamed. The two sounds came in two separate places as they had come for a hundred and six days. The boards overhead kept four nails and four and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts did not meet.

Cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, and the wool did less than the night wanted, riding back from the wrist so the cold found the bare place. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and had not been wiped, because nobody had been given an instruction about the thread and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. He wanted the crust two inches from his ear, and the breast, and the bucket’s lip two paces off, and the number of his days said aloud by somebody who would know what it counted, and he could reach none of them and could ask for none.

Somewhere off the bed, in the shed where the wheel stood, the small bench knife lay on its sill with six years standing in a mark a thumb had worn almost flat and the seventh nearly gone and still held, the one entry in this house the house could not check. The breath held behind it had not been spent. A loan that can hear is a loan that can be called; the sentence that would call it had never been said under this roof, and nothing had given yet.

His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down. The count stood at one hundred and six, unspent, and the dark under the roof kept it without a figure.

The Chronicle of Chapter 22

On the fifteenth of Seed the hands the strips had been said to want went into the worked ground east of Low Kiln, seed out of fists in handfuls nobody counted and no figure spoken. A woman would not say what her fist held; a man with a barrow shaped the number of hands and shut his mouth. Berr Clay came down off the north road, read one in twelve as the notice says, said a number goes into a slate, not into a strip, and went off with his box locked. Sella Vail gave the instruction to the bucket and the broom before anybody and never to the basket, and said the house where another woman would have said I. The boy, one hundred and four days old and told by nobody, held the direction beside the number; his hand went up an inch toward the worked strip and came down.

At the loaf shelf a neighbour said the old place at the row’s end had come in with the planting and went off with her pot, never seeing the empty place at his ear. Sella gave nobody a figure and told the fire it was the thaw; the fire answered four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been.

On the sixteenth men came in before the grey and spoke low in the room off the wheel-shed that stood a finger’s width open, and Jorra Vail gave them no name. He came out with grey slate dust past the bent little finger, spoke to the wheel first, told a pot it would do, and put his bent finger into the cold damper ahead of Sella’s unfinished sentence, so the household straightened around his silence. At the well Tarn agreed with those who wanted the thaw early and late, wanting the late because it puts off the paying; his knife held six counted years and a seventh nearly gone, and his broom went over the collector’s road without turning. No collector came.

On the seventeenth Sella set the basket at the end of the fish-trade shade and gave the instruction to the wool and the water, never to the basket. Mira Silt worked the eels, answered a question with her own answer and put her thumb to the grey cord when a buyer named a figure, showing a second cord twisted twice with neither knot counted, her board beside Orra Weir’s without touching. The soft-cuffed clerk still sat for a measure nobody had called. Three doors down Alda Reed laid her birth-slates before the fold, crossed two fingers at the birth-flower and covered the sign with a dead woman’s date, said Warm and breathing, exact and not true. Under the roof the boy found his file unchanged: six hearings and no seventh, the word forty unspoken, two shelf gaps and one crust in rooms that did not touch. The count stood at one hundred and six, unspent.
