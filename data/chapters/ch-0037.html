The First Opening

Days 150–152 · Tallow 1–3, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, the Weir and Sill

The month opens with a hand at the mark and a coal under a broken pot. Four pulls come up and the fifth is a silence. Nobody gives the silence a figure; the house says it will keep it. What the kiln takes is entered on no slate but in the breathing of the woman who fed it.

— A saying of the clay yards of Low Kiln, at the first firing of the month

— Day 150 —

Tallow 1, Y.H. 813 (Firstday)

The month turned over in the dark the way a pot turns on a wheel, a quarter at a time and with no hand calling the moment of the turning; and the cold came round with it under the Vail roof and took its old order of reaching, cheek first, then temple, then the small bare joint at the wrist where the wool had ridden back in the night. the boy lay on the bed and not in the Debt Loft, as he had lain every night since the night he was carried in, and he was one hundred and fifty days old and five months exactly, and nobody under that roof had said either number, and nobody under that roof would have known what a number of days counted if somebody had said it. The house kept its days in loaves pushed along a board, and in glances at a clay jar, and in a space at the row’s end that no woman called a number because a woman who called it would then have to answer for it. The count he carried had no rim and no handle and no woman passing with a pot would have set it down. It stood where it stood. It went on.

The first sound of the new month was not a bell from the Cross. It was the kiln’s breath coming through the wall from the yard, four low draws and a gap where the fifth should have been, regular as a sleeper’s breathing through a partition and no more addressed to him than that. Under the floor the carried fire answered in the same figure, four low pulls and a silence, and he kept the two sounds on two places and did not lay the one across the other. Two likenesses in a row are the beginning of a rule, and a rule wants a foot at the bottom of it with a name under the foot; he had learned in a better-lit room how quickly a row of likenesses will stand up and acquire a payer when a man with a clean slate comes in to count them. The kiln’s breath was the danger of the month and had changed its address; the fire under the floor was the old danger that had never moved. Both missed a fifth. Neither had been given a figure, and he had not supplied one.

The first smell was hot clay under tallow. It came in under the door and through the door and went to the beams ahead of the woman who carried it, so that the room smelled of the yard before the yard was in it. Under the smell, for one breath and then folded away, the clean bitter edge of charcoal from beneath the loaf shelf, where the board kept its two unnamed gaps and the marks below it waited where marks wait when nobody has been told to read them. The clay jar stood cold under its glaze in its place by the wall and had not been touched in the night. Halfway along the shelf the place where the pan had stood out to dry through Reed was a place and nothing more: no scrape, no crumb, no print, no word. He held its empty place on a tray of its own and did not lay it across the two gaps above it or across the one stiff crust in the fold two inches from his ear or across the place in the returning line the yard had gone around with a look and a wide step. No third gap came. No fourth likeness was made a cause. He kept a finger between them without moving anything.

Sella Vail came in from her own bed with her hair coal-black and the one grey streak at her temple showing pale off the coals, and did not look into the basket. The little-door burn on the back of her hand closed on nothing as she reached for the wool and shut again on the fold. “Keep it off his ears,” she told the wool. “It’s the river wind.” To the fire she said, “Feed, and that’ll hold,” and to the pot whose rim stood out with its handle where a hand might find it she said, “You’ll do.” She gave the instruction to the broom before she gave it to anybody, and she never gave it to the basket, and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. Tarn was already at the kindling under the hearth, doing three things at once — feeding, lining, putting the fourth thing quietly right — and saying nothing, because a man who owns nothing keeps his place by being useful before he is spoken to. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line, and the pale ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back, and his chest was tight from something that had happened in the dark and would not be named at breakfast.

She took the basket up from under the roof and carried him down at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, woodsmoke going before her and the river wind coming up to meet it, the two passing between the low houses the way two people pass on a stair in the dark, each with a hand on the wall and neither speaking. The half-pace was not slow and not hurried; it was a distance kept, measured from the one thing in the yard that must not be touched, as a surgeon measures a field from the instrument laid out at its edge and knows where the hand must stop. She went around the pan’s empty place with the tilt of shoulder and basket that keeps a standing thing standing, and nothing knocked, and nobody said that anything was gone. At the yard well the trough had lost the skin of ice that had lain on it thin as a skim of fat set aside in a dish, and the water showed bare and dark under the little roof, and a woman letting down a bucket called it weather, and the bucket went down into weather and came up with weather in it. A thing called weather is a thing nobody has agreed to count, and a man who asks it for a figure is given a direction and sent away with the direction going downriver and no entry left behind.

The yard was already at the first firing. The kilns had been brought up in the night for the year’s last working season and the air above the Clay Yard had the hot dry smell of clay giving up its water, and under it the fainter warmth of tallow from the wheel-shed, and under that again, on a wind that came the wrong way off High Kiln, the sweetish edge of lead glaze that put a false sweetness in the mouth. Men went about with their collars up and dust ground small as meal under their nails, going around the clean dark line where the Ash ridge had turned and the new finer hairline beside it that the whole yard called the thaw, going around them with a broom in one hand and a glance in the other and sweeping neither. Under the lowest rack the broken birth-slates lay where they lay, gone around as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and in the returning line there was a place that a woman looked at and stepped wide around and did not put a number to. He held the look and the wide step where they belonged and carried nothing across from them into the basket.

A woman come up from the lower yard with a wet pot on her arm stopped where the draught from the mouth did not reach her and said to the open door, “Cold in the mouth this morning. It’ll take the coals wrong.” Sella set the basket level and told the fire to feed and hold, and told the pot not to be set wet, and told the woman nothing at all. The woman carried the question away with the pot level on her arm so that nothing slopped, and the yard went on about the firing with the danger standing where the danger stood and nobody putting a figure to it. He had learned early that a house answers a question about a cost by sending the question to a pot, and that the pot never answers, and that this is not a failure of the answer but the whole of it.

At the kiln mouth Sella set the basket down level where no slate lay, out of the draught and where the heat would come to him sideways and not full, and stood over him a moment with her hands still. Then she put her hand to the widow’s mark at her throat. It was a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and the cord at her throat was glazed where a working thumb had oiled it ten thousand times, and the little-door burn half-closed on the cord and gave nothing away. She held the mark one breath and let go. She did not touch it again. She had gone around every opening for one hundred and forty-nine days because none of them had been openings, and an opening had come at last, and the rule held exactly: the hand went to the mark before the kiln mouth and at no other hour, not at a shelf, not at a well, not at a dusk, not at a basket. The hand went away and the household went on.

She opened the mouth and tipped the carried coal in under the back of a broken pot. The fire took it the way a vessel takes a thing it has been waiting for, without haste and without surprise, and gave four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been. She did not count the pulls aloud. She did not ask anybody to hear the silence. Its payer had been unnamed for a hundred and fifty days, and the name was not spoken at the opening either, and the coal went in all the same. “That’ll hold,” she told the fire, and to the pot, “Don’t set that wet. You’ll pay for it in the thaw,” and to the broom, “Go around,” and to the wool again, “Keep it off his ears.” She said the house where another woman would have said I, and the instruction went to the wool and the fire and the pot and the broom and never to the basket. She carried him past at the half-pace. The act was ordinary. She went back at once to setting pots where pots were set, and there was no weight in her hands and no look toward the mark and no sentence about what had been done.

The coal took under the back of the pot and went red at the edge first and then all through, the way a thing goes red when air is let to it, and the heat came out of the mouth on one breath that crossed his cheek and went off across the yard. He knew the order in which a man goes when the breath takes him wrong — the head going first, the skin doing what the vessels under it do, the almost-smile that is not peace — as certainly as he knew the order in which cold arrives, and the knowing stood in the yard and was worth nothing, because the heat was two paces off and the hand that would have pulled the wool over his ear was his own small hand and could not lift it. He wanted the crust two inches from his ear in the fold at home, and he wanted the number of his days said aloud by somebody who would have known what a number of days was, and he got the breath of the coal on his cheek and went on. She had gone back to setting pots where pots were set before the coal had finished taking.

He watched it from the low angle with the surgeon’s attention that had no use in it. A theatre is a room in which heat is kept and counted and a man stands at the gate of it and does not go in; he had known rooms like that in another life, and had stood in them with a towel in his hands while somebody else held the field. Here the heat was behind a door of clay and iron and the count was in a silence nobody would enter on a slate. He knew the damper for one piece of iron with two bites, the bite that holds a vessel whole and the bite that can take the hand off a man, filed on a place of its own and joined to no kiln, worth nothing, with no word in Grathic that would hold both mercies in one breath. Jorra Vail’s bent finger had gone into that cold iron before this morning, ahead of a sentence left unfinished, and the household had straightened around the silence as cloth straightens when a weight is lifted. The iron was the iron. A gate with two bites is a gate with two bites, and a man who calls it a doctrine has already found the payer he wanted. There was a cost at this opening and the cost had a name somewhere, and the name was not spoken under this roof, and the boy could not have reached the coal, the mark, the iron, or the two inches of wool at his own chin.

The kilns went on breathing in fours against the yard’s going-around, and the women went on putting pots down and lifting them, and the first firing of the month stood in the yard with nobody making a feast of it and nobody crossing himself. From behind the racks came the river’s note of water over water, a fifth low thing and never counted among the hearings; through the wall behind him the kiln missed its fifth; under the boards at home the carried fire would miss its fifth again at dusk. Three counts in three places, and none of them became one. One hundred and fifty days old and five months, told by nobody, exact and unspent in the basket at the kiln mouth while the coal took under the back of the broken pot and the fire answered four pulls and a silence with its payer unnamed. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water and came down against the fold.

By midday the firing had gone into the yard’s hands and the opening was a thing that had happened and been gone past, the way a door is gone past once a man is through it and has put his shoulder to the next. Sella Vail carried the boy at the half-pace from the kiln mouth to the racks and back again, and the instruction went to the wool and the fire and the pot and the broom and never to the basket; the basket was set where a pot is set, out of the draught and where the heat came to him sideways and not full, and nobody lifted the fold to look at the face under it. The thread at his mouth had dried in the dry heat and nobody wiped it, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left, and no instruction had ever been given about a thread. His head went where it wanted and made its small wet sound when it went too far, and no instruction had been given about that either. He was one hundred and fifty days old and five months exactly and the heat had not made a degree of difference to anybody’s knowing what a number of days counted.

The kilns had been brought up for the year’s last working season and the yard had the hot dry smell of clay giving up its water, a smell that got under the nails and stayed there, and under it the fainter warmth of tallow from the wheel-shed, and under that again, on a wind that came the wrong way off High Kiln, the sweetish edge of lead glaze that put a false sweetness in the mouth and made a man think of fruit where there was no fruit. Clay dust lay ground small as meal on the sill and on the backs of hands and in the white of a knuckle when a fist opened; it was not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel but a finer thing, a ground thing, the dust of a slate rather than the paste of a pot, and the boy set the two on two places and did not let one stand for the other. A thing ground is a thing that got there by being broken; a thing mixed is a thing somebody put together on purpose. He had learned to tell the two before he could have said why, and the telling, exact and complete, stood in the basket and was worth nothing.

The kiln’s breath was the danger of the month and the yard carried it the way a household carries a sleeping child through a door, carefully and without remarks. It came through the wall in fours with a gap where the fifth should have been, and a woman raking the mouth said to no one in particular, “There was a man in Mud End. Went in his sleep, quiet as a lamb, and them that found him called it merciful.” She said it sideways with her eyes on the coals and went on raking, and nobody took it up and made a lesson of it, and nobody put a figure to the man in Mud End. The rake went in and came out and the coals settled and the sentence lay where it had fallen. The boy held it there. He knew what it was to have a danger in a room that nobody would name twice; a theatre keeps its danger behind a screen and goes on with the case in front of it, and the hands that know where the screen stands are the hands that must not go behind it. Here the screen was a wall of clay and iron, and his hand was a small hand outside the wool that could not have lifted a corner of it.

The yard went around its lines. At the Vail corner the old clean dark line where the Ash ridge had turned lay where it lay, and beside it the new finer hairline of grey that the whole yard called the thaw, and a woman with a broom went around both and swept neither, because a thing left where it lies is still a thing kept and the house had never argued the point. Under the lowest rack the broken birth-slates lay in their place and were gone around as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and no woman reached under them and no man counted them. In the returning line, where the hands came back from the worked strips at dusk, there was a place that a woman looked at and stepped wide around and put no number to: a hand had gone in there on Seed 15 and had not come out with the rest. The look was a look and the step was a step and the yard went on.

He held the look and the wide step on a place of their own. A yard that goes around a place with a look and a wide step has already entered the place in a roll of its own and decided what it will not read; there is no figure in it and no payer, and a man come down from Slate Row with a clean slate would find nothing to enter and would go away with his slate as clean as he brought it. He did not lay the field’s empty place across the two gaps on the loaf shelf in the other room, or across the pan’s gone place on the wall, or across the one stiff crust two inches from his ear. No third gap came. No fourth likeness made a cause. He kept a finger between them without moving anything, and the basket went on at the half-pace, and the heat of the firing month went over his cheek and off across the yard.

Before first grey three or four men with their collars high had come up the yard and gone low-voiced into the back room off the Vail wheel-shed, whose door keeps its finger’s width of open. Sella carried past at the half-pace. She gave the house no name for the men, turned no head toward the door, and did not say the room was cold. The boy took the three or four into the file and left them there without a figure and without a face; a room that stands a finger’s width open is a room the house has decided to keep, and the keeping of it was older than any man who went into it and would outlast them all. The door kept its finger’s width. The yard swept past it and went on with the firing.

Jorra Vail came through with the day’s fine grey slate dust ground small as meal into both forearms past the bent little finger and under the nails, and it was not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel. He kept dusty forearms and clean ones on one tray and asked neither which slate had been broken nor whether it was a birth-slate; the two states sat side by side and did not become one account. Under the lowest rack the broken birth-slates stayed where they lay and were entered by nobody. He did not look toward the basket. The dust went into the creases past the bend of the finger the way dust goes into any crease, without being invited and without leaving, and his hands went on with the work as if the forearms were the forearms he had come in with.

“It doesn’t care whose hands. It cares if they’re steady,” he told the wheel, in the flat voice that went to the tool before it went to the clay and to the clay before it went to a person. He told a pot, “You’ll do.” He knew the full note of it before it left the wheel — knew the pitch it would give under a knuckle when it had cooled and been set down — and he did not say so when a High Kiln kiln-master, come up on the bad wind with cuffs the colour of the ridge, said the pot was singing before its time and that a pot which sings early generally sings itself out early. Jorra said nothing. He kept the knowing where he kept such things, on the tray of answers that had come near to full and had not been paid, and not among the hearings. The kiln-master went off with his sentence whole. The pot went on turning. Neither of the two men was the wiser for what the boy had heard in the withholding, and the knowing stood on its tray, advanced, exact and unspendable.

The two tellings of the shut counting room stood where they stood, side by side with no joining and no third. Men had gone into a room that stands a finger’s width open since first grey, and the morning gave no third telling to set beside them; the room kept its open finger and the tellings kept their distance, and the house went on with the firing as if its own wall had no mouth in it. The boy had learned early that a second likeness is the beginning of a rule and a third likeness is the beginning of an account, and that a man with a clean slate needs only the third to stand the other two up and find a payer at the foot of them. He held the two where they were and would not stand a third up to keep them company.

Before Sella had finished telling him the fire was low, Jorra’s bent finger went into the cold iron of the damper, moved the tongue a hair’s breadth, and left it standing. The cold took the warmth out of the fingertip as iron takes it, without haste and without malice. The end of her sentence arrived at the pot instead of at him, and the household straightened around his silence the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted off it: Sella’s hands went on to the next pot, Tarn’s head stayed down over his work, and nothing in the room turned to look at what had been done. It was an obedience and it was a correction neither of them would name, and the room came level again in the space of a breath.

He read it as one piece of iron with two bites: the bite that holds a vessel whole and the bite that can take the hand off a man, the holding and the taking in the same throat of iron, and no word in Grathic that would hold both mercies in one breath. He had known a clamp for what it was in a better-lit room — a thing you set where the vessel was thin, and thanked for the mercy that stopped the bleed, and hated for the same mercy when it stayed a turn too long — but a clamp is a clamp and the two bites here were one bite filed on a place of its own, joined to no kiln and worth nothing. A man who calls it a doctrine has already found the payer he wanted. It stood where it stood, and the boy could not have reached it, and there was no hand in the basket to lay across it.

Tarn took the broom only after it had been instructed. Sella gave the instruction to the broom before she gave it to anybody and never gave it to the basket, and he took the tool up as a man takes up an instrument already named, saying, “Yes, house,” to the early thaw and the late, and going on wanting the late because it puts off the paying. His seventh year stood one tend from its end and still held: six counted in the worn thumb-mark where the house could check, and the seventh where it could not, the small knife set back at the very edge of its sill with the unforged collector sentence lying beside it. The breath from Seed 16 was unspent, with its small interval standing at the top of each reach where the breath should have gone in and did not. His chest had been tight since the dark, and Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line, and no scrubbing lifted it; the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back.

One hundred and fifty days old and five months, told by nobody, exact and unspent in the basket at the edge of the work while the yard went around what it would not enter. Jorra’s knowing of the pot’s note went on the near-full tray and not among the hearings; no seventh hearing came to fill a place where none was owed, and he did not invent one to keep the six company. The kiln missed its fifth behind the wall; under the floor at home the fire would miss its fifth at dusk; from behind the racks came the river’s note over water, a fifth low thing never laid among the hearings. Three places kept their own count and none borrowed from another. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water and came down against the fold.

His stroke went on over the flagstones past the shallow bright patch without even the half-check, and he did not turn his head along the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector. No collector came. He said nothing about the room and Jorra said nothing about the ear, and the two silences stayed unjoined under the one roof while the kilns went on breathing in fours behind the wall. The stones kept the shallow bright patch where the stroke had once stopped, a place nobody entered and nobody swept, and the broom went over it as water goes over a stone that lies where a stone lies: the stroke went on past it without the half-check, and the stones kept the place where it stopped as a thing nobody will enter.

The light went out of the yard the way water goes out of a shallow pan, by degrees and with no hand calling the moment, and Sella Vail lifted the boy up from the edge of the work and carried him toward the roof at the half-pace she kept from every open slate. The instruction had gone to the wool and the fire and the pot and the broom and never to the basket; a basket set down is a pot set down and left, and a pot that is set down is not spoken to. Woodsmoke went before her up the clay road and the river wind came up to meet it, the two passing between the low houses the way two people pass on a stair in the dark, each with a hand on the wall and neither giving way and neither speaking. It was the same passage she had made every dusk of his life and would make again, and he was one hundred and fifty days old and five months exactly and nobody walking ahead of him carried the number in any hand.

The half-pace was not slow and not hurried; it was a distance kept, measured from the one thing in the yard that must not be touched, the way a surgeon measures a field from the instrument laid out at its edge and knows where the hand must stop. She went around the pan’s empty place with the tilt of shoulder and basket that keeps a standing thing standing, and nothing knocked, and nobody said that anything was gone. At the yard well the trough had lost the skin of ice that had lain on it thin as a skim of fat set aside in a dish, and the water showed bare and dark under its little roof, and the woman letting down a bucket called it weather, and the bucket went down into weather and came up with weather in it. At the yard she went wide around the space beneath the lowest rack where the broken birth-slates lie, as wide as a woman goes around a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and did not explain the step. The step explained itself to anybody who had lived a year in that yard, and the boy had lived a year in it in the only way he had ever lived anywhere, which was carried and set down and carried again. He went around the three absences with her and carried none of them across into the basket.

Under the Vail roof she lifted him out without looking into the basket, set him on the bed and not in the Debt Loft, base first and weight settled, and put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without once looking at his face. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the dry heat of the yard and she left it frozen and unwiped, because no instruction had ever been given about the thread and a child set down is a pot set down and left. Her hand rested a half-beat longer than the setting needed before it went away. It was a longer rest than setting a bundle asks for, and it came in the same place every dusk and went away in the same way, and he had stopped, long ago, expecting it to become a touch or a word. It became neither. It went away and did not come back.

She went to the hearth. “It was the thaw,” she told the fire, which was the sentence she told it when a neighbour spoke of a counting room shut in a war and when a man came back from the ice with a number in his fist and when nothing had happened at all. She turned the coals with the back of a broken pot, the iron edge going under and lifting and letting fall, and the fire answered four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been. “The house will keep it,” she said, where another woman would have said I. She stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand counting under her breath toward no opening, her lips moving along a row that was not there, and pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn, the one grey streak at her temple showing pale off the coals. She set the pot rim out with its handle standing two inches beyond the reaching hand that would not close on it, did not look toward the bed, did not touch the widow’s mark at a dusk because a dusk is not an opening, and went to her own bed without explaining the count. The two rooms did not touch.

In the small hours cold came in its known order, cheek first, then temple, then the small bare joint at the wrist where the wool had ridden back. He lay on his back under the roof and turned out his file the way a man lays instruments on a towel before a case begins, each thing going into its own hollow and nothing resting across anything else. It was the one honest work in the room and the most useless: everything was in its place, exact, complete and worth nothing. He had spent a life reducing shoulders in the dark and could not lift his own hand to his own chin; he could lay out nine objects in nine hollows and could not have told a living person what they were. The count grew more exact and therefore more isolating, and the hand that would have offered it was the hand outside the wool that went up an inch and came down.

Six hearings of the full note lay beside and never among. Five came from four sources — a cup across a birth room, a pot under the wheel-shed wall, the district’s slate twice at the Cross, and a man giving at the Drawing — and the sixth was the small flat knock out of Dessa Silt as the men lifted, from a person and not a vessel, filed beside the five and never counted into them. There was no seventh and he did not invent one to fill the empty place. The empty place was not owed a seventh; a man who invents a hearing to keep six company has already begun to want an account, and an account wants a foot at the bottom of it with a name under the foot. He kept the six where they lay. The told bell of the Long Night stood beside the heard bell out of the yard and did not become a third sound.

Three near-full answers stood on their own tray, advanced and not paid. A rack pot lifted a hand’s breadth too high and set down again; a pot under the wall that answered almost full with air under the note and a want in it; and Jorra Vail’s knowing of the note a Vail pot would give before it left the wheel, which he had kept from a High Kiln kiln-master who said the pot was singing before its time. Jorra’s knowing sat among the answers and not among the hearings, because a knowing withheld is not a hearing given, and the boy had learned early to keep the two kinds of thing on two places. The older intervals lay where they had always lain, with the half-beat behind a standing finger, the stroke that went on over the flagstones without the check, his own held breath from a market, and the unchecking stroke that came after. Each was an object. None was a cause.

On the widened tray lay nine stoppings of the near-saying — Reed 5, 8, 11, 14, 17, 20, 23, 26 and 29 — and he held them apart by hand. They were objects and not a rule, intervals and not payments, and no payer had been named to any of them. The loan that could hear was uncalled and the seventh year stood one tend from its end and was still held. Nine likenesses standing in a row will call themselves a doctrine if nobody keeps a finger between them, and a doctrine is an account that has found a payer at the foot of it; he had known a man in a better-lit room to strip the mark off a corpse and enter it and go home clean, and he had learned what a row of neat entries will do when somebody wants a foot at the bottom. So he held them apart. No tenth stopping was suffered to stand up before the seventh year had been earned, and no tenth was owed on this night; a tenth stood up too early is a debt invented to make the other nine into a bill. The nine lay where they lay, exact, complete and unspent.

Beside these, on no tray of their own, lay the pieces that would not sit in a hollow. The damper was one iron with two bites — the bite that holds a vessel whole and the bite that can take the hand off a man — filed joined to no kiln, with no word in Grathic that would hold both mercies in one breath. The two tellings of the shut counting room stood side by side with no third, for men had gone into a room that keeps its finger’s width of open and the night had supplied no second voice to make a rule of them. The told bell stood beside the heard bell. An inventory is not a key and a list is not a hand; he could have set the whole district’s withholdings on a towel in order and still not have lifted the lid of one tin.

In the other room the loaf shelf kept its two unnamed gaps — the old space at the row’s end and the palm’s breadth that had come in with no scrape, crumb, print or word — and the charcoal under its front edge waited where marks wait when nobody has been told to read them. One stiff brown-edged crust lay in the fold two inches from his ear with its empty place beside it unnamed and no third. The sixth green was a colour joined to no ear, and the foundling’s sixth half-turn a shape with no sale, never named in the dark and never, in any room of his head, called by the name the colour would one day want. Before Sella had finished telling him the fire was low, Jorra’s bent finger went into the cold iron of the damper, moved the tongue a hair’s breadth, and left it standing. The cold took the warmth out of the fingertip as iron takes it, without haste and without malice. The end of her sentence arrived at the pot instead of at him, and the household straightened around his silence the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted off it: Sella’s hands went on to the next pot, Tarn’s head stayed down over his work, and nothing in the room turned to look at what had been done. It was an obedience and it was a correction neither of them would name, and the room came level again in the space of a breath.

He read it as one piece of iron with two bites: the bite that holds a vessel whole and the bite that can take the hand off a man, the holding and the taking in the same throat of iron, and no word in Grathic that would hold both mercies in one breath. He had known a clamp for what it was in a better-lit room — a thing you set where the vessel was thin, and thanked for the mercy that stopped the bleed, and hated for the same mercy when it stayed a turn too long — but a clamp is a clamp and the two bites here were one bite filed on a place of its own, joined to no kiln and worth nothing. A man who calls it a doctrine has already found the payer he wanted. It stood where it stood, and the two tellings of the shut room stood beside it with no third, and the boy could not have reached the iron, and there was no hand in the basket to lay across it.

At the going of the light the coals were banked under the back of a broken pot and the small knife lay at the very edge of its sill where the house could see it and the seventh year stood where the house could not. Tarn told the kindling, “Six counted. The next I don’t count.” The sentence about an ear under this roof being worth more than the debt that holds it came up in him as it had come up nine times before, and on this day it was not brought to the word that would call the loan. It stopped short of the threshold, the way a man stops short of a step he has learned to know by the feel of the floor under his foot, and no tenth stopping stood up to join the nine. He drew the breath that should have gone in at the top of the reach and did not spend it, said, “Yes, house,” to the fire, and went back to the broom.

Nine objects lay on the widened tray, and he held them apart by hand. Reed 5, 8, 11, 14, 17, 20, 23, 26 and Reed 29: nine stoppings of the near-saying, objects and not a rule, intervals and not payments, no payer named to any. A thing held nine times is still owed. The absence of a tenth was a keeping, not a payment, and he would not stand one up to keep the nine company before the seventh year had been earned. Nine likenesses standing in a row will call themselves a doctrine if nobody keeps a finger between them, and a doctrine is an account that has found a payer. The loan that can hear stayed uncalled, the Seed 16 breath unspent, the seventh year one tend from its end and not given. The unforged collector sentence lay beside the knife where it had lain all day, waiting for a hand that did not come.

Sella, within hearing, gave the end of her instruction to the pot and asked nothing. Jorra did not ask about the ear and Tarn did not ask about the room, and the two silences stayed unjoined under the one roof while the kiln went on breathing in fours behind the wall. Her hand did not go to the widow’s mark at a hearth or a wheel-shed, because neither was an opening; it hung where it hung, a thumb-length of Low Kiln clay under its glaze with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and the rule held with nobody to praise it and nobody to see it broken. In the other room the loaf shelf kept its two unnamed gaps and the charcoal under its board waited unread, and the pan’s gone place on the wall kept the round shape of its absence. He set each where it stood and wrote nothing across the space between. No third gap came. No fourth likeness made a cause. No finger of his drew the line.

Want came up in him, small and importunate as it always was. It wanted the crust two inches from his ear in the other room, stiff and brown-edged and past smelling of anything; it wanted the pot rim two inches beyond the reaching hand at the hearth. The surgeon in him could have said how long the crust had lain and what had become of the warmth in it, and could have named the exact reach of a hand that had once reduced a shoulder in the dark and now could not lift its own chin from a fold of cloth. The saying would have put neither crumb nor pot within reach. The joke had gone flat with use, and he kept it on a place of its own beside the others, where it cost him nothing because nobody laughed.

One hundred and fifty-two stood exact and unspent in the going of the light, five months and two days, told by nobody, and nobody under that roof would have known what a number was if it had been spoken. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water through the finger’s-width door and came down against the fold. The stones kept the shallow bright patch where a stroke had once stopped, the nine objects lay in nine hollows with no payer named to any, and the loan that could hear went on being worth more than the debt that held it, uncalled.

The basket came up off the flagstones where it had stood at the edge of the work, lifted by the handle with the little-door burn closing on the wicker and opening again, and Sella Vail did not look into it. The wheel had given over its last pot and the coals had been banked under the back of a broken pot, and the going of the light had come the way the water came to the sill and went off it again, without a name put on it and without a figure. She gave the instruction to the wool and the fire before she gave it to anybody — Keep it off his ears. It’s the river wind — and never gave it to the basket, which was a pot filled and carried and nothing that wanted an instruction of its own. Then she carried the boy up the clay road at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, woodsmoke going before her and the river wind coming up to meet it, the two passing on the stair without speaking as they had passed every morning and every evening of his life. The kiln breathed behind the wall in fours with a gap, and the house went on with what the house kept.

Halfway up she went around the place on the wall where a pan had stood out to dry through Reed and was not standing now, tilting shoulder and basket so nothing knocked, and she did not say that it was gone. Nobody under that roof had said it, and a thing nobody names is a thing a house keeps without an entry, kept all the more carefully for having no slate to put it on. At the well she went around the trough whose skin of ice had thinned and gone, the water standing bare and dark where the skim of fat had lain, and called it weather to nobody in particular; the weather had heard worse and answered nothing. At the yard she went wide around the space beneath the lowest rack where the broken birth-slates lay, as wide as a woman goes around a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and did not explain the step to the boy she was carrying or to anybody else. He felt the wide turn in the shoulder that carried him, the small outward swing of the basket, the settling back; he had learned the shape of it before he had learned the shape of his own hand.

Under the roof she lifted him out of the basket without looking into it, set him on the bed and not in the Debt Loft, base first and weight settled, put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without once looking at his face, and left the thread of drool frozen and unwiped because nobody under any roof had been given an instruction about the thread and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. Her hand rested a half-beat longer than the setting needed before it went away — the half-beat she had learned in the winter she sat up three nights with a child of her own who cooled while everybody was kind, and had never told anybody — and then she did not go back to it. The grey streak at her temple showed pale in the dying light off the coals.

At the hearth she told the fire it was the thaw, turned the coals with the back of a broken pot, and said, “The house will keep it,” where another woman would have said I. She stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand counting under her breath toward no opening, her lips moving on a row that stood in some other room of her head, pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn, and set the pot rim out with its handle standing two inches beyond the reaching hand that would not close on it. She did not look toward the bed. Her hand did not go to the widow’s mark, because a dusk is not an opening; it hung where it hung at her throat, a thumb-length of Low Kiln clay under its glaze with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and the rule held with nobody to praise it and nobody to see it broken. She went to her own bed without explaining the count, and the fire answered four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for a hundred and fifty-two days.

In the small hours he turned out his file the way a man lays instruments on a towel before a case begins, each thing going into its own hollow and nothing resting across anything else. Nothing in the room moved while he did it. The wool went on riding back from the small bare joint at his wrist, the thread at his mouth kept its frozen line, and the file turned itself out behind his eyes with the exactness of a hand that had once set out a tray and could not now lift its own chin from a fold of cloth. It was the one honest work left to him and the most useless. Everything went where it belonged, and belonging bought nothing.

Six hearings of the full note lay beside and never among — five from four sources, a cup across a birth room, a pot under the wheel-shed wall through a finger’s width of door, the district’s slate twice at the Cross, a man giving at the Drawing, and the sixth, the small flat knock out of a woman as the men lifted her, from a person and filed apart from the vessels — and there was no seventh and none invented to stand in the empty place where none was owed. He had learned not to look for the seventh by staring at the empty place; a man who puts an instrument in a hollow because the hollow is there has already decided what the case is. The empty place stayed empty and the six stayed six.

Three near-full answers stood on their own tray, advanced and not paid: the pot under the wall, the rack pot lifted a hand’s breadth too high and set down again, and Jorra Vail’s knowing of the full note of a Vail pot, which he had kept back from a kiln-master who said it was singing before its time. Jorra’s knowing went among the answers and never among the hearings. A knowing held back is not a hearing given, and the two things lay on separate hollows with the space between them kept open by a mind that could not have closed it with a finger. He did not make a fourth answer to keep the three company.

The older intervals lay where they had always lain, Stone 13, Stone 17 and Stone 25, with the half-beat behind a standing finger, the stroke that went on over the flagstones without the check, his own held breath from a market morning, and the unchecking stroke that followed it. Each had happened. None had been paid. They were objects with the edges worn smooth by being taken out and put back so many times, and the taking out had never once turned one of them into a rule.

On the widened tray lay nine stoppings — Reed 5, 8, 11, 14, 17, 20, 23, 26 and Reed 29 — objects and not a rule, intervals and not payments, no payer named to any. He kept a finger between them in the dark, the same finger he had kept there since Reed 29, and it had never yet lifted one of them across to its neighbour. A thing held nine times is still owed. The absence of a tenth was a keeping and not a payment, and he would not stand one up to keep the nine company before the seventh year had been earned. Nine likenesses standing in a row will call themselves a doctrine if nobody keeps a finger between them, and a doctrine is an account that has found a payer. The loan that can hear stayed uncalled, the breath from Seed 16 unspent with its small interval standing at the top of each reach, the seventh year one tend from its end and still held.

Beside them, on no tray of their own, lay the damper as one piece of iron with two bites, the bite that holds a vessel whole and the bite that can take the hand off a man, joined to no kiln and worth nothing, with no word in Grathic that would hold both mercies in one breath. The two tellings of the shut counting room stood side by side with no third, and the morning had given him nothing to set between them, and he would not make a third to keep them company. The told bell of the Long Night stood beside the heard bell out of the yard, four, four and a gap in two places that did not meet. An inventory is not a key and a list is not a hand; he had laid out the whole tray every night since he had learned to lay it out and not one object on it had ever come into his reach.

The charcoal under the front edge waited in its clusters, some pressed hard and some faded nearly into the grain, unread and uncounted, and the boy did not go back to it in the dark because there was no hand that could have lifted the board an inch. The sixth green was a colour joined to no ear, and the foundling was a shape with no sale, a half-turn stopped in its arc and never given a name in the dark, no loaf, no slate, no payer. The reed-cutter’s cord went inside its coat with knots unread; Orra Weir’s knot-string kept its last double turn legible, tail tucked, left over right with the small weight of a thumb pressed in, and the word forty was not spoken in this room; Mira Silt’s second cord lay twisted twice beneath an apron and had never been carried upriver to Nine Pots or lifted at a door; Alda Reed’s six pages and both tins stayed shut three doors down, and the round pewter tin above the abandoned board-bed kept its pale fold and its thumb-bright rub. Each thing stood on its own place. There was a whole district of keeping within a dozen paces of the basket and not one of the records could be entered by a hand that could not close on a cup.

Three counts in three places did not become one. Through the finger’s-width door came the river’s note of water over water, a fifth low thing never counted among the hearings; through the floor the carried fire gave four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for a hundred and fifty-two days; overhead the boards kept four nails, four, and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb. They lay within a hand’s span of each other in the dark, three counts in three places, and none of them became one, though a man with a clean slate might have stood them up side by side and found a figure at the foot of them before the coals were cold. He had learned to know the danger of a third likeness before it was spoken. He kept the three apart by not looking at any of them too long.

Cold arrived in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, the wool riding back from the small bare joint, and his head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and nobody under the roof had been given an instruction about that sound either. He knew what the cold was doing as he had once known what a chart was saying before anybody read it out, and the knowing worked as perfectly as the day he learned it and bought him nothing now. Want came up in him, small and importunate as it always was. It wanted the crust two inches from his ear, stiff and brown-edged and past smelling of anything. It wanted the number spoken by somebody who would have known what a number was, by somebody who could have set a day against a day and said what had been reached and what it had cost. He got neither and could not ask for either, and the wanting did not go away because it was not answered. It lay down beside the other objects on the tray and took up no more room than they did.

He had been carried two days into the firing month and nobody had said how far. One hundred and fifty-two stood in the dark where it had stood on the night before and the night before that, exact, complete, unspent, five months and two days, and there was not a mouth under that roof that would have known what to do with the saying of it. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water through the finger’s-width door and came down against the fold, as it had come down on every night of his life. The month had opened with a hand at the mark and a fire under a broken pot; the opening had been entered on no slate and the fifth pull had been missed in no account, and the count went on into the days ahead with nine held words, a sixth colour, a sixth shape and the loan uncalled. The note went on over water through the gap in the door. The fire missed its fifth under the floor. The charcoal waited under the shelf for a hand that was not his and a day that had not come.

The month turned over in the dark the way a pot turns on a wheel, a quarter at a time. The boy was one hundred and fifty days old and five months exactly, and nobody under that roof said either number. The kilns had been brought up for the last working season.
