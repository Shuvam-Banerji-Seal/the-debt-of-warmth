The Road Knows the Weight

Days 163–169 · Tallow 14–20, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, the Weir and the Cross, Sill

The slate goes up the road edge-on and comes back the weight it went. The bell says four, four and a gap, and nobody enters the gap. A hand beneath a board knows what the hand above it will not say, and a road carries what it is not asked to read.

— A saying of the Clay Yard, spoken at the second reading of Tallow

— Day 163 —

Tallow 14, Y.H. 813 (Fourthday)

The first thing the boy knew of that morning was a smell: hot clay under tallow, the yard giving up its water into the dry cold. Under the smell came a sound that was not a bell. The kiln drew breath behind the wall in fours, and there was a gap where the fifth breath should have been, the way a man’s sentence can have a hole in it that everybody hears and nobody fills. He lay on the bed under the Vail roof in Low Kiln, one hundred and sixty-three days old and five months and thirteen days, and nobody under any roof said so.

The fold was at his ear and under his chin, wool gone damp where his breath had settled and frozen again in the dry heat. One stiff brown-edged crust lay two inches from his ear, gone leathery since the planting, with an empty place beside it that had no name and had never needed one. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the night and nobody had wiped it, because nobody under this roof had been given an instruction about the thread, and a basket set down was a pot set down and left. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and no instruction existed for that sound either.

Through the finger’s-width door came the note of water going over water, a fifth low thing belonging to no count he could enter. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward it and came down against the fold. It could not close two inches on the rim of the basket that held him. It could not lift to his own chin. The surgeon who had once reduced shoulders in the dark now lay under a fold of wool and counted the nails overhead, four on the near board, four on the next, and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb.

He had stopped expecting to be told what any of it counted, which was not the same as not wanting it. One hundred and sixty-three stood exact in the dark, complete and unspent, and he set it against the house’s arithmetic in loaves and glances and spaces at the row’s end without letting the two agree.

Sella Vail had been at the hearth before the grey. The carried fire lay under blind ash and the back of a broken pot where she had turned it in the night. She told it it was the thaw and fed it, and it answered four low pulls with a silence where the fifth should have been. Its payer had had no name under any roof for a hundred and sixty-three days and she did not supply one now. She turned the coals with the back of the pot and said Feed the fire before you feed yourself, which was a proverb of a dead man’s and an instruction to the pot, not to a person. The little-door burn across the back of her left hand shut on the pot’s weight and opened on nothing when she took her hand away; the grey streak at her temple showed pale off the coals.

Every instruction that morning went to the wool, the fire, the pot and the broom and never to the basket. Keep it off his ears, she told the wool. It’s the river wind. To the fire she said There, then. It wanted feeding, as if the wanting were a thing a fire had and a woman might notice the way one notices a child has stopped coughing. She said the house where another woman would have said I, and she kept her half-pace from every open slate in the yard, going around them as a woman goes around a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. The widow’s mark hung on its cord at her throat where it had hung since the first firing and she did not touch it, because a morning and a shelf are not openings and the hand goes to the mark before the kiln and at no other hour.

The kiln had been brought up in the night for the third working week. At the kiln mouth the clay stood in the hot dry smell of the month, dust ground small as meal under the nails of whoever had worked it, and the breath went in fours with its gap behind the wall. That breath, and not the fire’s missing fifth, was the danger the house kept through all of Tallow; Sella went about it with her back turned, as if a thing not looked at could not be entered. On a wrong wind there came a sweetish edge off High Kiln that the yard called weather, and she told the fire it was the thaw and would go when it goes, and gave the wind no figure and no name.

Out in the Clay Yard the women went around the places the yard kept without naming. The broom went around the clean dark line where the Ash ridge had turned its corner a year and more ago, and around the new hairline of grey beside it, finer than the old, which the house called the thaw and would go when it goes. It went around the space beneath the lowest rack where the broken birth-slates lay, entered by nobody, and around the empty place in the returning line where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out. They went around all of it with a look and a wide step and no number, the way a person goes around a grave in a yard they must cross anyway, and swept none of it. A morning is not an opening, and a yard that goes around a thing has not entered it.

At the well the trough stood bare and dark, its skin of ice gone off in the lean week, and a woman with her sleeves wet to the elbow called the water weather and went off with her pail carried level so that nothing slopped. A woman come up from the racks with slate dust dried grey in the creases of her wrists asked what the month’s firing would want set down there now the kilns had been brought up. Ask the weather, Sella said. Ask the river. The house has said it. She gave the instruction to the broom and not to the woman and went on at the half-pace, carrying the unanswered question away level in her hand so that nothing slopped. The woman went off with a direction where a figure should have stood, and the boy filed the shape of it beside the other answers that had had their questions taken out, keeping it from standing up with them into a cause, because a likeness in a row will call itself a doctrine if no hand holds it apart. He was carried past at two paces and wanted the trough and the weather and got neither. Under the roof the clay jar stood cold on the shelf by the wall, and Sella’s eye went to it once as she passed and no further; she did not touch it, and no figure came off it into the morning. A jar looked at was a jar looked at. A thing kept cold under a glaze was not the same as a thing kept warm in a fold, and he set the two on separate places and drew no line between them.

Tarn stood with the broom waiting in his hand until she had given it its instruction. She gave it to the broom before she gave it to him: Go around. It cost enough to come in. It’ll cost more to take out. Yes, house, he said, and took it up as a man takes up a tool that has already been named.

He was twenty-two and bent at the shoulders from six years in the slip pit, which made him look older than his term. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line and no scrubbing lifted it, and the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what little light there was and gave it back. Six years stood in a mark worn almost flat by his thumb where the house could check them; the seventh stood at the lip of the sill where it could not, and the small knife lay at the very edge with a dead man’s unforged sentence beside it. His chest had been tight since the dark. The breath that should have gone in at the top of each reach was still unspent, a small interval standing where it should have been, and he did not spend it now.

His stroke went over the flagstones past the shallow bright patch without the half-check, the stones keeping the place where it had stopped and joined to no cause. The bright patch lay where the stroke had stopped one morning weeks ago, and the stones kept it as they kept the other places, without a figure and without a payer, and Tarn went over it without looking down. It went over the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector, over and over, without once turning along it, and no collector came. Nine stoppings stood where they had stood — Reed 5, 8, 11, 14, 17, 20, 23, 26 and 29 — objects and not a rule, intervals and not payments, no payer named to any of them, and the loan that could hear stayed uncalled. A tenth had not stood up out of the nine before the seventh year was earned.

The fraction from the morning of Day 161 stood where it had been left: a hand held back from the cold iron until a sentence had finished, held and unspoken, with no payer named to it and no slate entered. Nobody repeated it. Nobody named a payer to it. Nobody looked toward the basket. Through the finger’s-width door the river kept its fifth low note, and the fire kept its gap under the floor, and the two things stayed in two places, as they had stayed every morning of his life.

He read the firing the way he had once read a room he was not allowed into: a theatre in which heat was kept and counted behind a screen, and a man stood at the gate with a towel in his hands and did not go in. The gate had two bites. The damper was one piece of iron with the bite that holds a vessel whole and the bite that can take the hand off a man, mercy and mutilation in the same narrow jaw, with no word in Grathic that would hold both. He had stood at such gates and known the heat behind the screen by its count and not by touching it, and he had known the shape of an answer that had had its question taken out of it. A field covered with a cloth before the instrument came down, a hand lifted out of a field before the dressing went on: the same motion performed on a person who could not object, and here performed on the morning while he lay in the fold and was the person who could not object. Here he could not enter, could not speak the warning, could not lift a hand to the iron. The cost of the heat was paid by a man whose name was not spoken under any roof, and the boy had lain a hundred and sixty-three days under this one and had never once heard it said.

The chart in such rooms was exact and the body on the table was not always saved, and nobody made the two agree. Here the question was taken out of everything before it reached the basket. The fire’s fifth was a silence with no payer; the river’s note was a fifth low thing never numbered among the full note; the boards overhead kept four nails, four, and a fifth that was a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts in three places, and he would not let any one of them stand up and become the others, because a likeness in a row will call itself a doctrine if no hand holds it apart, and a doctrine is an account that has found a payer. No payer stood to any of these.

The yard’s heat went on around the clean dark line and the thaw and the rack space and the empty place in the returning line, and the house’s arithmetic went on in loaves and glances and a space at the row’s end nobody called a number. He lay in the fold at the low angle where the underside of a board becomes a roof and took in the morning through the two places it came to him: hot clay under tallow at the hearth, water over water through the finger’s-width door. Neither came close enough to reach.

His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water through the finger’s-width door and came down against the fold. One hundred and sixty-three stood exact, complete and unspent in the dark of the wool. The fire missed its fifth under the floor, its payer unnamed for a hundred and sixty-three days; the river kept its fifth over water; the boards kept their knot. The kiln went on breathing in fours with its gap behind the wall, and the morning’s work went on around the places nobody entered, and the count went on where nobody could hear it.

— Day 164 —

Tallow 15, Y.H. 813 (Fifthday)

The boy woke with the grey still unborn and the kiln already breathing. It drew in fours behind the wall and left a gap where the fifth breath should have been, the way a man leaves a word out of a sentence and goes on as though the sentence were whole. He had lain one hundred and sixty-four days under that gap and had never heard anybody fill it. Nobody under any roof counted his days aloud; he counted them himself in the dark, the way a man might count the beats of a horse under a blanket and be no wiser for the number.

The basket sat at the angle where the underside of a board becomes a roof, and the underside was his ceiling. Wool at his ear, wool under his chin, the thread at his mouth frozen from the night because no instruction existed about the thread. Hot clay under tallow came down off the banked fires, and the mineral tang of the Ash ridge lay along the skirting board where no broom had been told to go. He was in Low Kiln, under the Vail roof, and the world reached him in two streams: heat through the floor from the hearth, water over water through the finger’s-width door. Neither came within reach. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the river and came down on the fold.

Then the men came.

Three or four went up the Clay Yard before first grey with their collars high against the sweetish wind, their voices so low that the words arrived at the basket as a shape and not as sound. They went into the back room off the Vail wheel-shed, which kept its finger’s width of open the way a scab keeps one edge lifted, and the door did not close behind them. Sella Vail carried past at the half-pace with the basket on her hip. She turned no head toward the door, gave the house no name for the men, and did not say the room was cold. The little-door burn shut on the basket’s weight and opened on nothing when she set him down. She told the wool to keep it off his ears and told the wool it was the river wind, and if she had a thought about the men it went where the other unspoken thoughts went, into the private roll nobody was shown.

Two tellings of a shut counting room stood side by side behind the wall: one from a man with a date in his mouth eighteen leagues east, one from a man who had only mentioned the room at a bucket-round and gone off with his bag and his mud. No third had come through all the mornings since. The boy kept the two apart and did not lay one across the other, because two tellings are two tellings and a joining would stand up and ask for a payer. The door kept its finger’s width. The room was not entered by him, by her, or by the men who went low into it so early; they entered it and gave nothing out.

Jorra came through the finger’s width with the morning on his forearms.

Fine grey slate dust was ground small as meal into both forearms, past the bend of the left little finger and under the nails, not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel. Jorra Vail was sixteen and built like a gate hinge, tall for a clay boy and narrow, with his hair cut short so that Ash could not nest in it, a thing the district noticed and he never explained. The dust had got there by being broken. The slip that dried to old bone on a working forearm had been mixed on purpose and laid on by hand, and the boy kept the two sorts apart as a matter of making: one a thing undone, the other a thing put together. Jorra kept the dusty forearms and the clean ones on one tray, two states of one morning, and did not make the dust say what it was not there to say.

Jorra asked neither which slate had been broken nor whether it was a birth-slate. The broken birth-slates stayed under the lowest rack, gone around by the yard all morning and entered by nobody, and the empty place in the returning line where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out kept its look and its wide step and no number. If the dust had come from one place and not the other, the forearms said so only to a man who already knew how to read them, and the boy was not going to ask a question that would have to be answered in a word.

He spoke to the wheel before he spoke to anyone. “It doesn’t care whose hands. It cares if they’re steady.” He said it to the wheel as another man might say it to a horse before he put his hand to the harness, and the wheel gave nothing back, only went on turning under the weight of his weight in the hollow his father had worn. He did not look toward the basket.

A pot stood near-full on the bench. “You’ll do,” he told it, in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either. He knew the full note that pot would give three turns before it left the wheel, knew the roundness under the knuckle and the true wall and the foot seated square, the note a sound vessel gives when nothing has been left out of it. He did not say so.

That was the shape of the knowing the boy had learned to recognise in the year and more he had lain under this roof: to know the note before the knuckle went to it, to read the chart while the cloth was still over the field. He had been in rooms where heat was kept and counted behind a screen and a man stood at the gate with a towel in his hands and did not go in, and the knowing in those rooms was worth exactly what it cost to keep quiet. Here it was worth a place on a tray. The knowing went to the near-full answers, advanced and not paid, beside the hearings and never among them, and no seventh hearing stood up to join the six. No pot was lost in the yard that morning.

The kiln-master came up on the wrong wind. He was from High Kiln, with Ash pale on his cuffs and the sweetish edge of lead glaze on his breath, and he looked at the pot on the bench as a man looks at a thing he has been asked to price. “It’s singing before its time,” he said. “A pot that sings early generally sings itself out early.”

Jorra said nothing. He did not say I haven’t said, which would have been an answer. He let the saying stand where the wind had put it and went on turning, and the kiln-master, having made his observation, took himself and his sweetish breath off down the yard without waiting to be corrected. He meant nothing by it. Men from High Kiln said such things about pots; it was the kind of thing a kiln-master said when a pot caught his ear and he had a minute to spend.

The boy kept the saying on no tray at all. It was not a hearing and not an answer and not a cost; it was a thing a man had said on a wind and the wind had carried off. He drew no lesson from it and put no figure to it, because a lesson and a figure both want a payer and no payer stood to either. The dust ground small as meal stayed ground small as meal in the creases of a forearm, and the sweetish edge went on coming down off High Kiln whenever the wind turned that way, and the kiln’s breath went on in fours with its gap behind the wall. The month’s danger gathered in these things the way heat gathers under banked ash, toward an edge that could be paid later. It was not paid now. Nobody in the yard had said a word that could be entered.

It’ll cost more to take out.” He said Yes, house and swept around the clean dark line where the Ash ridge turned and the new finer hairline of grey beside it that the house called the thaw, and around the rack space and the place in the returning line, with a look and a wide step and no number. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line and no scrubbing lifted it, and the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back. Six years stood in a mark worn almost flat by his thumb where the house could check them; the seventh stood at the lip of the sill where it could not. His stroke went over the shallow bright patch without the half-check and over the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector, over and over, without once turning along it. No collector came. He did not ask about the room, and Jorra did not ask about the ear, and the two silences stayed unjoined under the one roof.

At midday she carried him in and set the basket down near the bench leg, at the low angle where the underside of the loaf shelf becomes a roof. She gave the instruction to the wool and to the fire and never to the basket, and said the house where another woman would have said I. Her voice went low at the hearth, low in the way weather goes low before a shift, and the household read it as it had learned to read Jorra’s quiet over three years: not as a thing to be asked about, but as a thing to stand around until it had passed.

It happened again as it had happened on the morning of Day 161, and because it happened again it was no longer a change. She had begun The fire’s low. It wants— and stopped, and Jorra’s bent finger went to the cold iron of the damper and stood there while she finished It wants feeding. He moved the tongue a hair’s breadth and left it standing. The household straightened around a sentence that had been allowed to finish the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted, and nobody said a word about the waiting, and nobody named a payer to it. The equality under the roof stood where it had moved on that other morning and was not moved again today.

The boy did not take the fraction up and lay it beside anything. He had put it on a place of its own when it first came, held and unspoken, not a reconciliation, not a hearing, not a payment, and a thing held is a thing kept where it was put. The iron had two bites, the bite that holds a vessel whole and the bite that can take the hand off a man, and there was no word in Grathic that would hold both in one mouthful. He had filed it as one piece of iron joined to no kiln, and he left it where it lay. The widow’s mark hung where it hung at her throat and she did not touch it, because a hearth at midday is not an opening and the hand goes to the mark before the kiln and at no other hour.

Through the finger’s-width door the river kept its fifth low thing, water over water. He put it on a place of its own, not a hearing of the full note, not a seventh to stand up beside the six and not invented to fill the empty place, not joined to the kiln’s gap under the floor or to the knot the width of a thumb overhead. Three counts in three places, and he would not let any one of them stand up and become the others, because a likeness in a row will call itself a doctrine if no hand holds it apart, and a doctrine is an account that has found a payer. No payer stood to any of these. The crust in the fold lay two inches from his ear with its empty place unnamed and no third; the shelf in the other room kept its two unnamed gaps and its charcoal unread; the round ring where the pan had stood kept its shape on a tray of its own. No fourth likeness was made a cause and no finger of his drew the line.

One hundred and sixty-four stood exact, complete and unspent in the dark of the wool. The reading stood somewhere ahead of it and nobody gave him its date, and nobody gave him the number of his days either, which was the same number and further out of reach than the crust. He had stopped expecting to be told, which was not the same as not wanting it.

The bent finger left the damper standing as it had stood on the morning before. Through the wall the fire answered four low pulls with the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for a hundred and sixty-four days. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water through the finger’s-width door and came down against the fold. The morning’s work went on around the places nobody entered, and the count went on where nobody could hear it.

— Day 165 —

Tallow 16, Y.H. 813 (Sixthday)

The boy came awake to the kiln counting in its sleep. Four low pulls behind the wall, a gap where the fifth should have stood, and then four again, the way a man breathes in his rest and leaves one breath out of the round without ever knowing he has left it out. He had lain one hundred and sixty-five days under that gap. Nobody under any roof had said the number to him, and nobody said it now. The grey came up between the boards and the heat came up through the floor, and neither of them carried a figure. He counted the day himself, as he had counted the others, because there was nothing else in the dark of the wool that would stay still long enough to be counted.

The basket sat where the underside of a board leaned down and became a roof. Wool at his ear, wool under his chin, the thread at his mouth frozen overnight because no instruction existed in this house about the thread. Hot clay lay under tallow in the air, and the mineral tang of Ash ran along the skirting board where no broom had been told to go. This was Low Kiln, under the Vail roof, and the morning reached him in two streams that did not meet: heat through the floor from the banked coals, water over water through the finger’s-width door. Neither came within the two paces of his hand. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the river and came down on the fold.

Sella Vail carried him out into the yard with the basket on her hip and the little-door burn shut on its weight. She gave the instruction to the wool before she gave it to the fire, and to the fire before the pot, and to the pot before the broom, and never once gave it to the basket. Keep it off his ears. It’s the river wind. She said the house where another woman would have said I, and kept the half-pace from every open slate the way a woman keeps clear of a cooking pot with a child at her skirt. The widow’s mark hung at her throat on its cord and she did not touch it, because a morning is not an opening and the hand goes to the mark before the kiln and at no other hour.

Out in the Clay Yard the firing month had made itself at home. The women went around the clean dark line where the Ash ridge had turned and the new finer hairline of grey beside it that the house called the thaw, and around the rack space with its broken birth-slates under the lowest rack, and around the empty place in the returning line where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out. They went with a look and a wide step and no number. The trough at the well stood bare and dark under its little roof and the water in it was called weather; the clay jar stood cold under its glaze, glanced at once and not touched. Nothing in the yard had been lost today and nothing in it had been entered. It lay among these as heat lies under banked ash, gathering toward an edge that could be paid later. It was not paid now. No man in the yard had said a word that any slate could take.

He read the firing as he read a theatre he could not enter: heat kept and counted behind a screen of brick, a gate with two bites, a man standing at the threshold with a towel in his hands and not going in. The damper was one piece of iron with two bites, the bite that holds a vessel whole and the bite that can take the hand off a man, and there was no word in Grathic that would hold both mercies in one mouthful. He had stood in such rooms while somebody else held the field, and the knowing there was worth exactly what it cost to keep quiet. Here it was worth a place from which nothing could be entered and no hand of his could reach the gate. He let the two bites lie where they lay, one iron joined to no kiln, and went on counting what was given to be counted.

Tarn stood with the broom in his hand until the broom had been given its instruction, and only then took it up.

“Go around. It cost enough to come in. It’ll cost more to take out.” He said Yes, house to the neighbour who wanted the thaw early and Yes, house to the one who wanted it late, agreeing so thoroughly that no argument could find a foot between them, and went on wanting the late one all the same, because a late thaw puts off the paying, though the paying had stood in the ruts since the water went. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line and no scrubbing lifted it, and the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back. Six years stood in a mark worn almost flat by his thumb where the house could check them. The seventh stood at the lip of the sill where it could not.

His stroke went over the shallow bright patch without even the half-check. It went over the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector, over and over, without once turning along it. No collector came. Nine stoppings stood where they had stood and no tenth stood up to join them, and the loan that could hear was not called and had not been called in all the days of the boy’s life. Tarn did not ask about the room, and Jorra Vail did not ask about the ear, and the two silences stayed unjoined under the one roof, as they had stayed every morning for three years. Garl Vail’s coat kept a broader man’s shape on its peg and the hollow wheel-seat kept its leftward lean, and between the two the house went about its work.

At midday a kiln-hand came down off the kiln mouth with the sweetish edge of lead glaze on his breath and crouched by the kindling under the roof to bank the coals. He said the thing sideways, as a man says a thing to a wall in the presence of a child who might repeat it, not to the basket and not to anybody. A man in Mud End had been taken quiet in his sleep. Them that found him had called it merciful.

The word lay a moment on the coals and went down with them. Nobody asked which man, or what had taken him, or what a quiet death had to do with a firing. The kiln-hand raked the ash over the glow with the back of his hand and went off the way he had come, and the yard went on with its work. The boy read the shape of it without entering it: the extremities going before the core, the skin doing what the vessels under it do when the Warm draws back, the core holding on after the rest has given out. He had known a room in which such a body was covered before the instrument came down, and a hand lifted out of a field before the dressing went on. The knowing was exact and worth nothing at two paces. He made no lesson of the lamb and put no figure to the quietness, because a lesson and a figure both want a payer, and no payer stood to either. Mud End kept its dead man the way the yard kept its empty place, and neither was laid across anything else.

At the going of the light Tarn banked the coals under the back of a broken pot and set the small knife at the very edge of its sill, where the house could see that it had been put back. Six counted where the house could check and the seventh at the lip where it could not. Garl’s unforged collector sentence lay beside the knife and was not entered. The wood stood in its four and the space where the fifth should have been, the shape a debt keeps when it will not say its own number.

“Six counted,” Tarn told the kindling. “The next I don’t count.”

The sentence about an ear under this roof being worth more than the debt that holds it came all the way to his teeth. It had come that far so many times that the boy knew the shape of it before the lips moved: the small intake at the top of the reach, the stop, the breath drawn that should have gone in and did not go in. The word that would call the loan stood behind the teeth with no figure on it and no name, and Tarn drew the breath and did not spend it, and turned the coals with the back of the pot and said Yes, house to the fire. The breath from Seed 16 stayed where it had stood for a hundred and sixty-five days, with its small interval at the top of each reach showing where it should have gone in. No collector came up the road. No tenth stopping stood up out of the nine. The seventh year pressed nearer its end than it had ever pressed and had still not given, and the loan that could hear was still uncalled. The boy set the stopping on the interval tray as an object among objects and not a rule, an interval and not a payment, with no payer named to any of the nine and no doctrine allowed to stand up out of nine likenesses. The fraction that had stood at the damper for four mornings stayed where it had been put, held and unspoken and not repeated; a thing held is a thing kept where it was put. He laid nothing across it. Tallow 20, Y.H. 813 (Tenthday)

The second Account-day and market-day of Tallow came up over the Cross the way the first had come, grey and close, with the river wind under the eaves and hot clay coming down on the same breath from High Kiln; and the boy lay in the basket in the moot-post’s finger’s width of shade with his cheek against cold iron while the crowd leaned over the reading block above him. He was one hundred and sixty-nine days old and five months and nineteen days, and nobody under any roof said so, because there was no instruction under any roof about telling a basket a number. Eight pigs had gone over the board before the light was properly in the boards and the potters were setting bowls out on the pot board with dust in the hollows of the rims, and the square went on with its market the way a body goes on with its work while a surgeon stands at the edge of the field, near enough to hear and not near enough to put a hand in. He could no more enter the square than he could enter the firing under the Vail roof. The cold iron at his cheek was the whole of his share of it.

He passed a finger over the figure. His eyes rested on it a breath longer than the writing required, the small check of a man assuring himself that the number was still the number he had set there, and he read it low into the crowd. “One in twelve, as the notice says.” The crowd leaned in because he never raised his voice, and the leaning gave the sentence more weight than the sentence could carry. “The levy is called. The bell went four, four and a gap. Nobody crossed himself.
