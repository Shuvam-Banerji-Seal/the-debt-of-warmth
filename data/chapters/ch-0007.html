Chapter

7

The Number They Give Out

Days 15–24 · Stone 21–30, Y.H. 812 · The Vail kiln-house and the Cross, Sill

Count the loaves, count the coals, count the days the house will keep you. When the moot gives out a number, count again. It will not be the same one.

— A Low Kiln saying, Sill

— Day 15 —

Stone 21, Y.H. 812 (Firstday)

The coals had held under their blind ash through the night the way a held breath is kept, without stirring, and the kiln had not been opened yet and the pot stood on the bench where it had stood and would stand, turned a little toward the light. Corin lay in the basket under the fold with the cold brown-edged crust at his side where it had been put six days ago and had not been moved since, and the drool at his mouth had frozen to a thread in the small hours and nobody had thought to wipe it, because a basket at the edge of a board is a pot set down and left. It was Stone 21, the first day of the last tend, and he was fifteen days old, and nobody under the roof of the Vail kiln-house had spoken to the basket.

Sella Vail went to the fire before she went to anything else. Her hand went to the widow’s mark at her throat, a thumb’s length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and held it the space of one breath, and let it go; then, and not before, she lifted the lid. The burn shaped like a little door across the back of her left hand had closed on nothing as she reached and opened on the iron handle as she took the weight and closed again when the lid was up. She did not touch the mark when she banked the coals at night, or when a reading was made in the yard, or when a neighbour came with a slate held turned to her own chest; the rule was exact as a stitch and she had never once been seen to break it. The boy had learned to tell the hour by the touch as other men tell it by a bell.

The counting that morning was of fuel and bread into the last tend before the Five, and it was done in the only coin the house had: loaves moved along a shelf, glances, the space where a thing should have stood and did not. Tarn came up from the Debt Loft with the broom under his arm and the Ash worked into the creases of his knuckles that no scrubbing lifted, and stood at the fuel stack with his head a little down, moving two bundles from the near rank to the far with his eyes rather than his hands, the way a man counts money he is afraid to touch.

“Three here,” he said, and laid his hand flat on the shelf. “Two there.”

Sella moved her lips and said nothing. She set three loaves to the left of the shelf and two to the right and put a third loaf a little apart with its brown edge turned up, and came back to it twice as she passed, and never once said what the arrangement meant. The boy knew the shape of it: a number kept where no collector’s thumb would look, in bits of bread and in the returning glance, so that no figure could be taken up and entered. In another life he had counted instruments onto a tray in a room with better light and a colder kind of order, and had known the right answer under his hands; here he knew the right answer and had no hands for it. He carried his own exact number alongside theirs and did not lose it when theirs changed shape.

Sella took the pot-shovel and turned the ash with the back of it, and the ash was cold enough that the iron stuck to her hand as she turned it; she counted the coals under it with her eyes and did not say what she counted. The room smelled of tallow and wet wool and the sweet mineral tang that came up off the ridge in the corner whenever the fire warmed it, and under these the small sour breath of a house that had kept a fire longer than it had kept a man. The boy’s head went where it wanted as she moved him, and made the small wet sound it made when it went too far, and she put two fingers behind his ear and set it right without looking, the way a woman sets a pot straight on a shelf. He had once put a shoulder back in the dark; he could not keep his own head on his own neck. The joke had a blade in it and he turned it gently, the way a man tests a tooth with his tongue, and it cost him the same as always.

Four, and four, and a space where the fifth should have been: that was the kindling stack as it had been measured a tend ago and had not been measured since, and the space was the part nobody filled. He set the gap of the stack beside the interval at the bench — the place where a pot had been turned toward and given nothing — and kept them separate as he kept two similar bones separate on a tray, because a space where a thing should be and the sound a thing did not make were different objects, and the cost of confusing them was a wrong answer written down in a neat hand. The count went into the fuel and could go no further, and beyond the fuel was Kindling, which he kept as a date and not as a number anybody in this yard would own.

Somebody had been out to look at the ice and come back with a number they would not give. The boy never saw the face; the number arrived under the roof the way such things arrived, as a fist shut on the door-latch and a glance toward the fold and a mouth that thought better of speaking. Whoever it was stood a moment in the doorway with the cold coming off them in a small grey cloud and said something about the weather. “It’s come a hair further,” the man said to the door, and shut his mouth on the rest, and kept his fist with the thumb tucked under it. The boy knew the grip: it was the way this yard held a number without giving it, at the well and at the loaf shelf and in any mouth that had counted what it had and did not care to say it. The number went over the top of the basket and was not given. Sella answered with the fire.

“It’s a cold,” she said to the coals, and fed them, and the neighbour went away with the fist still shut.

The household kept the number badly, in the arrangement of the loaves and the space in the stack and the glance that had not settled anywhere; the boy kept it exactly, as he kept all the numbers that were not given to him, and had no place to put it and no hand to write it. the Ilm lay under its skin of ice a walk away and had a hairline in it that ran and stopped short of the edge, and the number that had come back from looking at it was a shape with the same stop in it, and he set the two side by side and made nothing of the likeness.

Jorra Vail came in from the Vail wheel-shed with slip dried to the colour of old bone up both forearms and the bent little finger of his left hand going in ahead of the rest of him. There was no slate dust worked into the creases past the bend, none of the fine grey that came back from the room the house did not enter; the boy read the forearms the way he had once read a senior who would not meet a resident’s eye, by what the face refused to report. “She’ll want a thinner slip,” Jorra said to the pot on the bench, and went to the damper before Sella had finished telling him the fire was low, and left the end of her sentence where it was. He did not say the back room was cold. He did not look toward the bed.

The kiln took its breath in fours under the roof and missed one. Four, and four, and a gap. The boy counted it without deciding to, as he counted the boards and the bell and the step that was not a step; the gap was the part nobody had thought to enter, and it lay in the kiln’s breath as it lay in the stack and the shelf. At the bank that night Sella turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and did not touch the widow’s mark — the rule held at the close of a Firstday as exactly as at the hour before an opening — and said to the fire what she said to the fire. “That’ll hold.”

The crust lay cold in the fold at his side, and nothing had been said to the basket all day.

— Day 16 —

Stone 22, Y.H. 812 (Secondday)

Tarn swept before light on Stone 22 with the broom under his arm and the Ash in the creases of his knuckles, and set the small bench knife on its sill where it could be checked, and told the yard he had done so, though the yard was empty. “I took the small knife. I put it back. You can check.” One year of the seven remained and he would not count it; six were behind him and counted every one, and the seventh he held back, the one entry in this house the house could not check. He said it to the kindling rather than to a person. “Six counted,” he said to the stack, “the next I don’t count.”

When the light had cleared the sill Sella sent him for a collector for a neighbour two doors down. “Go,” she said, and gave the errand to the yard and not to him. Tarn went with his head down and the broom left against the wall, and the house went on about its ordinary work without him: Jorra at the wheel turning a pot nobody would look at twice, Sella feeding the fire, the crust where it had been put. The boy lay under the roof with the fold at his chin and the cold coming in at cheek, then temple, then wrist, and heard the yard go on without the broom in it, which was a small silence with the shape of a larger one.

He came back with a collector before the morning was old. The man smelled of wet wool and soap and the flat poor smell of a room somebody had died in; his cuff was soft from washing and turned back twice at the wrist, and the fingertips that showed below it were the colour of skimmed milk gone off, a grey that was not the grey of the ridge along the skirting board but something gone wrong in the blood. He carried a flat satchel. He was not the woman in the grey coat and not the soft man who laid his hand on the slate at the Cross; the boy set him on a tray of his own on the strength of the fingertips and joined him to nothing, the way he kept two similar bones apart.

Sella lifted the basket and carried it two doors down while the collector went ahead with Tarn, and set it at the edge of the light in the neighbour’s room where he could see the work and not be part of it, well away from any open slate. The path was counted in his head without his deciding to count it: the loose board, the joint by the well, the place where the trodden ash went thin at the threshold, and the door that stood a finger’s width open, which they went in by. A woman at a tub looked up as the basket passed and went back to her work without appearing to have seen it, which was the kindness this yard did a thing it could not enter. She had set him at the edge of a room like this once before when a measure was drawn and did it now without a word, and stood a half-pace off with her burn shut on nothing.

The collector’s hand went to the slate before it went to the bed. That was the order the boy had learned to recognise in midwives and neighbours and men with iron tubes and women who kept fire: the record first, because the record would still be there when the person had been carried out. The grey fingertips touched the upper line and rested there, and the boy filed the colour on a separate tray, unjoined to the ridge and to the pale stone left in another house and to the other grey things this house kept, because a wrong answer in a neat hand cost more than the trouble of keeping them apart. He had seen the same grey once on a man who came with a cold iron tube and a reason, and had put it in its own place that day, and put this in its own place now, which was not the same as joining the two men. A body went that colour when the heat had gone somewhere nobody had entered, and he knew the look of it the way he knew a pulse that had stopped, before anybody said the word for it. The man said one sentence to the slate and not to the room, in the soft grammar that took the speaker out and left an account standing in his place.

“The measure is taken,” he said, “and the account stands as entered.”

The woman on the board-bed made a small sound and the collector did not look up, and presently two of them carried something out through the door in the flat satchel, and the boy could not lift his head far enough to follow it.

On the way back a person carrying a vessel went past the door of the Vail house and the vessel gave its flat note as it went. It was a pot with something in it, or a cup, carried at the hip and never set down; the sound came small and flat across the trodden ash of the Clay Yard, not the full note of a whole vessel but its dead shadow, and died in the yard before it reached the roof. Tarn’s head turned a degree toward it. He did not look at the basket and he did not call the sound a sound, and the hand went back to the broom and the broom went on with its stroke.

Under the roof, with Sella lifting him over the threshold, the boy heard something he could not place — too far, too muffled to confirm, the shape of a note rather than the note — and put it on the tray with the two intervals rather than among the five hearings from four sources. An interval was not a hearing and a thing half-heard was not a thing heard; the count stayed five. Nobody else under the roof registered either the turning head or the note that had gone past the door. A loan that could hear had been exercised and bought nobody anything: no hand had reached a vessel, no slate had been tapped for him, and the two silences under one roof kept their usual shape.

At evening Tarn set the small knife back on its sill and told the yard where it was, and Sella banked the coals with the back of a broken pot and put the fold right without looking at his face, and did not touch the mark. Garl Vail’s coat kept its salt shape on its peg behind the door and his seat in the shed kept its hollow on the left, and his proverb went to the fire and not to the basket: leave the crack showing; people trust a thing they can see has been handled. The Ash ridge ran to the corner and turned it without a break and would not be swept before the Last Day. Six years of the seven were counted and the seventh held back; the knife was back where it could be checked; the flat note had gone past the door and died in the yard, kept by two people under one roof and entered by neither. The kindling stack stood four, four and a space where the fifth should be, and the boy lay under the roof with the crust cold in the fold and the count going on because there was nothing else that was his.

— Day 17 —

Stone 23, Y.H. 812 (Thirdday)

For five mornings the slip that came back from the Vail wheel-shed had been the colour of old bone and no grey had been worked into the creases past the bend of the little finger, and Corin had kept the absence the way he kept the space in the kindling stack — as a fact with a shape, entered nowhere. He lay in the basket under the fold at the edge of the Vail kiln-house floor, with the cold brown-edged crust lying where it had been put eight days ago and not moved since, and the drool at his mouth frozen to a thread that nobody thought to wipe, because a basket at the edge of a board is a pot set down and left. Stone 23 was Thirdday and he was seventeen days old, and the fifth clean morning was already going into the tally he had no hand to write.

Then the absence was over. Jorra Vail came in from the shed with fine grey slate dust worked into the creases of his forearms past the bend of the little finger, grey as the ash under the pot-shovel and not the bone of the wheel, lying in the seams of the skin the way silt lies in the joints of a drying riverbed. He said nothing about where he had been. The boy read the not-looking the way he had once read a resident who kept his eyes on the chart when a senior asked him a question he did not want to answer: not the face, which was arranged, but the part of it that refused to report. The face said the wheel, the pots, the fire. The forearms said a room off the shed with a door that stood shut.

He had sat outside a curtained bed once while a senior asked a resident whether the family had been told, and the resident had kept his eyes on the chart and said the chart would show what had been said, and the boy had known before anybody spoke that the family had not been told at all. It was not a gift. It was a thing a man learned by standing in rooms where the truth had been arranged for him, and he had learned it early and brought it into a country that had no word for the arrangement. Jorra’s face was arranged the same way. The dust were the chart.

The cold came in at cheek, then temple, then wrist, in the order he knew and could not argue with, and the body made its small claim against the older one: he wanted the breast, wanted the crust even cold, wanted a hand that could point at the dust and ask the one short question, and got none of the three. He had reduced a shoulder in the dark once and could not keep the wool on his own shoulder now. The joke had a blade in it and he turned it gently, the way a man tests a tooth with his tongue, and it cost him the same as always.

“She’ll want a thinner slip,” Jorra said to the pot on the bench, and then, to the wheel through the wall, “The wheel doesn’t care whose hands. It cares if they’re steady.” Sella Vail told the fire the fire was low, and he went to the damper before she had finished telling it, the bent little finger going in ahead of his hand and taking the iron with it, and left the end of her sentence where it was. He did not say the back room was cold. He did not look toward the bed.

The pot on the bench was turned a little toward the light and would not be looked at twice. Jorra took it up and set it down again, and the boy turned his head toward the set-down before the hand had quite left the clay, and nothing came — the same small wooden silence he had filed twice already, and he did not make a third entry of it, because a thing that happens again in the same shape is the same object on the tray and not a new one. The count stayed five from four sources. Jorra’s bent finger came off the damper and he went back through the door without a word, and the boy did not turn his head after him, because a head that goes where it wants makes a small wet sound when it goes too far.

There had been low talk in the back room for three years: men come in whose names the house carried on no roll it showed, speaking in voices lowered for a door, and Jorra coming out with dust in his skin and saying nothing a person could take further. The boy had never seen the men. He had seen only the shape they left behind — a door kept closed off the shed, dust where slip should have been, and the way the house went around the place with the care a woman uses going around a pot that has been set down and may yet give a sound. Under the drying racks was a space where whole slate should have stood, and the yard went around that too without entering it. He kept his question where he kept the others, in the place questions could last for years, because a question asked in this house acquired an answer nobody wanted to own, and the answer would be written in a neat hand by somebody else.

Sella got quieter, and the yard straightened around the quiet as a pot settles on its foot. Tarn swept the trodden ash with his head down and the Ash worked into the creases of his knuckles that no scrubbing lifted, and when the broom came to the place by the bench where a pot had once given nothing he kept his eyes on the ash and swept around it.

“I swept it this morning,” he said to the floor. “I’ll sweep it again.”

Six years of the seven were counted and the seventh held back, and he had said that to the kindling rather than to a person; the boy had heard it and kept it, and neither of them had said a word to the other about the other thing they both kept. Two silences under one roof were nearer to an equality than anything this house had a name for, and it had no name for it, and the loan that could hear had been exercised again and bought nobody anything.

He counted the creases on the forearms as he counted the nails in the boards, four on the near arm and four on the other, with the grey worked in past the bend where slip had lain for five clean days, and he kept the clean forearms and the dusty forearms as two separate entries on the tray, the way a man keeps two similar bones apart until one of them is proved different. He did not put the dust with the ridge because the ridge had been left where it fell and the dust had been carried in on a man, and the difference mattered more than the colour.

The boy put the grey on a tray of its own beside the ridge along the skirting board, the charred slate lying black in the kiln ash, and the barrow of grey that had gone out through the north gate toward the Bone Orchard, and made nothing of the likeness between them. Five clean days had made the absence of the dust into a fact; one morning of grey had turned it back into a question with a shape and no hand to write it. A wrong answer entered in a neat hand cost more than the trouble of keeping four similar greys in four separate places on a tray, and he had learned that in a room with better light, where the neat hand had been his own.

The loaf shelf still carried the days to the Five as a shape no collector could take up, and the kiln took its breath in fours under the roof and missed one. Four, and four, and a gap. Garl Vail’s coat kept its salt shape on its peg and his seat its hollow on the left, and his proverb went to the pot and the fire — leave the crack showing; people trust a thing they can see has been handled — and never to the basket. At the bank that night Sella turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and did not touch the widow’s mark; the rule held at the close of a Thirdday as exactly as at an opening. The crust lay cold in the fold. The dust stayed worked into the creases of the thrower’s forearms, grey and fine, and the boy lay under the roof with a question he could not ask and no hand to ask it with.

— Day 18 —

Stone 24, Y.H. 812 (Fourthday)

At mid-morning on Stone 24 Alda Reed came under the roof with soap and warm milk still on her and the birth-slates under her arm, and went to the bench before she went to the basket. The boy had learned the order of reaching in midwives and neighbours and men with iron tubes and women who kept fire, and he had never once seen it vary: the hand to the record before the hand to the person, because the record would still be lying where it had been put when the person had been carried out of the room. Alda laid the birth-slates writing-down on the bench and only then turned toward the fold. Sella stood a half-pace off with her little-door burn shut on nothing, and the two women did not look at each other, and nothing passed between them that could be entered.

The two women had traded a silence since the boy was three days old. Sella had never asked what Alda wrote, and Alda had never asked why Sella took the child, and the arrangement held without a word passing between them, the way a door holds on a latch neither hand will lift. The boy lay at the edge of it and was the thing kept on both sides of it: entered in a roll he had never seen and in notes he would never read, warmed from a dish nobody entered against him, washed in an order that never varied. He had learned to recognise a room where the truth had been arranged before he came into it. This was such a room, and the arrangement had been made by two women who were kind, which was why he could not take it apart in his head without finding his own hands in it.

Alda smelled of the lye she washed in and of milk warmed for somebody else’s child, and under those the faint mineral tang of a corner she had promised herself she would sweep tomorrow and had not swept in thirty years. Her nails were scrubbed to the quick and clay still lived under them. When she took the fold at his shoulder she pressed it flat too long, longer than a fold required, and her two fingers stayed on it after the wool had been set right, the way a woman’s hand will stay on a stone she has just put down, feeling whether the heat is in it.

Inside her bodice, on its cord, the little tin lay against her ribs. Her hand went to it once and rested there and came away without opening it, which was the whole of the gesture: a record touched in the dark where no officer’s thumb would think to look, and not read even by the woman who kept it. The boy had watched that hand go to the tin and come away again and again and had never once seen it open the tin, and kept the two facts on separate places on the tray, because a tin touched and a tin opened were different objects and the cost of confusing them was a wrong answer in a neat hand.

Sella said, “He lies quiet.”

Alda turned the top slate a little, though it was already turned down. “Warm and breathing,” she said, “that’s all I’ve set down.” It was exact, and it was not true. The boy lay with the cold coming in at cheek, then temple, then wrist, and the crust going cold in the fold, and heard the lie delivered as an entry, in the voice a room uses when it wants a chart to read clean.

She saw the red edge of the birth-flower at his wrist where the wool had ridden back, and crossed two fingers at the corner of it before she knew she had done it. He had seen the sign made at him again and again, and she had never once known she was making it in front of him. She covered it the way she always covered it, with a date nobody had asked for.

“Stone seven,” she said, “the account went up on the eighth, before the paper went out.”

Then came the cooper’s seventh in Mud End and a potter’s third child in Stonemouth, and the dates went on arriving after there was no question left for them to answer, faster than a question had any need of them, the way a woman will keep pouring milk into a cup that is full already because pouring is the thing she knows how to do. She gave the dates the way other women set a pot near the fire — because it was the thing to do and because a room in pain wants something counted — and the boy, who had counted everything done to him since he could count, took them in and kept them with the rest of the unspendable numbers: one in twelve, a ridge of two thumbs, nineteen steps, a figure that went around the yards in a fist. None of them could be handed back. These were given by a woman with milk on her sleeves and were worth no more than the others. She did not say the sign was a sign, and she did not say why she made it, and the two fingers went back to the slate.

She took up a narrow slate. On it she wrote what she did not leave lying about, in a second hand no Court physician had ever seen, and when she had finished she turned it writing-down into her shawl, toward a floorboard in a house the boy could not name, where a further note would go into the tin still unread. The writing went down as a woman puts a cup down in a room where somebody is sleeping: carefully, and with the flat of her hand, so that nothing should be heard. She did not leave the slate lying about. That was the whole of it — not a word, not a name, not a figure, only a narrow slate turned face-down into wool and a hand that knew where the floorboard was. The boy had watched a hand reach for a record before it reached for a person in a white room once, and had signed the record himself without reading the note turned down beside it, and here was the same gesture performed by a woman who had washed him and meant him well, and he could no more hand it back than he could lift the slate by one edge.

He smelled the barley in the cold crust at his side and wanted it, and his hand went up an inch and came down on the fold, and the want was childish and loud and true, and the thing he wanted to know was quieter and had been quiet for eighteen days. Both were true, and the second was louder only when the room was still; here a woman was giving dates to a basket and a craft was being hidden in wool, and the child’s want won the argument without a vote, as it always did.

He could not read a word of Grathic on it. He could not ask which house held the floorboard, or ask why a woman who wrote everything down within three days kept a second record that no mother and no officer would ever read. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the shawl and came down, and the hand that had once put a shoulder back in the dark could not turn the corner of the slate.

Two records had gone into the same morning under one roof: grey dust worked into a thrower’s forearms by the breaking of something nobody would name, and a narrow slate turned writing-down into a shawl by a woman who would not say what she had written. Neither had been entered where a Court’s thumb would look. Alda went on with the dates after the question had stopped, the birth-slates stacked writing-down on the bench beside her, the little tin still shut inside her bodice; Jorra’s grey dust stayed worked into the creases of his forearms in the shed where nobody had asked about it; and the boy lay in the fold with the sign on his wrist that nobody had read.

— Day 19 —

Stone 25, Y.H. 812 (Fifthday)

Stone 25 was Fifthday and he was nineteen days old, and the cold had come down another degree in the night the way a hand comes down on a sleeping child’s quilt and tucks it, without asking whether the child was warm. The oiled paper at the windows of the Vail kiln-house lifted harder than it had lifted the day before, lifting and letting go against the frame with a dry small sound; the Slip Brook behind the drying racks had taken a skin the colour of skimmed milk gone grey, and the skin held under the wind without breaking, which was a thing the children of Low Kiln read as weather and nothing more. Corin lay in the basket at the edge of the floor with the cold brown-edged crust at his side where it had been put ten days ago and had not been moved in all that time, and the thread of drool at his mouth had frozen again in the small hours and nobody had thought to wipe it, because a basket at the edge of a board is a pot set down and left. The cold came in at cheek, then temple, then wrist, in the order he knew and could not argue with. The man inside the child marked the order the way he had once marked the order in which a body gives up its heat; the child inside the man wanted the breast and wanted the crust even cold, and the wanting was louder, and neither was answered.

Sella Vail went to the fire before she went to anything else. Her hand went to the widow’s mark at her throat, a thumb’s length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it, held it the space of one breath and let it go, and only then lifted the lid. The burn shaped like a little door across the back of her hand opened on the iron and shut on nothing, and shut again when the lid was up. She counted the fuel into the last tend with her eyes, three here and two there, and moved her lips along the loaf shelf, and came back to the loaf set a little apart with its brown edge turned up, and never said what the arrangement counted. There was still bread in the house and ash under the pot and a fire that had not gone out in two years, and the household kept the days the only way it knew how: in loaves, glances and the space where a thing should have stood and did not. He carried his own number alongside theirs and did not lose it when theirs changed shape.

She carried him out to the racks after the light had cleared the sill and set the basket down with his face toward the kiln wall and his cheek against the fold, so that the draught off the brook should not go into his ears. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. “It’s the river wind.” Then she went three paces to the fuel stack with her little-door burn half closed on nothing, and the yard went on about its work around the basket the way a room goes on around a pot set down and not looked into.

Two paces from his head a pot in the Clay Yard cracked all the way through.

The woman at the racks had set it down. The pot gave its small flat knock. The hand on the rim stayed a moment too long and came away.

He knew the sound before he knew where it was. It was the dead shadow of the full note, the knock a vessel gives when the fault has run from lip to foot and there is no wall left in it to hold with, and he had heard it across a birth room, through a shed wall, and twice from a slate under a bell that did a temple’s work. Here it came in the open yard with nothing between the clay and his ear, in the middle of the ordinary morning, and his head went the wrong way.

He tried to turn toward it. The head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound it made when it went too far; the fold caught at his ear; the effort spent itself, and by the time it had the yard had gone on with its work. He had once found a bleeder in a white room by the sound a strip of metal made going into a basin and placed it to the centimetre under a light that did not flicker. He could not place a cracked pot two paces from his own head.

“That’s a pot gone wrong,” the woman said to the drying racks, and went on with her work.

Nobody called the knock a sound. There was nothing in Grathic to name what it was except the word for a pot gone wrong, which was a word for the vessel and not for the thing that had happened to it. Nobody tapped a pot for him, or asked the yard to set a name beside the noise, or looked toward the basket to see whether anybody lying there had heard it. The cracked thing was a pot gone wrong, and a pot gone wrong is a thing a household sweeps around and goes past, and that was the end of the matter in the yard.

He put it on the tray with the two notes that did not come — the pot turned toward on the bench that had given nothing, and the thing half-heard past the door on a carried vessel — and made a third object there, and did not make a sixth hearing of it. A hearing, in the arithmetic he had kept since the first day under this roof, was a sound with a place: a room, a vessel, a hand that had set it down. This had a place and he could not turn to it, which was a failure of a different kind and therefore its own object. He kept the three intervals on one tray and the five hearings from four sources on another, and the two trays did not touch. The count stayed five. No hand had reached a vessel for him, no slate had been tapped, and the thing he knew exactly had bought him nothing, as it always had.

Tarn came past with the broom under his arm and the Ash worked into the creases of his knuckles that no scrubbing lifted. Six years of the seven were behind him and counted every one, and the seventh he held back, the one entry in this house the house could not check. His broom checked at the place where the pot had stood, half a stroke, and went on; the grey kept moving under the bristles. He did not look at the pot. He did not look at the basket. He said nothing about either, and the boy said nothing, and the two silences — one under the roof and one in the yard — kept their usual shape, which was the shape of a loan that could hear and had not been called in. A loan that could hear was worth more than the debt that held a man; nobody in this house had ever put the sentence into words, and it lay in the yard all the same, under the trodden ash, where a man sweeping might have found it if he had been looking for a sentence instead of grey.

The woman swept around the cracked pot the way the house swept around the charred slate in the kiln ash — leaving it where it lay because a thing left where it lay could be gone past without anybody having to decide what to do with it — and Garl Vail’s proverb went to the pot and the fire and never to the basket: leave the crack showing; people trust a thing they can see has been handled. The fault stood open to the light and nobody picked the thing up. His coat kept its salt shape on its peg, his seat in the shed kept its hollow on the left, and the ridge ran to the corner and turned it without a break, and all of them were gone past a dozen times that morning without a word spoken to any of them.

Jorra Vail came in from the Vail wheel-shed with slip dried to the colour of old bone up both forearms and no grey worked into the creases past the bend of the little finger, none of the dust that had come back with him on Stone 23. “The shoulder’s thick,” he said to the pot on the bench. “I’ll thin it.” He went to the damper before Sella had finished telling the fire it was low, the bent finger going in ahead of his hand, and left the end of her sentence where it was. He did not say the back room was cold. He did not look toward the bed. The boy kept the clean forearms and the dusty forearms on separate places on his tray, as he kept two similar bones apart until one of them was proved different, and made nothing of the fact that both had belonged to the same pair of arms two days ago.

The kiln took its breath in fours under the roof and missed one. Four, and four, and a gap. He counted it without deciding to, as he counted the boards and the bell and the step on the Fishstairs that was not a step; the gap was the part nobody had thought to enter, and it lay in the kiln’s breath as it lay in the kindling stack and on the loaf shelf and now on the third place of the interval tray. At the bank that night Sella turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and did not touch the mark, because a bank is not an opening and the rule was exact as a stitch. She put the fold right at his ear without looking at his face, and fed him from the house’s own dish with two fingers behind his ear and the spoon wiped on her sleeve, and gave the instruction to the dish and the fire, and the warmth crossed into him and was entered nowhere.

The oiled paper kept lifting at the window. The brook kept its skin under the wind. The cracked pot stood swept around on the trodden ash with the fault showing, and the boy lay under the roof with the cold crust at his side and a third object on the tray of things that did not join, and the count going on because there was nothing else that was his.

— Day 20 —

Stone 26, Y.H. 812 (Sixthday)

Stone 26 was Sixthday and he was twenty days old, and the house went about an ordinary day. It was not Account-day. There was no bell in the morning and no slate read aloud in the yard, no figure set out where two hundred people could lean over it and go home with a fist shut; there was only the work — mending, drying, sweeping, eating — which was the work the house did whether anybody read a number over it or not. He woke with the fold at his chin and the crust cold at his side, eleven days where it had been put, and smelled barley smoke in it when the wind turned, and wanted it, and could not lift a hand to it.

The number for the Five went around the yards that morning in mouths and fists. It reached the house the way such things reached it, not as a figure spoken to the basket but as an absence with a shape: a mouth stopping short on a word, a hand closed on a latch, a glance that went toward the fold and thought better of it. He had learned the grammar of it in a white room where a chart went around a table and came back to the bed with the speaker taken out of the sentence; here there was no chart, only the yard and the wind off the brook and the loaves on the shelf, and the figure went over the top of him and could not be entered.

“Three days,” a neighbour said at the yard well, with her slate held to her chest and the bucket not going down yet. “Three, and the Five on us. I’ve counted it twice.”

“Two,” said a second, come up from the racks with grey on her sleeves. “You’ve counted the Fifthday twice. It was two yesterday and it’s two today.”

“It’ll be whatever the moot gives out,” said a third, and set her pot on the stone lip, and went about her business without another word.

The argument was about a figure nobody wanted to own. They passed it back and forth like a cup too hot to hold — three, two, whatever the moot gave out — and each of them put it down somewhere else before it could be entered against them. Then the wind came off the brook and somebody said it was a cold snap, and that was the end of it, because weather was a thing a person could say without owning a single day of it. One neighbour who wanted the thaw late said a late thaw was a longer week before the paying, and another who wanted it early said the ice would not wait for the moot, and the yard went on sweeping. The boy heard the figures go past and could not spend them, correct them, or hand them back. He had once sat across a table from a man who wanted a number changed, and had known which line of the form would let the change through and which would not, and had got it through on the wrong line and carried it ever since. Here he lay under a fold with the numbers going over him and no form in the house that would take the weight of his hand.

He kept the one number none of them would give. The days were counted one at a time, and one day after tonight was tomorrow, and after tomorrow there would be another, and when five of them had gone Kindling would come. The district could give out three, or two, or whatever it had already decided to pay, and the days would not agree with any of them, and the disagreement was the whole of what was his.

Sella went around the days in loaves and glances rather than a figure. She moved three loaves along the shelf and two back and came to the one set a little apart with its brown edge turned up, and came back to it a second time as she passed, and never said what the arrangement counted. When she fed him from the house’s own dish, gruel thinned with milk, with two fingers behind his ear and the spoon wiped on her sleeve, she gave the instruction to the dish and the fire and not to the basket, and the warmth crossed into him and was entered nowhere, as the stone had been and the crust had been and every kindness in this house had been. He had learned that a gift which could not be answered could not be refused, which was a lesson in bread before it was a lesson in anything else.

Jorra came in from the shed with slip dried to the colour of old bone up both forearms and no grey worked into the creases past the bend of the little finger, and said to a pot on the bench that it would do, and went to the damper before Sella had finished telling the fire it was low. He did not say the back room was cold and did not look toward the bed. The absence of dust was a fact in the yard today as the presence of it had been a fact on Stone 23, and the boy kept both facts on the same tray without putting one over the other, because he had learned that two things could be true of the same forearms and neither of them enterable.

Tarn swept the trodden ash twice, once before light and once after the mending, and set the small bench knife on its sill and told the yard it was where it could be checked, though the yard was empty. Six years of the seven were counted and the seventh held back. His broom checked at the place in the yard where the pot had cracked the day before, half a stroke, and went on, and he did not look at the place or say what had happened there, and the boy did not look at him. The loan that could hear stayed where it was, called in by neither man. The cracked pot stood where the woman had swept around it, and everybody went past it, and nobody in the yard called it anything but a pot gone wrong.

The crust lay cold in the fold, stiff at the edges, smelling of smoke and barley when the wind turned. Sella neither took it out nor named it, and the boy neither ate it nor gave it up. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward it and came down on the fold, and the hand that had once reduced a shoulder in the dark could not lift eleven days of bread to his own mouth. He had learned to find the joke funny without laughing, which was a way of laughing that cost less and kept the same blade in it.

Garl’s coat kept its salt shape on its peg and the wheel-seat its hollow on the left in the shed, and the ridge ran to the corner and turned it without a break and would not be swept before the Last Day. His proverbs went to the fire and the pot: feed the fire before you feed yourself; leave the crack showing, people trust a thing they can see has been handled. Nobody said them to the basket. The uncalled debt against a firing of tiles lay in the space where a sentence about the district’s paying should have stood, and the district had gone back to its ordinary day without calling it, which was how a debt stayed alive in this house: by nobody saying the word for it.

At the bank Sella turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and did not touch the widow’s mark, because a bank is not an opening and an ordinary Sixthday is not a reading. The kiln took its breath in fours and missed one. Four, and four, and a gap. The loaves were rearranged on the shelf for another day, three here and two there and the brown-edged one set apart and come back to, so that the days to Kindling stood as a shape no collector could take up and no figure could be entered. The count went on because there was nothing else that was his.

The cracked pot stood swept around on the trodden ash where the woman had left it, the fault open to the light and nobody picking it up, and the household’s loaves stood rearranged for another day, and the boy lay under the roof with the crust cold in the fold and a number in his head that nobody in the yard would say.

— Day 21 —

Stone 27, Y.H. 812 (Seventhday)

Stone 27 was Seventhday and he was twenty-one days old, and the cold had come to stay in the thatch the way a debt comes to stay in a house, not all at once but by degrees, until a man looked up and found it had been there the whole winter and would be there after him. The dry frost ticked in the straw over the Vail kiln-house with a sound like a woman clicking her tongue at a pot set down wrong, and the Slip Brook behind the drying racks carried its grey skin without breaking, and the Ilm below the sill lay under ice that had stopped being a thing a man looked at and had become a thing a man walked on. Corin lay in the basket at the edge of the floor with the old brown-edged crust at his side where it had lain since Stone 15, stiff as a shingle and smelling of smoke when the wind turned, and the thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had thought to wipe it, because a basket at the edge of a board is a pot set down and left. Cold came in at cheek, then temple, then wrist. He marked the order as he marked all orders, and the child inside him wanted the crust even stiff and wanted the breast, and the wanting was louder, and neither was answered.

Sella Vail went to the fire before she went to anything else. Her hand went to the widow’s mark at her throat, the thumb’s length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it, held it the space of one breath and let it go, and only then lifted the lid. The little-door burn across the back of her hand opened on the iron and shut on nothing, and shut again when the lid was up. She counted the fuel into the last tend before the Five with her eyes, three here and two there, and moved her lips along the loaf shelf so the days to Kindling stood as a shape no collector could take up, and came back to the loaf set a little apart with its brown edge turned up, and never said what the arrangement counted. “Feed it before you feed yourself,” she said to the fire, which was Garl Vail’s saying improved by twenty years of use, and the fire gave nothing back and the house went on around the basket the way a room goes on around a pot set down and left. the Ash lay along the skirting board in a ridge near two thumbs wide that ran to the corner and turned it without a break, and it would not be swept before the Last Day.

She carried him out to the racks after the light had cleared the sill and set the basket with his cheek against the fold and his face turned away from the wind off the brook. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. “It’s the river wind.” The yard was already arguing.

A woman come up from High Kiln with sweetish lead-glaze dried at one corner of her mouth stood among the racks with her shawl pulled tight and said the thaw would come early. “It came early last year,” she said. “The ice runs thin where the water’s fast. You can hear it under the bridge.”

“You can hear it,” said a neighbour who wanted it late, with her slate held to her chest. “And you’ll hear the levy when it comes with an early thaw, sooner than you’ll hear your own kettle. Late’s the weather for me.”

“Late puts off the paying,” a second said, and that was the whole of the argument, and everybody went on sweeping.

The boy lay with the argument going over him and kept it as a shape, not a cause. He had learned the difference in a better-lit room where two men had set a cluster of facts side by side and declared that one had made the other happen, and he had carried the mistake the rest of a life. The hairline in the river ice ran in from one edge and stopped a thumb’s breadth short of the other; a woman wanted an early thaw and a yard wanted a late one because the levy fell in the thaw; the two things stood in the same morning without one of them explaining the other. He put them on the same tray and drew no line between them. Sella, asked, said only that it was weather, which was a word that let a woman go home warm without owning a single day of it.

The ice-mice were found in the thatch before the morning was old. That was what the children of Low Kiln called the small grey patches that came up in straw in a hard winter — a thumb’s width here, a finger’s there, spreading where the wind got under the eaves — and by noon a child two doors down had the same word on the back of her hand, a numb patch the colour of skimmed milk gone grey that spread while somebody held the hand and looked at it and did nothing to it. “That’s ice-mice,” a woman said, and the women at the tubs said yes, that was ice-mice, a thing the cold did, and went back to their work. The hand went on being held. The patch went on spreading. Nobody called it anything but what the cold did, because what the cold did was weather, and weather was the one thing that could take a child’s hand without anybody having to answer for it. He watched it the way he had once watched a cooling foot in a room where the nurses kept saying it was only cold, and kept the seeing where questions can last for years.

Tarn swept the yard with the Ash worked into the creases of his knuckles that no scrubbing lifted, and six years of the seven were behind him counted every one, and the seventh he held back, the one entry in this house the house could not check. He stopped under the eaves where the grey patches showed and said to the thatch what he would not have said to a person. “A late one,” he said. “Late’s mine. A longer week before the paying.” The thatch ticked in the wind and gave him no answer, which was a relief, because an answer would have been a thing he would have had to enter. His broom checked half a stroke at the place where the cracked pot had stood two days ago and went on, and he did not look at the place, and the boy did not look at him, and the two silences kept their usual shape.

Under the roof Jorra Vail came in from the Vail wheel-shed with slip dried to the colour of old bone up both forearms and the bent little finger leading his hand, and told a pot on the bench that the shoulder was thick and he would thin it, and went to the damper before Sella had finished telling the fire it was low, and left the end of her sentence where it was. He did not say where he had been and he did not look toward the bed. The kiln took its breath in fours under the roof and missed one. Four, and four, and a gap. The boy counted it as he had counted it every day since the first, and set it beside the figure going around the yards — three, then two, then whatever the moot had already decided to give out — and kept the two numbers apart, because one of them was a shape a household kept in loaves and glances and the other was a shape the fire kept without being asked. They did not agree, and the not-agreeing was the part that was his. Nobody in the yard would have put the breath of a kiln and a figure for the Five in the same sentence, and if they had they would have called it weather, which was what this house called everything that had a shape and no hand to enter it.

At the bank Sella turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and did not touch the mark, because a bank is not an opening and the rule was exact as a stitch. She put the fold right at his ear without looking at his face and fed him from the house’s own dish with two fingers behind his ear and the spoon wiped on her sleeve, and gave the instruction to the dish and the fire, and the warmth crossed into him and was entered nowhere, as it always was. Garl’s coat kept its salt shape on its peg and his seat its hollow on the left in the shed, and his proverb went to the pot and the fire and never to the basket: leave the crack showing; people trust a thing they can see has been handled. The old crust lay stiff at his side and was neither taken out nor named, and the ice-mice ticked in the thatch, and the kiln kept four, and four, and a gap. He was twenty-one days old and the count went on because there was nothing else that was his.

— Day 22 —

Stone 28, Y.H. 812 (Eighthday)

Stone 28 was Eighthday and he was twenty-two days old, and the smell of the eels came up the Clay Yard before the woman who carried them, which was how Mira Silt arrived in any place — a smell of river silt and salted perch and tallow smoke going ahead of her, and then the small smile kept on her face the way another woman keeps a door on the latch, and then the pale slime dried on her sleeves. House Silt stood three doors down the yard with the morning smoke going up from it, and the path from its door to the drying racks was nineteen steps long to a woman who had counted them every day for six years, though nobody had counted them this morning yet, and Mira came the other way, up from the fish-trade at the bridge with a basket on her hip. She came past the yard well without looking into it and went on toward the racks.

Her head was held a little to the right against the cold where the top of her left ear had been, that being the side a hook had taken by her account and a knife had taken by the scar’s, and nobody in the yard had ever made anything of the difference. When a neighbour asked her whose basket was going past, meaning the one at the edge of the floor under the Vail wall, Mira answered with a question and then her own answer. “Whose do you think? A basket’s a basket. It came from a warm house, that’s all, and it’s going back to one.” She flicked an eel-bone against her thumb, put it away, and went on with the eels.

A woman at the well said a figure for the Five out loud, three or two or whatever it was that morning, and Mira’s thumb went under her apron to the grey wool cord at her waist and stayed there while she decided something, and came out again with another bone between finger and thumb. The boy watched the hand and not what the hand was at. He had learned to read the movement and leave the counting of it to the woman who owned it: he had no word in Grathic for a knot, and if he had there would still have been no hand to turn the cord and no standing to ask what the knots were for. The hand went under and came out. That was the whole of what was entered in his file. The smile on her face did not move, which was how he knew the hand had moved.

Then she gave him the crust. There was no speech about it and no pause in which a speech could have been made — she was three paces off and the basket was at the edge of the floor and she crossed the space with the briskness of a woman putting a bowl down before a child can ask for it, and put a fresh brown-edged barley crust into the fold at his side and went back to the eels without looking in. “It’s a crust,” she said over her shoulder to nobody, “not a sermon.” The new one smelled of barley smoke and the Vail fire. The old one lay where it had lain since Stone 15, stiff and cold and smelling the same when the wind turned, and two warmths lay side by side in the fold now, one given and one spent, and neither was entered against anybody, and neither woman named either one. Sella at the fire did not look up and did not take the old one out, and where she was concerned not seeing and not refusing were the same thing. He could smell both and could lift neither. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth and came down on the fold, and the surgeon who had once reduced a shoulder in the dark could not get a crust to his own mouth. He had learned to find the joke funny without laughing, which was a way of laughing that cost less and kept the same blade in it.

When she shifted the basket’s weight to her other hip, a second cord twisted twice under her belt showed for a moment below the grey one — a record of a different account, on a string that had never been taken up the river to Nine Pots and never lifted at a door. The boy saw the twist of it and kept it in its own place beside the first cord, as he kept two similar bones apart until one of them was proved different, and did not join them. There was a child upriver whose name he had never heard and would not have known how to ask for, and the cord lay in the fold of her apron below the other cord, and the two cords went off up the yard with the eels. The knots on neither were counted. The counting belonged to her, and the record was wool, which no Court’s thumb would think to look for and no slate could take up.

Three doors down, at the end of nineteen steps from the pits, Dessa Silt came to the yard mouth with slip-grey nails and wet clay dried at her hem and her shoulder against the jamb as if the jamb were the only thing holding her up. The cough came up out of the pits in a low short way and she put her hand over her mouth, and when it had passed she said it was weather. “It’s the pits,” she said to the ground. “Comes up in the cold. Weather.” Then, because a truth had come near and truths needed a handle, she got specific: nineteen out and nineteen back, four loaves on the seventh of Stone in eight hundred and twelve, three to the levy and one eaten with her feet up, and bits to the woman who came with a basket. Her thumb went to the pale sieve-wire scar up the inside of her left forearm and smoothed it once, the way a woman smooths a fold she does not want to see, and the door-shut smile came down over her face and meant that nothing further would be said. She did not look into the basket going past her shade. She had not said the number in three years and had not said the name in three years, and the boy going past her in the fold of another woman’s wool did not know that he was the person the count kept out of the room.

He could not lift either crust to his mouth, could not ask about either cord, could not tell the cough from the weather, and he kept all three in the private file with the others, where nothing was joined and nothing was spent. Two women had put warmth into the fold without a word and kept their accounts in materials no Court could confiscate — a cord of grey wool and a thumb’s worth of steps — and he was inside neither account and could thank neither woman. He had learned that a gift which could not be answered could not be refused, which was a lesson in bread before it was a lesson in anything else, and the old crust going cold beside the new one was the same lesson said twice. The kiln took its breath under the roof in fours and a gap, and the yard went back to its work, and the boy lay with the fresh crust tucked in beside the cold one, Mira’s thumb going back under her apron to the grey cord as she walked off with the eels, and Dessa’s door-shut smile at the end of nineteen steps.

— Day 23 —

Stone 29, Y.H. 812 (Ninthday)

Stone 29 was Ninthday and Corin was twenty-three days old, and the Clay Yard was making ready for the Last Day the way a house makes ready for a burying it has put off as long as it can — not with grief, but with brooms. They came out of the sheds in the morning, the stiff ones worn to a stump where the straw had been beaten against doorposts, the new ones still bound with wire, and the dry tick of thatch went along under the the Vail kiln-house eaves all morning like a woman clicking her tongue at a pot set down wrong. A date was said aloud two or three times, the way a date is said when everybody knows it and wants to hear another mouth say it, and a woman at the tubs told the girl beside her that the grey would be left where it lay until the Last Day and burned the morning afterward and called dealt with, as though a thing called dealt with had ever been a thing finished.

The grey was the Ash. It lay along the skirting board under the Vail wall in a ridge near two thumbs wide, running to the corner and turning it without a break, and Sella Vail laid her thumb into it there and left the thumb a moment, the way a man will lay two fingers flat on a wound before he brings a knife near it, taking the shape first so the hand cannot lie about what it found; then she walked two doors down to the death-house, where the ridge was a finger’s width thicker, and laid the same thumb into that, and came back up the yard without ever saying which ridge was which. Both turned their corners without a break. Neither would be swept before the day. The keeping of a house was done with a thumb and a walk and nothing said; he set it on the tray beside the charred slate and the barrow of grey and made nothing of the likeness, because a likeness is not a cause and he had made that mistake once in a better-lit room and carried it ever since.

At the door the uncalled debt against a firing of tiles went around the talk the way a woman goes around a pot left on the floor in the dark — a careful circle and no word for the thing circled. Garl Vail’s coat kept its salt shape on the peg behind the door and nobody took it down; his seat in the shed kept its hollow on the left and nobody filled it; his proverb went to the pot and the fire and never to the basket: leave the crack showing, people trust a thing they can see has been handled. The boy lay at the edge of the floor with the old crust stiff at one side and the fresh one given at the other, and the two lay side by side the way two facts lie side by side in a room where neither will be entered, one spent and one still smelling faintly of the Vail fire when the wind turned. Cold came in at cheek, then temple, then wrist. He marked the order as he marked all orders, and the child in him wanted the crust that was given and wanted the breast, and the wanting was louder, and neither was answered.

Tarn came up from the shed with the Ash in the creases of his knuckles that no scrubbing lifted and the pale clip-ring plain at his left wrist, and set the small bench knife on its sill with the care of a man putting down a thing he can be asked about. “It’s on the sill,” he said to the yard, which was empty of everybody but the basket. “You can check.” He looked at the ridge where Sella’s thumb had been and then took his eyes off it, the way a man takes his eyes off a woman’s face when she has been crying. “I can check the width, house. I’ve a thumb as good as any.” Six years of the seven were behind him counted every one; the seventh he held back, the one entry in this house the house could not check. He said it to the broom and not to a person: “Six counted. The next I don’t count.” The broom checked half a stroke at the place where a pot had stood and given nothing two days before, and went on, and he did not look at the place, and the boy did not look at him, and the two silences kept their usual shape.

Jorra Vail came in from the Vail wheel-shed with slip dried to the colour of old bone up both forearms and the bent little finger leading his hand, and there was no grey worked into the creases past the bend, not today. The sweeping talk going around the racks had made him quieter, the way a certain quiet in a house is louder than a shout, and the yard had straightened around it without anybody giving the order. He told a pot on the bench that the shoulder was thick and he would thin it, and went to the damper before Sella had finished telling the fire it was low, going in with the bent finger ahead of his hand and leaving the end of her sentence where it was. He did not say the back room was cold and he did not look toward the bed. the full note was in a Vail pot before it left the wheel and he would not have said so in front of a kiln-master, who would have called it early and made something of it; the boy knew this about him the way he knew the man’s left little finger, and kept both where questions can last for years.

Under the roof the kiln took its breath in fours and missed one. Four, and four, and a gap. The boy counted it as he had counted it every day since the first, and set it beside the thumb in the grey, and beside the date said aloud two or three times, and beside the careful circle around the tiles, and kept all four apart. One of them was a shape a household kept in loaves and glances, one was a shape the fire kept without being asked, and two were shapes a man went around so that they would not have to be looked at directly. None of them was a number anybody would put on a slate, and if they had been put on one he would not have been able to read it, which was the whole humiliation and the whole safety of it. At the bank Sella turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and did not touch the mark, because a bank is not an opening and the rule was exact as a stitch. She put the fold right at his ear without looking at his face and gave the instruction to the fire and the wool, and the day went down over the yard with the brooms standing in the doorways and the two crusts where they had been put, and the ridge turning its corner without a break, and the kiln keeping four, and four, and a gap.

— Day 24 —

Stone 30, Y.H. 812 (Tenthday, Account-day)

Stone 30 was Tenthday and Account-day and the last market of the year, and he was twenty-four days old. Before the light had cleared the sill Sella Vail went to the fire, and her hand went to the widow’s mark at her throat, the thumb’s length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it; she held it the space of one breath and let it go, and only then lifted the lid. The little-door burn across the back of her hand opened on the iron and shut on nothing and shut again when the lid was up. It was a reading, not an opening; there would be no touching of the mark at the Cross today, and she carried the basket down with the fold over his face and her half-pace kept from every open slate all the way, the way a woman carries a pot filled that must not be slopped. He had learned the rule of that hand better than he had learned any rule of his own: before the opening and at no other hour, not at a bank, not at a reading, not at a dusk. A thing kept that exactly had a reason, and the reason was a thing a house could not say in front of a man from Slate Row.

She set him at the north end of the square in the moot-post’s finger’s width of shade, with the cold iron of the post at his cheek and the two crusts in the fold at his side, one spent and stiff from Stone 15 and one given and still smelling of barley smoke when the wind turned. Then she went a half-pace off and stood with her burn shut on nothing, and gave the instruction to the wool: keep it off his ears, it’s the river wind. The last market of the year went up around him — pigs driven over the wet boards, turnips heaped where figures were left standing side by side without anybody making them agree, hoops, eels, the sour smoke of a brazier, the river wind coming under the fold — and the boy lay with his cheek to cold iron and counted what the square gave him because there was nothing else that was his.

The bell went four, four and a gap, longer than a church bell, and nobody crossed himself. It was the only sound in the square that wanted nothing from him and he listened to it longer than he needed to, counting the strokes the way he counted the nails in a floorboard: four on the near board, four on the next, and a gap where the fifth should have been. A Court instrument doing a temple’s work and nobody in Sill finding the two ends of it worth joining. He set the bell’s gap beside the kiln’s gap and the floorboards’ gap and the kindling’s empty fifth place and kept the four in four places, because he had learned that a likeness laid on a likeness will grow a cause whether or not anyone put one there, and he had once signed a page that said a room had done a thing when a person had done it.

Berr Clay came to the block with the red cord of his office folded under his coat and the green of tasted glaze at one corner of his mouth, and wiped his thumb twice on his apron before he touched the slate. “One in twelve, as the notice says,” he read low into the top of the crowd, and passed a finger over the figure to make sure it was still where he had put it, and said the levy was called for the feast and was not called by him. He went around the uncalled debt against the firing of tiles as carefully as Sella went around Ash left in a corner, set the printed figure where everyone could see it, did not look into the basket, and went back to the pigs by dusk believing he had done the district a kindness. The boy heard the sentence take the speaker out of it the way a chart takes the hand out of a room, and had nothing to answer with. He had once let an institution call a failure of a man a failure of the hospital, and the grammar was the same one, and it went down as easily here as it had there.

The household’s stores went into the last tend before the Five in loaves and glances and absences — three here, two there, one set a little apart with its brown edge turned up and come back to and never said — and the Five were spoken of over the wash of the market as three days off by a district that had counted what it had and did not want to say it. A neighbour said three, a second corrected it to two, a third said whatever the moot had already decided to give out that morning, and the argument ended in the weather the way such arguments did, because weather let a man go home warm without owning a day of it. The figure went around in fists and mouths and came over the top of the basket, and he could not spend it, could not correct it, could not hand it back to anybody. He kept the one number none of them would give: one day after tonight was tomorrow. That was the whole of it, and nobody in the square knew he was keeping it, and the keeping changed nothing except that it was his.

At the fringe of the market, at the Court’s table at the Cross, a collector’s clerk with a cuff turned back twice from washing laid his hand on the top slate before he looked at the woman waiting, and said it was entered as it stood and there was nothing in it the household need correct. Slate Row was spoken of from that end of the square as a direction, not a place anybody meant to go that day. The woman went away with her fist shut. The upper line of the slate was in the strange neat dark hand, the one that set a name down before anybody looked at a face, and the small stop between two clusters of strokes came there a fifth time — at the orange flare light, on the birth-slate, on the square’s slate on Stone 10, at this table on Stone 20, now on this slate at the market’s edge — and lay beside the other four without joining them. There was no word in Grathic for the small stop and no hand in the basket to make one; the grammar of the Return took the woman out of her own business as neatly as it took the speaker out of Berr’s sentence, and finished her without raising its voice. The Drawing collector’s ordinary hand, the one with no pause in it at all, stayed where it was in its own place, filed apart as a hand that wrote a measure and did not stop, which was a different thing from a hand that stopped and did not finish. A pause was not a hearing and an interval was not a hearing either; the ear that held five hearings of the flat note from four sources had never added a sixth, and the five stops on the slates were not among them and never had been.

For one beat the light broke through without summons and without a name. Orange on wet snow. A hand in a grey sleeve writes and stops between two clusters, and the hiss of snow taking the heat goes up past the writing hand, and a hand goes into a coat pocket and finds wool where a small lit rectangle with a name lay, and he does not look at the name. Then it was past, and he was back in the post’s thin shade with the cold iron at his cheek, the spent crust and the given crust in the fold, and the river wind coming under the wool. Nothing had crossed between the two places that he could carry back. He had a fifth sighting and no more than that: no power to decide with, no word to speak, no hand to point with, no standing to enter one stroke of the upper line. If the slate had been laid in the basket he could not have read it, and it was not laid there. If he had known the name in the grey sleeve he could not have said it, and he did not know it. If he had had a hand he would have had to decide what to do with the five pauses, and a decision was the one thing he had kept from making for twenty-four days, because four likenesses laid side by side are four likenesses and the joining is a man’s work, and he had no standing for the work. The count changed. Nothing else did. The market went back to its pigs and its turnips and its figures left standing where nobody made them agree, and a barrow of grey went out through the north gate toward the Bone Orchard, heaped a little above the sides where the load had shifted, and nobody in the square looked twice at it, because it was the afternoon’s work and the same work that went out every market-day.

At dusk she lifted the basket off the post and carried him up the yard with woodsmoke and river wind caught under the fold, and set the fold right at his ear without looking at his face, and set him on his bed under the roof and not in the Debt Loft, and banked the coals with the back of a broken pot. She did not touch the mark. A reading was not an opening, and the rule held at the bank as exactly as it had held before the lid, and the little-door burn shut on nothing all the way up the yard. Tarn’s small knife was back on its sill where it could be checked, and he had told the yard so once more, unasked, and six years of the seven were counted and the seventh held back. Jorra Vail had moved the damper and not said the back room was cold, and had not looked toward the bed; the grey dust stayed where it did not show on his forearms, and the bone-coloured slip went on drying to the colour of old bone. The house kept its shape around a man who was not in it: the coat on the peg, the hollow seat, the ridge turning its corner unswept, the proverb going to the fire and the pot, the uncalled debt lying where it lay, neither paid nor settled nor done with. The kiln took its breath under the roof. Four, and four, and a gap.

He lay with the two crusts in the fold at his side, one cold and one given, and the fifth pause lying with the four others and not joined to them, and the Drawing collector’s hand with no pause staying in its own place, and the figure for the Five going around the district in fists and mouths, and one day after tonight was tomorrow. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth and came down on the fold, and the surgeon who had once reduced a shoulder in the dark could not lift a crust to his own mouth. The coals hissed once under the ash and held. The door stood a finger’s width open, and the fire kept four, and four, and a gap. Kindling was tomorrow.

The Chronicle of Chapter 7

The last tend of Stone came down on the Vail house and the boy counted it because there was nothing else that was his. Sella Vail touched the widow’s mark before each opening and at no other hour, then lifted the lid. Fuel and bread went into the ten days in loaves and glances, one brown-edged loaf set apart, and nobody said what it counted. Fifteen days old in the fold, he carried his own exact number down to the kindling stack, four, four and a space where the fifth should be. Somebody back from the ice brought a number in a shut fist and called it a cold; Sella answered with the fire.

Tarn, six of seven years counted and the seventh held back, set his knife on its sill and went for a collector for a neighbour. The collector came with fingertips the colour of skimmed milk gone off, hand to the slate before the bed: the measure was taken, the account stood. A carried vessel gave its flat note past the door and Tarn’s head turned a degree. Jorra came in with grey dust in the creases of his forearms after five clean days and moved the damper before Sella had finished.

Alda Reed came smelling of soap and milk, laid the birth-slates on the bench before the basket, touched the tin inside her bodice unopened, crossed two fingers at the flower unknowingly, and covered the sign with a date; on a narrow slate she wrote what she would not leave lying and turned it face-down toward an unnamed floorboard. The cold deepened and the Slip Brook took a skin; a pot cracked two paces off with the flat knock he knew and nobody called a sound, and he could not turn to place it. The number for the Five went around in fists and mouths, three, then two, then whatever the moot gave out, while he kept one day after tonight.

Mira Silt put a fresh crust into the fold beside the cold one of Stone 15 and went off with two cords; Dessa coughed from the pits and called it weather. On the Last Day’s eve the brooms came out and Sella measured the Vail ridge against the death-house ridge. On Stone 30 Berr Clay wiped his thumb twice and read one in twelve; the Five were three days off. At the market’s edge the pause in the neat dark hand came a fifth time and did not join the other four. For one beat the flare light was orange on wet snow and a hand in a grey sleeve found wool where a lit phone with a name had lain; he did not look. At dusk Sella set him on his bed under the roof, banked the coals with a broken pot and did not touch the mark. The fire kept four, four and a gap. Kindling was tomorrow.
