25

The Last Tend Holds

Days 113–115 · Seed 24–26, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, Sill

A figure kept in a fist costs nothing; a place kept on a shelf costs a loaf a day and nobody counts it.

— A saying of the clay-bound, Low Kiln

— Day 113 —

Seed 24, Y.H. 813

The last tend of the planting stood in the worked strips east of Low Kiln with the seed up and nobody giving a figure for the hands that had set it. First grey came over the Clay Yard the colour of wet ash rubbed thin on a board, and the rows beyond the drying racks kept their small green by the skin of the cold. The hands that had gone in on Seed 15 came out at dusk and went home and came back, and the yard counted them the way it counted everything that must not be written: in glances and handfuls and the space where a man’s arm should have been. A woman with seed in her fist had shut the fist rather than say what it held. A man with a barrow had shaped a number with his mouth and thought better of it. The seed stood all the same. It had wanted hands and it had got them, and the figure for the hands had gone nowhere, and the last tend held.

The boy was carried to the edge of the strips in a basket under a fold of wool, the way a pot is carried that must not be slopped, and set where he could see the direction and not enter it. He was one hundred and thirteen days old, three months and twenty-three days, and nobody under the roof knew there was a number to be known, and nobody said so. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the worked ground and came down. He could no more close two paces on the strip than he could lift his own head from the fold; the distance was a direction with no figure at its foot, and a direction with no figure at the foot of it was still a direction, which was the whole of what he could enter. He had once stood at the head of a table and sorted a room’s worth of wounded men into the ones who would live and the ones who would not, and he could not put a hand to his own chin. The joke of it was exact and cost him nothing, because there was nobody under that roof to tell it to who would know what a chin was worth.

Cold arrived in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, and the wool did less against it than the cold wanted, riding back from the bare joint so the cold found the place it had come for. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound it made when it went too far, and nobody put it right, because the instruction had gone to the wool and not to the wandering. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had wiped it, and nobody under the roof had been given an instruction about the thread. The last tend held. He lay in a basket at the edge of it and could not close two inches on the crust in the fold.

By the time the light got under the Vail kiln-house roof Sella Vail had given the bucket its instruction, and the broom, and the pot, and the fire, in that order, before she had given anybody anything. “Round it,” she said to the broom, meaning the corner where the sour mineral tang came up out of the unswept Ash. “Go around what the sweep left. It cost enough to come in.” To the pot she said nothing that a pot would need to hear twice, and to the fire she said it was the thaw, and to the basket she said nothing at all. The instruction went into the wool before it went into a person, and the person came last or not at all, which was the arrangement the house had run on for three years. She said the house where another woman would have said I, and kept a half-pace from every open slate, going around a slate set out to dry as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. Her little-door burn shut on nothing as she passed the basket and opened on the bucket’s handle.

The slate set out to dry at the rack’s end lay with its writing turned under and nobody turned it up, and she went around it as she went around the space beneath the lowest rack, as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. The yard went around that space too and entered it none of them and did not say what lay beneath it. The boy kept the going-around on a place of its own, exact and worth nothing, and made no approach to it with his one free inch of hand.

At the loaf shelf she moved her lips along the row without making a figure of it, the way a woman counts what she will not count aloud. The board had two gaps in it, one at the row’s end and a second beside it that had come in the night of Seed 6, and her lips went over the bare places and came back to the same spot twice the way they always did, and nothing was said. The clay jar on the wall shelf was cold under its glaze. Above the kiln the woodsmoke had gone flat under the roof and lay there with the smell of wet wool and the faint sour tang that came up from the corner in cold weather. A hard heel of barley bread sat at the row’s end where a thumb had pushed it along, and the space beside it was worn smooth by hands reaching past it for something else. There was no figure in it. There had never been a figure in it. There was a saying among the loaf shelves of Low Kiln that two gaps stay two gaps until a finger lays them together, and that an account then wants a name; no finger in the district had laid these two together, and the boy kept his own finger off them for the same reason another man might keep his off a clamp that stood open on a table.

❦

A woman came up from the yard well with wet sleeves and water on her hands, and asked what the planting had cost now that hands had been in the strips. She asked it of the doorway and not of anybody in it, which was how the yard asked its questions when it wanted the household to answer and no house to have answered. The question stood a moment in the cold between them, the way a pot stands on a bench before somebody decides where to set it. She said it again to the pot as though the pot might have an answer the doorway had not, and wrung a sleeve without taking her eyes off the shelf. A bucket went down into the well behind her and came up with the cold iron of the handle two inches beyond the boy’s hand.

Sella’s eyes went once to the clay jar under its glaze and came back. It was the only look she gave it, and it was not an opening. “Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there,” she said. “The house has said it.” She left the space at the row’s end with no figure in it, pushed the heel of bread along the shelf a finger’s breadth, and went on with the loaves.

“Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there. The house has said it.” The instruction went to the pot. It did not go to the woman, and it did not go into the basket, and the woman stayed with her sleeves wet and her question still in her mouth, reaching for a pot she had come for and would set down somewhere and leave. The boy, who was the only grown man under that roof and had the use of none of it, watched the order of it: the record before the person, the pot before the question, the shelf before the child. He had watched that order in a better-lit room and had learned what it meant, which was that the thing written down would still be there after the person was carried out. Here nobody was being carried out. The order went on all the same, exact and worthless, the way instruments laid out on a towel are exact and worthless until a hand takes one up.

The woman took the pot by the handle and held it a moment with the instruction still in her ear, not minding that it had not been given to her, or minding and finding the arrangement ordinary. He had seen a chart handed to a man and thanked for it, and an order given to a nurse and written down, and both had taken a thing addressed to the object as though it had been addressed to them. Here the pot went off under her arm and the question went with it, and the shelf kept its two gaps.

Later, when the light had got into the well’s mouth and the buckets were going down and coming up, a man came up the yard toward the well with a number in his fist. He had brought it up out of the fist as he walked, the way a man brings up a thing he has been carrying in his pocket to see whether it is still there, and he held it in the open palm a moment for the yard to see. Chaff from the worked strips stood in the creases at the base of his thumb. A woman at the tubs called the morning cold and did not look up, and the yard went on with its buckets; nobody asked him twice. He looked at it. Something in the looking made him shut his hand again, the thumb going over the top the way a thumb goes over a thing that is not to be read aloud, and he put the fist back into his sleeve and went off toward the road without giving it. The sleeve was worn at the cuff and smelled of the yard and of tallow, and the fist went down into it without a sound, the way a thing goes into a pocket that will not be looked in again.

He read the shut fist the way he had once read a chart laid closed with a neat line drawn over a note nobody had opened: exact, complete, worth nothing. It was the same work he had done at the foot of a bed, closing the chart on a man already beyond the room and going on to the next with the line drawn straight and the note left where it lay. In that room the neat line meant a man had been sorted and the sorting entered, and the neatness of it was the whole of the harm. Here the fist shut on a figure and the figure went back where it had come from, and the harm was smaller and no less complete. The figure had been brought up out of the fist and looked at and put back, and it had arrived nowhere, and nobody in the yard had made an event of it. He wanted to lift a hand to his own chin and could not. The fist went off up the road under a sleeve with the number still shut in it, and the number went with it, and the strips kept their seed and their handfuls and their glances and gave nothing up.

The work went on around the withheld figure the way water goes around a stone set in it. The woman with the wet sleeves wrung them out at the well and went back to her pot. A broom went around what the sweep had left at the corner rather than over it, the bristles making the small dry sound bristles make on flagstones, and nobody called the place by a name. At the shelf loaves moved along the board a finger at a time, the hard heel going where hard heels go, and the two gaps stayed where they were and were not read. Sella shifted the pot the woman had been sent to a little further from the draught, set it down where it could be found in the dark, and left it. Nobody joined the number in the man’s sleeve to the seed in the strips. Nobody joined anything to anything. A girl at the racks called to somebody about a pot and got no answer and went on with the racks. Two men passing the well spoke of the weather as a thing that had been expected and did not look at the sky to check it.

Under the roof the carried fire lay under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, giving back four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for a hundred and thirteen days. The boy did not ask who had paid, because every warmth in this house had a payer and none of them stood in the room to be thanked.

She did not touch the widow’s mark at the well or at the shelf or afterward, because a well is not an opening and a shelf is not an opening and a row of loaves is not an opening, and she had long ago stopped needing to be told how a house keeps what it keeps. The mark hung where it hung under her coat with the Vail kiln scratched into it and went unread by every hand in the yard. The pot stood where it had been set. The broom went on around the corner. Her lips moved along the loaf row and came back to the same bare place twice, and the boy’s small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the worked strips and came down, while one hundred and thirteen stood where nobody had put a figure to it and the seed stood up in the last tend with the hands counted in glances and handfuls and never in a figure.

The middle of the day came under the Vail kiln-house roof without arriving, the way a pot already on the bench comes to a woman’s hand: the light lay flat and the woodsmoke lay flat under it, and between the two flats the room held wet wool and the faint sour tang that came up out of the unswept Ash corner. The boy was at the loaf shelf still, set where his eye could take in the row and his hand could not take in anything, which was the whole of the arrangement — a direction with no figure at its foot, a row with two bare places in it that nobody called by a name. Cold arrived in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, and the wool did less against it than the cold wanted, riding back from the bare joint so the cold found the place it had come for. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound it made when it went too far, and nobody put it right, because the instruction had gone to the wool and not to the wandering.

Another neighbour came for a loaf with a pot hooked over two fingers. Her hand went past the bare board before her eyes went anywhere, the way a hand goes to a familiar gap in a shelf it has used for years: it opened for the half-beat it takes to find what is not there, that small pause a hand makes over an absence the way a tongue finds a hole in a mouth, and closed on the hard heel of a loaf beyond. Only after that did her eyes come to the basket, the fold, the small curved shape of a head under the wool that was not turned toward her. There was chaff at the base of her thumb and a wet cuff, and she carried the pot the way a woman carries a thing she will set down somewhere and leave.

“That end place came in with the planting,” she said to the board, meaning the gap at the row’s end; and then, in the same breath with no stop between the two, “and that there’s a child’s appetite. It’ll want filling come Reed.” She said it to the bare board and to her pot, not to anybody under the roof, the way the yard said its questions when it wanted the house to answer and no house to have answered. A woman who says a thing to a shelf is not asking to be contradicted by a basket.

She did not see what she had done in one breath. She had laid the old gap at the row’s end and the empty place at the boy’s ear under one cause and called them both the planting’s doing, and gone off with her pot thinking she had said two plain things about two plain boards. She did not see that the two bare boards on this one shelf were one absence wearing two shapes. A cause, once laid, is a thing that wants a name at the foot of it; this one went off under her arm with the loaf and stood in no slate that any clerk could read. Her hand had gone to the board before her eyes had gone to him, and the order of that was the order of the whole district — the record before the person, the shelf before the child — and she went on with her pot and her loaf as though nothing had passed between them.

The boy kept three places and did not let them touch. On one stood the shelf’s two unnamed gaps, in a room that did not touch this one: the old space at the row’s end, and the second that had come in the night of Seed 6 with no scrape, crumb, print or word, a bare board worn smooth by hands reaching past it for something else. On another stood the fold’s one stiff brown-edged crust with the empty place beside it that had no name — a little hard heel of barley gone past smelling of anything, and beside it the shape of the absence where a small pale stone had once lain warm against a belly and was gone and spoken of by nobody, left in the fold of grey wool on the abandoned bed in House Silt. On a third stood the neighbour’s one-breath naming, a shape with the shelf’s old absence and the absence at his ear folded together inside it. He set them edge to edge and wrote no cause across the space between them. He had long ago learned to feel an empty place with an exactness that had nothing to do with temperature: he had felt the place of the pale stone in the fold in House Silt with the same faculty he had once used to find a rustled joint under a man’s skin, and the feeling bought him nothing here, because there was no hand on the other end of it that he could reach. He had learned in a better-lit room what three likenesses in a row will do to a man who wants an answer: laid one on another they grow a cause, and a fourth would make the cause with a clean hand at the foot of it, and the clean hand was the one object in this world he had spent two lives declining to become. He did not make her joining his own. He kept her saying on its own tray, the way a man keeps a clamp laid open on a towel — exact, complete, and not to be closed on anything that was not his to close. A fourth likeness would have joined shelf to fold and fold to appetite and given the whole of it a clean hand and a name, and there was nobody under this roof who had asked him for either.

Sella Vail moved her lips along the row and came back to the same bare place twice. She did not give the neighbour a figure, and she did not give the board one, and she did not give the woman who had gone off with her pot one. Her eyes went once to the clay jar cold under its glaze on the wall shelf and came back to the loaves; it was the single look she gave it every day and it was not an opening. She did not touch the widow’s mark, because a shelf is not an opening, and a row of loaves is not an opening, and no opening had come under this roof that day. The neighbour’s errand stayed an errand. It was not turned into a walk to Slate Row, and nothing she had said was carried out of the house to be entered anywhere. Her little-door burn shut on nothing as she passed the loaf row and opened on the pot’s handle. The loaves went along the board a finger at a time where a thumb had pushed them, the hard heel going where hard heels go, and the two gaps stayed where they were and were not read.

At the going of the light she carried him up at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, going around the space beneath the lowest rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and set him on his bed under the roof and not in the Debt Loft. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without looking at his face, and left the thread of drool at his mouth frozen and unwiped, because nobody in this house had been given an instruction about the thread and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. The going-around was a thing he kept on a place of its own, exact and worth nothing, and he made no approach to it with his one free inch of hand.

He turned out the private file as a man lays instruments on a towel, each object going to its place under a hand that knew where it lay. Six hearings of the full note stood beside and never among, five from four sources and the sixth out of a person, and no seventh came in this day: no whole vessel’s note had come past the door, and the wheel’s and the pots’ notes were not hearings. Three near-full answers stood on their own tray, advanced and not paid. Three intervals stood in their own row, and beside them the small half-beat of a broom’s check went as another place where a stroke should have gone on and did not, laid there and not explained. The word forty stood unspoken, with the readable shape of the double turn beside it and the weight of a thumb on wicker apart from both, and neither was entered. Four low things kept their four places and the river’s note of water over water was a fifth on a place of its own, not a hearing and not joined to any of the four. The two tellings of the shut counting room stayed joined to no kiln, and no third telling came. the damper’s double bite — the clamp that holds a vessel whole and the clamp that can take the hand off a man, with no word in Grathic for both mercies — lay filed as an object worth nothing, joined to no kiln. The loan that can hear lay uncalled, and the sentence that would call it had not been said under this roof.

He added what the day had given without entering any of it: the number brought up out of a fist, looked at, and put back in the sleeve; the woman with wet sleeves sent to the pot; the neighbour’s one-breath naming of two absences as one appetite. He laid each on its place and went over none of them with his thumb. Three things had come into the room and none of them had been given a figure, a name or a standing; the withheld number, the sent-to-the-pot question and the joined appetite stood edge to edge like three instruments laid out that no hand had been given to hold. Nothing had moved. Nothing was paid. Nothing was joined. The file was complete and useless, the way a chart is complete in the moment before the man who needs it is carried out of the room, the neat line drawn straight over the note that nobody opened. He was the only grown man under this roof and had the use of none of it, and the joke of that was exact and cost him nothing, because there was nobody here to tell it to who would know what a chart was worth.

At the fire she told it it was the thaw and turned the coals with the back of a broken pot, the way a woman turns a thing that has been trusted to her and will go on being trusted to her when she is not in the room. “The house will keep it,” she said, where another woman would have said I. She stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand, counting something under her breath that no man could take up as a figure, toward no opening: not a door, not a shelf, not a slate turned writing-down, not the space under the rack, nothing that would have asked for a hand on the little-door mark. She pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn, and the grey streak at her temple showed pale in the dying light off the coals. She set the pot where it could be found in the dark and did not look toward the bed. It was told of her in the yard that she had once sat three nights with a child of her own who cooled while everybody was kind, and that such a house has had practice; the story had come from other mouths and never from hers, and she had never once laid it beside anything under this roof, and least of all beside the bed.

The carried fire took its coal under blind ash and the back of the broken pot and gave back four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for a hundred and thirteen days. The door stood a finger’s width open. Through the gap came the river’s note out of the Ilm, water going over water; through the floor came the kiln’s four, four and a gap. Two counts in two places that had not met in a hundred and thirteen days and would not meet tonight. Above him the boards kept four nails, four, and a fifth that was a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts did not become one. Cold came in its known order and the wool rode back from the bare wrist, and the crust lay two inches from his ear where he could smell nothing from it and could not close the distance to it.

His small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down. One hundred and thirteen stood where one hundred and twelve had stood, exact, unspent and spoken by nobody under the roof, while the river went over water beyond the finger’s-width gap and the fire missed its fifth breath under the floor, and nothing joined, nothing paid, nothing ended.

— Day 114 —

Seed 25, Y.H. 813

Before first grey the yard had the look of a pot left covered overnight, the cold settled into it and nothing yet lifting the cover. Men came up out of the clay road with their collars high and their shoulders drawn in against what the dark had left of the wind, and crossed the Clay Yard toward the wheel-shed with the quiet step of men taking a stair they would rather not have heard. Their boots went through the ruts without speaking. The low voices came after them and went into the room off the wheel-shed and stayed there, and the door of that room kept its finger’s width of open, as it had kept it on other mornings that the boy could not now count without wanting a figure he had no standing to ask for. The boy lay in his basket at the edge of the light, where Sella Vail had set him, because the edge of the light was a place a basket could be set and no slate lay open upon it. The voices did not carry so far as the wool over his ear. What carried was the small settling sound the cold makes in a house before anybody has given the fire its instruction, the iron and the flagstones taking in the night and giving nothing back.

He had learned to hear a room’s temperature in its silence the way another man might read a chart by the line of a note laid down and not opened. A room that holds a low voice is a room shut against the yard, and a door a finger’s width open is not the same thing as an open door; it is a door with a decision left standing in the gap. Sella gave the house no name for the men. She did not say the room was cold. A woman who says a room is cold has made a statement about a room, and a statement, in this district, is the first thing an account wants to fasten on; she gave her morning to the fire and to the pot instead, and the two of them asked nothing back. The boy kept the finger’s width on a place of its own, exact, complete and worth nothing, and laid no other morning beside it to make a row. Three such rows would have made a cause, and a cause would have wanted a hand at the foot of it, and the clean hand was the one object in this world he had spent two lives declining to become.

Jorra Vail came through the finger’s-width-open door with the morning already worked into him. Fine grey slate dust was rubbed into both forearms past the bend of his left little finger, ground small as meal, not the bone-coloured slip that dries pale on the wheel and comes off in flakes. The dust had gone into the creases the way grit goes into a field after rain, and no washing of the yard would bring it out of there before the day was done. He did not say where he had been, and Sella did not ask; a question about the dust would have been a question about the room, and a question about the room would have been the first line of something written down. The boy kept dusty forearms and clean forearms on one tray as two states of one morning, the way a man keeps two slides under the same lens and refuses the comparison until he has both. He asked neither which slate had been broken nor whether it had been a birth-slate. Those were the two questions the dust stood for, and he had learned to leave a question standing where it was rather than put a hand to it and have the hand go down in the record.

Under the lowest rack the broken birth-slates lay where they had lain, and the yard went around the space above them as wide as a pail of hot water set down where a child might knock it. Nobody entered the space. Nobody swept beneath the lowest board, and nobody lifted the board to see what was under it, and nobody said what had become of the slates or why they were in pieces. The space above them was not stepped into, and the boy was not carried across it; Sella took the long way around with the basket held level, the little-door burn closing on the handle and opening on nothing, and the keeping of the space was a thing she performed with her feet and never with her mouth. A thing gone around is a thing kept, and a thing kept is not a thing explained. He set the going-around beside the finger’s width of open door and the dust on the forearms, three objects in three places, and wrote no cause across the space between them.

Jorra spoke to the wheel before he spoke to anyone, as he had spoken to it a hundred mornings and would speak to it a hundred more. “It doesn’t care whose hands,” he said. “It cares if they’re steady.” He said it to the wheel and not to the yard, in a flat voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either, the voice a man uses to an animal that must be steadied before it is worked. The wheel took the house’s time as it had taken Garl’s before it and would take somebody’s after it, and the hollow in the seat kept the leftward lean of twenty years that nobody had filled. Jorra put his weight into the hollow as into a thing he had not yet earned and meant to keep. He set a pot on the bench and said, “You’ll do,” with no more feeling than a man who passes an instrument across a table. He knew the full note of a Vail pot before it left the wheel — knew it in the hand and the shoulder and the thin place near the foot, before any knuckle had been laid on it and before it had gone under fire — and he would not say so before a kiln-master come up from High Kiln, who would call the knowing early and make something of it. A thing said aloud in this district is a thing that can be entered, and the knowing was the one part of the wheel Jorra had kept out of every account.

There was a sweetish edge on the wind that came up off the glaze road from High Kiln, the taste of lead that had been fired badly somewhere and carried on a bad wind into the yard. The boy knew the taste from other mornings and had no word for it in Grathic that any grown person here would have used. He filed the knowing — that Jorra could hear a pot whole before it left the wheel and would not say it to a kiln-master — on a tray of its own, beside the bent finger and the dust, and let it lie there. The adult in him recognised the shape of a man protecting one exact skill from men who would turn it into a price; the child in him wanted the breast and wanted the crust two inches from his ear and could not have told either want to a person who would understand the first. Both were true. The second was louder, and neither had a standing in this yard.

Sella had not finished telling him the fire was low. “The fire —” she said, and the rest of it never arrived at him. His bent finger went into the cold iron of the damper ahead of the sentence, going in first as the finger always went in first, ahead of the hand and ahead of the word, and the iron took the cold out of the fingertip and gave a little of it back warmer, the extremity leading the core in the reverse of the way a body loses heat. He moved the tongue of iron a hair’s breadth and left it standing there. The end of her instruction went past him and arrived at the pot, and the household straightened around his silence the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted off it: the broom, the bench, the basket at the edge of the light, all coming level at once without anybody giving the order to come level. He had not answered with a word. He had not looked at her. Obedience arriving before the instruction that calls it is obedience and a correction at the same time, and neither of them named which of the two had just gone through the room.

The boy read the iron once more as he had read it on other mornings and as he would read it again: the same bite that holds a vessel whole and can take the hand off a man, mercy and mutilation in one piece of cold iron, with no word in Grathic for both mercies. There was a tourniquet in the room he had lived in before this one, a clamp of iron with the same double nature, and he did not bring it across, because there was no door between the two rooms that any person in this yard could have opened and he had no standing in either. He filed the reading as an object worth nothing, joined to no kiln, and kept his finger off the place where it would have joined the two tellings of the shut counting room that had come into this yard from eighteen leagues east. Those two tellings stayed on their own tray, joined to no kiln, and no third telling came this morning.

Tarn took the broom only after it had been given its instruction. Sella gave the instruction to the broom — to go around what the sweep had left and around the space beneath the lowest rack and over the flagstones toward the road — and only after that did his hand close on the handle, as a man takes up a tool already named and not a thing he has chosen. He said Yes, house to an early thaw and a late one the yard was arguing before the light, agreeing so entirely that the argument had nowhere to put its foot, and went on wanting the late because a late thaw puts off the paying, though the paying had stood in the ruts since the water went. His chest had been tight since the dark; there was a small interval at the top of each reach where the breath should have gone in and did not, the held breath from Seed 16 still unspent. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line, and no scrubbing lifted it. The pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back.

Yesterday, behind the wheel-keeper’s standing finger, his stroke had checked for half a beat on the flagstones. Today it did not. The bristles went on over the stones without a pause and without a turn of the head, out into the yard and toward the road, over the place where the stroke had stopped the day before and past it, going on as though the place were stone like any other stone. The flagstones kept that place all the same. They kept the small interval where a stroke had stopped as a thing nobody would enter: no foot set into it, no broom drawn back across it, no hand pointing it out. The equality under the roof held at the place it had moved to, which was not the place it had held yesterday, and neither man made a word of the difference. Jorra did not say that Tarn had gone on. Tarn did not say that Jorra’s finger stood in the iron. The loaf shelf kept its two unnamed gaps in a room that did not touch this one, the old space at the row’s end and the newer palm’s breadth that had come in the night of Seed 6 without a scrape, crumb, print or word, and the two rooms had not touched in a hundred and fifteen days and would not touch tonight. Below the sill a child’s foot stayed dark inside three turns of wool, cold to one woman and weather to the yard, the extremity going before the core in the order he had learned in a room that was not this one, the afterdrop waiting on the far side of a warming nobody would perform. The reading was perfect and bought nothing: no silt-leach, no cause, no cure, no name, only the cold doing what cold does and a man who knew the whole of it and could not close two inches on a fold. The clean dark line where Ash had turned lay unswept beside the new finer hairline of grey that had gathered next to it, and the house called the two of them the thaw and would go on calling them that until someone said what they were, which nobody had. The space under the lowest rack had been gone around again this evening as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and entered by nobody. No opening had come under this roof on Seed 26, so the widow’s mark hung where it hung, untouched at a basket and a bed, and the private roll the house kept stayed unshown.

The carried fire lay under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees the way a thing warms that is trusted to a woman who will not be in the room to watch it. It gave back four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, as it had given on every day of this child’s life, and its payer had gone unnamed for a hundred and fifteen days. Every warmth in this house had a payer and not one of them stood in the room to be thanked. The milk, the coal, the seed, the hands in the strips, the loaf that had gone off the shelf on Seed 6, the fire under the pot — each had crossed into the house from somewhere and left the giver standing outside in the yard with the giving unentered. He had learned early to ask who paid before he asked what a thing was worth, and the asking had never once produced a name.

At the fire she told it it was the thaw and turned the coals with the back of a broken pot, the way a woman turns a thing that has been trusted to her and will go on being trusted when she is not standing over it. “The house will keep it,” she said, where another woman would have said I. She stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand, counting something under her breath that no man could take up as a figure, toward no opening: not a door, not a shelf, not a slate turned writing-down, not the space under the rack, nothing that would have asked for a hand on the little-door mark. She pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn, and the grey streak at her temple showed pale in the dying light off the coals, a single pale thread in a head of coal-black that she said had come in the winter Garl died and would not discuss further. She set the pot where it could be found in the dark and did not look toward the bed. It was told of her in the yard that she had once sat three nights with a child of her own who cooled while everybody was kind, and that such a house has had practice; the story had come from other mouths and never from hers, and she had never once laid it beside the dark foot below this sill, and least of all beside the bed.

The door stood a finger’s width open. Through the gap came the river’s note out of the Ilm, water going over water, the small steady sound the boy had kept for a fifth thing on a place of its own since the first warm days. Through the floor came the kiln, four, four and a gap under blind ash. Two counts in two places that had not met in a hundred and fifteen days and would not meet tonight, though both went on under one roof and both lacked the same fifth thing. Above him the boards kept four nails, four, and a fifth that was a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts did not become one, and the three counts did not become one either. He had long ago given up trying to lay them edge to edge. A man can hold a great many separate things in his head and keep them separate, and it is the whole of the work and the whole of the loneliness, and it buys nothing.

Cold arrived in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist. The wool rode back from the bare joint so the cold found the place it had come for, and the thread at his mouth was frozen into a small stiff line that nobody had been instructed to wipe. He wanted the crust two inches from his ear, and the breast, and the bucket’s lip two paces off instead of the empty distance where it stood, and the number of his days said aloud by somebody who would have known what a life counted when they said it. He could reach or ask for none of them. He was a man who had reduced a thousand shoulders in a room that was not this one, who had decided in ninety seconds which of twenty injured bodies would be given the next breath of air, and he could not close two inches on the fold that smelled of wet wool and nothing else. Both facts were true, and the second was the one that was loud here, as it had been every night since the stone at his belly went cold, and the first was the one he had spent two lives trying to put down and could not.

His small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down. One hundred and fifteen stood where one hundred and fourteen had stood, exact, unspent and spoken by nobody under the roof, while the river went over water beyond the finger’s-width gap and the fire missed its fifth breath under the floor and the two counts did not meet. Nothing was joined, nothing was paid, nothing was named, nothing was opened, and nothing ended. On the morrow one hundred and sixteen would stand in the same place with the same number of nails above it and the same gap under the floor, and nobody under this roof would know that a number had moved.

The Chronicle of Chapter 25

On the twenty-fourth of Seed the last tend stood with the seed up and no figure for the hands that set it; a man brought a number up out of a fist toward the well, looked at it, and put it back in his sleeve, so the figure arrived nowhere. Sella Vail gave the bucket, broom, pot and fire their instructions before anybody and never the basket, and sent a woman to ask the pot what a wet thing costs, because the house has said it. Another neighbour reached past a bare board and in one breath called the old space at the row’s end a thing the planting brought in and the empty place at the child’s ear a child’s appetite, never seeing she had laid two rooms under one cause. The boy kept the three places apart.

On the twenty-fifth men came before first grey into the finger’s-width-open room off the wheel-shed, and Jorra Vail gave them no name. He came through with grey slate dust in both forearms past the bent finger, spoke to the wheel before anyone, and put that finger into the cold damper ahead of Sella’s unfinished sentence, so the household straightened around his silence. The change was held and not repeated: Tarn’s broom went on over the flagstones without the half-check of the day before, and the stones kept the place where it had stopped. Three doors down Alda Reed laid her birth-slates on the bench before she turned to the fold, crossed two fingers at the birth-flower unknowing and covered the sign with a dead woman’s date; warm and breathing was exact and not true, and her sixth page stayed face-down.

On the twenty-sixth, at the Fishstairs, Mira Silt answered whether the run would be thin --- Thin? Who’s to say thin? It comes when it comes. Ask the river --- and put her thumb under her apron to the grey cord when a buyer named a figure; a shift of a creel showed a second cord twisted twice beneath it, neither knot counted. The clerk sat at the landing for a measure nobody had called. At the Cross a soft-handed man from Slate Row handed a figure to a slate held at the edge, and rested his thumb on the corner afterward.

On his bed at the going of the light the boy turned out his file and found it unchanged: six hearings beside and never among with no seventh, three near-full answers advanced and not paid, the word forty unspoken, four low things with the river’s note a fifth, the shut-room tellings joined to no kiln, the damper’s double bite, the loan uncalled. Sella told the fire it was the thaw and said the house would keep it. His hand went up an inch toward the water and came down. One hundred and fifteen stood unspent and spoken by nobody; nothing was joined, paid, named or opened, and nothing ended.
