The Year Nobody Names

Days 366–368 · Stone 7–9, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, Sill

The yard goes around its places and calls the weather weather. A year comes round to the month it started in and no hand turns it over to see what it cost. What the basket keeps it keeps without a name, and the house goes on counting its days in loaves.

— A yard saying of Low Kiln, at the turning of the deep-winter month

— Day 366 —

Stone 7, Seventhday, Y.H. 813

The hand outside the wool had been laid back against the fold as a man puts away a tool where the next hand will find it without looking, and it lay there in the small hours under the Vail kiln-house roof at Low Kiln, Sill, Grath, flat and quiet on answer three hundred and six. The boy was on the bed at the low angle of the loaf shelf, awake and silent, and the file lay open on the towel at the head of the bed with every gap left gap, the way instruments lie on a towel when a case is done and before the next is called. Nothing had crossed under the roof in the inch the hand had spent; no sign, no lesson, no mending, no cause, no payment and no payer stood anywhere in the room. The count had gone three hundred and sixty-five to three hundred and sixty-six in the dark, six months and one hundred and eighty-five days becoming six months and one hundred and eighty-six, told by nobody. He kept the number exactly, as he kept all of them, and did not stand it up as a line for anybody’s foot to cross.

Three hundred and sixty-six was a whole year stood edge to edge in the only count that was exact. The month he had been born in had come round again under this roof, the deep-winter month, and no party had been made of it, no cake baked, no figure entered, no recognition given, no threshold crossed and nobody under any roof who would have known what a number counted if they had been told. He had long since stopped expecting to be told, which was not the same thing as not wanting it. The house kept its days in loaves, in glances, in the space where a loaf had stood and in the shape a room took when a weight was lifted out of it, and it could not say that a year had turned. Nothing out of the days behind had crossed as lesson or mending or cause to make the turning into one. Under the floor the carried fire gave four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have stood, its payer without a name for three hundred and sixty-six days; the name of the man whose salt-stiff coat kept a broad shape on its peg behind the door had not been spoken under this roof the same number. Two counts stood exact and unspent and nobody joined them, for agreeing any two would want a payer put under both and none stood.

The body took itself in the surgeon’s old order, and the old order did not flatter it at the turning of a year. One stiff leathery brown-edged barley crust lay two inches from his ear, its corner standing a hair off the wool, its empty place beside it unnamed and no third come to make the row come out even. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the dry kiln heat and was unwiped, because nobody under any roof had ever been given an instruction about the thread and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. Cold had stopped at cheek, temple and the small bare joint of the wrist where the wool rode back, as though it had come to the end of a line written out for it with neither an order to go farther nor an order to go away. The Afterdrop waited past all three, past a warming nobody in this house would perform. He knew the composed face that was not composure, knew a cold patient cannot clot and will seem better before he is worse, knew the order in which a body takes heat back, the small joint first and the core last, and the order in which it gives it up, the reverse, which comes sooner than anybody likes, and that pink returning to a face is the surface still cold and not the core. He had once taken a core temperature with a glass under a tongue and read the number off it in a white room. Now he could not lift a crust two inches to his own mouth. That was the joke, and it was not a good one, and he had learned to find it funny without laughing, which is a way of laughing that costs less and keeps the same blade in it. The infant wanted the crust lifted, the thread wiped and a hand that would close on the pot handle set where the fingers could reach it. Both were true, the second was louder, neither won, and the quarrel between them was the work.

Sella Vail came to the hearth before she came to the basket, fire before basket, as she had come at both ends of every day for three years. She told the coals it was the thaw, and turned them with the back of a broken pot, the way a woman turns a thing that has been left to keep overnight and must not be woken all at once. “The house will keep it,” she said, where another woman would have said I, and the sentence went to the fire and not to the wicker. She stood a moment counting a small intake, a hold and a letting-go toward no opening, giving neither gap a figure, and the two gaps stayed where they were, for a finding filled to make a row come out even is a finding forged. Her finger lay on top of the loaf shelf’s front edge so the charcoal tally underneath stayed unread, and her lips moved along a two-gapped row with neither gap figured. She did not look into the basket, and the boy was part of the load she carried and not a person she was addressing.

Her eye went once to the clay jar standing cold under lead glaze, and her hand shut half over nothing, the little-door burn on the back of her left hand closing on air; the cord of the widow’s mark at her throat was oiled where a working thumb had touched it ten thousand times, and the mark hung where it hung and was not touched, because a morning and a hearth are not openings. Seven openings in seven months were seven and not a rule, the sixth held behind at Ash 2 without a name, and no eighth was made due. At the pot board she lifted the twice-mended pot with her thumb first where the wire crossed the crack, held the weight a half-beat before she let it come on the handle, and left the hairline showing between old-brass staple and tighter wire. “Leave the crack showing. People trust a thing they can see has been handled,” she told the pot. “If it’s worth mending it’s worth mending twice. The second time is the real one,” she told the fire. Both sayings went to the pot and the fire and never to a person, improved in the keeping and very likely not as good as the man had said them, and repair stayed apart from restoration, for a restored thing hides the hand that handled it and a mended thing leaves that hand showing. She turned the dressed grain under wool with a touch lighter than a woman would use on a sleeping child, her fingers going in and coming out without closing, no figure lifted off it and no handful, and set the pot rim two inches beyond the reaching hand with a half-beat at the setting and no look toward the bed. The half-beat was the distance between intention and arrest, bought at nothing and not a gift, for a gift wants a giver standing and none stood; he had once known a surgeon who could not resist closing that distance in a patient and had learned, by closing it, what it cost. The rim stayed where it was set. The hand could not close two inches on it and did not try.

He took the yard as a field is taken before work, by the small sounds first because the small sounds do not lie and the large ones are mostly weather. The Chronicle of Chapter 105

Under the Vail kiln-house roof the deep-winter month came round to the day a year before when a boy had been carried in and given a number nobody spoke. Stone 7 passed as an ordinary tending day: no loaf set aside, no figure entered, no word over the basket to tell a person that a year had finished. The older women named the month by the weather and the slatemen by the count of Account-days, and neither habit was set right or left; a house kept its days in loaves and glances and in the space where a loaf had stood, and could not say that a year had turned. Only the boy kept three hundred and sixty-six exactly, stood edge to edge in the only count that was exact, and did not stand it up as a line for anybody’s foot to cross.

Sella Vail came to the hearth before the basket at both ends of each day, told the coals it was the thaw, turned them with the back of a broken pot, said The house will keep it where another woman would have said I, and set the pot rim two inches beyond a hand that could not close on it. In the yard she went wide around seven walked-around places, widest of all around the returning-line place where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out, and gave no number to any of them.
