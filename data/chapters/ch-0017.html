The Lean Week

Days 78–84 · Silt 19–25, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, Sill

A house keeps its days in loaves pushed along a shelf and a space at the end nobody will name. A boy keeps his in days nobody will speak. The two figures sit side by side and do not agree, and nobody makes them agree, because a week that has been counted is a week that can be borne.

— A saying of the rack-end, Low Kiln

— Day 78 —

Silt 19, Y.H. 813

The mud had set into a brown skin over the ruts, and it held the print of a boot the way a cracked cup holds the shape of the hand that lifted it, faithful to a thing gone. It gave the boot back slowly. All morning the Clay Yard went around that faithfulness with brooms and buckets, the way a ward walks around a bed where somebody lies who will not be moved and will not be spoken of, and nobody said what the brown skin remembered. The water had gone out three days ago and left this behind: not a flood now, not weather, not even the thaw that was, but a surface with a memory in it, taking a heel and letting go of it a breath later as though the ground had changed its mind about what it had seen.

Sella Vail stood at the rack end with a broom in both hands, the burn across the back of her left hand shut on the ash handle. “Go around,” she said to the broom. “It cost enough to come in. It’ll cost more to take out.”

She gave the broom its instruction before she gave it to anybody, and the two women at the pan-stack waited until the instruction had arrived before they took up their own sweeping. She said the house where another woman would have said I, and she did not look down into the basket at her hip. Corin, seventy-eight days old and carried at the edge of the work, kept her half-pace from every open slate without being told to, which in this yard is the same thing as being told and not needing the telling again.

“There’s a boot in it,” a woman said, resting her broom to point with the handle. “You can see the whole sole. Heel and all. The mud’s took it and won’t let go.”

“It’ll go when it goes,” the other said.

“Ask the pot,” Sella said to the first. “It’ll tell you what a wet thing costs set down there.”

The woman went back to her sweeping as though she had been answered, which in this district is the same thing as being answered. The broom went around the pans and around the little stand where a slate had once lain, and came back to the brown skin as though it had never left it. The boy watched the bristles go around a finger’s width of dark at the corner of the Vail wall. He had seventy-eight slates set edge to edge in a place no clerk would look, with no writing on any of them and no hand to set any of them down; the house kept its count in loaves nudged along a shelf, in a glance at the clay jar on the wall, in a space at the row’s end that nobody called a number. Two accounts of the same days, one exact and one kept badly, and neither of them spoken aloud. The surgeon who had once read a chart with the note turned down knew the difference between a thing entered and a thing left lying, and the infant in the basket was hungry, and the hunger was the louder of the two.

A man come up from the road with a figure shut in his fist, the way a man carries a hot coal he means to set somewhere and has not decided where. “Four loaves short of what was,” he said, meaning the week and its baking and the three days of water in one breath. He looked at the women, and the women looked at the mud, and he opened his hand in the general direction of the well. The figure went out of the fist and travelled as far as a figure can travel when nobody will receive it, and arrived nowhere. The boy set it where it fell and did not enter it. He had learned in a better-lit room that a figure read aloud is not the figure paid, and here the figure was not even read aloud, only carried from a fist to a shoulder and let fall into the general air.

At the corner the broom went around two lines and took up neither. The clean dark line where the Ash ridge had turned lay where it had lain for a year and two days; the new hairline, finer than the old, lay beside it and was called thaw and would go when it went. Sella stood over both with the broom in her hand and swept neither. Under the drying racks the space beneath the lowest rack was gone around as the water had gone around it, and the broken birth-slates lying there were entered by nobody. She carried the basket past at the half-pace and did not turn her head toward the space, and the boy kept the two lines and the space under the rack on three places that did not touch.

Under the Vail kiln-house roof the carried fire lay where it had lain all week, under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees and missing one breath in every four. Four, four and a gap. The boards overhead kept four nails and four and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts did not meet, and nobody had thought to enter the place where they failed to. Nobody had said who paid for the coal that kept the fire giving, and the payer went unnamed as he had gone unnamed for seventy-eight days; warmth came into the house from somewhere the boy could neither see nor reach, and he set the payer on a place of its own and wrote no cause across it. The fire took what it was given and gave back what it could, and the gap fell in the fourth breath as regularly as a fault in a stone.

Garl Vail was kept by his salt-stiff coat on its peg, by the seat worn hollow on the left, by the clean dark line and by a proverb that went to a pot with no crack in it. Leave the crack showing. People trust a thing they can see has been handled. The proverb cost more now that there was nobody to say it in his own voice, and the house went on saying it to the pots and the fire. The debt against the firing of tiles remained neither paid nor settled nor done with and went around another year as Sella went around Ash. The boy kept the coat, the seat, the line, the new grey and the uncalled debt in five places that did not touch, and did not make a sixth of them.

Tarn had set the small bench knife on its sill before the light and told the empty yard where it was, the way he had told it every morning of six years. Six years stood in a mark worn almost flat by his thumb, and the seventh was nearly gone and still held, the one entry in this house that the house could not check. “Six counted,” he said to the kindling. “The next I don’t count.” He said it as a man says the weather, and went on holding the breath he had decided not to spend, though his chest was already tight with it. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line, and no scrubbing lifted it, and he did not scrub at it. His broom went over the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector, over and over, without once turning along it. From the Vail wheel-shed came the small sound of a pot set down and the wheel turning over, and his head did not turn, and he did not name what had come past the door. A loan that can hear is a loan that can be called; the sentence had never been said under this roof and lay under it all the same, and the two silences kept their equality without a word between them.

At the yard well in the afternoon a slate stood propped at the rack end to dry, its writing turned under, and Sella went around it as wide as she would go around a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it over. She drew with her little-door burn closing first on the rope and then on nothing, and gave the bucket its instruction before she gave it to anybody. “That’ll do,” she said to the bucket. She did not touch the widow’s mark at the well, because a well is not an opening and a basket is not a door, and when a woman asked her for a figure she said the house and told her to ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there.

Below the sill the child’s cold foot was still the cold to the woman who had wrapped it and weather to the yard, and the two facts went past each other again in the light of the afternoon and never met. The boy carried the wool and the dark foot on a place of his own with no name, no cause and no cure. He knew the extremity going before the core, the blood going back too fast and too cold, the afterdrop waiting on the far side of a warming nobody would perform; the reading worked perfectly and was worth nothing. He could have named the order of it in the tongue of the other life and had no word for it in Grathic, and the woman who had wrapped the foot went on calling it the cold while a woman passing with a bucket called the water weather, and neither altered by a word. He did not lay Sella’s not-going beside it as a cause. It was told of her that she had once sat up three nights with a child of her own who cooled while everybody was kind, and he had put that under her roof and not across the yard, beside the fold put right without a look and a woman’s not-looking and a man’s taking-himself-out-of-his-sentence, four not-lookings of one shape and not a cause.

One brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold at his ear, gone past smelling of anything weeks ago, and the empty place beside it was still unnamed. No third had come through the three days of water and none came now. He wanted the crust, wanted the breast, wanted the bucket two inches beyond the reach of his hand, and the wanting was a thing the body did without asking him. The adult who had once sorted twenty injured people in ninety seconds and chosen who would be worked on and who would be waited on lay in a basket at the edge of a well and could not lift a hand to any of the three. Cold arrived in its known order, cheek and temple and wrist, and the wool did less than the wind wanted, riding back from his wrist so the cold found the place.

Near the well a small foundling in cast-off clothes worn like a uniform stood watching the grown faces, with a gap where a front tooth had been and a bit of green glaze on a string at his neck. A kiln-hand set a pot down on the well-lip. The boy’s head went toward it before the knuckle and before the note. The child’s head went toward it half a beat after the boy’s, after the knuckle and after the note, and stopped and came back. His thumb found the green sliver and rubbed it, and he could not say why he had turned; he looked as surprised as anybody at the answer his own body had given him. Nothing passed between the two heads, and no slate was seen.

Into the file went four objects and not one joined to another: a head turned after the knock and not before it, a green sliver rubbed with a thumb, a warm loaf that had gone into a pocket holding no slate, a mouth that had opened on a sentence and had not finished it. The shape of a keeping that changes hands without a slate. He had learned in a better-lit room what three likenesses in a row will do to a man who wants an answer, and here were four, and he kept four. His own turning toward the pot lay beside the child’s and did not touch it. No second loaf was given, no secret changed hands that any clerk could read, and no betrayal was paid.

Dusk came on without a bell for it. Sella lifted the basket off the ground with her knee and carried him back up the yard at the half-pace from every open slate. At the corner she stood a moment with the broom and went around the two lines with her eye and not with the bristles, taking up neither the clean dark line nor the new grey finer than it. “Go around,” she said to the broom. “It’s an old yard. It’ll stand another winter.” The boy was carried past with one brown-edged crust stiff in the fold and the empty place beside it still unnamed, and he kept the broom’s going-around on its own place beside the fire’s four, four and a gap, and did not let either become the other.

Behind them at the well a pot was set down on the lip, and the small foundling’s head went toward it a half-beat late, and his thumb found the green sliver at his neck. The boy did not turn back to look at the turning; he carried the shape with him in the basket where the day’s other shapes were carried, and wrote no cause across any of them.

Under the roof she set him on his bed and not in the Debt Loft, put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without looking at his face, and left the thread at his mouth frozen and unwiped, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool, and the wool did less than the wind wanted. The door stood a finger’s width open. Through the gap came the river’s low muttered note, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, and through the floor came the fire, four, four and a gap, and the boy kept the two sounds in two places as he had kept them for seventy-eight days. Six hearings of the full note stood at six, three near-full answers stood advanced and not paid, the word forty lay on a place of its own with the readable double turn beside it, and one crust lay stiff in the fold with its empty place beside it, and none of these had joined and none had been spent.

His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the water and came down. The count stood at seventy-eight.

Sella Vail carried him down out of the Clay Yard at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, the basket held against her hip the way a woman carries a pot filled to the lip and does not trust the road. The mud had dried in the night into a pale skin with long shallow seams in it, and here and there a stone the water had turned lay with its wet side still dark, the way a wound shows under a scab when a hand goes looking. Corin, seventy-nine days old, went where he was carried with his cheek against the fold and his hand outside the wool. The cold had him by the ear before they reached the road. It came in its known order, cheek and temple and wrist, and the wool rode back from the wrist as though the wind had a hand there and meant to find the place.

“Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. “It’s the river wind.”

The wool did less than the wind wanted, and she did not say it to the basket. She said the house where another woman would have said I, and she kept the half-pace without seeming to keep it, the way a woman keeps a finger on the edge of a hot pot without looking at the fire.

At the Cross she set the basket down in the moot-post’s finger’s width of shade with his cheek to the cold iron, and did not look into it. Iron at the cheek is a cold with a shape, flat and without mercy, and he lay with the side of his face against it and measured it against the warm side. A reading was not an opening; the widow’s mark hung where it hung under her collar and she did not touch it, because no opening came with a figure and a figure was all this morning had to give. She stood a half-pace off with the little-door burn across the back of her hand shut on nothing, facing the square and not the basket, the way a woman faces a pot she has set down and will not be answerable for.

The square had four ways out and he counted them without deciding to: the road down to Slate Row, the steep street up to High Kiln, the north bridge, and the road that went toward the Bone Orchard. On Account-day the four ways had been choked with people leaning in; this morning they were not. The crowd stood thinner than at the flood’s reading, and the thinness was not in the figure anybody would give for it. It was in the gaps between shoulders, in the stall at the turnip heap where two women did the work of three and did not hurry, in the pig-board with a rail propped under one end where a man’s hand had been, in the space where the family from the low houses used to stand and now stood nobody. The water had taken bodies out of the market and the lean week had taken more, and what remained went on with the selling in the same voices, as though a room had lost a corner and the people in it had agreed not to look at the wall.

“There’s no Hobb at the trough,” a woman said, setting down a basket with room in it. “Nor his two. The water took the step from under them and they’ve not come back.”

“They’ll come when they come,” the other said, and went on laying out her turnips in a row that had a gap in it she did not close.

The market had a grammar for a household gone as for a step gone: it comes when it comes, and a person goes home warm on the saying. The boy kept the gap in the turnip row on its own place with the gap in the crowd and the space at the end of the loaf shelf up the yard, three absences that did not become one absence because he did not put a hand to any of them.

He had learned in a better-lit room that a chart can close over a thing left lying and still be a beautiful chart, and here the whole square was a chart closed over what the water left. Nobody counted the empty places. The house up the yard kept its days in loaves pushed along a shelf and a space at the row’s end nobody would name; the district kept its Account-day in one in twelve printed and unchallenged and the true subtraction spoken by nobody. Two arithmetics, one neat and one that would not be said, and the space between them was where the bodies had gone.

Berr Clay came up to the block at the middle of the morning. The red wool cord of his office was folded under his coat instead of worn across it, and the green of tasted glaze showed at one corner of his mouth, a small wet leaf of colour that he did not wipe away. He smelled of hot clay and of nothing at all under it, which is the most expensive smell in Grath. His hands were large and clean. He wiped his thumb on his apron before he touched nothing, and then he wiped it a second time, and only after that did he let his finger pass over the figure on the narrow slate he held against his forearm. The crowd leaned in because he never raised his voice and they had to.

“One in twelve, as the notice says,” he said, low, into the top of the thinner crowd.

He let the words lie a moment so that the households which had heard otherwise could hear they had heard wrong. Then he passed his finger over the figure once more, the way a man checks a clamp before he lets go, and put himself out of his own sentence.

“The levy is called. It is not called by me. There’ll be a slate at the watch if the house wants it again.”

He did not say I. He said the notice and the levy and the house, and took himself out of the sentence as neatly as a surgeon takes his hand out of a field before the dressing goes on. The figure stood as read. The crowd went back to the pigs.

All of this went over the top of the basket. The true subtraction went over it too, unspoken, in the space between one shoulder and the next where a man from the low houses had stood last Account-day and a woman with a wet hem had stood before the water. He lay in the moot-post’s finger’s width of shade and heard the printed figure and the true one pass on two trays that did not touch. A figure read aloud is not the figure paid. A figure let fall from a fist in the direction of a well is not entered either; he had watched one go from a fist to a shoulder and into the general air three days ago and arrive nowhere, and the yard had gone on sweeping around what the water left. The printed figure would go into a slate in a good hand and be read again next Account-day over fewer people, and the empty places would stay exactly where they were, which was in no account at all.

Berr went off toward High Kiln with the red cord folded square and the box in his house still locked and the true deduction inside it. He believed he had done the district a kindness, and there was no cruelty in his face, only the clean thumb and the green at the mouth and a good reason standing where a man should have stood. The boy kept that on its own place and wrote no cause across it, because a better-lit room had taught him what a good reason looks like when it has been worn for fifteen years.

The bell went then. It struck longer than a church bell, four, and four, and a gap where the fifth should have been, and went on ringing in the square after the strokes had stopped. Nobody crossed himself. A woman shifted a pig and a man called a price and the figures on the turnip heap stood side by side without being made to agree. The boy kept the heard bell beside the told bell from the Long Night story, and both beside the early note out of the stone and the kiln’s own missing breath under the roof up the yard, four things in four places, and made nothing of the likeness, because four likenesses in a row will grow a cause whether or not a hand puts one there.

At the Slate Row fringe of the square the Court had set its table in the open air, as it did on market-days. The clerk behind it had a cuff gone soft from washing turned back twice from his wrist and ink in two fingers of his writing hand. When a woman came to the table he put his hand to the top slate before his eyes went to her face, and said it was entered as it stood and there was nothing in it the household need correct. The boy watched the order of the reaching from the moot-post’s shade: hand, then slate, then person last. He filed it on its own tray. It was not Marra, whom he had known only as a coat and a pause at the edge of flare light in the other life; it was not the grey-fingered collector at the Drawing; and it was not the soft-cuffed clerk who sat down the road at the Fishstairs. Three hands that reached for the record before the person stood in three places on his private file and did not join. No neat dark hand showed itself in the square this morning. The pause he had seen five times in five places did not come a sixth, and he did not go looking for it.

“It’s entered as it stands,” the clerk said again to somebody who had begun to ask, and the somebody went away as though answered, which in this district is the same thing as being answered.

In the late morning Sella Vail lifted the basket off the post and carried him down the road toward the river. The road went downhill and the mud on it had set hard enough to take a heel without giving it back, and she walked as though the basket were a thing that might spill if she hurried. Below the north bridge the Fishstairs had come back out of the water four steps, four, and a landing a hand’s breadth out of line where the fourth step should have met the fifth. A whole market went up and down over the out-of-line landing carefully all morning, with skirts gathered and creels tilted, and nobody made a fall of it and nobody propped it. The boy set the landing beside the weak board in the plank walk that was gone or trodden flat under silt, and beside the hairlines in the ice that had stopped a thumb’s breadth short of the edge, three places that did not touch and had no word written across them.

The soft-cuffed clerk sat at the landing with his slate held to his forearm for a measure nobody had called. His cuff was grey from washing and turned back twice, and his hand went to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls, over and over, while the eels were sold above the top of him. Nobody had taken him off. He was not Marra, not the Drawing collector, not the Court-table clerk up at the Cross, and his order of reaching stayed in the fourth place on the tray where the boy had set it, apart from the other three. No sixth sighting of the neat dark hand came with him down the road; there was only the ordinary hand of an ordinary clerk set at a step for an account nobody had asked for, ahead of a death nobody would enter.

Mira Silt worked the eels at the outer boards below the bridge with pale slime dried on her sleeves and river silt in the hollow of her neck, her head held a little to the right against the cold in the place where the top of her left ear had been. The small smile stayed on her face the way a door is kept on the latch: it could be shut without anybody noticing the movement. She did not look into the basket when Sella set it down in the shade where no slate had reached.

A weir woman come down the steps with salt white at her wrists asked whether the run would come thin after the water had gone down.

“Thin?” Mira said. “Who’s to say thin? It comes when it comes. Ask the river.”

She flicked an eel-bone against her thumb while she decided, moved two more from hand to hand, and put one away when the deciding was done. The answer had been her own, and it had given her the room to choose which answer the woman got.

A buyer in a coat with the river still in its hem named a figure for a pair, and named it again lower, the way a man repeats a weight he hopes will be taken differently the second time. Mira’s thumb went under her apron to the grey wool cord at her waist and stayed there while she decided. Shifting a creel showed the second cord twisted twice beneath the grey, never carried upriver to Nine Pots, never lifted at a door, neither knot counted by any thumb but her own and the knot for the thin child upriver not touched. The two cords lay one above the other and did not change places.

“Two marks the pair and two short,” she said. “You’ve said it twice and I’ve counted it twice. There’s nothing in the sentence that wants an answer.”

The buyer went away with the eels. Mira put another bone away and went on with her knife. It was six years that Berr paid her two marks short of six and she gave him two eels short of six, and neither had ever put the difference into a sentence; the small war went on under the figure like a seam under a patch, held and entered nowhere. Her board lay beside the knot-string board of Orra Weir and did not touch it. One woman counted what had been taken and one counted what had been kept, and the boards lay side by side in the fish-smell and the cold with a finger’s width of air between them that no clerk had thought to enter. Her not-looking was a keeping where Berr’s not-looking at the Cross had been a pricing, and he kept the two on places that did not touch and had no word in Grathic for either.

He wanted the crust in the fold and the breast that had gone out of the world before he had a name for wanting it, and the eels went over his head in the noise of the stalls and were gone. One brown-edged crust lay stiff at his ear with the empty place beside it still unnamed, and the buyer’s figure went into the noise and was spent there, and the printed figure stood up at the Cross unchanged over a crowd that had fewer people in it to hear it.

In the late afternoon she carried him back up the road and set the basket off the post again for a moment while she settled the wool with a hand that did not go near his face. The cheek that had been against the moot-post kept its shape of cold after the iron was gone; he carried it up the Clay Yard with him as the body carries a thing pressed into it long after the pressure stops. The square behind them went on with its arithmetic in the same voices, one in twelve and the true subtraction, the heard bell and the told bell, the empty places that nobody entered, the box locked in High Kiln and the mark untouched under a collar. The printed figure and the true one lay on two trays that did not touch. The count went with him exactly, as it had gone through seventy-eight, and the house up the yard would keep its days badly in loaves and glances and a space at the row’s end that nobody called a number.

At the middle of the day Sella Vail carried him three doors down the Clay Yard at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, the basket held against her hip the way a woman carries a pot filled to the lip and does not trust the road with it. The mud had gone hard in the night and took a heel without giving it back, and the ruts stood with their edges sharp in the light, so that the yard looked less like a place that had been under water than a place that had been cut and left to dry. Corin, eighty days old, went where he was carried with his cheek against the fold and his hand outside the wool. He was not told where they were going. Nobody tells a basket anything. A pot is carried where it is carried and is not consulted, and he learned the errand the way a body learns a room — by the light, by the smell, by the thing that is in the place where a thing should be.

She had said nothing all morning that required an I. She said the house where another woman would have said I, and gave her instructions to the fire and the bucket before she gave them to anybody, and when she set the basket down nothing in it was addressed. The half-pace held without her seeming to hold it, the way a woman keeps a finger on the edge of a hot pot without looking at the fire. He knew they were going to House Silt before the door came into view, because the light off the wall of the shed went off at the third door and came back thin through a door standing ajar. He had been carried to that door before and had lain on the floor of that room while it was still a birth room and not a room with an empty bed in it.

The door stood a finger’s width open. She set the basket just inside it, at the edge of the light and away from every place a slate might lie — away from the bench where the writing would be laid, away from the flat stone, away from the shelf, away from the shadow a writing hand might fall into. It was a placement a clerk would not have noticed and a mother would have. She stood a half-pace off with the little-door burn across the back of her hand shut on nothing, facing the room and not the basket, the way a woman faces a pot she has set down and will not be answerable for. She opened no slate. She turned no errand into a walk to Slate Row. the widow’s mark at her throat hung where it hung and she did not touch it, because a room with a bed in it is not an opening and a basket is not a door.

From the low angle of the basket the room arranged itself in the order a room arranges itself for a person who cannot lift his head. The bench by the door. The pot on the bench. The draught under the door going along the floor to the corner, felt at the ankle before the cold is felt at the cheek. The brown ceiling stain above the bed, the shape of a spilt thing nobody had scrubbed out. Four nails on the near board, four on the next, and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb. He counted them without deciding to, as he had counted them in this room when the room had been a birth room, and the count had not changed and the room had.

Above the abandoned board-bed the round pewter tin kept its place. He had seen it before from this same height and had entered it nowhere. It held a scrap of cloth from a birth, folded, and the fold showed pale at the seam of the lid, and the lid had a thumb’s bright rub where somebody had worried it over years without ever lifting it. The rub was brighter than the tin around it, the way a handled thing is brighter than the shelf it stands on. A hand had gone to that lid often enough to polish it and had never once gone under it. Nobody had opened it. Nobody had said what it held beyond the scrap, and nobody said it now.

Below it the fold of grey wool lay where it had been laid on the third morning of his life, and it held only the hollow where the small pale river stone had once lain. The wool kept the shape of the stone the way a bed keeps the shape of a body that has got up and gone. He knew the hollow without being told; he had lain against the stone when it was warm and had felt it go from warm to not-warm while the women arranged the room around a child who was being given away. The warmth had crossed to him there and had not been entered against anybody, and the stone had been left behind when he was carried under another roof. Nobody said the stone was gone. The house had left the hollow where it was and left it unnamed, and the boy who had been warmed by the stone lay in a basket on the floor and could not reach the fold to put a hand into the place where it had been.

Alda Reed came short into the room with the birth-slates under her arm and the smell of soap-and-milk gone cold under river silt coming ahead of her, and under the soap the sour tang of lye. She smelled of a house where somebody else’s child had just been washed and somebody else’s floor just scrubbed. Her nails were scrubbed to the quick and still had clay under them, the clay that stays under a nail after a day of births and stays there through the scrubbing. She was built like a woman who has spent her life lifting other people’s children, with the stoop that comes from leaning over beds, and her hands were steady. When a mother grabs a wrist rather than an arm, it is these hands she grabs.

It travelled the way such word travels in that quarter, not as news but as the thing that comes after news, passed off a boat to the bank and from the bank to a woman with salt dried white at her wrists, who said it over her shoulder to a basket rather than to the child in it. Corin heard it from the low angle of the basket and made nothing of the way it was given, because a thing said over a shoulder is a thing said to nobody, and a thing said to nobody can be gone around by everybody without anybody owning it.

Sella Vail had carried him down before the light with the little-door burn across the back of her hand closing first on nothing and then on his weight. She set the basket where a pot would have been set, out of the draught that came off the water, and gave the morning’s instruction to the wool and not to the basket, keep it off his ears, it’s the river wind, and kept her half-pace from every place a slate might have lain, though there were no slates out here, only boards and salt and the rope of the traps stiff with old water. She had brought him to the Weir as she brought him to the Fishstairs, to see a trade and not be seen into, and she did not look down as the weir woman spoke. The salt at the woman’s wrists had dried in white seams that cracked when she moved her hands, the way dried slip cracks over a pot set too near the fire, and the boy noted the likeness and let it go, because a likeness is not a thing and he had learned to keep them on separate places.

“Gone, then,” the woman said. “Or trodden. Who’s to go down and see?”

Nobody answered, and the not answering went off into the morning with the rest of it.

The boy had learned early that a danger which cannot be placed is a danger nobody has to act on. The weak board had been heard of in Ilmen, heard of again in Silt, said plainly to be still there and trodden by nobody, and now said to be gone; through all of it nobody had asked which board, because a board without a place is a story, and a story can be told over a shoulder and carried on with. He had once watched a man in a better-lit room ask which vessel, which bay, which hour, and been impatient with the questions until the answers came and he saw what the questions had bought; here nobody asked them at all, and the not asking stood in the same relation to the gone board that the gone board stood to the men who might have crossed it. Two absences, one shape, and he kept them on two places rather than lay one over the other.

The boy set the gone board where he had set other absences. It lay on a place of its own beside the missing step at the Fishstairs and the hairline that had run a thumb’s breadth short of the edge, three gaps in three places that did not touch. He had no word of Grathic in which to call any of them a danger and no hand that reached two paces to point at one, and the surgeon in him read the three as he had once read three entries on the turned corner of a chart — a thing looked at, a thing looked past and a thing left lying where the next man would step — and wrote no cause across them. A blade left in its sheath is still a blade; a sheath is also a thing a whole district can walk past and call a board. He kept the reading and bought nothing with it, as he had bought nothing for eighty-three days, and the infant in him, who wanted the crust and the breast and the bucket two inches beyond his hand, won that argument as he had won every other.

By noon the whole Weir had the word in its past tense. The honeyed ice that had made the water taste of something held a moment too long, the hairlines that had stopped short of the edge, the low note the Singing Sill had given before its time — all of it was spoken of now as the thaw that was, as though a thing said after the fact were the same as a thing said before it. A man who had been certain the river would break early said it had broken early, and a man who had been certain it would break late said it had broken late, and neither remembered having been uncertain, and the argument that had held the yard a month went home warm on the word. Nobody owned the day. It had happened to the river, which was weather, and weather was what this district called the things it had not kept.

He had been awake through the days when those things had not been past tense at all but present and under argument: the taste in the water, the vein in the ice, the note that came early and was answered Weather. To hear them recalled now as the thaw that was was to hear a man describe a wound after it had closed and call the closing the whole of the story. The warmth of the telling was the warmth of an ended thing. It was the afterdrop read backwards — the recovery named and the fall that preceded it left out — and the distortion was exact and ordered and went the one way, toward a season everybody could go home warm from. He had no word for afterdrop in Grathic and no hand to put on the cold extremity of a district that had stopped feeling it; he kept the reading as he kept the rest, and it worked and bought him nothing.

The boy had kept the before and the after on two trays that did not touch. He had heard the ice called honeyed when the current ran under it in veins and had filed that beside the stopped hairline, the early note and the higher clay-heavy brook, four things in four places, and the district had folded the four into one past tense the way a hand closes over four eel-bones and comes away with one. A chart closed with a neat line and a signature at the foot does not change what the note beneath it said; it changes who has to read the note. He had learned that in a better-lit room, where the past tense had been a clean department and the note had been somebody else’s to carry, and he had no Grathic for it here and no standing to say it. He lay in the basket at the edge of the salt and kept the trays apart, and the market went up and down the steps over the out-of-line landing as though a season named after the fact were a season anybody had survived by naming.

The steps at the Fishstairs had come back four, four and a landing a hand’s breadth out of line, and a whole market went up and down them carefully without making a fall of it. A woman with a creel shifted her weight at the landing, felt the give of the board under her heel, stepped across the place where a step should have been and did not look down at it. Another followed. A man with a jar of eels set it down for a breath at the out-of-line landing, took it up again and went on, and nobody said the step was wrong, because to call it wrong was to ask who had put it right, and that was a question with a levy in it. The mud line lay along the risers where the water had stood and gone, a thin brown edge that stopped and did not go around the whole step, and the boy set it on the tray with the stopped hairlines rather than with the steps, because an edge that stops is one sort of thing and a step that a market walks over is another.

The soft-cuffed clerk sat at the landing with his slate to his forearm for a measure nobody had called. His cuff had gone grey from washing and his hand went to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls, the way a man puts a hand to a rail before he looks over the edge. Mira Silt’s board lay a few paces off; his board lay where it lay; neither was the other’s. The boy had set the clerk’s order of reaching on a tray of its own months ago, apart from the woman in the grey coat at the flare, the grey-fingered man at the Drawing and the clerk behind the Court’s table at the Cross, four hands in four places, and no sixth sighting of the neat dark hand had come to join them. The clerk did not look into any basket and did not speak, and the boy did not make a fifth of him or set him down into any woman he had seen before. A measure nobody had called was still a measure set down ahead of a death in this district; the order of things here put the slate down before the body arrived, and he kept the uncalled measure with the other things that stood ahead of a death and made nothing follow from it.

The measure sat on the landing the way a pot sits set out for a guest who has not come, and the market went around it with its creels and its jars, and the boy set the sitting measure beside the sitting pot in the Court’s room and the sitting cup across the birth room, three vessels set down ahead of bodies that had not yet arrived or had already gone, and kept the three in three houses that did not join. A clerk who sets his slate down before he looks up is not doing a cruelty; he is doing the order of his trade as the boy had once done the order of his own, hand to chart before hand to patient, and the chart had been clean and the patient had died, and the two facts had sat side by side in a room where nobody made them agree. He did not forgive the clerk the order and did not condemn him for it. He entered it and went on.

Mira worked the eels below the bridge with pale slime dried on her sleeves, silt in the hollow of her neck and her head held right where the top of her left ear had been, the small smile kept on her face the way a door is kept on the latch. The weir woman with the white wrists came down the out-of-line steps and asked whether the run would be thin now the water had gone off.

“Thin?” Mira said. “Who’s to say thin? It comes when it comes. Ask the river.” She moved two eel-bones from hand to hand while she decided whether to lie and put one away when the deciding was done, and she did not look into the basket.

When a buyer named a figure her thumb went under her apron to the grey wool cord and stayed there while she decided; shifting a creel showed the second cord twisted twice beneath it, never carried upriver to Nine Pots, never lifted at a door, neither knot counted by any thumb but her own and the knot for the thin child upriver untouched. Her board lay beside Orra Weir’s knot-string and did not touch it. The boy kept the not-looking, the bones moving hand to hand and the thumb to the cord on three places of their own: a keeping, a deciding and a record, and not one of them a cause. Mira’s not-looking was a keeping where the clerk’s not-looking at his slate was a measure and the man on the block’s had been a pricing, and he had no word in Grathic to tell one from the other and kept them apart without one. Three likenesses in a row will grow a cause whether a hand puts one there or not, and he had learned in a better-lit room what a man can make of three; so he kept the three on three tray-ends and let them stay three.

The bell came back from the Cross in the middle of the afternoon, four, four and a gap, longer than a church bell, and nobody crossed himself at it. The boy listened to it longer than he needed to, as he had listened to it through every market of his eighty-three days, because it was the only sound in the district that wanted nothing from him. He kept the heard bell beside the told bell of the Long Night story and both apart from the low note that came out of the stone when the river ran fast under its skin, and kept the told gaps with the intervals on the tray where the gaps lived. Six hearings of the full note stood at six — five from four sources and the sixth out of a person — beside and never among, and no seventh came to join them. Four things in four places, and the gap in each of them the part nobody had thought to enter. A Court instrument doing a temple’s work, and the district’s one word for the whole of it weather, and no cause written across any of it.

At dusk Sella Vail carried him up the Clay Yard at the half-pace and set him on his bed under the Vail kiln-house roof and not in the Debt Loft. In the corner the clean dark line where the Ash ridge had turned its own corner lay where it had always lain, and the new hairline of grey, finer than the old, lay beside it unswept, because the house said it was the thaw and it would go when it goes. She stood over both with a broom in her hand and swept neither, and gave the instruction to the broom and the fire and the wool and never to the basket, and did not touch the widow’s mark, because a bed is not an opening and a dusk is not an hour for one. The carried fire kept four, four and a gap under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees and missing one breath in every four, its payer still unnamed. Through the finger’s width at the door came the river’s low muttered note, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, and he kept it in its own place apart from the fire’s gap in the floor, as he had kept them apart for eighty-three days.

She said the thaw to the fire more than once that week, and the boy had stopped hearing it as an explanation and begun hearing it as a word she used the way another woman uses a hand over a child’s mouth: not to hide the noise but to keep the cold out. The new grey was finer than the old line and lay beside it without touching, and the two of them made a record no roll could take up, the one line for what had been left and the other for what had come back on its own and been left too. He had learned in this yard that a house can keep a thing by leaving it where it fell, and keep it more surely than a house that puts the thing on a shelf and writes it down; a shelf can be emptied and a slate can be turned, but a line in a corner that nobody sweeps is a line the whole household steps around, and stepping around is a form of knowing. He kept the two lines on two ends of one tray and did not lay the thaw over either.

One brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold at his ear with the empty place beside it still without a name. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the water and came down. Corin was eighty-three days old and nobody had told him so. The count crossed toward the last day of the week, unspent.

The bucket-round at the yard well had begun before the light had decided what it wanted of the day, and the rope came up stiff from the cold, keeping the shape of the hand that had hauled it. A skin lay on the water where the bucket broke it, thin as the skin on milk gone cold, and the women at the tubs spoke of that skin to the bucket and not to the child in the basket. Keep it off his ears. The instruction went to the wool and the wind, and Corin, eighty-four days old, lay in the lee of the well’s little roof with the fold turned right under his chin and was not part of the sentence. Sella Vail had carried him down before the light at the half-pace from every open slate, going wide around one propped at the rack end as though it were a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and she had not looked into the basket since. She said the house where another woman would say I, and gave every instruction to the fire, the wool and the bucket and never to the basket. A basket set down is a pot set down and left; that was the whole of the rule, and he had learned to lie under it without expecting to be asked.

A second man came down the road from the north with road-mud dried grey on his boots to the calf and the salt-and-hot-clay smell of the east road on him, a smell that opened the yard’s nostrils the way a door opens a warm room. He was not one of theirs. He waited his turn at the bucket with a bag across one shoulder and the patience of a man who has walked far enough to know a queue when he sees one, and when the bucket came up he held it a moment before he drank, as a man who has come that far does not put his mouth to water without looking at it first. He was not the man who had stood here in Ilmen with the same smell on him and the same mud; that one had gone back down the road with his bag and his story and had not been seen again. This one had the same walk and a different set to his shoulders, and the yard looked at him and at his mud and went on with its buckets.

“You’ll have come from Greybank,” a woman at the tubs said.

“From east of it,” he said. “Near eighteen leagues. The road’s a pot that’s boiled over since the thaw.”

He drank and wiped his mouth with the back of a hand that had carried a load, and then he told them, because a man who has walked that far owes the yard a thing to say for the loan of its water. He told it the way such things are told so that nobody has to believe them, with a date set into it like a nail driven to hold a board.

“There was a counting room shut up in the last war,” he said. “East of us, in a house I could name if I had a mind to. Shut for the duration, they said, with the men inside it keeping the accounts. They closed the damper to save the coals —” and here he drew air in through his nose and let it go against his teeth, the small sound of iron shut on iron, a thing he had no word for in his own mouth “— and the men in the room died of the breath. Not the war’s hand. Their own heat went out of the room and there was no way to feed it. That was a year two wars back, before your folk counted it.”

The boy watched the man’s hands as he drank. Broad knuckles, a pale line where a strap had worried the palm, nails grey with road-dust: the hands of a man who had carried a load and meant to carry another. He held the bucket with both hands and would not let it rest on the lip, as though water set down were water lost. A pulse beat in his throat, slow and steady, and the breath came short through a nose caked with clay dust. Nothing about him was dangerous. There had been a night when the boy sorted twenty bodies in ninety breaths by just such signs — the grip, the breath, the colour under the nails — and got every one right; here he could not sort one stranger out of a queue or tap his chest and ask him to say it again. The skill had not left him. It had lost its handle. He had watched another man tell this same story over this same well in Ilmen, and had kept that man’s hands and that man’s pause in a place of their own. Now there were two men and one story, and the second reading went where the first had gone, without either being laid over the other.

The yard took it as it had taken it once before, as a thing that had happened eighteen leagues east and was therefore nobody’s to own. A woman with her sleeves in the wash said the war did for them, which settled it. A man at the racks said a room shut up was a room nobody had entered on any slate and the men in it had died of being uncounted, a joke that cost nothing because the dead could not hear it. Somebody said the frost would take the pits early at this rate, and somebody else said the pits had been worked early already and the colour of the water would tell you. The thaw took the argument after that: early, said one, and late, said another, and the man who wanted it late said a longer week of a man’s own fire was worth having, and the woman who wanted it early agreed with him so entirely that there was nowhere for an argument to put its foot, and they went home warm on the word. Nobody said kiln. Nobody said Vail. Nobody looked toward the chimney across the yard standing with its breath held under blind ash. The story went around the bucket and came back as an old curiosity with a date, and the yard took up its buckets where it had left them.

Sella heard all of it, as a woman who keeps a fire hears every word said about heat, and made nothing of it. She told the fire it was the thaw and told it to be fed. “Cold in the east,” she said to the coals that were not there, and then, to the bucket: “Easy. There’s a skin on it.” She had lost a husband to the thing the man had just described and had never once described it that way herself. The house kept its own low word for it, a word that named the danger and the mercy in one breath, and she had said that word years ago to a boy at a kiln mouth and warned him away, and did not say it now. A room shut up eighteen leagues east was a room shut up eighteen leagues east; the mouth that told it had mud on its boots and no reason to lie, and no reason to be heard either. If she would have put anything away she would have put it where she put the rest of it, in the private roll nobody was shown. There was nothing to put away. She touched the widow’s mark at no hour of this day, because a well is not an opening and a basket is not an opening, and a story told over a bucket is not one either.

The boy heard the breath and the damper where the yard heard a date. There was no word in Grathic that held either thing the way the man held it, not for a room, not for the stopping of air in a room, and the two phrases lay in the basket beside him like tools with no handle. He knew the room before the man had done describing it, having lain awake under a roof and counted the kiln’s breath in the dark — four low pulls and a silence where the fifth should have been, four and a gap, night after night. He knew the anatomy of it as he knew a wound that closes over a cavity and seals what is in it: a vessel with no way to feed what it holds, the contents cooking their own heat out of the air, the face arranging itself in the ordinary way at the end. He had once been the man who wrote down how such a room would finish, in a calm voice, and gone on to the next body. The register did its work and said nothing of where it had learned it. Here there was no word at all, and no room to put one, and no hand to open a door if there had been one.

He filed the second telling on the same tray as the first. The first man’s telling lay where it had been set down on Ilmen 26, with the small sound of iron closing on iron in it and a year two wars back pinned to it like a label; this man’s telling went beside it with the same facts in it and the same date loosely kept, and the two did not touch. He had noticed that the second man drew his breath in through his nose where the first had made the sound in his throat, and that both had said the words not the war’s hand, and he kept the likeness and the difference on the same tray without letting either become the other. Beside that tray, in four places that did not touch, he kept the bell told in the dark of the Long Night, four, four and a gap; the bell heard in the square at the Cross, four, four and a gap with nobody crossing himself; the early note out of the stone at the Singing Sill; and the breath of Vail kiln-house itself, four low draws and a missing fifth, warming the boards by degrees under blind ash. Four low things in four places. The river’s own note of water going over water was a fifth thing on a place of its own and not a hearing at all. The telling of a shut room had come twice into this yard and had stayed a story with a date both times, and he wrote no cause across any of them, because a likeness laid on a likeness will grow a cause whether or not a hand puts one there, and he had learned in a better-lit room what a man can make of two tellings of the same thing. He kept them four. The tray did not become a cause. It was the whole of the work available to him and it bought him nothing he could lift a hand to.
