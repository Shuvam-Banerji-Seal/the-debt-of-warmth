The Number That Did Not Know

Days 309–311 · Bone 10–12, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, Sill

The slate is read twice and the account stands once. Four readings in a year are four, and a rule made out of them is a thing with nobody standing under it to pay. The grain lies covered under wool and the number that goes over it does not know what it cost.

— A saying of Sill, told under the moot-post at Bone’s first reading

— Day 309 —

Bone 10, Tenthday, Y.H. 813

The hand lay against the fold the way a tool is laid down where the next hand will find it without looking, and under it the hundred and ninety-second answer kept its place and asked nothing of the dark. The boy had set it there in the small hours and had thought about it no more than a man thinks about the last instrument on a tray once the list is finished and the tray wiped; there was no reward in the keeping and no payer named to it, and he had stopped looking for one a long way back. Three hundred and eight days stood behind him, exact, complete, unspent, spoken by nobody in Low Kiln, Sill or Grath; and here under the Vail kiln-house roof the count went to three hundred and nine. Six months and one hundred and twenty-nine days were told by nobody. The house kept its days in loaves and glances and the space where a loaf should have stood, so the number lay in him alone, carried in the mouth the way a surgeon carries staples between the lips, always there, almost never spat out.

Under the floor the carried fire gave four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have stood. Its payer had been unnamed for three hundred and nine days and nobody lifted the floor to find one: the fire answered as it always answered, four and a gap, and the gap stayed a gap. Garl Vail’s name had not been spoken beneath this roof the same number of days. He was kept in objects and not in a story — the salt-stiff coat with the crease at the left shoulder on its peg behind the door, the wheel-seat keeping its twenty-year lean and the hollow nobody would fill, the clean dark line at the corner with the finer grey beside it called the thaw, unswept both. The keeping of him was maintenance, and no lesson had been made of it in nine days or in three hundred.

This was the tenth of Bone, the month’s first Account-day, and the reading and the market and the bell belonged to it by right. They stood ahead of the small hours like a pot set on a high shelf, and he did not reach for what the morning had not yet earned. The day would earn them in its own time: the Cross would hold its crowd when the light came and the bell would go over the square after it, and neither was brought into the dark to keep a boy company. Bone had come on without a boundary drawn. The older women of the district named the months by what the weather did, the slatemen by the count of Account-days gone into them, and the two habits lay side by side, neither set right nor left wrong in so many words. Nothing from the nine days behind had followed him in as a sign, a lesson, a mending or a payment; the yard’s small keepings were left where the yard had put them, and a day that has not earned a thing does not get to keep it.

Then the body, taken in the old order. One stiff leathery brown-edged barley crust lay two inches from his ear. It had come out of a woman’s hand in another house and had gone from warm to cold without anybody saying so, and now it kept the shape of a thing that had once been bread and had long since stopped being food. Beside it the empty place kept its name to itself, and no third had come into the nine days of Bone to make the row come out even; he did not ask one to. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the dry heat and was left unwiped. No one under any roof had ever given an instruction about the thread; a basket set down was a pot set down and left, and the instruction went to the wool and the wind and never to the basket, and the thread lay where it lay. Cold had stopped at cheek, temple and the small bare joint of the wrist where the wool had ridden back, as though it had come to the end of a line written out for it and had neither an order to go further nor an order to go away. The Afterdrop waited beyond all three, past a warming nobody in this house would perform.

He knew what the ease on a cold face meant. It was not ease. A cold patient could not clot and would seem better before he was worse, and had to be warmed from the outside and slowly — never hurried, never rubbed, never set too near the fire. He had once said such things to people who wrote them down, and had thought nothing of saying them. He wanted the crust lifted the two inches to his mouth and the thread wiped away, and he could not close two inches on the pot handle somebody had set beyond his reaching hand. Both were true. The second was louder. The surgeon took the inventory in its old order — crust, unnamed place, thread, cold, the waiting beyond — and entered it nowhere, not even on the place where he kept the things he did not enter; and the infant underneath wanted, and the wanting had no words and did not need any, and the two voices argued without either winning, which was the work and not a virtue.

Three counts stood in three places and none rose into another. Under the floor the carried fire kept its four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have stood; under the finger’s-width door the river went water over water, a low thing over a lower thing, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming and never numbered among the hearings; overhead the boards kept four nails on the near board, four on the next, and a thumb-width knot where a fifth nail should have stood. Four and a gap, water over water, four and a knot: three counts, and agreeing any two would have wanted a payer put under both, and no payer stood to any of them. Six hearings of the full note stood beside and never among, five from four sources and the sixth — the small flat knock out of as the men lifted her — filed where a thing out of a person must go and never with the things that came out of vessels; the seventh place kept its emptiness and none was invented to fill it. The bell he had heard on the last market of Frost, four strokes and four and a gap with not one hand in two hundred going to a face, stayed in its square beside the told bell of the Long Night. It had not been heard again and had not been lent an ear it had not earned.

Sella Vail went to the fire before the basket. That was the grammar of the morning under this roof and had been the grammar of a thousand mornings: the coals told it was the thaw when the sour mineral breath came off the unswept corner, and turned with the back of a broken pot, and given the instruction before anybody gave it to a person. “The house will keep it,” she said to the fire, where another woman would have said I. She was a broad woman with forearms the colour of dried slip and a burn across the back of the left hand shaped like a little door — two straight sides, a lintel and a threshold — and the burn shut on nothing as she worked, then closed on the pot’s weight when she lifted it. Her hair was coal-black with one grey streak at the temple that she had said came from the winter Garl died and would say nothing more about; she pushed it back with the wrist that carried the burn, and the streak showed pale in the low light.

She kept her finger on top of the loaf shelf’s front edge while she worked, so the charcoal tally beneath stayed unread, and she moved her lips along a row standing with two gaps and figured neither gap. Her eye went once to the clay jar standing cold under lead glaze on the wall shelf, and her hand shut half over nothing and did not touch it. The widow’s mark hung where it hung on its cord at her throat, a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and she did not touch it: a market-day morning under this roof was not an opening, and five openings in five months were five and not a rule. The hand went to the mark before the kiln and at no other hour, and this hour was a morning, a hearth and a pot, none of them an opening.

The twice-mended pot stood on the pot board with the hairline of the old crack showing dark between the staples. She put her thumb first where the wire crossed the crack, felt what the thumb had to feel, and only then let the weight come on the handle; and she said both sayings to the pot and the fire and never to a person — If it’s worth mending it’s worth mending twice. The second time is the real one and Leave the crack showing. People trust a thing they can see has been handled. The sayings had improved in the keeping and were very likely not as good as the man had said them. She set the rim two inches beyond the reaching hand with its handle turned toward it, and there was a half-beat between the setting and her going away, and she did not look toward the bed. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without a look at his face, and left the thread where it lay.

Out at the kindling Tarn had the small knife at the very edge of its sill, where the house could check it and where it could slip. Six years stood in the worn thumb-mark where the house could check, and the seventh stood at the lip beyond the mark where it could not, and Garl’s unforged collector sentence lay beside it with no name on the man who had turned back at the frozen ford and no figure on the debt. Six counted. The next I don’t count, he told the wood in the tone of a man stating the weather, and put the knife back where it could be checked, announcing it to nobody because the yard had already been told. He called everyone house, even her, and said I owe where another man would say I am; he had said Yes, house to the early thaw and the late and had kept wanting the late because it puts off the paying, though the paying had stood in the ruts since the water went.

The sentence about an ear under this roof being worth more than the debt that held it came up to the word that would call the loan and stood there. He did not say the word. It came as a remembered ninth stopping with no tenth before the seventh year at its very end and still ungiven, and the breath he drew at the top of the reach did not go in and was not spent; the Seed 16 breath stayed unspent with it. His chest had been tight since the dark. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line and no scrubbing lifted it, and the pale clip-ring at his left wrist, in place since Y.H. 806, held what little light there was and gave it back. Up in the Debt Loft, which reached the boy only as a sound with a direction, the stone she had given him in the winter Garl died lay cold in its unmarked pot, and he did not reconcile it with the debt and did not try, which is not forgiveness. The loan stayed uncalled, and no collector came up the clay road or went down it.

At the edge of the Vail wheel-shed Jorra Vail came through the finger’s-width door with the day’s meal-fine grey slate dust ground small as meal past the bent little finger and under the nails, white in the creases where a fist had opened, and not bone-coloured slip. He kept dusty forearms and clean ones on one tray and parted them as a surgeon parts clot from vessel, asking neither which slate had broken nor whether it had been a birth-slate. He spoke to the wheel before he spoke to any person. “It doesn’t care whose hands. It cares if they’re steady,” he said, and told a pot “You’ll do” in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either. The men who had gone low into the back room before first grey had no name from him and the room was not called cold; to the boy it was a direction and a sound.

He knew the full note that pot would give three turns before it left his hands, with air under it and a want in it, and withheld the knowing on the near-full tray beside the hearings and never among them; he would not say it before a kiln-master come up from High Kiln who would have called it early and made something of it. Before Sella had finished telling him the fire was low his bent finger went into the cold iron of the damper, moved the tongue a hair’s breadth and left it standing, so that the end of her instruction arrived at the pot; the boy read one piece of iron with two bites, the holding and the taking, with no Grathic word for both mercies, joined to no kiln and entered in no slate. The burned slate stood at five tellings and he put a finger between the fifth and the four and supplied no sixth; the two tellings of the counting room shut in the war stood at two and had not gained a third; the three pot sayings stood with the Smoke 14 note on a place of its own and no fourth likeness made a cause; the row stood at five askings with one shaping and no witness. He’s not Vail stayed sealed as tightly as on the hour it was spoken, and the promise of the foundling’s name to the men who burn slates was neither given nor withdrawn.

In the small hours, with the first light beginning to come under the finger’s-width door and find the edges of things, he turned out the file as he had once turned instruments out on the towel at the end of a list, laying each thing on its own place and asking nothing of any of them. The held interval lay where it had been laid; the empty interval lay empty; the missing fifth under the floor was missing and nobody had supplied it; the two shelf gaps stood unfigured; the ninth hand stood nine with the strip in a sleeve and no tenth; the fifth telling stood at five with the finger between it and the four. Nothing had been spent to make a row come out even. Nothing had stood up out of its place as a reach, a hearing, a seventh, a stopping, a payment, a held change, a key, a power, a freedom, a gift, a doctrine or a rule.

The told figure stood beside the paid figure with no signature on the space between, and Frost’s three readings with their three true subtractions rode locked up the clay road in Berr Clay’s box toward High Kiln, one in twelve printed and unchallenged; three readings were three and not one account, and the finding sat on a place of its own with no payer under it, not a lesson, not a grievance, not a mending, not a doctrine, and not laid across the house’s not-doings, the dressed grain lying covered under wool at the rack end, or his own kept gaps. The edges nearly touched and the finger went between. A finding with no payer is a thing a man can carry a long way without its becoming a rule.

The hand outside the wool came up an inch toward the river’s note and took a small intake from the room whose floor does not touch his. He let it go whole on the same low note, a restraint and not a payment, borrowing no house count and supplying no payer, carrying it into neither the fire’s missing fifth, the bell’s gap left behind in the square nor the river’s low over lower. The hundred and ninety-third answer was exact, complete and worth nothing at two inches of air; it did not stand up as a reach, a hearing, a seventh, a stopping, a payment, a held change, a key, a power, a freedom, a gift, a doctrine or a rule, and no payer was named to it. The older man took a clean satisfaction in the keeping of the number; the infant wanted the crust lifted the two inches to his mouth and the thread wiped away; both were true and the second was louder, and neither won, and the argument was the work. The hand came down on the river’s note as it always did and laid itself back against the fold as a man puts away a tool where the next hand will find it without looking.

Three hundred and nine stood exact, complete, unspent and spoken by nobody in Low Kiln, Sill and Grath; six months and one hundred and twenty-nine days were told by nobody; the carried fire’s fifth was unpaid the same number of days with its payer unnamed, and Garl Vail’s name had not been spoken under the roof the same number. One hundred and ninety-three honest answers stood on the sixth place, each an object and not a power, and neither the number nor the small hand-movings that made it stood up and called themselves a doctrine. The file lay open on the towel at the head of the bed with every gap left gap, the hand laid back against the fold on the one hundred and ninety-third answer, and the morning light came under the finger’s-width door without anybody having said what the day would read.

Sella Vail carried him down from the Vail kiln-house in the last of the morning with the fold drawn over his ears against the river wind, and the instruction went to the wool and to the wind and never to the basket. One stiff leathery brown-edged barley crust lay two inches from his ear where it had lain since first light; the thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the dry heat and was unwiped, because nobody under any roof had ever been given an instruction about the thread; cold had come to cheek and temple and the small bare joint of the wrist where the wool had ridden back, and had stopped there as at the end of a line written out for it. The Afterdrop waited beyond all three, past a warming nobody in this house would perform. He wanted the crust lifted and the thread wiped and could close two inches on nothing, and the wanting went on underneath the inventory without once interrupting it.

The square came to him the way a room comes to a man who steps into it in the dark: by the small sounds first. A cup was turned upon a board and set back. A pig grunted. A slate went down on a rail with the flat wooden sound of a thing laid where somebody meant to find it again. Above all of it the moot kept up its low arguing, never loud, the way people arguing about arithmetic are never loud, because the arithmetic is the part they are afraid of. The small sounds did not lie. The large ones could be made to say one in twelve as easily as one in three, but a cup set down was a cup set down, and a man’s voice with a number in it was neither.

At the Cross she set the basket in the moot-post’s finger’s width of shade where no slate lay, with his cheek turned against the cold iron, and stood half a pace off. She gave the instruction to the wool and to the river wind and gave none to the basket, and she kept him out of the reach of every open slate that came near the rail. A man with a slate under his arm passed and did not look in. A woman with a market basket passed and looked at the iron rather than at what the shade held. An assessor’s boy came down the steps with a heap of slates and turned them writing-down, and the shade was a finger’s width and held exactly what a finger’s width of shade will hold, which was the boy and nothing else.

Two hundred leaned in over the reading block, their backs making a wall with a gap in it that opened and shut as people shifted. Behind them eight pigs went over the wet boards, driven with a stick that caught the mud and let it go with a small sucking sound; a Sill assessor wrote the eight down in a slate before anybody could ask which eight he meant, his hand arriving at the writing before his eyes arrived at the rail. A printed notice was square on a post at the block’s edge, soft at one corner where many thumbs had worried it, and the soft corner was the only part of the notice anybody could have read without being told what it said. A number went from a fist to a mouth to a shoulder in the crowd and did not arrive anywhere; the figures stood side by side on a heap of turnips and nobody made them agree. Somebody’s hand put a slate down near the basket’s shade and took it up again, and the boy kept his finger between the shade and the slate without either becoming a place the other occupied.

Berr Clay came up short off the clay road. The red wool cord of his office was folded square under his coat instead of worn across it, and the green of tasted lead glaze stood at one corner of his mouth; under the hot clay that came off him was the smell of nothing at all, the most expensive smell in Grath. His hands were large and clean. He wiped his thumb twice on his apron before touching nothing, once for the slate and once for the figure that would be on it, and held a narrow slate unopened to his forearm with the writing inward where no goer-by could take a figure off it. He passed a finger over the figure and let his eyes rest on the writing a breath longer than the writing required, the way a man checks a dressing he has already set and knows he will not have to redo.

“One in twelve, as the notice says,” he read low into the top of the two hundred. The figure went twice over the basket, once for the moot and once for the households that had come late, and neither of those households was standing under the figure when it went over them. He called the levy and denied it with “The levy is called. It is not called by me,” and to a man who had brought a number up out of a fist he said “your house,” never I. He took himself out of his own sentence as a surgeon takes a hand out of a field before the dressing goes on, leaving the field with nobody’s fingers in it and the dressing neat and the patient none the wiser. The boy had once done that himself in a room with white lights and a chart turned down, and had thought nothing of it. Here the grammar had a green mouth and a clean apron, and went on standing where it stood as if a sentence with nobody in it were a sentence that had been honestly spoken.

The two hundred went back to the pigs. The assessor’s eight went into a slate with the ninth still unclaimed and nobody claiming it. The printed figure stayed on its post with its soft corner, and the man who had brought a number up out of his fist put the fist back in his sleeve and went off toward the turnips, and the turnips remained turnips with the figures standing beside them unjoined. Berr went up the clay road with the cord folded and the narrow slate under his arm and the box locked behind him, Bone’s first true subtraction going up inside it beside the three from Frost, believing he had done a kindness.

The small sounds came back first when the reading was over. A pig shook itself and the mud came off with a slap. A woman set a pot down on the edge of the rail and the pot gave its note and held it, and the boy’s head went toward it a degree before the knuckle arrived to check what it was: the note was whole, and a whole note was the sort that could be trusted to tell the truth about a vessel, and he filed nothing because nothing had been broken. A goer-by with a slate under his arm came too near the shade and Sella went half a pace around him the way she went around a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and came back to her place without a word and without looking toward the basket. The instruction had gone to the wool and the wind and never to the basket, and the shade kept what the shade held. Cold iron at his cheek drew the heat out of the skin in a slow even line, and he let it, because two inches of air were two inches of air whether the air was a pot handle or the side of a moot-post, and the crust lay where it lay. Three hundred and nine days stood in him exact and complete and unspent, and nobody in the two hundred could have told him what the number counted if they had tried, which they did not.

The printed figure stood unchallenged. The figure read was still not the figure paid, and no signature stood on the space between them. Four arithmetics of the year now stood behind that one printed figure — the three of Frost locked in the box and Bone’s first going up the road behind them — and four were four. The district’s arithmetic had changed for the month in a way that could not be changed back, and the change was done to the square and not to the basket: nothing in the shade had moved, and the crust lay two inches off and the thread lay frozen and the cold had stopped where it had stopped. A rule made out of four readings wanting a payer put under all four, and no payer stood under any of them. A doctrine was an account that had found a payer; this account had found nobody, and so the finding sat on a place of its own with no payer under it, not a lesson, not a grievance, not a mending, not a doctrine, and not laid across the house’s not-doings, the dressed grain lying covered under wool at the rack end, the four held changes on their four places, or his own kept gaps. The edges came near to touching and the finger went between. He had carried such findings a long way already without any of them turning into a rule, and a finding with no payer can be carried a long way further.

At the rack end at home the dressed grain lay covered under wool away from the hearth, turned in these mornings with a progressively lighter touch and given no figure. The arithmetic that had just gone over the square went over that covered grain as well, being the kind of number that travels without a hand to put it down, and it did not know what the grain had cost. It answered nothing for the man carried out on Day 188, whose cost went into no slate and whose name stood in no figure; it answered nothing for the hand that had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come back out of the worked strip; it answered nothing for the hare gone off toward the north gate road, which had been answered with the weather. Three absences and a covered heap of grain, and the number that passed over them did not know the weight of any of them. The boy set the unknowing on its own place and did not make a cause of it, because a cause wants a payer standing somewhere and none stood here either.

The bell went over the square after the reading, longer than a church bell, and the boy counted it as he had counted it at Frost’s last market: four strokes, four strokes, and a place where the fifth should have stood. Not one hand in two hundred went to a face. The gap had a shape in the body, an inch held over nothing with the breath kept whole against it, and he kept the shape and did not fill it. He held his inch through the stroke that did not come and came down on the stroke after it, supplying no stroke to fill the gap, making the gap neither a seventh hearing of the full note nor a fifth pull of the carried fire nor an agreement with the boards’ thumb-width knot. Three counts in three places and none rose into another, for agreeing any two would have wanted a payer put under both and none stood to either. The heard bell stayed in its square beside the told bell of the Long Night, and the square went on with its pigs.

Before noon Hessa Rale went up the clay road toward Greybank with Berr Clay’s narrow slate held edge-on to her forearm and the writing turned to her sleeve. The second strap was let out for the long road and the dried pale scurf of mud stood unbrushed on the skirt she had shortened for it; her thumb lay along the grain of the frame and nothing pressed where the writing was. Her hand arrived at the slate before her eyes arrived at the crowd, and the small flat correction of the strap and the turn of the wrist brought the edge round as she walked. To the women who asked what she carried she said “I don’t read it. I carry it. There’s a difference, and the road doesn’t care which.” To the one who wanted more she said “Cross to Greybank. Reading goes up before noon. I’ve got a road to walk,” answering the question put and not the one behind it, without rudeness and without apology. No figure came off the slate. The boy filed the order of reaching once on a place of its own and did not file it twice; her hand stayed the eighth such hand in the district, eight and not a rule, joined to none.

Below the north bridge the soft-cuffed Fishstairs clerk stayed set at the out-of-line landing where a fifth step should have stood, for a measure nobody had called. His cuff was grey from washing and grey again by midday, and the slate lay against his forearm with the writing inward where no goer-by could take a figure off it. His hand went to the slate before his eyes found the man who had said the buyer’s number, and the number went in before the looking, and he did not rise out of the market when it sold over him. It sold over him the way water goes over a stone that lies where a stone lies; the stone keeps its place and the water keeps its own, and neither is entered to the other. The boy filed that order apart from Marra, from the Drawing collector with his grey fingertips, from the clerk at the Court’s table, from Berr’s twice-wiped thumb and from Hessa’s edge-on slate. Five record-first hands on five places with a finger between every pair, and no sixth likeness stood up and called itself a cause.

Across the square at the pot board a woman stood saying nothing with clay dried pale under her nails in the small crescents and a pale line across the back of one hand where something had once been worn tight and long. The sweetish green of lead glaze lay in the crease beside her lip. She turned a cup a quarter without lifting it and set it back exactly where it had stood, and went on looking at the pots. The boy called the colour green and nothing else. It was the seventh green at a mouth and there was no eighth of it: no name, no slate, no sale, no hearing and no cause. At the market’s edge a small shape stood in cast-off clothes worn the way a uniform is worn by a boy who was never measured for one, a man’s shoulders set on a child’s frame, breeches cut off at the knee, silt dried pale in the creases of the wrists, a gap where a front tooth had been. A green sliver of lead glaze on a string at his throat caught the light as he turned and went dull again as he turned back. A pot was set down nearby with a small flat sound, and the small shape turned half a beat after the knock, not before it. That half-beat was a turn after a sound and nothing more, filed on its own place beside the boy’s own turning toward such sounds and not joined to it. The seventh shape stayed a shape. Neither was named, neither was spoken to, neither spoke, and no loaf, slate, sale, hearing, cause or romance came off either of them across the whole length of the reading.

The fifth telling of the burned slate went through the reading untouched, as it had come up from Slate Row on the fifth of Bone: a word on the same breath as the price of barley and the weather, with no owner standing under it, no room, no hand at the flame and no reason. The reading changed the district’s arithmetic for the month and did not touch that word. No sixth voice came up over the boards, no witness stood to be asked, no hand was named at any flame, and no payer was put beneath it. The claim went on gathering interest against Jorra Vail’s promise to the men who burn slates, the promise neither given nor withdrawn, while Jorra under the roof neither paid nor refused and said nothing of the word at all. He’s not Vail stayed sealed. The boy kept the fifth telling on its own place with a finger between it and the four, five tellings and not five witnesses to one fire, and made no sixth due on a day that had not earned one.

Bone 11, Firstday, Y.H. 813

The small hours under the Vail kiln-house roof went as small hours go in a house that keeps its warmth by never speaking of it: the fire gave what it gave under the floor and said nothing of what it did not, and the boy lay on the bed at the low angle of the loaf shelf with his face turned into the fold and one crust lying two inches from his ear. The room smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and, under them, the sour mineral breath of the Ash ridge along the skirting board that had not been swept in two years. A clay jar stood cold on the shelf by the wall under its skin of lead glaze; the oiled paper at the window let the dark through in a grey square. He had come into his three hundred and tenth day and nobody in Low Kiln, Sill or Grath had said so. Six months and one hundred and thirty days were told by nobody. Three hundred and ten stood in him exact and complete and unspent, the count going over from three hundred and nine as quietly as water goes over water under the finger’s-width door, and under the floor the carried fire answered four low pulls and kept the silence where the fifth should have stood, its payer unnamed three hundred and ten days. Garl Vail’s name had not been spoken under this roof the same number. Firstday came on without a market in it and without a bell, and brought nothing in with it: the day came the way a pot comes to the wheel, on its own weight, with no hand lifting yesterday into it.

The body was taken in its old order, the way a wound is taken in the order its parts arrive, because the order was the only honesty available. One stiff brown-edged barley crust lay two inches from his ear, gone leathery in the dry heat since the morning behind, and the empty place beside it kept its name to itself; no third had come to make the row come out even, and a row of one is a row nobody has to answer for. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen where it lay and was unwiped, because nobody under any roof had ever given an instruction about the thread and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. Cold had come to cheek and temple and the small bare joint of the wrist where the wool had ridden back, and had stopped there as at the end of a line written out for it, with neither an order to go further nor an order to go away. The Afterdrop waited beyond all three, past a warming nobody in this house would perform.

He knew the composed face that was not composure, the ease that cold puts on a face which is not ease, the patient who will seem better before he is worse because a cold patient cannot clot; he knew a man must be warmed from the outside and slowly, never hurried, never rubbed, never set too near the fire. The knowing bought nothing at two inches of air. He wanted the crust lifted to his mouth and the thread wiped from his lip and could close two inches on nothing — not on the pot handle set beyond his reach, not on the wool folded under his chin. Both were true and the second was louder. The older man took the inventory; the infant kept the want; neither won, and the argument between them was the work and not a virtue. He had once reduced a shoulder in the dark by feel alone and now could not lift a crust to his mouth; the thought came and went without bitterness, which was its own small economy.

The room came to him as a field comes to a man checking it before the light: by the small sounds first, because the small sounds do not lie. Under the floor the carried fire gave its four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have stood, and nobody lifted the floor and nobody supplied the pull. Through the finger’s-width door the river kept water over water, a low thing over a lower thing, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, and put no number to itself. Overhead the boards kept four nails on the near board, four on the next and a thumb-width knot where a fifth nail might have been driven and was not. Three counts in three places, and none rose into another, because agreeing any two would have wanted a payer put under both and none stood to either. The house kept itself by its not-doings — by the coals left at one hour and turned at another, by the shelf’s front edge with a finger kept on top of it, by the two gaps in a row that nobody figured — and the boy kept his finger between those omissions and his own kept gaps, and did not lay one across the other, because the edges nearly touched and the finger was the whole of the work.

Sella Vail went to the hearth before she went to the basket, as she had gone every morning of this month and every morning of the months behind it. The instruction went to the wool and to the pot board and to the broom and to the running water, and never to the basket. She told the coals it was the thaw and turned them with the back of a broken pot, and said The house will keep it where another woman would have said I, and stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand counting something under her breath toward no opening — small intake, hold, letting-go — and gave neither gap a figure. She pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the burn, and the one grey streak at her temple showed pale in the first of the light. The widow’s mark hung where it hung on its cord at her throat, a thumb-length of clay under lead glaze with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and she did not touch it, because a morning and a hearth are not openings and the hand goes to the mark before the kiln and at no other hour. Five openings in five months were five and not a rule; the next stood far off in a later month’s first firing, not hoped for and not counted toward.

On the pot board the pot stood with its handle mended twice, the older brass staple and the tighter wire crossing the crack with the crack left showing dark between them; she put her thumb first where the wire crossed the crack, felt what the thumb had to feel, and only then let the weight come on the handle. Leave the crack showing. People trust a thing they can see has been handled, she said to the pot, and If it’s worth mending it’s worth mending twice. The second time is the real one, she said to the fire, and to neither did she say whose crack it was or who had paid for the mending. The sayings had improved in the keeping and were very likely not as good as the man had said them. She set the pot rim two inches beyond the reaching hand with a half-beat and no look toward the bed, put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without a look at his face, and left the thread where it lay. The little-door burn on the back of her hand shut on nothing as she went away.

At the rack end the dressed grain lay under wool away from the hearth, and she turned it beneath the wool with a lighter touch than the day before and gave no figure to it, because the grain had a weight and the weight had no number standing under it. A number had gone over that covered grain on the day behind without knowing what it cost; it had answered nothing for the man carried out on Day 188, whose cost went into no slate and whose name stood in no figure; it had answered nothing for the hand that had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out of the worked strips; it had answered nothing for the hare gone off toward the north gate road, which had been answered with the weather. The boy kept the unknowing on a place of its own with no payer under it, not a lesson, not a grievance, not a mending, not a doctrine, and did not lay it across the house’s not-doings or the returned half-check standing on the flagstones in its own place among four held changes or his own kept gaps. The edges came near to touching and the finger went between. Four readings were four and not one account, and a rule made out of them wanted a payer standing under all four, and none stood; the printed figure stood unchallenged up the clay road with no signature on the space between figure and price, and a finding with no payer can be carried a long way without becoming a rule.

Tarn was at the kindling when Sella carried the basket past on her way to the rack, the fold drawn over his ears against the river wind, and the instruction went to the wool and to the wind and never to the basket. The small knife lay at the very edge of its sill where it could be checked and where it could slip, and six years stood in the worn thumb-mark where the house could check and the seventh at the lip beyond the mark where it could not. Beside the knife lay Garl’s unforged collector sentence — the man sent for a collector who had turned back at the frozen ford — with no name on the man who turned and no figure on the debt. Six counted. The next I don’t count, he told the wood, in the tone of a man stating the weather, and put the knife back where it could be checked and announced it to nobody because the yard had already been told.

Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line and no scrubbing lifted it; the pale clip-ring at his left wrist, in place since Y.H. 806, held what little light there was and gave it back. His chest had been tight since the dark, and the breath that should have gone in at the top of the reach did not go in, the Seed 16 breath staying unspent with it — a small interval showing where it should have been, and the man refusing to spend it. The ear-sentence came up to the word that would call the loan and stopped there with no figure and no name, a remembered ninth stopping with no tenth before the seventh year at its very end, and still ungiven. He said Yes, house to the one who wanted the thaw early and to the one who wanted it late, agreeing so entirely that no argument could find a foot, and went on wanting the late because it puts off the paying, though the paying had stood in the ruts since the water went. No collector came up the clay road and no collector went down it; the loan stayed uncalled, and the warm stone in his clay pot in the loft lay cold in its unmarked pot, unreconciled by not trying, which was not forgiveness.

At the edge of the Vail wheel-shed, where she set the basket out of the draught and stood half a pace off with her burn shut on nothing, Jorra Vail came through the finger’s-width door with the day’s meal-fine grey slate dust ground small as meal past the bent little finger and under the nails, not bone-coloured slip; three men unnamed had gone low into the back room before first grey, and he gave the house no name for them and did not say the room was cold. He kept dusty forearms and clean ones on one tray and parted them as a surgeon parts clot from vessel, asking neither which slate had been broken nor whether it was a birth-slate. He spoke to the wheel before he spoke to any person: It doesn’t care whose hands. It cares if they’re steady. To a pot on the tray he said You’ll do, in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either, and he knew the full note that pot would give three turns before it left his hands, with air under it and a want in it, and withheld the knowing from any kiln-master out of High Kiln who would have called it early and made something of it, singing before its time and singing itself out early.

Before Sella had finished telling him the fire was low his bent finger went into the cold iron of the damper, took the cold out of the fingertip, moved the tongue a hair’s breadth and left it standing, so that the end of the instruction arrived at the pot and the household straightened around his silence. The boy read one iron with two bites — the bite that holds a vessel whole and the bite that can take the hand off a man — with no Grathic word for both mercies, joined to no kiln and entered in no slate. Behind that standing finger the fifth telling of the burned slate stood at five with a finger between the fifth and the four, no witness standing to be asked, no hand named at any flame, no sixth due on a day that had not earned one; the claim went on gathering interest against his promise to the men who burn slates, the promise neither given nor withdrawn. The shut-room tellings stood at two and not three; the pot sayings at three with the note from Smoke 14 on its own place apart; the row at five askings with one shaping and no witness; and he’s not Vail stayed sealed as tightly as the hour it was spoken. He did not look toward the bed or the basket.

Outside the wool the hand came up an inch toward the river’s note and took a small intake from the room whose floor does not touch his, and set it beside a small letting-go with the finger brought down between — an empty interval laid beside a held interval, the one kept open, the other kept whole, and the finger between them so neither should learn the other’s shape. It was the hundred and ninety-fifth answer, exact, complete and worth nothing at two inches of air; it stood up as no reach, hearing, seventh, stopping, payment, held change, gift, doctrine or rule, and no payer was named to it. The older man took a clean satisfaction in the keeping of the number; the infant wanted the crust lifted the two inches to his mouth and the thread wiped from his lip; both were true and the second was louder and neither won, and the argument was the work. The hand came down on the river’s note and laid itself back against the fold as a man puts away a tool where the next hand will find it without looking.

The file lay open on the towel at the head of the bed with every gap left gap, the empty interval and the held interval lying side by side on it with the finger withdrawn and neither spent, and the first light of the day after the reading going off the pot board without a word having been said about the Cross. Three hundred and ten stood exact, complete, unspent and spoken by nobody in Sill or Grath; six months and one hundred and thirty days were told by nobody; the carried fire’s fifth was unpaid the same number of days with its payer unnamed; Garl’s name had not been spoken the same number. Bone went on without a boundary drawn.

The skin on the trough was a second covering over a second depth, and the plate of ice clouded with silt, which she had lifted on Frost 9 and set on the flat stone by the well out of the path of feet, was a third covering with the depth lifted up and set where no foot went. Three coverings in three places, all called weather and entered nowhere. She told the weather where to stand. The boy kept a finger between every pair of them, because the same hand that covers a trough covers a well-lip and the same cold had made the plate, and a cause made out of three likenesses would want a payer put under all three and none stood to any. The month was a hunting month and a repairing month, and its hunts and repairs went on as directions judged by the carrying arm and the weather, never as figures. A man came down the clay road with a hare across his shoulders, the hind legs along one collarbone and the head tucked against the other, hill frost grey in the fur at the throat; he judged his own carrying by the ache in the arm that did it and went off toward the north gate road while the yard went on with its work. A woman with a pot asked what the month’s carrying had taken now, and Sella told her the weather and pointed with her chin toward the worked strips, a direction that left no entry behind it.

“That isn’t a figure,” the woman said.

“Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there,” Sella said. “The house has said it.” She said it to the pot and the weather and not to the woman, and went around the trough with the basket held level.

The order of it was what the boy read: the cost asked before the work was done, the question arriving ahead of the carrying arm. A neat hand can write a cost down before anybody has lifted a tile, and the neatness does not make the cost true; he had learned to read past the tidy figure to the place where the payer ought to have stood, and in this yard the place was empty. No man pricing a repair stood up to be asked. A roof wanted mending two doors down and the men who would mend it talked of the weather and the carrying of the tiles, and the tiles went by in armfuls judged by the arm, and nobody wrote what they weighed. Bone 12, Secondday, Y.H. 813

The twelfth of Bone came on in the small hours the way a twelfth of any month comes, with no door opened anywhere and no hand lifted to mark it, and under the Vail kiln-house roof the count went from three hundred and ten to three hundred and eleven exact. Six months and one hundred and thirty-one days were told by nobody. There was no market on a Secondday, no the Cross, no bell; the reading of two mornings ago had gone up the clay road in a locked box and had not come back, and nothing of it came now as a sign or a lesson or a mending or a cause or a payment. The boy lay on the bed at the low angle of the loaf shelf and did not know the day had changed its name; he knew only that the number had gone over once more, the way a man knows a page has turned without looking at the page. In Low Kiln, in Sill, in Grath, nobody could have said what three hundred and eleven counted, and he had stopped a long way back expecting anybody to say it. The not-knowing was its own small room, and he had lived in it so long that it felt less like a deprivation than like a place he had been put and had made warm.

Under the floor the carried fire gave its four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have stood.
