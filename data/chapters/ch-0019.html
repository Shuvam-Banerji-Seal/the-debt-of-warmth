The First Planting Week

Days 91–93 · Seed 2–4, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, Sill

Sow with the hand and count with the hand; the slate lies in the sun and takes no seed. A strip is a direction until somebody puts a back into it, and a direction has no figure to be entered.

— A saying of the seed-grounds east of Low Kiln

— Day 91 —

Seed 2, Y.H. 813

The brown skin the water had left over the Clay Yard held the print of a boot and gave it back slowly in the morning cold, faithful to a thing that had gone and would not come again in that shape. The first work of Seed had been sweeping. Today, the second day of the planting month, the work had moved east of Low Kiln, out past the last houses and onto the warmer ground where the barley fields lay in long strips the boy had carried until now only as a direction. There was no harvest in them yet and no green worth the name, only the brown skin of the turned earth and the pale seam where a broom had gone along it. Cold came off the ground in flat sheets. Wet wool and woodsmoke went before the women; the tallow in the shed had softened in the warm and set again in the cold, and the whole yard smelled of a fire that had been fed and then left. Seed was a name for putting things into the ground, and the ground east of the town had begun to take them.

Corin was carried at the edge of it, as he was carried everywhere, ninety-one days old and three months, in a basket that was not his under a fold of wool that was not his, and he watched the first planting work begin with a broom and a look and no figure spoken. Sella Vail had set the basket where the work went past and the wind did not come straight at it, the cold iron of the handle two inches beyond his hand. The women went along the strips with their skirts hitched, and their brooms went around what the sweep had left, and seed came out of a fist in handfuls that nobody counted aloud. A fist went down into a pocket and came up again. A woman pushed the bristles around a stone and looked at the ground a moment longer than the stone required, and that look was the rest of the measuring. There were no slates out here and no figures. The yard had counted what the water took in loaves and glances all through the lean week and had brought the same instruments to the planting: a hand, an eye, and a space nobody would name. In the life that was not this one he had watched a surgeon sort twenty bodies in ninety seconds by the give of one shoulder against another, and the sorting had been exact and had never once been written down. Here the sorting was seed and stone and the weather, and it was just as exact and just as little use to him.

A man with a barrow said the strips would want hands before the week was out, and a woman at the drying racks said the weather would have the last word, and neither put a number to either thing. “Hands,” the man said, and left the word standing in the cold. “Weather,” the woman said, and went back to her broom. No figure came into the middle between them. A slate takes no seed, and a fist takes no figure. The boy set the strips on a place of their own, a direction still and not a number. A direction can be carried for a month without becoming anything a clerk could enter; a strip is a strip until somebody puts a back into it, and a back is not a figure either. He kept ninety-one exactly, set edge to edge with the ninety before it, while the house kept its account badly in loaves pushed along a shelf, a glance at the clay jar on the wall, and a space at the end of the loaf row that nobody called a number. He wanted the number of his days said aloud by somebody who would know what it counted, and nobody in the field or the yard had any use for it. There was no word in Grathic that held a direction and a number in the same breath, and if there had been he had no voice to spend on it.

Berr Clay came down from the north road to look at the strips, short as a man comes short who has been looking at a thing from a height. He had the red wool cord of his office folded under his coat instead of worn across it, and the green of tasted glaze at one corner of his mouth, and he smelled of hot clay with nothing under it, the most expensive smell in Grath. His hands were large and clean. He stood a moment over the furrows and looked as if a figure might be lying in them waiting to be picked up, and gave the yard none. He wiped his thumb twice on his apron before touching nothing, and held a narrow slate unopened against his forearm with a finger resting on what was written there. To the man who brought a number up out of a fist and asked what the planting would cost now that seed had gone into the ground, he said, “A number goes into a slate, not into a strip. If the house wants it, there’ll be a slate at the watch.” And then, to the yard at large, “The levy is called. It is not called by me.”

The true subtraction went over the strips unspoken. The printed figure stood where it stood, and a figure read aloud was not the figure paid. He never said I. He took himself out of his own sentence as neatly as a surgeon takes a hand out of a field before the dressing goes on, and went off toward High Kiln with the box in his house still locked and the true deduction inside, believing he had done the district a kindness. The man with the barrow went back to his barrow with the number still in his fist, and the women went back to their handfuls, and nothing in the strip had changed its figure because a man had looked at it. The boy put the twice-wiped thumb on a tray of its own, where other record-first hands lay, and did not join it to any of them.

Back in the Clay Yard Sella gave the broom its instruction before she gave it to anybody, standing in the Vail corner with the bristles across her two hands and the grey of the ground showing between them. “Round it,” she said to the bristles. “The month’s for planting and there’s nothing planted here. It cost enough to come in; it’ll cost more to take out.” The little-door burn across the back of her hand shut on nothing. Then, and only then, she let Tarn take the broom out of her hands. She went around the clean dark line where the Ash ridge had turned its corner, and around the new hairline of grey finer than the old that lay beside it unswept, and around the space under the lowest rack where the broken birth-slates lay, going as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. She swept none of the three. The new grey was the thaw, and the thaw would go when it went; the house had said so all through Silt and said it again at the turning of the month, and a line the house had not swept in a year was left where it lay and called a season. She gave no instruction to the basket. A basket had no ears, and a thing set down was a thing set down.

Tarn set the small bench knife on its sill before the light and told the sill first and the yard after where it was, six years standing in a mark his thumb had worn almost flat and the seventh never cut, nearly gone and still held, the one entry in this house the house could not check. “Six counted,” he said to the kindling. “The next I don’t count.” He said it as a man says the weather and went on holding the breath he had decided not to spend, though his chest was already tight with it. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line and no scrubbing lifted it; the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back. “Yes, house,” he said to the woman who wanted an early thaw, and “Yes, house,” to the man who wanted a late. He agreed so entirely that no argument could find a foot, and went on wanting the late because a late thaw puts off the paying, though the paying had stood in the ruts since the water went. His broom went over the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector, over and over, without once turning along it. No collector came. From the Vail wheel-shed came the low note of the wheel and a word spoken to a pot, You’ll do, and no figure came out of the shed onto the yard. A vessel gave its note past the door and Tarn’s head did not turn, and he did not name the sound, and the boy did not call it a seventh hearing.

Under the Vail kiln-house roof the carried fire lay under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees and missing one breath in every four. Sella told it it was the thaw and turned the coals with the back of the broken pot and fed it, and the fire answered with four low pulls and a silence where the fifth should have been. Its payer had gone unnamed for ninety-one days. The coal the brine-gull took upriver weeks back was entered in no slate and missed in none; one warmth less had gone out of the world with no account the poorer, and the planting month had come over it without noticing. Every warmth in the house had a payer and none of them stood in the room to be thanked, and the boy had learned to ask who paid before he asked what a thing was worth, which was a surgeon’s question in a country that had no use for it. She did not touch the widow’s mark, because a strip, a broom, a basket and a bed are not openings and no opening had come into this house since the year turned, and she had lost a husband to that danger and had never once described it that way herself. The clean dark line lay where it had always lain and the finer grey lay beside it. The boards overhead kept four nails and four and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts did not meet.

The body humiliated him exactly and in its known order. A thread of drool had frozen at the corner of his mouth in the small hours and no hand came to wipe it, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and came back. Cold arrived at cheek, then temple, then wrist, the wool riding back from the wrist so the air found the bare place and stayed there. One brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold against his ear, gone past smelling of anything, and the empty place beside it had come into Seed 2 without acquiring a name; no third came. He wanted the crust, wanted the breast, wanted the lip of the the yard well bucket two inches beyond his hand, and the three wants sat in him with the strict equality of three instruments laid out for a procedure he would never perform. The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water going over water behind the racks and came down. He had once known where every vessel in a room lay by the sound of it and could put a clamp on the one that was leaking; here he lay under a fold and could not lift that hand to his own chin. He was the only adult in the field and the least use in it. The joke had never once been funny enough to say aloud.

At the turn of the wind Sella lifted the basket level and carried him up at the half-pace from every open slate, going around the space under the lowest rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and set him on his bed under the roof and not in the Debt Loft. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without looking at his face and left the thread at his mouth frozen and unwiped. The door stood a finger’s width open. Through the gap came the river’s low muttered note from the Ilm, and through the floor came the kiln’s breath, four, four and a gap held under blind ash, the two sounds in two separate places as they had come for ninety-one days. One brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold with the empty place beside it still unnamed, and no third came. The new finer grey lay unswept beside the old clean dark line. Nothing had been put to a figure in the strips or in the house; the seed was in the ground and the count crossed into the afternoon of Seed 2 unspent.

The afternoon came into the Clay Yard without a mark of its coming, the way water goes over a stone without stopping to be counted. The light shifted off the basket and onto the stone lip of the yard well, and the women at the drying racks kept the work they had in their hands. Corin was carried at the edge of it, ninety-one days old and three months, under a fold that was not his and in a basket that was not his, with the cold iron of the handle two inches beyond his hand. He had watched seed go into the strips east of Low Kiln on a fist and a look and no figure spoken. Now the work had come back to the well, and the question came with it.

A woman with wet sleeves let the bucket hang on the rope and asked what the planting would cost, now that the seed was in the ground. She did not ask anyone in particular. She asked the yard the way people ask a pot, with the question left lying where it fell.

Sella Vail stood in the the Vail kiln-house doorway with the little-door burn shut on nothing. She moved her lips along the loaf shelf and did not say what her lips were counting. Loaves stood where loaves had stood, and at the end of the row was the place where one had stood and stood no longer, and she came back to that place twice the way a man comes back to a figure he does not trust, and gave it no figure at all. Her eyes went to the clay jar on the wall and stayed there one breath and came away. The jar had been fetched from a house three doors down and set on the shelf by the wall, and it was the house’s, and nothing in it was entered against anybody.

“Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there,” she said. To the woman she said, “The house has said it.” And that was the whole of the answer.

The woman with the wet sleeves took it as an answer, because it was the kind of answer a yard can carry home. She let the bucket down. Sella gave the bucket its instruction before she gave it to anybody, as she gave the instruction to the broom before she gave it to the woman who took it, and as she had given it to the wool that morning and never once to the basket. “Keep the silt out,” she said to the bucket. “The water came up thin twice and it’ll not bear a hard hand.” The bucket went down on a slack rope and came up with its belly full and its lip level, and the woman set it on the stone with the care of a pot filled that must not be slopped, the water going quiet in it by degrees. Then Sella gave an instruction to the women, and after that to nobody, and to the person in the basket last if at all. A basket had no ears. A thing set down was a thing set down.

The boy wanted the number of his days spoken by somebody who would know what it counted. He had no voice to ask and no standing to be told, and there was nobody in the yard who would have known what the number meant if they had spoken it. In the life that was not this one he could have told the hour a burn was dressed and the term a debt had left to run, and both of those numbers had been useful and neither had been kind. Here he could not lift his hand to his own chin. He had reduced a thousand shoulders and he lay under a fold of wool with a crust beyond his reach and a drool freezing at his mouth. The joke had never once been funny enough to say aloud, and he had learned to find it funny without laughing, which cost less and kept the same blade in it.

He checked the file as a man runs a thumb down a row of instruments laid out on a towel, feeling for the one that has moved, though no instruments had been laid out and there was no thumb to run. Six hearings of the full note stayed six — five from four sources, a cup across a birth room, a pot in the Vail wheel-shed through a wall, the district’s own reading slate twice at the Cross, and a man giving at the Drawing — and the sixth, the small flat knock out of a woman who had been warm that morning, from a person. The sixth lay beside the five and never among them. No seventh came into the yard with the bucket or went out of it with the question. Three near-full answers stood advanced and not paid, neither hearings nor the third thing, and no hand had come to pay them. Three intervals stayed on another tray, Stone 13 and Stone 17 and Stone 25, objects with the shape of sounds that had not been sounds. The word forty remained a saying on a place of its own, unspoken this afternoon, with the readable shape of the double turn beside it and the weight of a thumb on wicker apart from both. It had been said once, low, by a woman who watched, and watching was not the same as telling, and the shape of a knot was not the name of one.

Four low things stood in four places: the told bell of the Long Night, the heard bell at the Cross, the early note out of the stone at the Singing Sill, and the gap under the kiln’s breath where the fifth pull should have been. The river’s own note of water going over water was a fifth thing on a place of its own and not a hearing at all. He had learned to keep the five things from becoming six by the simple expedient of not joining them, which was the only procedure in this country he could perform with his hands where they were. The two tellings of the shut counting room stayed on their own tray joined to no kiln in Low Kiln, the likeness kept and the difference kept — breath drawn through the nose against breath made in the throat, both men saying not the war’s hand — and neither was turned into the other, though two men had now carried the same danger into the same yard and gone home with their bags and their road-mud, and one woman under this roof had lost a husband to it and told the fire it was the thaw. The loan that could hear stayed uncalled. Below the sill a child’s foot was still the cold to one woman and weather to the yard, wrapped in wool by one hand and gone past by a dozen, uncured and unnamed. He knew the extremity going before the core and the afterdrop waiting on the far side of a warming nobody would perform, and the knowing was exact and had the usefulness of an instrument held behind glass.

It lay there now where it could be checked, six years standing in a mark his thumb had worn almost flat and the seventh never cut, nearly gone and still held, the one entry in this house the house could not check. Two ears and two silences kept their equality under one roof, and the sentence that would call the loan was not said. A loan that can hear is a loan that can be called, and a man whose ear is worth more than his debt is a man a house might one day learn to count; but the yard went on with its buckets and the shed went on with its wheel, and nothing in either place had been joined to the thing under the roof that was not said.

She had not touched it at the well and would not touch it at any bank before an opening that did not come, because a bucket and a question and a basket are not openings, and she had lost a husband to that danger and had never once described it that way herself. The woman at the well had put no figure to the place at the end of the loaf row, and nobody had put a name to the place at his ear, and the two places lay in two rooms and did not touch. Nothing had been joined, paid, explained or ended. Six hearings stayed six beside and never among, the three near-full answers stood advanced and not paid, the word was unspoken, the two tellings stayed on their tray, the loan stayed uncalled, and the cold foot stayed the cold. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water going over water and came down. The boy was ninety-one days old and nobody had told him so, and the count crossed into the evening of Seed 2 unspent.

Seed 3, Y.H. 813

Before the light the room off the Vail wheel-shed stood a finger’s width open, the same width it had kept through all the dark hours, and the dark lay in that width like water held behind a sluice. Men had come in since the first grey and spoken low behind the door, and Corin knew they had come not by any name spoken aloud but by the dust that came out after them and by the particular quiet that settled on the house while they were inside it — a quiet with a held quality, as though a vessel had been covered with a cloth so the note could not get out. Jorra Vail gave the house no name for them. He did not say the room was cold. There was nobody in the house who would have asked for a name and nobody who would have taken one if he had given it, so the names stayed where they had been spoken, behind the gap, and the boy, carried at the edge of the Clay Yard in a basket that was not his and under a fold that was not his, heard the low talk only as a change in the air, the way a man in a dark room hears that a window has been opened without seeing which way the cold comes from.

He was ninety-two days old and three months and could not hold up his own head for the length of a sentence. The head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and the wool had ridden back from the wrist so the cold arrived in its known order, cheek and then temple and then the bare place at the wrist, and the hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth and came down. A thread of drool had frozen at his mouth in the small hours and nobody had wiped it, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left. He had reduced a thousand shoulders in the life that was not this one and here he could not lift a hand to his own chin; he had learned to find the joke without laughing, which cost less and kept the same blade in it. One brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold with the empty place beside it still unnamed, and no third came, and the cold got on with its work around him while the house got on with its keeping.

Then Jorra came through the finger’s-width-open door.

The dust was worked into both forearms past the bend of the little finger, grey and fine, ground into the creases the way silt grinds into a seam, and it was not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel. On the clean mornings the slip dried to old bone up both forearms and the bent finger led at the damper and nothing grey came past the bend; on this morning the grey had come. The boy kept the two states on one tray as two states of one man’s mornings and asked neither which slate had been broken nor whether it had been a birth-slate, because a question spoken makes an object of an answer and there was no answer here that could be carried. The broken birth-slates lay under the lowest rack where whole slate should have stood, and Sella Vail and every woman working the racks went around the space as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and nobody entered it and nobody swept it. The space kept its shape the way a missing tooth keeps a shape in a man’s mouth, and the yard went on around it all morning.

He spoke to the wheel before he spoke to anyone. That was the order of the house and had been since the boy could notice orders: the wheel first, the pots after, the people last if at all, and the basket never. “It doesn’t care whose hands,” he said to the wheel. “It cares if they’re steady.” The low note of the wheel went on under the word, and a pot turned on it with the thrower’s weight settled into the hollow his father had worn on the left side over twenty years of leaning. Garl Vail’s salt-stiff coat kept a broader man’s shape on its peg and nobody took it down; the seat kept its lean and nobody filled it, which was a distinction the house had never explained and the boy had never asked it to.

“You’ll do,” Jorra said to the pot, in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either.

He knew the full note of a Vail pot before it left the wheel; the boy knew that he knew, the way he knew the bent finger and the short hair cut so Ash could not nest in it. Jorra would not say so before a kiln-master come up from High Kiln, who would call it early and make something of it, and the boy kept the knowing where questions can last for years, on a place of its own with no name written across it. The full note itself was a thing he could not make and could not spend: six hearings of it stood in his file and stayed six, five from four sources and the sixth, the small flat knock out of a woman who had been warm that morning, from a person, filed beside the five and never among them. No seventh came into the shed with the dust and no seventh went out of it with the pot. Three near-full answers stood advanced and not paid on their own tray, and three intervals stayed on another, Stone 13 and Stone 17 and Stone 25, shapes of sounds that had not been sounds. The word forty was a saying with the readable shape of the double turn beside it and the weight of a thumb on wicker apart from both, and it was not spoken this morning. Two tellings of a room shut in the last war stayed on their own tray joined to no kiln in Low Kiln, likeness kept and difference kept — breath through the nose against breath made in the throat, both men saying not the war’s hand — and the loan that could hear stayed uncalled. Below the sill a child’s foot was still the cold to one woman and weather to the yard, wrapped in wool by one hand and gone past by a dozen, uncured and unnamed. He knew the extremity going before the core and the afterdrop waiting on the far side of a warming nobody would perform, and the knowing was exact and had the usefulness of an instrument held behind glass.

She had not finished telling him the fire was low when his bent finger went into the damper ahead of her sentence. The instruction had been going to the fire first, as every instruction in that house went to the fire or the wool or the bucket or the broom and never to the basket, and the person came last if at all; it never reached him. The cold iron took the cold out of his fingertip as his finger went in, drawing it out of the flesh the way a poultice draws, so that the hand that moved the damper was warmer at the point of contact than the hand that had come into the room. He did not look at her. The end of her sentence arrived at the pot instead of at him, and the household straightened around his silence the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted from it: the wheel kept its note, the women at the racks went back to their pots, and Sella shut her mouth on the rest of the instruction and gave it to the fire.

He had not answered her with a word and had not looked at her, which was how he obeyed her instantly. The boy, carried at the edge with the cold going on at his wrist, read the damper as any iron that stops a thing from running. It was a gate set across the way the heat went, a bite that held the house’s warmth in and let it out by degrees, and the same bite that kept a vessel whole could take the hand off a man: close it and the room held; close it wrong and the breath went out of it. He had once held such a bite between his own fingers and known it for mercy and mutilation in one piece of iron, and here there was no word in Grathic that held both mercies in one. The house had one word for the thing and it was the damper, and the word carried only the holding, never the taking, which was why the men who came into the back room could speak of a counting room shut with the damper closed and make a curiosity with a date of it, eighteen leagues east, while under this roof a woman who had lost a husband to that danger told the fire it was the thaw. The two tellings stayed on their tray joined to no kiln. The boy kept the reading to himself. It was an anatomy of the house’s danger, exact and worth nothing to anyone who might have had the use of it.

He did not look toward the bed or into the basket. The basket might as well have been another pot set down at the edge of the floor, and the bed might as well have been the hollow seat. He’s not Vail stayed sealed as tightly as a lid nobody had tried to lift in three years, and the promise of the foundling’s name to the men who burned slates was neither given nor withdrawn; a man can hold two opinions as neatly as two tools and use neither, and Jorra had held these since the hour he spoke the sentence and the boy was carried in under the roof. He wanted the wheel to stay in the house, the boy outside the name, and the hollow seat for himself, and had said none of the three. The wanting sat in him with the dust in his forearms. Sella, who had never taken the coat down, asked for nothing and was not asked; the keeping went around the wanting the way the yard went around the space under the rack, and neither of them made a thing of it.

Under the shawl, on the other side of the room in a house he could not name, her sixth narrow slate lay turned face-down toward a floorboard: six pages written in a second hand no Court physician had seen, all of them unread, the sixth set down on a day three months back and not to be set down again, no seventh written. The hidden record stood at six and went on standing at six.

Two private records in two materials stood apart in one room. The round pewter tin above the empty bed kept its pale fold and thumb-bright rub, unopened; the narrow slate under a shawl going home to a floorboard lay face-down toward the boards, unread. Neither was read by anybody and neither was carried to a door. The boy set them on separate places and wrote no cause across the space between them. He had learned what three likenesses in a row will do to a man who wants an answer, and here were two in one room with a woman between them who did not know she had made either, and he kept them apart with the only instrument he had, which was attention, and it bought him nothing he could spend.

❦

Alda gathered the slates writing-down and put them back under her shawl, and went out past the bench without a word about the fold or the sign or the date laid over it, the way a woman goes out of a room where she has done exactly what she came to do. In the back room the neighbour’s song flattened out and stopped, and the lye water went on at the board. Sella stood a half-pace from the basket with her burn shut on nothing and asked no question about any of it. The two women had traded a silence for three years, and the silence had a door in it and neither of them opened it. The boy lay under his fold and heard the order of the day going out of the room: the slates first, the fold after, and nothing spoken about either, and the waiting done by the record and not by the person.

At the going of the light Sella lifted the basket level and carried him up at the half-pace from every open slate, going around the space under the lowest rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and set him on his bed under the Vail kiln-house roof and not in the Debt Loft. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without looking at his face and left the thread at his mouth frozen and unwiped, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left. She told the fire it was the thaw and turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and fed it, and the fire answered with four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been. Its payer had gone unnamed for ninety-three days, and the coal that went up with the brine-gull was entered in no slate and missed in none. She did not touch the widow’s mark, because a basket and a door and a bed are not openings and no opening had come into this house on Seed 4; she had lost a husband to that danger and had never once described it that way herself.

Under the roof the private file was checked the way a man checks instruments laid out on a towel, and it was unchanged. Six hearings of the full note stayed six beside and never among, five of them from four sources and the sixth, the small flat knock out of a woman who had been warm that morning, from a person; no seventh had come and no seventh would be made by wanting one. Three near-full answers stood on their tray, advanced and not paid. The three intervals stayed on another. The word forty was unspoken, and the readable shape of the double turn stood beside it on its own place, and the weight of a thumb on wicker stood apart from both. Four low things kept their four places, with the river’s note of water going over water a fifth thing and not a hearing. The two tellings of the room shut in the war stayed on their own tray joined to no kiln, likeness kept and difference kept, breath through the nose against breath made in the throat, both men saying not the war’s hand. The loan that could hear stayed uncalled. Below the sill a child’s foot stayed the cold to one woman and weather to the yard, uncured and unnamed, and the knowing of what it was and what it would do had the usefulness of an instrument kept behind glass.

One brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold with the empty place beside it still unnamed, and no third came. The place at the end of the loaf row and the place at his ear lay in two rooms and did not touch. Ash was worked into the creases of a man’s knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line, and the new hairline of grey, finer than the old, lay beside it unswept and was called the thaw and would go when it went. The boards overhead kept four nails and four and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts did not meet. Three doors down a tin stayed shut and a slate stayed turned down and a date stayed over a death no living person could lift off; under this roof a fold held one crust and an empty place, and the arithmetic went on in loaves and glances with nobody putting a figure to either.

The door stood a finger’s width open. Through the gap came the river’s low muttered note from the Ilm, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, and through the floor came the kiln’s breath, four, four and a gap under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, the two sounds in two separate places as they had come for ninety-three days. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water going over water and came down. Nothing had been joined, paid, explained or ended. The boy was ninety-three days old and nobody had told him so, and the count stood at ninety-three, unspent, with the tin unopened three doors down and the narrow slate face-down toward its floorboard and the first planting week gone into the ground with the arithmetic unchanged. Day 94 waited in the worked strips with its seed covered and its figure unspoken.

The Chronicle of Chapter 19

On the second day of Seed the work went east of Low Kiln onto the strips the boy had carried for a month only as a direction. Brooms went around what the sweep had left; seed came out of a fist in handfuls nobody counted, and nobody put a figure to hands or strips. Berr Clay came down with his office cord folded under his coat, wiped his thumb twice before touching nothing, and told the yard a number goes into a slate, not a strip, that the levy was called but not by him.

At the well Sella Vail left the empty place at the row’s end with no figure and told a woman to ask the pot what a wet thing costs; the house had said it. She gave the bucket its instruction before the women and never to the basket. The boy kept ninety-one exactly against the house’s loaves and glances.

On Thirdday Jorra Vail came through the finger’s-width-open door with grey dust past his bent finger, told the wheel it cared only for steady hands, and put that finger into the damper ahead of Sella’s unfinished sentence. He read the damper as the bite that holds a vessel whole and can take a hand off a man, with no Grathic word for both. Tarn set his knife on its sill with six years counted and the seventh held, told the kindling Six counted. The next I don’t count, and swept his broom over the collector’s road without turning. No collector came.

At the Fishstairs Mira Silt answered a question about the run with Ask the river and put her thumb under her apron to the grey cord when a buyer named a figure, shifting a creel to show the second twisted twice beneath it, neither knot counted and the knot for the thin child upriver untouched. The steps came back four, four and a landing out of line that a market walked over without a fall; word came that the weak board at the Weir was gone or trodden flat and nobody had gone down to see which. The soft-cuffed clerk still sat for a measure nobody had called.

Three doors down Alda Reed laid her birth-slates writing-down on the bench before she turned to the fold, crossed two fingers at the birth-flower before she knew it and covered the sign with a dead woman’s date. Warm and breathing, that’s all I’ve set down, she said, exact and not true. Her tin stayed shut; her sixth slate stayed face-down, six pages unread. At dusk Sella set him on his bed, put the fold right without looking at his face and left the thread frozen. The river’s note came through the door and the fire’s gap through the floor, in two places. He was ninety-three days old; nobody had told him so. His hand went up an inch toward the water and came down. Nothing had been joined, paid, explained or ended.
