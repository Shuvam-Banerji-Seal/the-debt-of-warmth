The Inch Asked Again

Days 195–197 · Ember 16–18, Y.H. 813 · The Vail kiln-house, Low Kiln

An inch is a long way when the hand is small. Ask the pot how far the handle stands; it will tell you the distance and not who set it there.

— A saying of the drying racks in Low Kiln, in the kiln month

— Day 195 —

Ember 16, Y.H. 813 (Sixthday)

The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water that came through the finger’s-width door, and came down again against the fold. It had done this every morning of his life and did it now without triumph or instruction, the small bare joint at the wrist riding back as the arm rose, the fingers opening on nothing and closing on wool. It was not a reaching. A reaching would have had a thing at the end of it. This had only the sound, and the sound was a note the river made going over itself under its skin of ice, and the hand went up and came down the way a man’s eye goes to a light and away again when he has other work. The count at one hundred and ninety-four went whole out of the dark behind him into this morning, exact, complete and unspent, a thing laid out on a towel that did not exist and could not now be put down. He was one hundred and ninety-five days old and six months and fifteen days. Nobody under any roof in Low Kiln, in Sill, in Grath, in all of Sarn, said so. The house kept its days in loaves and glances and the space at the end of a row, and a day begun was a day begun whether anybody counted it or not. The fold took the hand down and did not move.

Smell came before sight, and sight before any sound that could be named: hot clay under tallow, wet wool under that, and under both the sour mineral breath of the corner nobody had swept in two years. The kiln drew in fours with a gap where the fifth should have closed. It was not a bell. A bell would have said a thing and gone on saying it; this drew and stopped and drew again with the small absence sitting in the middle of it, the way a man’s breath sits when he has not let the last of it go. The crust lay leathery two inches from his ear, brown-edged and stiffened by the dry heat of the kiln month, with the empty place beside it still without a name and no third thing to fill it. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and was frozen still, unwiped, because nobody under any roof had been given an instruction about the thread. Cold had come cheek, temple, the small bare joint at the wrist where the wool rode back, and had stopped there as if it too had come to the end of an instruction and had no authority to go further. The afterdrop waited on the far side of a warming nobody in this house would perform.

Through the floor the carried fire answered four low pulls and kept the silence where the fifth should have been. Its payer had been without a name for a hundred and ninety-five days. Through the finger’s-width door the river went on with its note of water over water, a fifth low thing that was never counted among the hearings, never made a sixth, never asked to stand up and be something it was not. Overhead the boards kept four nails, four, and a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts in three places, and none of them rose up and became the others, because a man who wanted them to agree would have to put a payer under all three and no payer stood to any of them. He kept them where they lay. A row of like things left standing long enough will call itself an account, and an account will want a figure, and a figure will want somebody to have paid it; he had learned that in a country that had no word for the thing he was doing when he refused it. He did not refuse it aloud. He had no voice for it. He simply laid the four-and-a-gap beside the four-and-a-gap beside the four nails and the knot, and let them lie.

Sella Vail gave the morning its instruction before she gave it to any person. She gave it to the wool first, pulling it up under his chin with the little-door burn half-closing on the cloth and opening again on nothing. “Keep it off his ears,” she said. “It’s the river wind.” She gave it to the fire next, and to the pot, and to the broom waiting by the wall, and to the running water at the well when she should come to it, and never once to the basket. She said the house where another woman would have said I. She kept her half-pace from every open thing, which was a way of walking that had long ago stopped being a thing she had to think about. Her eye went once to the clay jar cold under its glaze on the wall and her hand started toward it and shut half over nothing without touching it. It was a jar fetched from three doors down in another month and set on the shelf for a reason nobody had been told, and the glaze on it was cold, and she did not touch it.

At the hearth she told the fire it was the thaw and turned the coals with the back of a broken pot. “The house will keep it,” she said, where another woman would have said I. Then she stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand and counted under her breath toward no opening — the small intake at the top, the hold, the letting go — the way she had counted at every going of the light and every morning since the kiln month came in, and never once toward a thing that came. She pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn, and the grey streak at her temple showed pale off the coals. It had come in the winter Garl Vail died. She would say no more than that.

She set the pot rim out on the board with its handle standing two inches beyond the reaching hand, and her hand rested a half-beat longer at the setting than the setting needed before it went away. Two inches was the whole distance. It was not a great gulf and it was not crossable; it was two inches of board between a small soft hand and a pot handle, and the boy could no more close it than he could lift his chin two inches to the crust at his ear. She did not look toward the bed when she set it. She never looked toward the bed at the setting.

She fed him barley gruel from the house’s own dish, holding his head with two fingers behind the ear the way one turns a vessel so the light falls where it is wanted, and wiped the spoon on her sleeve and not on his mouth. The gruel was thin and warm and tasted faintly of smoke, and it went down and did what gruel does, which was to put off the question of hunger for a while without answering it. He finished what came. It was not his to finish and he finished it anyway; waste had offended him in another life and the offence had outlived the life. Two fingers behind the ear, spoon on sleeve, mouth left as it was. The instruction had gone to the spoon and the dish and the fire that had warmed the dish, and never to the boy, who was carried and fed and discussed in the third person and knew every word of it.

The widow’s mark hung on its cord at her throat where it had hung since the first firing, a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it. It had been touched once, before the kiln on the first morning of Ember, held one breath, let go, and at no shelf, well, rack, road, basket, morning, afternoon or dusk since. A morning and a shelf were not openings. There had been no opening since before the kiln on Ember 1. People noticed the burn first and the mark last, which was the order she preferred, and the boy had learned to notice in the same order so that nothing in his face would tell her he had noticed at all. He lay with the crust at his ear and the thread frozen at his mouth and the mark behind her coat, and the two rooms did not touch: hers held a pot, a fire and a woman counting under her breath toward nothing entered, and his held a boy who could not close two inches and a number nobody would say.

Out in the Clay Yard the kilns stood at their heat in the second working week of the kiln month, and the district went on calling Tallow the firing month and Ember the kiln month and neither name was corrected. A neighbour come to the threshold with dry scurf of mud on her hem, the mud left from the flood and dried pale in the creases of the cloth, asked what the firing would want now the account had gone up the road.

“Ask the pot what a firing costs set down there,” Sella said. “The house has said it.”

She gave the weather as the rest of it, and the neighbour took the pot and the weather away as an answer and went off level with her pot, nothing priced, nothing refused, nothing slopped and nothing entered. The figure had gone up the clay road in a locked box with the true subtraction inside and a printed one in twelve standing unchallenged where two hundred people had stood and heard it and could not now unhear it. Here under the Vail roof a cost questioned as a direction arrived nowhere, and this was the whole method of the house for staying warm. The boy kept the shape of it and made no rule of it. A rule was an account that had found a payer, and no payer stood to the pot or the weather.

The sweet edge of lead glaze came up off High Kiln on a wrong wind, sweetish and metallic under the clay smell, and went out again on the same wind. Sella answered it as a direction and not as a second edge paid. Nobody under that roof named what had ailed the man carried out on Day 188, no collector was called for him, no figure given, no slate opened, and the pale line at his gum stayed on a place of its own at two paces, exact, complete and worth nothing. No cause was spoken in Grathic; the wind was a wind and went where winds go. The boy turned his head toward the edge and away with it, and filed the turning where a direction goes and not among the hearings, not as a seventh and not as a cause.

Back under the roof the note of water over water came through the finger’s-width door, low and patient, a thing that had been going under the ice since before the house was built and would go on after it. The hand outside the wool went up toward it an inch.

This time he asked it to stay.

It was the asking that was new and not the inch. The inch was an old small fact of muscle, a thing the body did without being told; the asking was the surgeon coming back into the room he had left years ago and putting his hand where the hand already was, as if a hand could be given an order and kept to it. He asked it to stay the way he had once asked a nurse to hold pressure in a cavity until the vessel took — not with words, for there were no words in this mouth that any ear in this house would have understood, but with the whole inward address of a man who knows that a thing held is a thing that has been told to hold. He had held pressure in a cavity for twenty minutes once, in a white room with the blood going out under the drape, and had not moved and had not let anybody else move, and when the vessel took he had taken his hands away and gone on to the next body. That was the register he brought to one inch of a small soft hand.

The hand stayed.

It was a small physiological thing, the keeping of it. The muscle at the shoulder took the weight and did not let it drop; the small bare joint at the wrist held where the wool had ridden back; the fingers stayed open on the air the way they would have stayed open on a thing handed up to them. Nothing in the hand was strong. An infant’s hand lifting an inch against no resistance is not a feat of strength, and a man who had once reduced a shoulder in the dark would have been the first to say so. What was held was not a weight but a duration, and duration was the thing the surgeon’s register had been trained to note — the pulse counted in tens, the interval between the injury and the arrival, the minutes a clamp stood before the vessel took or did not. He noted this interval as he had noted those, without deciding what it meant, and the noting was a habit and not a gift. It bought nothing. It would not close two inches or make a mouth speak. He noted it and put it down.

It stayed for the length of one of the breaths Sella counted at her fire in the room that did not touch this one — the small intake at the top, the hold, the letting go — a rhythm he could not hear and a fire he could not see, counted against the note that came through the door. It was an asking made before and an answer given before, an inch held and let go, and an inch held twice might start in time to look like a reaching, which was a thing he would not let it become. He filed it on a place of its own beside the others: not a hearing of the full note, not a seventh, not a stopping, not a payment, not one of the three held changes, not a key, not a power, not a rule. No payer was named to it. Nothing was stood up out of it to call itself a sign. He had no towel and no instruments and no case, and he laid it away anyway in the dark with hands that could not have lifted the least of what they had once held.

Then the hand came down against the fold, and the fold took it without a word. He still could not close the two inches to the pot handle, lift his chin two inches to the crust at his ear or speak a number anybody would have known what a number counted. The body humiliated him and moved by an inch at the same time, and both were true. Nobody had seen the holding. It was worth nothing. It had been asked and it had been answered, and that was the whole of it, and he kept it exactly, complete and unspent.

One hundred and ninety-five stood exact in the dark of the wool, spoken by nobody under any roof. Cold had stopped at cheek, temple and the small bare joint at the wrist where the wool rode back, the thread was frozen and unwiped, the crust lay leathery two inches from the ear with its empty place unnamed and no third, and the afterdrop waited on the far side of a warming nobody in this house would perform. The carried fire missed its fifth with no payer for a hundred and ninety-five days; the river kept its note of water over water; the boards kept four nails, four and a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts in three places, and none rose up and became the others. The widow’s mark hung where it hung, the charcoal under the shelf was unread, the stone lay cold in its loft pot, the Seed 16 breath stood unspent, the loan was uncalled, and the cost of the man carried out stood entered nowhere. Nothing had been joined, named, opened, called, spent or ended. The hand had gone up an inch, held, and come down, and the count went forward into the next day by that much and by no more.

The afternoon thinned without ending. The heat went off the high air first, as though a hand had lifted it from the upper boards and set it down again lower, and it stayed longest in the low places: under the loaf shelf, behind the pot, in the fold of wool where the boy lay with the crust at his ear. The underside of the shelf made a roof that stopped at the edge of his reach, and the heat came down that roof in a slow slant and stopped at the same place, as if it too had been given an instruction and had come to the end of it. He lay under the roof of the shelf under the roof of the house, two roofs, and neither reached past the small bare joint at the wrist where the wool had ridden back. Cold had come that far in the small hours and no farther. Between the cold and the warmth there was the width of a joint, and that was the whole of his country.

Sella Vail stood at the shelf with her back to the basket and moved her lips along a row. It was not a song and not a count anyone could have taken down. The loaf at the end stood where a loaf had stood; the old space at the row’s end had kept its place through the firing month; and a palm’s breadth of bare board had come in on the night of Seed 6 and had not been filled since. Two gaps stood in the row. She gave neither a figure. A figure spoken over a shelf would travel, but a figure kept behind the teeth stayed where it was put, which was a thing a woman could do with a gap as with a pot: set it down and leave it standing. She had set both down. Her lips went along them and came back to the same place twice, the way her hand went to the same place twice when she counted fuel, and she gave nobody the thing she counted.

Her eye went once to the clay jar cold under its glaze on the wall. Her hand started toward it and shut half over nothing without touching it, the little-door burn closing on the air and opening again. The jar had been fetched from three doors down in another month and set on the shelf for a reason nobody had been told. Its glaze was cold. She left it so. Her finger, going to the shelf’s front edge, stayed on top of the board and did not go beneath. Beneath that edge lay the charcoal tally, put there in another month by a hand that was not hers and read by nobody under this roof, uncounted, uncopied. Once, years ago, she had held a finger under the board for the length of a counted breath without looking, and that beat stood where it had been left, on a place of its own in the boy’s keeping. It was not repeated now. She straightened the board with the flat of her palm and turned from the shelf, and the two gaps stayed in the other room, where a shelf’s absences do not touch the one crust lying two inches from a boy’s ear.

At the hearth she told the fire it was the thaw. She said it to the fire and not to the room and not to the basket, and turned the coals with the back of a broken pot, the pot’s back going in under the ash and lifting and letting the heat come up through the gaps. “The house will keep it,” she said, where another woman would have said I. The fire answered as it had answered every morning and every going of the light since the kiln month came in: four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have closed, its payer without a name for a hundred and ninety-five days. She did not supply one. A man who wanted the four to agree with the fifth would have had to put somebody under them, and no payer stood to the fire’s gap, and she had long ago stopped looking for the hand that had taken the fifth breath away.

Then she stood a moment with the back of the broken pot in her hand and counted under her breath toward no opening. The small intake at the top, the hold, the letting go. She had counted this way at every going of the light and every morning through the kiln month, and never once toward a thing that came. The count went out of her and arrived nowhere, and this was the whole of the house’s method for staying warm: a cost asked as a direction, an answer given to the pot and the weather, nothing entered. The boy, who could not see her face from where he lay, knew the shape of the count by the pause in her hands. He had learned it the way a man learns the rhythm of another man’s breathing in the next bed. It was not his breath. It was not his fire. There was a wall between the hearth and the bed, and the wall held.

She pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn, and the grey streak at her temple showed pale off the coals. It had come in the winter Garl Vail died; she would say no more than that. Then she set the pot rim out on the board with its handle standing two inches beyond the reaching hand, and her hand rested a half-beat longer at the setting than the setting needed before it went away. Two inches was the whole distance. It was not a great gulf and it was not crossable. He could no more close it than lift his chin two inches to the crust at his ear or speak the number that stood in his head. She did not look toward the bed when she set it. She never looked toward the bed at the setting, and the room that held the pot and the room that held the boy went on not touching each other, as they had touched in no hour of his life.

At the kindling the small knife lay at the very edge of its sill, six years standing in a worn thumb-mark where the house could check them and the seventh at the lip where it could not, the one entry in this house that no house could enter. Beside it lay the unforged sentence of Garl, the one about the man sent for a collector who turned back at the frozen ford. It lay there as a tool lies that has never been beaten into shape, neither a proverb for the yard nor a charge against a man, and nobody under the roof had forged it in all the boy’s days.

Tarn took the broom only after the broom had been given its instruction. He did not take it when it was merely lying there; a tool that had not been spoken to was a tool with no work in it, and a clay-bound hand who took up a tool before its work was named was a hand that would be entered for it. Sella gave the instruction to the broom, and only then did his fingers close on the handle. “Yes, house,” he said, and told the kindling what it had been told so many times that the words had worn smooth as the thumb-mark: “Six counted. The next I don’t count.” He said it in the tone of a man stating the weather, a statement of fact that invited somebody else to laugh so that he would not have to.

A neighbour wanted the thaw early; another, coming off the clay road with mud on her hem, wanted it late. He said “Yes, house” to each, agreed so entirely that no argument could find a foot between them, and went on wanting the late one, because a late thaw puts off the paying, though the paying had stood in the ruts since the water went. His broom went over the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector, over and over, without once turning to look along it. No collector came up or went down the clay road that afternoon. He swept the length of it as a man sweeps a step he does not mean to descend, and kept his eyes on the bristles.

His chest had been tight since the dark. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line at the corner, and no scrubbing lifted it out; the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back. The sentence about an ear under this roof being worth more than the debt that held it came up to his teeth as he worked. It went all the way to the word that would call the loan, and stopped there with no figure in it and no name. He drew the breath he should have spent at the top of the reach — the breath that had stood unspent since Seed 16 with its small interval where the going-in should have been — and did not spend it. Nine stoppings stood where they had been set, objects and not a rule, intervals and not payments; no tenth stood up among them before the seventh year was earned, the seventh year stood nearer its end than it had ever stood and had not given, and the loan that could hear was not called. His stroke went on over the flagstones and past the shallow bright patch kept there from another day without the half-check, the stones holding the place where the stroke had once stopped and nobody entering it, and he did not look toward the bed or ask about the room.

In the shed’s edge, where a finger’s width of glass let the afternoon in thin, Jorra Vail spoke to the wheel before he spoke to anyone. “It doesn’t care whose hands,” he said. “It cares if they’re steady.” The wheel turned on. He kept the dusty forearms and the clean ones on one tray of his attention, the grey worked small as meal past the bend of the little finger and under the nails, white in the creases where a fist had opened, not bone-coloured slip; and he asked neither which slate had been broken nor whether it was a birth-slate. The broken birth-slates lay under the lowest rack and were entered by nobody. To a pot coming round under his hands he said, “You’ll do,” in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either. He knew the full note that pot would give three turns before it left his hands and withheld the knowing from a man come up on a wrong wind who stood a moment at the shed’s edge with the sweet edge of lead glaze at one mouth-corner; the man went off up the yard with his silence taken for the silence of a man at work, and no pot was lost. The knowing went to the near-full tray beside the hearings and never among them, advanced and not paid.

When Sella’s voice went low and unfinished at the hearth — “The fire’s low. It wants—” — his bent finger went into the cold iron of the damper ahead of his hand and held there. He held it while she finished the sentence with feeding and gave its end to the pot; then the tongue moved a hair’s breadth and the finger was left standing in the iron. It was a remembered repetition, the same holding he had made on older days, not a change made again, not paired with the beat under the shelf or the half-check on the flagstones and not stood up with either into a rule. The iron was one piece with two bites, the bite that holds a vessel whole and the bite that can take the hand off a man, and there was no word in Grathic that would carry both mercies in one mouthful. He joined it to no kiln and entered it in no slate. The household straightened around his silence the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted, and nobody spoke of the waiting.

At the going of the light she carried him home up the clay road. The instruction went to the wool and to the river wind before it went anywhere near a person, and never to the basket: “Keep it off his ears,” she said, and the wind went on doing what it had been told and the basket was carried without a word. Woodsmoke lay in long low bars before her, laid one above another across the falling air, and the river wind came behind her with the taste of water over water. The two passed without taking each other’s colour, the smoke going up and the water going over, and she walked between them at her half-pace as a woman walks between two things she has been given no instruction to join.

The arm that had carried him a hundred and ninety-five mornings kept its record without a word said over it. The height of the handle, the give of the shoulder at a step’s end, the small turn of the wrist that set the basket level again — these were held in the muscle the way a surgeon’s hands hold the feel of a cavity they have packed, a record of weight and not of cost. The arm knew what it had lifted and had never been told what the lifting was worth. It did not arrive anywhere. It went on carrying.

She went wide around the pan’s gone round ring on the wall’s low place, where nothing had stood for a month and no scrape, crumb, print or chip had come; around the trough at the yard well, bare and dark with its water called weather; around the space beneath the lowest rack of the drying racks, where the broken birth-slates lay and were entered by nobody; around the empty place in the returning line where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out; and around the place the carriers had crossed with a man breathing and the pale line at his gum. Each got a look and a wide step and no number. It was a way of walking a yard that has learned not to step where a thing is missing, the way a woman goes around a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it; and she gave the yard no name for any of the places, because a name is a handle and a handle is the beginning of a cost.

Under the roof she set him on his bed and not in the Debt Loft. She put the fold right at his ear without looking at his face, left the thread at his mouth frozen and unwiped, and set the pot rim out with its handle two inches beyond the reaching hand, her hand resting a half-beat longer at the setting than the setting needed. The widow’s mark hung where it hung on its cord at her throat. A dusk was not an opening, and there had been no opening since before the kiln on Ember 1, and nobody under that roof had praised what the one opening had mended or seen what it had broken. She told the fire it was the thaw, banked the coals with the back of a broken pot, and went to her own bed. The two rooms did not touch: hers held a pot, a fire, a woman counting under her breath toward nothing entered, and his held a boy who could not close two inches and a number nobody had spoken. The wall between them kept both.

In the small hours he turned out the file the way a man lays instruments on a towel, with no towel and no instruments and no case, but the whole order of the laying. Six hearings of the full note stood beside and never among: five from four sources and the sixth out of a person as the men lifted her, filed where a thing out of a person must go and never with the sources. No seventh came and none was invented to fill the empty place, for an empty place kept empty is a finding and a finding filled to make a row come out even is a finding forged. Three near-full answers stood on their own tray, advanced and unpaid; the told bell lay beside the heard, the told figure beside the paid. Nine stoppings lay as objects and not a rule; under the floor the carried fire missed its fifth with no payer, the Seed 16 breath stood unspent at the top of every reach, and the loan was uncalled.

Three held changes stood on three places — the fraction at the iron, the beat under the shelf, the half-check on the stones — and no hand of his laid them across one another. The small dry scrape of a pot’s foot set down on loft stone stood on a fourth place; the pale line at a gum, read at two paces and worth nothing at two paces, stood on a fifth. The crust’s gap, the shelf’s two gaps, the pan’s gone ring and the field’s empty place lay on trays that did not touch, with no third gap come, no fourth likeness made a cause and no finger of his drawn across them. And now the held inch lay on a sixth place of its own, the asking and the answer of this morning, taken down before it could stand up and call itself a reaching. Each thing was laid in its own hollow and nothing rested across anything else, for a rule is an account that has found a payer and no payer stood to any of them. He had no muscle to lift the least of what he laid out, and he laid it out anyway in the dark.

The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water that came through the finger’s-width door. He asked it to stay. It held for the length of one of the breaths Sella counted at her fire in the room that did not touch this one — the small intake at the top, the hold, the letting go — and came down against the fold. It was the same holding as the morning’s and worth no more. He still could not close the two inches to the pot handle, lift his chin two inches to the crust at his ear or speak a number anybody would have known what a number counted. The body humiliated him and moved by an inch at the same time, and both were true.

One hundred and ninety-five stood exact in the dark of the wool, complete and unspent, spoken by nobody under any roof. The fire’s fifth had had no payer for a hundred and ninety-five days; the river kept its note; the boards overhead kept four nails, four and a knot the width of a thumb, and none of the three rose up and became the others. The mark hung where it hung, the charcoal under the shelf was unread, the stone lay cold in its loft pot, the Seed 16 breath was unspent, and the cost of the man carried out stood entered nowhere. Nothing had been joined, named, opened, called, spent or ended. The hand had gone up, held and come down, and the dark kept what it had been given until another day should ask for it.

— Day 196 —

Ember 17, Y.H. 813 (Seventhday)

The kiln month’s second working week went on without anybody drawing a boundary across it. The district called Tallow the firing month and Ember the kiln month, and neither name was corrected, because a name that has stood long enough stops being a question and turns into a tool, and a tool is not argued with before breakfast. Under the Vail kiln-house roof the morning came in its old order: smell first, hot clay under tallow, wet wool under that, and beneath both the sour mineral breath of the corner nobody had swept in two years; then the small sounds; then the light, which came last and had no authority over any of them. The kilns stood at their heat. The boy lay with the crust at his ear and counted what there was to count, there being nothing else and the habit having outlived the life that made it. He was one hundred and ninety-six days old and six months and sixteen days. Nobody under any roof in Low Kiln, in Sill, in Grath, in all of Sarn, said so. The house kept its days in loaves and glances and a space at the end of a row, and a day was a day whether a man had a word for it or not.

Through the floor the carried fire gave four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have closed. Its payer had been without a name for a hundred and ninety-six days. Through the finger’s-width door the river kept its note of water over water, a fifth low thing never counted among the hearings, never asked to stand up and become a sixth; overhead the boards kept four nails, four, and a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts in three places, and none of them rose up and became the others, because a man who wanted them to agree would have had to put a payer under all three and no payer stood to any of them. He laid them where they lay.
