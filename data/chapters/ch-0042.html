The Last Reading of Tallow

Days 173–179 · Tallow 24–30, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln and the Cross, Sill

The slate reads one in twelve and the house keeps its count in loaves. The box goes up the road locked and the bell goes four, four and a gap; what is read aloud is not what is paid.

— A saying of Low Kiln, spoken at the last market of Tallow

— Day 173 —

Tallow 24, Y.H. 813 (Fourthday)

Hot clay lay under the tallow and the wet wool before the light came, and under the clay the sour mineral breath of the corner nobody had swept in two years; it was the yard giving up its water to the dry cold, and it was the first thing the boy knew of the fourth day of the last tend. Under the smell came a sound that was not a bell. The kiln drew breath behind the wall in fours, and there was a gap where the fifth breath should have been, the way a sentence carries a hole in it that everybody hears and nobody fills. He lay on the bed under the Vail roof in Low Kiln, one hundred and seventy-three days old and five months and twenty-three days, and nobody under any roof said so, because there was no instruction under any roof about telling a basket a number.

The fold was at his ear and under his chin, wool gone damp where his breath had settled and frozen again in the dry heat. One stiff brown-edged crust lay two inches from his ear, gone leathery since the planting, with an empty place beside it that had no name, had never needed one, and would not call a third to keep it company. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the night and nobody had wiped it, because nobody under this roof had been given an instruction about the thread, and a basket set down was a pot set down and left. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and there was no instruction for that sound either. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the low note of water going over water beyond the finger’s-width door and came down against the fold. It could go no further.

In the dark before first grey Sella Vail gave the fire its instruction, and the pot’s, and the wool’s, and the broom’s, and the running water’s, and never once gave an instruction to the basket. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. “It’s the river wind.” To the pot she said, “Don’t set that near the door. You’ll pay for it in the thaw,” though the pot had not moved and had no door to be near, and to the broom, later, “Round it. Go around.” She said the house where another woman would have said I, and had said it so many mornings that the words had worn smooth in her mouth like a thumb-mark rubbed into a sill. At the hearth she told the carried fire it was the thaw and turned the coals with the back of a broken pot. “The house will keep it,” she said, where another woman would have said I.

She stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand and counted under her breath toward no opening, the small intake at the top, the hold, the letting go. Her eye went once to the clay jar standing cold under its glaze beside the wall, and her hand started toward it and shut half over nothing without touching it, the way a hand closes on a thing it has decided not to lift. She pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn, and the grey streak at her temple showed pale off the coals. She set the pot rim out on the board with its handle standing two inches beyond the reaching hand, and her hand rested a half-beat longer than the setting needed before it went away. She did not look toward the bed. The widow’s mark hung on its cord at her throat where it had hung since the first firing, and she did not touch it, because a morning and a shelf were not openings and the hand goes to the mark before the kiln and at no other hour.

Under the floor the fire answered her with four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, four, four and a gap, the same hole the kiln had left in its breathing behind the wall. Its payer had had no name for a hundred and seventy-three days. Through the finger’s-width door the river kept its note of water over water, a fifth low thing that had never been counted among the hearings and would not be invented to stand up as a seventh. Above the bed the boards kept four nails, four, and a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts in three places, and none of them rose up and became the others; he had spent a hundred and seventy-three days taking them down again one at a time, with nobody to help and nobody to see.

At first grey she carried him out under the roof and down the clay road to the Clay Yard at the half-pace, the pace that keeps a filled pot level and a basket clear of a slate’s open edge. The instruction went to the wool and to the wind and to the road under her feet, and never to the thing in the basket, because a basket set down is a pot set down, and a pot is told where to stand and is not asked what it wants. She kept the half-pace from every open slate at the drying racks and the well. The arm that had carried him a hundred and seventy-three mornings kept the weight of him in muscle and gave no account of the cost; he kept the count of her steps without deciding to, as he kept everything that would not leave him alone.

The yard had opened its day around the things it would not enter. At the corner where the ridge lay, the clean dark line of the old Ash was broken by a new finer hairline of grey that the district called the thaw, and the two lay side by side with nobody’s broom near either. Beneath the lowest rack the broken birth-slates lay where nobody went in, and in the returning line there was the empty place where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out, and the yard went around it with a look and a wide step and no number. At the well the trough stood bare and dark, its water called weather, and the rope on the rim was wet and stiff in the cold. Where a shallow clay pan had stood mouth-up against the wall through Reed there was now only the round ring of its gone place, which the boy kept on a tray of its own and did not lay across anything.

A rack woman come up from the worked strips with dry slip dust white in the creases of her knuckles reached past the bare board with her hand before her eyes found the basket, and went off with her pot without a word spent on what she had reached for. Two neighbours at the well settled the morning between them, one calling it a cold and the other saying there was a wet one under it, and the argument ended where such arguments end, in weather, with nobody owning either day. Sella carried past them at the half-pace, turned no head, and gave the pot and the running water the rest of what needed saying.

Tarn took the broom only after it had been given its instruction, never before. He came up over the flagstones with his chest tight from the dark and grey Ash worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line, so that no scrubbing lifted it; the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was under the roof and gave it back. “Yes, house,” he said to a woman who wanted the thaw early and again to a man who wanted it late, agreeing so entirely that no argument could find a foot, and went on wanting the late in the private part of his head because a late thaw puts off the paying, though the paying had stood in the ruts since the water went. Six were counted in the worn thumb-mark at the kindling where the house could check; the seventh stood at the very lip of the sill where it could not, and the small knife lay back at the very edge of its sill with Garl’s unforged collector sentence beside it. The sentence about an ear under this roof being worth more than the debt that holds it came up into his teeth and went no further. He drew the breath he should have spent and did not spend it. The Seed 16 breath stayed unspent at the top of each reach, with its small interval standing where a breath should have gone in and had not, and the loan that could hear was not called.

His stroke went over the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector, and went over it again, and did not once turn along it. The bristles dragged and lifted on the stone, and the grey meal of clay dust went up and settled back into the seams between the flags. No collector came. He had brought the half-check of Tallow 22 to the bright patch on the stones and let it go on without stopping, and that place stayed where it was, in a yard, and was not repeated here or made to stand for anything under a wall or a board.

The month had come into its last tend and the danger had an order and no name and could not be carried into a chart. Dust lay ground small as meal on the sill and under the racks and in the white of a knuckle when a fist opened; on a wrong wind the sweetish edge of lead glaze came up from High Kiln and lay on the tongue before the throat knew to distrust it. A wet pot set down wrong gave its high thin note before its time, and the boy filed the sound where a danger goes, not among the three near-full answers standing on their own tray advanced and not paid, and not among the six hearings of the full note either, which stood beside and never among. A note out of season was a fault in the handling and not a fourth answer to anything. No pot was lost in the yard and no lesson was made.

In the basket he kept nine stoppings where they had stood — Reed 5, 8, 11, 14, 17, 20, 23, 26 and 29 — objects and not a rule, intervals and not payments, no payer named to any, held apart by hand because a doctrine is an account that has found a payer. A tenth had not stood up out of them before the seventh year was earned, and would not stand up now to make a bill of nine. The fraction from the morning of Day 161 lay where it had been left, held and never spoken, with no payer named to it and no slate entered. Beneath the loaf shelf the beat that had stayed under the board on Day 166 stood on a place of its own and not upon the first, the reason standing between knowing the board by heart and being told not to look; no finger of his had gone beneath it this morning. And the third movement, come on Tallow 22 when the wheel-keeper’s bent finger stood in the cold iron and the clay-bound man’s broom checked half a beat behind it on the flagstones and went on without a word or a turn of the head, stood on a third place beside the other two. Three held changes, not a pair and not a doctrine, no word spoken over any and no payer named to any; he would not let them stand up together and call themselves a rule, for a rule is an account that has found a payer and no payer stood to the three. The shallow bright patch on the flagstones was a place in a yard; the waiting at the iron was a place in a wall; the beat under the board was a place in a room that did not touch either. He had not repeated one of them this morning and did not make one stand in for another.

He read the firing as a theatre he could not enter. Heat was kept and counted behind a screen and a gate of two bites, and the cost of it was paid somewhere by a man whose name was not spoken under any roof, so that the warmth arriving under the roof at dusk came as an instruction and never as an account. He had a surgeon’s eye for the kept and the counted, and the file he held was exact and worthless: an inventory that was not a key and a list that was not a hand. He was the only adult under the roof and the least use in it, with no case to begin and no instrument he could lift. The certainty was not triumph. It lay in its own hollow with the rest and bought him nothing at two paces.

The yard went on around its two unswept lines into the middle of the morning. Tarn’s broom went over the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector, and went over it again, and did not once turn along it; no collector came. Behind the wall the kiln breathed in fours with its gap; through the finger’s-width door the river kept its note of water over water, a fifth low thing never counted among the hearings; under the floor the carried fire gave four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer without a name for a hundred and seventy-three days. Overhead the boards kept four nails, four, and a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts in three places, and none rose up and became the others.

His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water through the finger’s-width door and came down against the fold. One hundred and seventy-three stood exact, complete and unspent in the dark of the wool, spoken by nobody under any roof, while the payer under the floor went on without a name and the charcoal waited under the shelf for a hand that was not his and a day that had not come.

Tallow 25, Y.H. 813 (Fifthday)

Before first grey they came up the Clay Yard with their collars high, three or four of them, and went low through the finger’s-width door of the back room. They had no names when they came and the house asked for none when they went in. The door kept its usual slit, a finger’s width and no more, and the cold came out of it in a thread and went nowhere that anybody could enter. Sella Vail carried the boy past it at the half-pace, the pace that keeps a filled pot level and a basket clear of a slate’s open edge; she turned no head and gave the house no name for the men and did not say the room was cold. The instruction went to the wool and to the river wind and to the road under her feet. “Keep it off his ears. It’s the river wind.” None went to the basket. None went to the men, who under this roof were a passage of coats through a door and nothing a slate need know.

The two tellings stood where they had stood. A man come downriver from Greybank had told one at this well with a date in it, eighteen leagues east: a counting room shut in the last war with the damper closed, and the men inside dead of the breath rather than of the war’s hand. A second man, come downriver on another morning, had told it again in the same place with the same date and the same ending, and the yard had taken both as history, the way a floor takes a spilled bucket that somebody will wipe on the morrow. The boy had kept the two tellings side by side and not touching: breath through the nose in the one, breath caught in the throat in the other, both saying not the war’s hand. No third telling had come to stand with them. Sella, who had lost a husband to that danger, had told the fire it was the thaw on both mornings and had never once described that room in the words she used for her own. He had learned early that a man can hear a thing eighteen leagues east and keep it eighteen leagues east without lying, and that a house can keep its breath in fours and a gap and call no likeness between the two.

She set him down at the edge of the light at the drying racks, base first and the weight settled the way a woman sets a vessel that must not rock, and took her hands off him without looking into the fold. One stiff brown-edged crust lay two inches from his ear and the empty place beside it had no name and had never needed one. The thread at his mouth had frozen again in the dry cold and nobody had wiped it, because nobody under any roof had been given an instruction about the thread. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the low note of water over water beyond the finger’s-width door and came down against the fold. It could go no further.

After the men had gone in and the door had shut itself to a finger’s width again, Jorra Vail came through from the Vail wheel-shed with fine grey slate dust ground small as meal into both forearms past the bent little finger and under the nails, white in the creases where a fist had opened and closed. It was not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel. Slip dried pale and flaked and could be brushed off on a thigh; this dust went in, the way a fine grit goes into the grain of a hand and stays there through a morning of washing. He kept dusty forearms and clean ones on one tray in the private file and asked neither which slate had been broken nor whether it had been a birth-slate. The two states were two states of one man’s morning, and a question put to either would have made them one thing with a cause under it. Beneath the lowest rack the broken birth-slates lay where the yard went around them and nobody went in, and that place kept its own shape, entered by none.

He spoke to the wheel before he spoke to anybody. “It doesn’t care whose hands. It cares if they’re steady.” He had said it so often that the saying had worn smooth as the seat worn hollow on the left by twenty years of another man’s leaning, and the wheel went on under it without reporting whose hands were on it. To a pot on the bench he said, “You’ll do,” in the flat voice he kept for pots, with no welcome in it and no unkindness either. Jorra knew the full note that pot would give three turns before it left his hands. The boy had learned to read that knowing the way he read a pulse in a neck: it stood on the near-full tray, beside the hearings and never among them, advanced and not paid. He did not give it to the air or to the yard. A kiln-master come up from High Kiln on the wrong wind stopped at the bench with Ash on his cuffs and the sweetish edge of lead glaze riding up from the terrace ahead of him, and listened to the pot turning, and said, “That one’s singing before its time. A pot that sings early generally sings itself out early.” He took Jorra’s silence for agreement, as a man will take a silence when he has come up a hill to say a thing and wants to go down again having said it, and went off up the yard with his saying. No pot was lost in the yard and no lesson was made of the morning. The knowing stayed where it had been put, a thing a man knew and had no hand to hand to anybody who would have known what to do with it.

Sella’s voice went low at the hearth, where the day’s instruction had been given to the fire and the end of it was still in her mouth. “The fire’s low. It wants — ” She did not finish. Jorra’s bent finger went into the cold iron of the damper and waited there for the sentence, ahead of his hand and ahead of the word. The iron took the warmth out of the fingertip and kept it, giving a little back as it warmed in the reverse of the core. He held it. This time he held it while she finished. “— feeding,” she said, and gave the end of the instruction to the pot rather than to her son. Only then did he move the tongue of iron a hair’s breadth and leave it standing. The household straightened around the silence the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted: Sella’s hand finished its half-circle with the broken pot, Tarn’s broom found its line, the wheel went on turning. Nobody had ordered the straightening and nobody had observed it. Nobody spoke of the waiting, and no payer was named to the fraction.

The boy read the iron once more as one piece with two bites, the bite that holds a vessel whole and the bite that can take the hand off a man, and there was no word in Grathic that would carry both mercies in one mouthful. He filed it where he had filed it before, an object joined to no kiln, exact and worth nothing at two paces. What had altered was not the iron but the holding: a hand kept at the cold until a sentence had been allowed to finish, the keeping remembered in the holding and not in any word. It was the same holding he had watched on other mornings, and its coming round again did not make it a change twice over or a third. A thing repeated is a thing kept. He had spent every day of his life taking likenesses down one at a time so that they would not stand up and call themselves a truth, and he took this one down now without a word, with nobody to help and nobody to see.

Tallow 26, Y.H. 813 (Sixthday)

Midday stood in the Clay Yard with the heat held in the clay and nothing to show for it but the dry smell and the lifting of the oiled paper at the windows of the Vail kiln-house. Sella Vail carried the boy at the half-pace, the pace that keeps a filled pot level and a basket clear of a slate’s open edge, and gave the instruction to the wool and the running water and the wind that came up off the river. “Keep it off his ears. It’s the river wind.” None of it went to the basket. A basket set down is a pot set down and left, and nobody under any roof had been given an instruction about what goes on inside one. The crust lay stiff and leathery two inches from his ear with its empty place beside it still unnamed and no third; the thread of drool at his mouth had frozen again and nobody had wiped it. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water beyond the finger’s-width door and came down against the fold. It could go no further.

At the well the rope on the rim was wet and stiff in the cold even at this hour, and the trough stood bare and dark with the water in it called weather by those who did not taste it. A neighbour come up from the worked strips with a pot just out of the tubs held it at arm’s length so that the water should not slop on her coat, and asked what a firing costs set down in the Vail house. The question was shaped like a fist and opened at the end, as such questions do; the woman stood with wet sleeves folded across her belly waiting for a figure the way a person waits at a counter for a thing that has been promised.

Sella’s eye went once, and only once, to the clay jar standing cold under its glaze beside the wall. Her hand started toward it, shut half over nothing, and came away. She did not touch the jar and did not say what was in it or what it had cost or what a number set down in a house might be thought to mean. “Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there,” she said. “The house has said it.”

The question went away level. Nothing slopped, nothing was priced, nothing was refused. The woman carried it off in the arm that carried the pot, and the answer went with her in the manner of an answer given as a direction: it arrived nowhere and left no entry, and was nevertheless an answer, exact and not spendable. The boy watched the going-away of it from the low angle of the basket and filed the shape beside the other answers that had had their questions taken out of them, keeping it from standing up among them into a cause, for a cost asked in one country and answered in another is still a cost, and a direction is the only change a house will make to a figure before it hands the figure back.

A second neighbour, come up with a bucket that had a skin of water on it, called the morning a cold. A third, who had been raking at the kiln mouth and had dry dust in the whites of her knuckles, said there was a wet one under it. They stood a moment, each with her own weather in her mouth, the one wanting the frost to hold and the other wanting the thaw, and the argument ended where such arguments end, in the weather itself, with nobody owning either day and nobody able to put a figure to either. The thaw was a hairline of finer grey beside the clean dark line where the Ash ridge had turned, and the yard had gone around both for a hundred and seventy-five mornings without sweeping either or naming either as a number.

Tarn took the broom only after the house had given it its instruction. He did not look toward the basket and the boy did not look toward him; the two silences under this roof kept their old shape and nobody put a word between them, as nobody had for a hundred and seventy-five days. His stroke went over the shallow bright patch on the flagstones and over the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector, and did not once turn along it. No collector came. Six were counted in the worn thumb-mark at the kindling where the house could check and the seventh stood at the lip of the sill where it could not, the small knife laid back at the very edge with the unforged collector sentence beside it. His chest had been tight since the dark; Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line and no scrubbing lifted it, and the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back. He said nothing of any of it, and the boy entered nothing on his account. An interval kept is an interval and not a payment; a stopped sentence is not a sentence spoken; a rule is an account that has found a payer, and no payer stood to any of these.

At the drying racks the women went around the clean dark line where the old Ash ridge turned and the new finer grey called the thaw, and around the space beneath the lowest rack where the broken birth-slates lay, and around the empty place in the returning line where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out, with a look and a wide step and no number. Dry slip dust lay in the shadows under the slats, fine as meal, and came up pale in the creases of a fist when a woman opened it. Lead glaze had left its sweetish edge on a wrong wind off High Kiln and gone; the smell went and came and was never entered in anything a slate could read. A pot set down wrong would sing its high thin note before its time. The boy filed that sound, when it came, as a danger of the month and not as a fourth near-full answer, the way he had filed it on other mornings, keeping the hearing tray clean of a thing that wanted only to be feared.

He watched the turns the way another man reads a face: not by what it says but by what it goes around. A space entered by nobody is a space with a shape, and a shape is a thing a man can keep without having a word for it; he had kept this one for a hundred and seventy-five days without letting it stand up and call itself a cause. Three likenesses standing in a row will call themselves a truth if a man leaves them standing, and he had spent his whole life taking them down one at a time, with nobody to help and nobody to see.

Under the Vail roof the afternoon came into the kiln-house along the low angle where the underside of the loaf shelf becomes a roof over the basket. Sella set him down with her back to him and did not look into the fold. She moved her lips along the loaf row, counting what the house kept in loaves and glances and a space at the row’s end nobody called a number. A rack woman come up from the strips with a wet pot said two bare boards and a child’s appetite were the same hunger in the end, putting the shelf’s old gap and the empty place at his ear in one breath as if a man might put two fingers into one wound and call it one. Sella heard the saying and gave nobody a figure. She gave the woman the weather and the river and the pot between them, and carried the unanswered question away level so that nothing slopped. Two sayings were two sayings and not one absence.

The shelf stood with its two unnamed gaps: the old space at the row’s end and the palm’s breadth that came in on the night of Seed 6. The one stiff brown-edged crust in the fold kept its empty place beside it and had no name and no third. The shallow pan’s gone place kept its round ring on a tray of its own, and the field’s empty place was where the yard had gone around it. Four absences stood in four rooms and on four trays and none lay across another. No third gap came into the fold, no fourth likeness stood up and made itself a cause, and no finger of his drew the line from one to the next, because a better-lit room had taught him what three likenesses in a row will do to a man who wants an answer. He had spent a hundred and seventy-five days keeping the trays apart with nobody to help and nobody to see, and the keeping was the whole of the discipline and the discipline was older than any word in this country.

From the low angle the charcoal marks waited under the shelf’s front edge in clusters pressed hard or faded nearly into the grain. Sella did not reach beneath the board this day. Her finger stayed where it was and went to the pot and the fire instead, and the beat that had stayed under the board on the morning of Day 166 stood where it had been left, held and unspoken, with no payer named to it and no slate entered. The boy did not read the marks, did not count them, did not copy them. And the third movement, come on Tallow 22 when the wheel-keeper’s bent finger stood in the cold iron and the clay-bound man’s broom checked half a beat behind it on the flagstones and went on without a word or a turn of the head, stood on a third place beside the other two — three held changes, not a pair and not a doctrine, no word spoken over any and no payer named. He would not let them stand up together and call themselves a rule, because a rule is an account that has found a payer and no payer stood to any of the three.

The two tellings of a counting room shut in the war stood side by side with no third, and the told bell stood beside the heard bell, four, four and a gap, with nobody crossing himself and nobody asking why the bell went longer than a church bell. Neither telling had produced a room. The sixth green was a colour joined to no ear and the foundling a shape with no sale, offstage and unspent, no loaf passing near the basket and no slate opened within reach of it; a colour is a colour and a shape is a shape, and a man who lays one across the other has made a cause where there were only two things seen.

In the other room the one stiff brown-edged crust lay leathery two inches from his ear with its empty place beside it still unnamed and no third. The loaf shelf kept two unnamed gaps, the old space at the row’s end and the palm’s breadth that came in on the night of Seed 6, and the charcoal waited under the front edge for a hand that was not his and a day that had not come. The shallow pan’s gone place kept its round ring on a tray of its own and the field’s empty place stayed where the yard had gone around it; neither was laid across the shelf’s two gaps or the fold’s crust and its gap, and the sixth green was not laid across any of them. No third gap came, no fourth likeness was made a cause and no finger of his drew the line. He had learned early that three likenesses in a row will stand up and call themselves a truth if a man leaves them standing, and had spent a hundred and seventy-nine days taking them down one at a time, with nobody to help and nobody to see.

Through the finger’s-width door the river kept its note of water over water, a fifth low thing never counted among the hearings and never invented to stand up as a seventh; through the floor the carried fire gave four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer without a name for a hundred and seventy-nine days; overhead the boards kept four nails, four, and a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts in three places, and none rose up and became the others. Ash lay thin along the skirting board in the colour of the clean dark line and the new finer grey called the thaw lay beside it, neither swept at this hour and neither with an instruction. Cold arrived cheek, temple, the small bare joint at the wrist where the wool rode back, and stopped there as if it had reached the end of its instruction; the afterdrop waited on the far side of a warming nobody would perform.

He wanted the crust, wanted the breast, wanted the pot handle standing two inches beyond his hand, wanted a number spoken by somebody who would have known what a number counted, and got none of them. The body wanted small and immediate things, the crust and the breast and the warm joint of a hand; the register wanted the figure that would set them in their places, and neither had a mouth under this roof. He had stopped expecting to be told, which was not the same as not wanting it. The figure read was not the figure paid; the box had gone up the clay road locked; the cords and the knot-string and the second twist stayed where they were carried, where no Court thumb would read them; the clerk stayed set at the out-of-line landing for a measure nobody called; the sixth green stayed a colour and the foundling a shape. One hundred and seventy-nine stood exact, complete and unspent in the dark of the wool, spoken by nobody under any roof. The surgeon’s register read the whole of it as an inventory that is not a key and a list that is not a hand — exact, complete and worth nothing at two paces, and he was the only adult in the room and the least use in it.

His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water through the finger’s-width door and came down against the fold. Nothing was joined, named, opened, called, spent or ended. The firing month went out without a boundary drawn and the count went forward into Ember with the fire’s fifth still missing, the ninth stopping still nine with no tenth, the Seed 16 breath still held, the three changes still on their three places and the charcoal still waiting under the shelf for a hand that was not his and a day that had not come.

The Chronicle of Chapter 42

Seven days the firing month took to die, and nobody drew a boundary around them. The kilns stood at their last heat; hot clay gave up its water to the dry cold and lead glaze’s false sweetness rode a wrong wind. Sella Vail gave her instruction to the wool, the fire, the pot, the broom and the running water, never to the basket, said the house where another woman would say I, set the pot handle two inches beyond the reaching hand, and left the widow’s mark where it hung, for a morning and a shelf and a dusk are not openings. A neighbour who asked a firing’s cost was sent to ask the pot; the question went away level, nothing priced, nothing refused. The boy kept what the yard would not: the kiln’s breath in fours with a gap, the carried fire’s four pulls and its missing fifth, the river’s fifth note through the finger’s-width door, and the boards’ four nails and a thumb-width knot. Three counts in three places, and none became the others.

Before first grey men went low into the back room and were given no name. Jorra Vail came through with slate dust ground small as meal into both forearms, told a pot it would do and kept its full note from a High Kiln kiln-master; no pot was lost. While Sella’s instruction waited unfinished at the hearth, his bent finger stood in the cold iron until the sentence reached the pot. Tarn wanted the late thaw because it puts off the paying; six were counted where the house could check and the seventh held where it could not. The sentence about an ear under that roof being worth more than the debt came to his teeth and went no further. No collector came.

On the last day of Tallow, the third Account-day, Sella set the basket in the moot-post’s finger’s width of shade where no slate lay and stood half a pace off. Berr Clay wiped his thumb twice, passed a finger over his slate and read one in twelve, as the notice says. The levy is called. It is not called by me, he said, and your house, never I, and went off with the box locked and the true subtraction inside, believing he had done a kindness. The bell went four, four and a gap, and nobody crossed himself. Hessa Rale carried the slate to Greybank unread: I don’t read it. I carry it. There’s a difference. The figure read aloud was still not the figure paid. At the going of the light Sella carried him home and set him on his bed. In the small hours the file lay exact and worthless. One hundred and seventy-nine stood complete and unspent in the dark of the wool. The fire missed its fifth, the charcoal waited under the shelf for a hand that was not his and a day that had not come, and the month went out without a boundary drawn.
