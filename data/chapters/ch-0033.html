Six at the Teeth

Days 138–140 · Reed 19–21, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, the Weir and Sill

The second Account-day comes with the reed month in its last tend. Read one in twelve where the notice says it and the true subtraction goes into the box with the key turned; the heard bell stands beside the told bell and a fifth green at a mouth is a colour and not a name. Hold a word six times at the teeth and it is a held word still, for a thing held six times is owed and not paid.

— A proverb of Low Kiln, said over the banked fire

— Day 138 —

Reed 19, Ninthday, Y.H. 813

The reed month stood in its last tend on the morning of Day 138, and nobody drew a boundary at its entering, the way nobody had drawn one at its coming-in or would draw one at its going-out, because the year in Low Kiln was counted in work and not in edges. The boy was one hundred and thirty-eight days old, four months and eighteen days, and nobody under any roof said so; if a woman had said the number aloud over the tubs, nobody listening would have known what it counted. Sella Vail lifted the basket from under the Vail kiln-house roof with the little-door burn closing first on the rim and then on nothing, and carried him down at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, woodsmoke going before her and the river wind coming up to meet it. She gave the instruction to the wool and to the wind and never to the basket. “Keep it off his ears. It’s the river wind.” It had come all the way down with them and it would go all the way back up, and that was the whole of what she said about it.

Under the wool his head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and nobody under any roof had been given an instruction about that sound either. The basket held him the way a pot holds what must not be slopped, rim level and the weight settled in the middle, and his small hand outside the fold kept going up an inch and coming down, not toward the bundle going past on a man’s shoulder but toward the direction of the water, which was the only direction he had. A trough by the well’s edge carried a skin of ice that had not stood there at morning and would be gone by morning, and every woman who stepped around it called it weather and went on with her wrists in the mud. The grey streak at her temple showed pale where her hair had been pushed back with the wrist that carries the burn, and the cold iron of a bracket at his cheek took the cold out of it and kept it, and neither thing was spoken of.

The yard had the grey of the season on it, not frost and not yet mud, a dry skin worked into the creases of wrists at the tubs, pale where the frost had bitten the ends of the reeds. The last bundles came downriver in armfuls judged by the arm that carried them, going down in the morning and coming back at the going of the light the way the water went, only with a man’s back under it. No slate stood in the ruts to be set against a bundle. The boy had learned in a hundred and thirty-eight days that a thing counted in the arm that carried it is not the same thing as a thing entered on a slate, and the distinction was exact and worth nothing he could hand to anybody. The surgeon’s register in him noted the order of the morning without asking to be paid: first the hand to the bundle, then the hand to the arm that had carried it, then the mouth to the weather. He had once known every instrument on a towel by the wear on its handle and could say which had been used and which had not; he could not close two inches of his hand on the rim of the basket two paces off. Both were true. The second was louder, and he had stopped expecting the first to make the second any smaller.

A cutter came up out of the reed beds with a bundle on his shoulder and the dry mud of the beds to his elbows, and a girl with a creel stopped him where the boards crossed the ruts. She asked him what went into a bundle that size.

He set the bundle down to rest his back, and the girl set her creel against her hip and waited. “Lift it,” he said. “Your arm’ll tell you what went into it, and that’s the whole of what it knows.”

She lifted it. It was heavier than it looked, and she let it down again with the weight still in her arm, and asked what the hands would want for cutting it.

“It doesn’t know cost,” he said. “Ask it how heavy it is.”

He took the bundle back onto his shoulder and went along the boards with it, and the girl stood with the creel against her hip and her arm still holding the shape of the weight. Another cutter coming back with an armful gave her the same answer in other words: the reed was in its last tend, the beds wanted what they wanted, and the hands would be paid when the weather had had enough. Neither gave her a figure. The question went on standing where they had left it, the way a pot stands where somebody has set it down and gone off and left it standing.

The boy took the two answers apart as another man might have taken an instrument to pieces to see what it was made of. An arm knows weight and not cost; a direction is not an answer and it is not a refusal; a question asked of the weather stays a question asked of the weather. Each was whole and each was worth nothing he could set on a slate, and he kept them on three places and did not lay one across another. This was the whole of the arithmetic the yard would give, and it was performed in the open where anybody could hear it, with no thumb upon it.

At the racks a woman wiped her forearm across her brow and left the dry mud in the crease of it, and asked nobody in particular what the cutters’ hands would be paid now the reed was in its last tend. A man going past with an armful told her the weather and the river. “Ask them,” he said. “They’ll tell you when they’ve had enough.” A woman at the next rack said the river had been low and would be lower before the tend was out, which was a kind of figure but not one anybody could have set on a slate; a third, tying a strip, said the second Account-day stood on the morrow and that the block would read what the block would read, and went on with her strip. No figure came out of any of it. The woman who had asked shut two fingers out of a fist, the way a body shuts a door against a draught, and went back to her strips with the two fingers still shut, carrying the unanswered question away level so nothing slopped.

Sella carried the boy past at the half-pace and gave the instruction to the wool and to the river wind and never to the basket, and did not turn her head toward the question. The house had said it where a figure might have stood, and the house’s saying went where it went and left no entry behind it.

The half-pace went around what the broom went around. At the corner beside the kiln-house the clean dark line where the ridge of Ash had turned lay where it had lain since the grey was burned and called dealt with, and beside it the new finer hairline of grey lay finer than the old, which the house called the thaw and said would go when it goes. She stood over the two lines a moment with her eyes on neither and swept nothing, because a morning in the yard is not an opening and she had no broom in her hand to instruct. The widow’s mark hung where it hung on its cord at her throat and she did not touch it, because a rack and a strip and a basket are not openings.

At the lowest rack she went around the space beneath it as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. The broken birth-slates lay under there where they had lain through the planting and through the cutting, gone around by every woman working the racks and entered by nobody, keeping their dark the way a closed hand keeps what it will not show. The boy saw only the dark under the lowest rail and the pale tips the frost had bitten beyond it; he had learned that a place gone around is a place kept, and that a thing kept under a rack is not a thing entered on a slate, and the two are different orders of keeping with no standing between them. He did not lay them together.

Beyond the strips the returning line of cutters came back with their bundles on their shoulders, and there was an empty place in it that the line went around the way water goes around a stone standing in it. A hand had gone in there on Seed 15 and had not come out with the last hands on Seed 27, and nobody in the yard gave it a name, a cause, a body, a figure or a day. A woman looked at the place a moment longer than a place requires and stepped wide around it; a man following her did the same without looking at all, his armful tilted to clear it, and neither said what the step was for. The boy filed the look and the wide step on a place of their own, not joined to the dark under the rack or to the shelf’s two gaps in the other room, and entered no number against them. The yard went around the place and went on, and the place kept what it kept.

One of the cutters came along the boards past the basket with a short length of grey cord inside his coat, gone grey with the oil of a working thumb, the grey a stitch-hole takes after a thread has been drawn through it a dozen times. He took the cord out and straightened it on his thumb where no slate lay, turned it once so the knots stood in the small light, tied one low with his teeth and a pull of the thumb, bit the end off and put the cord back inside his coat, drawing the coat closed over it with the flat of his hand. He did not count it where anyone could hear him count and gave nobody a figure to carry away, and went along the boards with his coat closed over it. The boy saw the cord go past and did not see what it held, and did not lay it beside the charcoal under the shelf in the other room, or beside the empty place in the returning line, or beside the two cords the eel-woman keeps under her apron and the knot-string on the weir-woman’s board. Four private keepings stood within a dozen paces of the basket in as many days, all visible and none enterable; he kept them on separate places and wrote no cause across any of them.

❦

By midday the last bundles had gone down and come back and the reeds stood grey in the ruts uncounted, and the women at the racks had gone back to their strips with two fingers shut out of a fist. Sella set the basket down for a moment at the edge of the work where no slate lay and gave the instruction to the wool and to the river wind and never to the basket, and stood her half-pace from every open slate. The carrying arm kept its account in muscle, registering every lift of the morning from the roof to the racks and back, what had been held and how long and how the weight had shifted where the boards crossed the ruts. It was exact and complete and told nothing of what any of it had cost. The cost lived in another room: the grey at her temple showing pale off the coals at the going of the light, the half-beat over the basket, the hand that rested longer than the setting needed. The boy had no standing to enter any of it.

He had learned that a record kept in a carrying arm tells what was carried and not what it cost, and that this is a true record and worth nothing anyone could pay. He had once laid out instruments on a towel in a white room and known each by the wear on its handle, and could say which had been used and which had not; he could not have said what the using had cost the man under the sheet, and here he could read the arm that carried him and not the grey it left behind. The surgeon in him noted the order of reaching — hand to the bundle before hand to the girl, hand to the cord before eyes to the road, hand to the strip before mouth to the question — as he had once noted the covering of a field before the instrument came down, and had no standing to enter it anywhere. The child in him was cold at the wrist and wanted the wool pulled up. Both were true and the second was louder, and neither had a hand to spend on the other.

Below the yard the the Ilm went over its stones with the note of water over water, a fifth low thing never counted among the six hearings of the full note he kept on a tray where nothing was laid across anything else. No Court thumb would read the arithmetic of this yard. It stayed in arms and weather and in the space where a figure should have stood, and there it would stay, exact and complete and worth nothing.

The small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water and came down against the fold. He could not reach the bundle going past on the shoulder, or the pot at the rack end, or the dark under the lowest rail, or the empty place in the returning line. The count went on going without anybody’s thumb upon it. One hundred and thirty-eight exact and unspent in the grey reed light, while the woman at the racks went off with two fingers shut out of a fist and the reeds stood grey in the ruts and were not counted.

❦

At the going of the midday the half-pace turned off the ruts and under the roof of the Vail kiln-house in Low Kiln, through the door that kept its finger’s width of open and shut nothing out, and the light changed from the grey of the yard to the brown of a room kept out of the wind. Sella Vail gave the instruction to the wool before she gave it to anything else — keep it off his ears, it was the river wind — and then to the pot on the bench, and to the broom standing where the broom was told to stand, and to the fire lying under its blind ash, and never once to the boy in the basket. A pot set down was a pot left where it was put, and the basket was a pot set down. Woodsmoke went in ahead of her; wet wool went with her; under both lay the faint sour mineral tang of the Ash that had lived in the corners of this house for two years and would not be swept.

She set him low and a little back against the bench leg, at the angle at which a man lying sees the underside of a board where a man standing sees only its top. It was an angle she had chosen every day of his life without once knowing she had chosen it; he had come to know it as the place where the house showed him what it did not show a person upright. the loaf shelf ran above him with its row kept the way a house keeps its days badly — loaves pushed along, the hard heels of them going toward the wall, and spaces where a loaf’s width should have stood. There were two of those spaces. The old one sat at the row’s end and had stood there longer than he had been under this roof; the second was a palm’s breadth that had come in on the night of Seed 6 with no scrape, no crumb, no print and no word, and had gone around the house’s mouth ever since as a thing nobody named. The front edge of the board lay level with his eyes and its underside opened above him like a low roof seen from beneath, the grain running the length of the room with the dark of the afternoon held in it.

The cold iron of the bench bracket was at his cheek. He had been set so near it that the cold came through the wool and would not give, and he lay with the small bare joint of his cheek against it while the grey light went over the top of the shelf where his eyes could not follow. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and nobody under this roof had been given an instruction about the sound. The thread at his mouth had frozen on the walk in off the yard and would stay frozen until somebody was told to wipe it, which nobody had been. A pot set down was left; a child set down was left; the two were the same act performed on different contents and he had long since stopped asking the difference to be spoken.

Above him she stood with her back to the basket and moved her lips along the row. It was not a counting any ear could take. The lips went along the loaves, stopped at the spaces, went on, the way a hand goes over a bare board and closes on the loaf beyond it. She glanced once at the clay jar that stood cold under its glaze on the wall shelf, and gave nobody a figure. The jar had come into the house when he was a month old and he had watched her glance at it for a hundred and thirty-eight days and had never once watched her touch it; whatever it held was held without a number, and the glance was the whole of the house’s account of it. She pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carried the little-door burn, the grey streak at her temple showing pale in the light off the coals — the streak she said had come in the winter Garl Vail died and would not discuss beyond that — and told the fire something low that was an instruction and not an account. She told the fire it was the thaw.

The dry warm smell of the shelf — bread and dust and clay carried past it a thousand times — lay over the room. For one breath it was crossed by another smell, sharper, mineral, a little bitter: charcoal, the charred stick drawn along the underside of the board and left there. It came from where the marks lay, under the board where nobody thought to look for a keeping, and it went as quickly as it had come, leaving the bread smell where it had been. He had once known the smell of a field in the moment before the cloth went over it, which is the smell of a thing done deliberately and then not spoken of; the knowing came into this room with him and found its use here, and he had no standing to enter it anywhere.

She did not look down at the board. That was the first thing he noted, because he lay at the angle where the underside was all there was to look at and her face was turned the other way, toward the row and the jar and the wall. Without a word she reached a hand behind her and down; the little-door burn on the back of it closed on the wood’s front lip and opened again as the fingers went under. He saw the knuckle set against the grain and the thumb come up, the way a hand goes under a shelf to feel for what it knows is there, not to find out what is there. The finger went under far enough for the knuckle to set. It stayed a breath out and came back. The burn closed on nothing as the hand withdrew, and she did not go back to the shelf.

From the low angle he saw the hand go under and come back and saw nothing of what the marks said. He had seen them before from this same angle — short dragged clusters a finger’s breadth apart, some black as a fresh coal and pressed hard enough to leave a smooth track where the stick had dug, some rubbed to the colour of old smoke and gone almost into the grain, faintest toward the row’s end as if the hand that made them had gone away from the task or grown tired of pressing. They did not make Grathic words. No slate had been set under them to catch what they meant. He did not count the strokes now, any more than he had counted them on the first Account-day or on any day after; he did not press a thumb where her thumb had been, or lay the underside of the board beside the two gaps above it, or lay either of them beside the one stiff crust lying two inches from his ear in the room the shelf did not touch. He did not make a cause of the habit. A hand that goes under a board without looking either knows the board by heart or has been told not to look, and the reason stood between the two and was not entered. The care of the hiding told more than the writing would, and the writing was not his to read; so he kept the care and the marks on a place of their own and left both where they lay.

A saying of the loaf-shelves of Low Kiln held that two gaps stay two gaps until a finger lays them together and an account then wants a name. The saying stood in the room with the two gaps and went unfulfilled, as it had gone unfulfilled every afternoon since the palm’s breadth came in on the night of Seed 6. Neighbours had laid the old space and the empty place at a child’s ear under one cause in a single breath and had gone off with their pots without seeing they had joined two rooms; he had heard the sayings and kept each on its own place, and had not made any of them his own. Now there was a third place under the shelf, and a finger had been under it and come away without a word, and still nothing touched. The keeping was exact and invisible and was the only work in this house that nobody had asked him to do.

He knew the weight of a paper turned face-down on a table, because what was written beneath it was meant for one reader and not the room, and he knew that the turning of it mattered more than the words. That was a knowing with no hand to spend it on. Here the hand went under without the paper, without the table, without so much as a look to see whether anyone was watching the shelf — a second account kept beneath the first, in a material no Court thumb could spell, on a board a child lay under and a woman stood over with her back turned. He set the hand beneath the board on the tray where the other record-first hands stood — the midwife reaching for the slate before the child, the thane wiping his thumb twice before he touched nothing, the clerk setting his figure down before he looked at the face, the woman at the bare board taking her loaf — and did not let it become a cause. Five likenesses in a row will stand up and call themselves a doctrine if nobody holds them apart, and a doctrine is an account that has found a payer. No payer had been named to any of these, no figure had been read, and no finger in the district had laid the marks beneath the shelf across the gaps above it; so he held them apart, which is what a man does with instruments on a towel before he loses the use of his hands.

The surgeon in him noted the order of the afternoon as he had once noted the order of a field being made ready — hand to the record before hand to the person, word to the fire before word to the child, the back turned before the hand went under — and had no standing to enter any of it. The child in him was cold at the cheek and wanted the wool pulled up over the small bare joint, and wanted the crust two inches from his ear in the other room, and wanted somebody to say the number of his days; the surgeon knew that wanting a thing and being able to reach it were two different orders of fact, and that neither order had a hand in this room but hers. Both were true and the second was louder, and he had stopped expecting the first to make the second any smaller.

A neighbour come up from the strips with dry mud worked into the creases of her wrists let herself in behind Sella and stood with a wet pot against her hip. She did not look into the basket; baskets were not looked into. “What’ll the row want before the tend’s out?” she asked, and asked it of the room, or of the board, or of Sella’s back, and not of any person standing there. It was a question about a figure wearing the coat of a question about the weather, and the house heard it as both.

Sella did not turn from the shelf. “Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there,” she said. “The house has said it.”

The woman shifted the pot from one hip to the other, and the water in the creases of her wrists went dark again where her sleeve rubbed them. “The pot doesn’t answer back.”

“No,” Sella said. “But it holds what you give it. More than most.”

The neighbour made a small sound that was neither agreement nor argument, and set the pot down on the bench where the pot was told to stand, and stood a moment with her hands on her hips looking at the row as if the row might yet volunteer a number. It did not. She took the pot up again and went back down toward the strips with the question still in her mouth and the pot held level against her so nothing slopped, and neither of them entered anything. A cost asked in the form of a direction had been answered with a direction, and the answer had gone home with her and left no figure standing in the room.

The boy watched her go and set her question on the place where the yard’s other unanswered questions stood — the reeds judged by the carrying arm, the hands that went in by the look, the weather, the river — and did not make a sum of them. The second Account-day stood on the morrow at Reed 20, and nobody in this room had said the date; the tend was in its last, and the question of what the row would want went out of the door ahead of the woman and down toward the strips, still wearing the weather’s coat, with no figure in it and no thumb upon it.

The widow’s mark hung at Sella’s throat where it hung, a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and she did not touch it. A shelf and an afternoon were not openings, and no opening had come under this roof in a hundred and thirty-eight days. The clean dark line where the Ash ridge had turned lay along the skirting board with the new finer hairline of grey beside it, and neither had been swept; the house called both the thaw and said the grey would go when it went. She told the fire it was the thaw, turned the coals with the back of a broken pot, fed them, and stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand counting under her breath toward no opening while the grey streak at her temple showed pale and the little-door burn shut on the pot’s edge and then on nothing. Whatever she counted went toward no opening and was not shared with the room, and the boy did not ask to be told what it counted.

Above him the shelf kept its two gaps — the old space at the row’s end and the palm’s breadth that had come in on the night of Seed 6 — and beneath it the charcoal marks lay pressed into the grain where the finger had been and come away without a word. The room that held the shelf and the room that held the crust and its empty place were two rooms, and neither touched the other. He lay at the low angle with the cold iron at his cheek and his hand outside the wool going up an inch toward the note of water coming through the finger’s width of door and coming down against the fold. One hundred and thirty-eight days old, four months and eighteen days, he kept the count exact and unspent and spoken by nobody, and the marks under the board waited for a hand that was not his and a day that had not come.

— Day 139 —

Reed 20, Tenthday, Y.H. 813

The second Account-day of Reed came up over the Cross the way a pot comes to the boil with its lid not quite on — a small sound, a smell, a leaning of everybody toward the same boards — and nobody in Low Kiln said the date aloud, though the thin finger of shade under the moot-post had fallen across the same ground on the tenth of Ilmen, the tenth of Silt and the tenth of Reed already, and would fall again on the thirtieth with nine days between. The boy was one hundred and thirty-nine days old, four months and nineteen days, and a woman saying the number over the pig rail would have been answered with a look, because a day is a small vessel and no house in the district counts what a child has lived through the way a Court counts embers. Sella Vail carried him down at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, woodsmoke going before her out of the clay road and the river wind coming up to meet it, and gave the instruction to the wool and to the wind and never to the basket. “Keep it off his ears. It’s the river wind.” It had come down with them and it would go back up with them, and that was the whole of what she said about the morning.

Under the wool his head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far. The crowd leaned over the reading block the way the pot leans with its lid not quite on: shoulders forward, chins down, the backs of coats making one long brown roof with a gap in it where the slate would be held. Eight pigs went over the wet rail behind them with a smell of warm mud and bristle, and an assessor from Sill wrote them into a slate held out of a rain that was not falling, his hand going to the writing before his eyes went to the rail. The boy set that order on a place of its own and did not ask it to stand for anything beyond itself. In a hundred and thirty-nine days he had learned that the hand is sent to the record before the eyes are sent to the person, and that the habit is neither honesty nor deceit but a way of keeping the figure where the figure belongs, which is under a thumb and not in the mouth of a room.

She set the basket in the moot-post’s shade where no slate lay, a hand’s breadth in from the edge of the boards, and stood a half-pace off with the little-door burn shut on nothing. The cold iron of the post’s bracket took the cold out of his cheek and kept it. The thread at his mouth had frozen on the walk down and nobody under any roof had been given an instruction about the thread, so it stayed where it had frozen. Across the square the pot board kept its shallow shelf with dust in the hollows, the light going along the rims, and beyond it the market stalls stood with their reed bundles and their eel tubs and the smell of wet wool and river silt laid over all of it, the last reeds of the month going off in armfuls judged by the arm that carried them.

Berr Clay came up short off the clay road a little before the top of the morning, with the red wool cord of his office folded square under his coat instead of worn across it and the green of tasted glaze at one corner of his mouth. Hot clay went in ahead of him, and over it the smell of nothing at all, which is the most expensive smell in Grath and the one this district has learned not to question. His hands were large and clean; he wiped his thumb twice on his apron before he touched nothing, as a man wipes an instrument before setting it down, and held a narrow slate unopened against his forearm with the writing turned toward his own ribs. He passed a finger over the figure and let his eyes rest on the writing a breath longer than the writing required. Then he read.

“One in twelve, as the notice says,” he said low into the goers-by, and the crowd leaned in, because a low voice makes a crowd lean, and a man who does not raise his voice in a moot is more frightening than a man who shouts. “The levy is called. It is not called by me. If your house wants it again, there’ll be a slate at the watch.” Your house. Never I. He took himself out of his own sentence as neatly as a surgeon takes his hand out of a field before the dressing goes on, and the sentence stood afterward with nobody inside it, which is the way this district likes its arithmetic to stand.

The figure went into the air over the pig rail and not into any house. The true subtraction sat in a locked box up the clay road in a house set toward High Kiln; no thumb at the Cross had been near the box this morning, and no voice had given the difference between what was read and what was paid. A figure read aloud is not the figure paid. The boy set the one on the boards under the moot-post and the other in a room up the road whose door he had never seen opened, and drew no line between them. He had the use of neither a thumb nor a slate, and the keeping of two figures apart was the only honest work a child in a basket could do.

Berr let the slate lie a moment against his forearm after the reading, the finger still where it had passed over the figure, and looked neither into the basket nor toward the woman standing across the square. He closed his clean hand on the slate’s edge and went off toward the stalls with the cord folded square under his coat, the narrow slate under his arm and the green at his mouth where it had been. The boy watched the large clean hands go and set the twice-wiped thumb on the tray where the other record-first hands stood, and did not let it take its place at the head of them. The square gave a performance of arithmetic: a figure carried from a slate to a mouth to the air above the pigs, and the true subtraction carried nowhere any ear could follow. He could read the performance and could not enter it. That was the whole of the morning’s difference and nobody in it knew there was a difference to be found.

Across the square a woman stood at the pot board looking at the pots. She said nothing and nobody spoke to her; she stood the way a pot stands where it has been set, with her weight settled and her attention elsewhere. Clay stood under her nails in the small crescents, and a pale line went across the back of one hand where something had once been worn tight and long enough to leave its mark behind when it was taken off. The sweetish green of lead glaze lay in the crease beside her lip, not painted on but tasted there, the way Berr’s green sat at his own mouth corner. She turned a cup a quarter with the fingers of one hand, not lifting it, so the light went along the rim and came off it; she held it there a breath; then she set it back exactly where it had stood, the handle pointing the way it had pointed before she came.

The boy took the green into his file without taking the woman with it. He had no name for her and did not make one, and no voice in him supplied a face for a colour to belong to. She was a mouth across a square and a hand with a pale line on the back of it and a cup put down where it had been found, three observations lying side by side and not laid across one another. The colour was green. That was all it was. He had filed the green at Silt 30, the green at Seed 28, the green at Reed 7 and the fourth at Reed 15, each on a place of its own, and Berr’s own glaze sat beside them on another place because a man’s mouth is not the same object as a woman’s, even when the colour in the crease of one is the colour at the corner of the other. This was the fifth green at a mouth and the sixth shape of the colour. Five mouths, six shapes, no name, no ear, no cord-mark, no pots carried up the road and no box opened up the clay road. A colour is not a face and a likeness is not a cause; the fact that a pale line crossed the back of the woman’s hand and that Berr’s thumb went twice over a figure did not make a joining, because the district was full of hands and only a payer makes an account of them.

The boy read the foot in the order his trade had taught him for any cold extremity: the part before the whole, the surface before the centre. The extremity goes before the core. The small vessels at the surface close down first and the blood goes where the warm organs can use it, so that a foot is given up to save a chest, and a child with a foot like that is further gone than a woman winding wool in a market can be expected to know. The face of severe cold is not a peaceful face; the almost-smile sits on it like a wrong lid on a jar, and the woman winding the wool would take it for sleep if she had not been looking at the foot. And the afterdrop waits on the far side of any warming anybody might perform, when the cold blood comes back to the heart and the core falls a degree before it rises, which is the moment a man is lost by people who have only just begun to be kind.

He knew the order. He knew the almost-smile. He knew the afterdrop and what it would do and at what hour it would do it, and there was not one thing in the shade he could put into the foot or lift out of it or carry up the steps. He kept the two states of himself on one tray without setting one across the other: dusty forearms and clean ones, this morning and another morning, both his and neither asking about the other. The dust was a record that named nothing, ground finer than the clean dark line in the corner and finer than the new hairline of grey beside it that the house called the thaw; it told what room a man had stood in and told nothing of what he had done there, and that was the whole of what it knew.

“It doesn’t care whose hands. It cares if they’re steady.”

He kept the count where no the Court thumb would read it, exact and unspent, and went on watching.

The reed-cutter came along the boards with a bundle on his shoulder, walking them as a man walks a floor he laid himself and knows where it gives. From inside his coat he took a short length of wool or reed-fibre gone grey with the oil of a working thumb, the grey a stitch-hole takes after a thread has been drawn through it a dozen times; it was the colour of the stone beside it and might have been mistaken for a piece of that stone but for the knots. He set the bundle down on the flat blessing stone with its broken ends squared to the edge, straightened the cord on his thumb, drawing it through his lightly closed fingers the way a man draws a measuring line and not the way a man opens a book, and turned it once so the knots stood up in the small light off the water. He tied one low with his teeth and a pull of the thumb, bit the end off level, put the cord back inside his coat and drew the coat closed over it with the flat of his hand. He did not show it to anyone and he did not count it where anyone could hear him count.

A weir woman come up from the traps with salt at her wrists asked what the cord held.

“You can ask the cord,” he said.

“I can’t read a cord.”

“No more can the Court.” He lifted the bundle off the stone and went on along the boards toward the traps, and the question went down into the water after him with no figure on it and no name.

The boy took the shape of the hand in as a man takes the measure of an instrument laid on a towel before anyone names what it is for. He had seen a knot tied low with teeth and a thumb before, and had not once counted what the cord held; a tie, a turn, a thumb-pulled tail, a record in a material no Court thumb would spell, exact and complete and worth nothing to anyone who wanted a figure. The greyness was itself a record of a hand that had been there long before any figure was wanted and would be there after the figure was forgotten, the way the worn thumb-mark on a tool tells which hand held it and tells nothing of the work the tool was bought to do. He read the shape and did not supply the want that would make it an account. He did not lay it beside the charcoal under the shelf two roofs up the yard, or beside the empty place in the returning line where a hand went in on Seed 15 and did not come out; the grey cord went back into the coat with its knots unread and stayed there, carried and not paid. Sella stood a half-pace off with the little-door burn shut on nothing and gave the instruction to the wool again, and did not look into the basket to see what he had been watching.

At the lower boards Orra Weir stood with her board on its side, turning it with the wrist that had turned it ten thousand times. Her hands were like old rope, the pale eel scars lay uncovered across both forearms, salt stood white at her wrists, and the grey streak showed first through the black hair when she moved her head. The smell of river silt, cold fish and tallow smoke went ahead of her, and the nasal half-song of the Fen came into her voice where she was tired, as it had come at the blessing and every morning since, and she had never entirely lost it. A cord of grey wool hung at her waist with its knots set close along it, the thing people noticed last. She pulled one knot down with her thumb and set it among the others, and did not look into the basket. The traps stood in the gaps of the weir with their mouths upriver, and the board she turned had the shape of ten thousand eels worn into it, and neither the board nor the cord had ever been laid where a slate could be opened beside them.

A woman at the rails asked what the levy would take now the hands were out in the reeds.

“The ninth of Ilmen,” Orra said. “The collector’s boat came round the bend on the flood and had six oars. One man stood at the stern and never touched them.” The half-song flattened the words and let them out slow, one after another the way a trap is lifted and set down.

“That isn’t the levy.”

“It’s a date. The river takes what the Court doesn’t. You can shout at either one. I’ve tried.” Her thumb went to the cord again at the end of the sentence, and the boy could not tell whether that was a lie or only the habit of counting, and made no cause of it. The half-song went quiet in her after the last word the way a man’s breath goes quiet when he has lifted something heavy and set it where it belongs.

A date given where a figure was asked is an answer with the question taken out of it — a chart closed over a note whose finding nobody had read, the neat margin left standing — and he set it where it belonged, on the place where the told bell stood beside the heard bell, and the man who keeps his hands off the oars stood apart from the midwife’s reaching hand and the thane’s twice-wiped thumb. He had no Grathic word for the shape of it and did not supply one. The date went on its own place and did not touch the cord in the reed-cutter’s coat, though both had passed along the same boards in the same hour; and the woman who asked went off with her question still in her fist, carried away level so nothing slopped. Two accounts with no figure in them are still two accounts, and he had long ago given up making them agree.

The last knot on Orra’s cord showed its double turn. The tail was tucked, it was tied left over right, and the small extra weight of a thumb had been pressed into it, the shape legible in the small light and the word that would have named it staying where it had stayed since the year of the flower. The boy read the shape without the name. He did not count it — not the last knot, not the ones before it — and he did not say the word that was not spoken anywhere on these boards, because a word spoken by a child who could not speak would have been a thing entered in an account he had no standing to open. He watched her watching the basket out of the corner of an eye that never quite arrived, and the watching got no answer from a bundle that could not lift a hand. The knot kept its double turn and kept its name, and that was the whole of what he was allowed to carry away.

Behind Orra, on the next board down, a second board lay that did not touch hers. Mira Silt worked the eels over it with pale slime dried in long grey strokes on her sleeves and river silt dried pale in the hollow of her neck, her head held a little to the right where the top of her left ear had been, her small smile kept on her face the way a door is kept on the latch. The two boards lay side by side the whole morning and no edge of one crossed the edge of the other. She worked fast and without a beginning, the way she did everything, her knife going in under the jaw and the eel-bones coming out onto the board in a small wet heap while the smile stayed where it was.

A weir woman come down to the outer boards asked whether the run would be thin now the hands were in the reeds. Mira flicked a string of eel-bones against her thumb while she decided whether to lie, moved two from hand to hand, put one away, and answered the question and then her own. “Thin? Who’s to say thin? It comes when it comes. Ask the river.” She said something low about a boat that had come up on the flood and nothing about the hand that had not come out of the strips. The bones went back into her pocket and the smile did not move on its latch. The boy set the order of it on the tray where the other record-first hands lay: the bones moved before the answer was chosen, the hand to the cord before the figure was priced; and he did not join her hand to the midwife’s or the thane’s or any other hand that had gone to a record before it went to a person.

A buyer in a coat with the river still in its hem named a figure for two pair. Mira’s thumb went under her apron to the grey wool cord and stayed there longer than the figure required, while she decided what the figure was worth to the woman who had said it. “Two marks the pair and two short,” she said. “You’ve said it twice and I’ve counted it twice. There’s nothing in the sentence that wants an answer.” The buyer took the eels and went. Shifting a creel showed the second cord twisted twice beneath the grey — the one never carried upriver to Nine Pots, never lifted at a door — and then the apron came down over it. Neither knot was counted by any thumb but her own, and the knot for the thin child upriver was not touched. She said nothing about the child and did not look into the basket; her not-looking was a keeping on a place of its own, the opposite of the pricing face a buyer turns across a market, and she had no word in Grathic for either one, and the two stood on separate trays and did not touch.

Three private records stood on the boards in wool and teeth and thumb, all within a dozen paces of the basket and every one visible and none enterable. The reed-cutter’s tally lay in the coat drawn close over it, gone grey where a working thumb had oiled it; Orra’s knot-string hung at her waist with its last double turn legible and its name withheld; Mira’s second twist lay twice beneath the apron, never taken to the door it belonged to. Each was kept by a hand that would not let a figure be written where another hand could read it, and each hand had passed over its record in the same hour. The boy laid them side by side and not across, the way a man lays instruments on a towel, knowing each by the wear on its handle and refusing to mistake three handles for one tool; for three likenesses in a row will stand up and call themselves a doctrine if no hand holds them apart, and a doctrine is an account that has found a payer, and no payer had been named to any of these. He entered none of them, and he did not carry the reed-cutter’s cord upriver to any door or lift Orra’s knot-string from her waist or draw Mira’s second twist out into the light, because a thing set down where nobody expects to be read is still a thing set down and not an invitation.

Below the sill at the fish-trade shade the morning went on at its own pace. A woman wound a child’s foot in three turns of wool and called the cold weather, and a neighbour with a pail said the water would go when it would go, and the foot stayed dark inside the wool with the cold in it that no wrap had taken out. The boy read it exactly — the extremity going before the core, the small almost-smile that is not peace, the afterdrop waiting on the far side of a warming nobody would perform — and the reading worked as perfectly as it had under another roof and bought nothing. No silt-leach, no cause, no cure, no word written across it; and he did not lay it beside the green that had gone off with the light across the square the day before, because a colour is not a foot and an absence is not an absence of the same thing, and likeness is not cause. One cold thing stood on its own place and kept it, the cold to the woman who had wrapped it and weather to the neighbour with the pail, and the surgeon’s register noted the order of it and had nowhere to put the note.

Sella set the basket at the very edge of the work where no slate lay, gave the instruction to the wool and to the running water and never to the basket, and stood a half-pace from every open board. The reed-cutter went up the planks with the cord inside his coat and the knots unread; Orra’s last knot kept its double turn, tail tucked, tied left over right with the small weight of the thumb pressed in, and kept its name unspoken; Mira’s thumb came out from under her apron when the buyer had gone and her board lay beside Orra’s without touching it. River wind came up to meet the smell of cold fish and tallow, and the note of water went over water and under the boards where no slate lay.

At the going of the light she carried him home at the half-pace, up the river road with the woodsmoke of Low Kiln going before her and the river wind coming up to meet it, and gave the instruction to the wool and never to the basket. One hundred and forty days old, four months and twenty days, and nobody on these boards or under any roof had said so. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down against the fold, exact, complete and spoken by nobody; the three keepings went on being kept where no Court thumb would read them, and his count went on going.

The river road gave onto the clay road without a seam, the way one worn thing gives into another, and the half-pace did not change. Woodsmoke went before Sella Vail and the river wind came up to meet it, and the two met somewhere about her shoulders and went on together, each keeping its own shape. She gave the instruction to the wool and to the wind and never to the basket — keep it off his ears, it had come all the way down with them and would go all the way back up — and the boy lay with his cheek to the side of it and the small wet sound coming and going as his head wandered and was not set right. The light went off the reeds behind them and came on the clay ahead, and the clay took it up red and gave nothing back. Low Kiln stood with its backs to the road, and the road went up between the houses the way a scar goes through a hand, known by feel and not looked at. A carrying arm is a record in muscle: it can say how often it lifted and never what the lifting cost. He had learned that before he could hold up his own head, and he did not need to learn it again.

At the bend she went around a pan set out to dry, tilting her shoulder to pass it, and around the trough by the well where the skin of ice had stood since morning and was called weather. She did not break the ice and did not lift the pan; a thing set down is a thing set down, and a woman carrying a basket has no second hand for either. Farther up she went wide around the space beneath the lowest rack, as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. A hand had gone in there on Seed 15 and had not come out with the rest on Seed 27, and the yard had gone around the place every morning since with a look and a wide step and no number, no cause, no body and no name. The broken birth-slates lay under the rack where they lay and were entered by nobody. Ash had turned the corner at the house and left a clean dark line behind it, and beside it ran the new finer hairline the house called the thaw, and neither line was swept. She carried past at the half-pace, gave the house no name for the space, and turned no head toward it. The boy watched the ground go by under the basket’s edge and noted the order of the going-around without deciding to: the shoulder went first, the eyes came after, and the place got no more than the wide step it had been given for a year.

Under the Vail kiln-house roof she lifted him out of the basket without looking into it and set him on his bed, and not in the Debt Loft, setting him down as a woman sets a pot down where it will not be slopped — rim level, weight settled, the whole of the thing arrived before the hand lets go. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without once looking at his face, and the thread of drool at his mouth froze where it lay and was not wiped, because nobody under any roof had been given an instruction about the thread. Her hand rested a half-beat longer than the setting needed before it went away. He had learned that half-beat as other men learn a hand’s pressure through a dressing: it told what was carried and not what it cost, it was exact, complete, and worth nothing to anyone who wanted a figure. The house kept its days in loaves pushed along a shelf and glances at a jar cold under its glaze, and it had no use for his number.

She went to the fire and told it it was the thaw. She turned the coals with the back of a broken pot, and the pot’s edge took the ash off and left the red underneath, and she fed them and said “The house will keep it” where another woman would have said I. Then she stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand, counting under her breath toward no opening, and pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn, the grey streak at her temple showing pale off the coals. She set the pot where it could be found in the dark, rim out and handle toward the reaching hand — the handle standing two inches beyond the hand that would have reached for it and would not close on it — and did not look toward the bed. The widow’s mark hung where it hung on its cord at her throat and she did not touch it, because a dusk is not an opening, and neither is a basket, a bed, a rack, a road or a fire gone down under blind ash. She went to her own bed and left the house to keep what it kept.

In the small hours he turned out his file the way a man lays instruments on a towel before a case begins: handle out, edge in, every piece in the place its own wear had made for it, and nothing laid across anything else. That last was the whole of the work. A file in which one object touches another is an account, and an account wants a payer, and no payer had been named to any of these.

There were six hearings of the full note, and they lay beside one another and never among: a cup across a birth room, a pot under the wheel-shed wall, the district’s slate twice at the Cross, a man giving at the Drawing, and the small flat knock out of Dessa Silt as the men lifted her. Five came from four sources and the sixth came from a person, and the sixth stayed where it was put, beside and not among. No seventh had come in three days, and he had not made one to fill the place. There were three near-full answers, advanced and not paid, each on its own place and none delivered: a cup across the birth room, a pot under the shed wall, a rack pot lifted a hand’s breadth too high and set again. There were the older intervals: the half-beat held behind a standing finger, the stroke that had gone on over the flagstones without the check while the stones kept the bright patch where it had stopped, his own breath drawn at the top of a reach and not spent. They lay as objects do, each complete, each exact, each worth nothing to a thumb looking for a figure.

Then the six stoppings of the near-saying, laid out as the first five had been laid and the sixth laid the day before: Reed 5, Reed 8, Reed 11, Reed 14, Reed 17 and Reed 20. Each had come all the way to the word that would call the loan and stopped there with no figure and no name. Each had cost a breath that went in where it should not have gone and was not let out where it should have been, and a man had gone on with his broom afterward, which is the shape his help takes when he is frightened. The small knife lay at the very edge of its sill with six counted where the house could check and the seventh where it could not; the unforged collector sentence lay beside it, and neither was entered. They were objects, six of them, and not a rule. He held them apart by hand because he had learned what six likenesses will do if a man stops holding them: they stand up together and call themselves a doctrine, and a doctrine is an account that has found a payer. No payer had been named to any of the six. The seventh year pressed nearer its end and had not given; the loan stood uncalled. Six intervals are six things that happened, and nothing in the counting made a seventh.

Beside them lay the damper’s double bite, filed as one piece of iron joined to no kiln: the bite that holds a vessel whole and the bite that can take the hand off a man, with no Grathic word for both mercies. The two tellings of the shut counting room stood together with no third, and the heard bell stood beside the told bell in two places that neither agreed nor disagreed. None of them touched. He did not lay the iron across the six stoppings, or the bell across the two tellings, or any of them across the cold foot below the sill, which was the cold to the woman who had wrapped it and weather to a neighbour with a pail, read exactly and worth nothing. A likeness is not a cause, and a colour is not a face, and an absence is not an absence of the same thing; he had bought those three sentences with three errors and would not spend them now.

In the other room the loaf shelf kept its two unnamed gaps — the old space at the row’s end and the palm’s breadth that had come in on the night of Seed 6 with no scrape, crumb, print or word — and the charcoal marks under the board stayed on a place of their own, unread. She had reached a finger beneath the front edge again that day without looking and without a word, the little-door burn closing on the wood’s lip and opening again, and brought it away, and the marks had not been counted and the finger had not been explained. Such a hand either knows the board by heart or has been told not to look, and the reason stood between the two, where it had stood for a hundred and forty days. One brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold two inches from his ear with its empty place unnamed and no third, and the two rooms did not touch. He did not lay the charcoal under the shelf across the two gaps above, or either of them across the one crust in the fold, because a hand that keeps its own record by hiding it is not an invitation to another hand, and three absences in three places are three absences until somebody joins them, and nobody had.

The word forty stayed unspoken with Orra Weir’s readable double turn beside it, and the boy kept the shape without the name as he had kept it since the year of the flower. The fifth green that had come to a silent woman’s mouth across the square was filed as a colour and not a name, joined to no ear and to no pale line on the back of a hand. The reed-cutter’s cord lay back inside the coat with its knots unread; Mira Silt’s second twist lay twice beneath her apron, never carried upriver to a door; Orra’s knot-string kept its last double turn, tail tucked, tied left over right with the small weight of a thumb pressed in. Three doors down in House Silt, Alda Reed’s six pages still lay turned toward a floorboard and both tins stood shut, and Dessa’s pewter tin above the abandoned bed kept its pale fold and thumb-bright lid closed. Below the north bridge the soft-cuffed clerk was still set for a measure nobody called, cuff grey from washing and grey again by midday, his hand to his slate before his eyes to the stalls. Every one of them was where it had been put, every one visible and none enterable, and every one worth nothing to a man who could only look.

Through the finger’s-width door came the river’s note of water over water, a fifth low thing that had never been counted among the six hearings and never would be, because it was water and not a vessel. Through the floor under blind ash the carried fire gave four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for a hundred and forty days now and not one day less. Overhead the boards kept four nails, four, and a fifth that was a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts in three places, and none of them became one. He had long ago stopped trying to make them. A surgeon who reads three counts in one body knows better than to add them; the pulse and the breath and the output are three accounts kept by three instruments, and the addition of them is a figure that cures nobody. Here there was not even a body to add, only a house keeping its warmth in the dark and a river keeping its note under the door, and the keeping of them apart was the only honest work in the room and the only work no slate would enter.

Cold arrived in its known order: cheek, temple, wrist, the wool riding back from the small bare joint where the hand could not hold it. He had learned the order in this body and in another, and the two knowings lay side by side and did not join. One hundred and forty days old, four months and twenty days, and told by nobody; if somebody had said the number aloud under this roof, nobody would have known what it counted, and the house would have gone on keeping its days in loaves and glances and the space at the row’s end nobody called a figure. He wanted the crust two inches from his ear and could not reach it. He wanted the number spoken by somebody who would know what a number was, and there was nobody in the room who could speak it and nobody under the roof who would know. The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down against the fold, exact, complete, unspent and spoken by nobody. In the next room the charcoal waited under the shelf for a hand not his and a day that had not come, and the count went on.
