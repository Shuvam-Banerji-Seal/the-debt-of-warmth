Eight at the Teeth

Days 144–146 · Reed 25–27, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, the Weir and Sill

Eight times a word may come to the teeth and go back, and eight times is a keeping and not a payment. The reed gives its last bundles to the water and keeps the count in the arm that carried them, and the arm does not know the figure. A word held at the teeth is a word still owed, and when the month goes out the account goes with it into the next house, unread.

— A proverb of Low Kiln, said over the last bundles of Reed

— Day 144 —

Reed 25, Y.H. 813 (Fifthday)

The reed month was going out the way it had come in, without a boundary drawn anywhere and without a thumb laid on a slate to say here and no further. It was the fifth day of the last week now; the last market stood five days off and the third Account-day six, and the bundles coming up from the reed beds were shorter in the arms that carried them, green at the broken ends, pale where the frost had bit the tips. Nobody in Low Kiln said the month had gone out. The yard had never been one to announce its own arithmetic, and what arithmetic it kept was kept in forearms and in weather, where no Court thumb would think to look for it. Sella Vail carried the boy down at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, woodsmoke going before her and the river wind coming up to meet it, the two passing through one another about her shoulders without either giving way. She gave the instruction to the wool and to the river wind and never to the basket — keep it off his ears, it had come all the way down with them and would go all the way back up — and he lay with his cheek against the fold and the small wet sound coming and going as his head wandered and was not set right. One hundred and forty-four days old. Four months and twenty-four days. Neither number was spoken under any roof that morning, and if a voice in the yard had said one aloud nobody there would have known what it counted.

The night’s cold lay in the ruts under a skin thin as the fat skimmed from a pot, and the wool at his ear had taken the river wind all the way down and was damp against his cheek. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had wiped it; nobody under any roof had been given an instruction about the thread, and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. He knew the order in which cold arrives — cheek, temple, wrist, the wool riding back from the small bare joint — as surely as he had once known the order in which a man’s breathing goes when the core is going. The knowing was the old life’s and the want was this body’s, and the want was louder. He wanted the wool up over his ear. He could not lift it. The two facts stood side by side and did not become one.

The cutters had been downriver since before first grey and were coming back up with the last of the cutting across their shoulders, armfuls laid against the cold the way a man lays a child against his coat. They came around the pans set out to dry and the trough by the well with its skin of ice, and the ice was called weather and left where it lay. At the edge of the work Sella set the basket down level, as a pot is set down where it must not be slopped, and stood a half-pace from every open slate. From the low angle he saw the ground and the hems of coats and not the faces: dry mud worked into the creases of wrists, a boot’s print held and let go, the grey reeds standing in the ruts in long ranks with nobody counting them.

A cutter come up from the beds with a bundle on his shoulder wiped his palms down his thighs before he set it down. A girl with a creel stood measuring it with her eyes, the way a woman measures a pot before she lifts it, finding the place her hands would go.

“Lift it,” the cutter said. “Feel where the weight settles. Your arm’ll tell you what went into it, and that’s the whole of what it knows.”

She got it up with the green ends shivering and the pale tips brushing her coat, and the creel bumped her hip as she turned. “And what’ll it know about the hands that cut it?”

He was a dozen paces down with another armful before the answer came back over his shoulder. “Nothing. It knows the weight and not the cost. Ask it how heavy it is; that’s a question it can answer.”

A man come up from the pans with a strip of reed in his hand put the older question to a cutter going past: what the hands would be paid now the tend was near out. The cutter did not break his step.

“The weather and the river,” he said. “Ask them. They’ll tell you when they’ve had enough.”

The man shut his mouth on the next part and went back to his pans. No figure went down the yard with him and no figure came back. The boy kept the exchange on a place of its own, because a cost asked in the form of a direction and answered with a direction leaves no entry for anybody to read.

It was the last work of the reed month done in the ordinary way, with no more ceremony than a pot set down to dry. Women worked the near rows with their skirts kilted and their forearms grey to the wrist, turning wet pots out of the shade and setting them where the light fell; the light went along the rims and came off them, and no woman called it a fine morning. Two men went past with a barrow that had lost its easy rolling in the flood, and neither put a number to what the barrow held. A girl at the slip pits lifted her hands out of a pan to show the woman beside her that they were clean, and the woman looked and went back to her own pan. From the basket there were hems and hands and no faces, which was the whole of the world at that height.

The yard went around its three places and its one empty place as it had gone around them every morning of the month. At the Vail corner Ash had turned and left a clean dark line behind it, and beside the line lay the new finer hairline of grey the house called the thaw, thinner and paler than the old mark, gathering where nobody had put a broom. Sella carried past with her shoulder tilted and both lines where they lay, gave the broom no instruction for them and swept neither. Farther on she went wide around the space beneath the lowest of the drying racks where the broken birth-slates lay, as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and did not look into the space and did not enter it. And past that, in the returning line where the hands came back at dusk, was a place the yard went around with a look and a wide step. A hand had gone in there on Seed 15 and had not come out with the rest on Seed 27. No name had come up, no body, no cause, no figure, and no hand had come up to be counted; a slate would be found and a figure made if one came up in a month or a year, and nothing had come up.

He had seen the going-around in the planting, in the flood and through the cutting, and each time it was the same piece of work performed carefully and without comment: an eye laid on the place, a shoulder turned, a step taken wide enough that no heel came near it, and then the ordinary work resumed as though nothing had been avoided. A surgeon knows the shape of an omission by the care spent on the surface around it; here the care was spent in a step, and the step was the whole of the record, and the record was a direction and not a figure. He did not lay that empty place under the empty space at the rack or under the gaps on the shelf; an absence in a line of hands is an absence in a line of hands until somebody puts a name to it, and nobody in the yard had put one in a year.

A reed-cutter came along the boards past the basket with a bundle and the short length of grey cord carried inside his coat, the wool or reed-fibre gone the colour of a stitch-hole a thread has been drawn through a dozen times, which is the grey a working thumb leaves and the colour of use and nothing more. He set the bundle down where no slate lay, straightened the cord on his thumb and turned it once so the knots stood in the small light; he tied one low with his teeth and a pull of the thumb, bit the end off and put the cord back inside his coat, drawing the coat closed over it with the flat of his hand. Nobody asked him what it held and he counted nothing where anybody could hear him count. The boy let the cord lie where it lay. A cord on a board is a cord on a board; a mark under the loaf shelf is a mark under a shelf; an empty place in a line of hands is neither of these. He did not lay the cord beside the charcoal under the shelf, or beside the empty place the yard had just gone around, or beside Mira’s two cords, one of which had never been carried upriver to Nine Pots or lifted at a door, or beside Orra Weir’s knot-string on its own board below the sill. He left them where they had been put, unread and not joined, as he had left them on the days before and would leave them on the days after.

The carrying arm was the record he could read best and the one that told the least. It knows what it has carried and never what the carrying cost: how often it lifted, how the weight sat, which shoulder took the longer armful when the wind came upriver, and never what the lifting took out of the woman who lifted. Exact, complete, worth nothing to a thumb looking for a figure. The yard paid its last week’s cut in armfuls and weather and questions answered with directions, and there was no entry for a Court thumb to read because there was no entry.

He noted the order of it again without deciding to. The hand went to the record before the hand went to the person: in a woman turning her shoulder half a beat ahead of her gaze, in a cutter feeling the weight settle before he looked at the girl who had asked, in a man shutting his mouth on a figure before he looked at the face of the man who had asked for one. He had learned that order in a room where a hand went toward a chart before anybody looked at the face on the table, and in a yard where a hand went toward a slate before anybody looked at the pig. The surgeon’s register noted it and had no standing to enter it anywhere, which was the whole of what the register had been able to do since he came into this body.

He had turned his file in the small hours and found seven stoppings of the near-saying lying on the interval tray — Reed 5, 8, 11, 14, 17, 20 and 23 — seven objects and not seven payments, with no payer named to any of them. The eighth belonged to the next day and he did not bring it early into the yard to make a row come out even. Seven likenesses standing in a row will stand up and call themselves a doctrine if nobody keeps a finger between them, and a doctrine is an account that has found a payer; he kept the finger between them because he had no other hand to keep it with.

The going of the light came on without anybody marking it, as the month had gone out without anybody marking it. Sella shifted the basket a hand’s breadth out of the draught and gave the instruction to the wool and to the running water again, and never to the basket. She went around the pan set out to dry and went around the rack space again on the way back, and did not touch the widow’s mark, because a yard is not an opening and a basket is not a door. The competence was the whole of the helplessness: he could read a carrying arm the way another man reads a chart, and he could not lift a hand to the bundle the arm had carried. There was no dishonesty in the reading and no use in it, and he held both facts in one breath without either cancelling the other. He wanted the bundle two paces off, and the pot under the roof, and the place in the returning line that had been given a look and a wide step; he could not reach any of them. His small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water that came from behind the racks, and came down against the fold. The last bundles stood grey in the ruts with nobody counting them. The cutter went back downriver with the bundle counted only in the arm that carried it. One hundred and forty-four went on exact and unspent with nobody’s thumb upon it.

Under the Vail kiln-house roof the light came down through the oiled paper in a brown sheet that reached the floor and went no farther, so that the far corner kept its darker air to itself and the bench kept its own shadow. Sella Vail came in from the yard with the basket held level against her hip, the way a woman carries a pot filled to the brim and will not have it slopped, and the boy lay with his cheek to the fold and the small wet sound coming and going as his head wandered and was not set right. Woodsmoke went before her and caught in the beams; under it lay the dry warm smell of the loaf shelf, a smell of crust and ash and old baking that was almost a warmth in itself. Crossed through it for one breath came the sharper smell of charcoal from beneath the board, clean and bitter, the smell of the inside of a banked kiln, and then it folded back under the shelf as though it had never been let out.

She set the basket low near the bench leg, at the angle at which the underside of a board becomes a roof to a man lying on the floor while a man standing sees only the top of it. The cold iron of the bench bracket was at his cheek. She gave the instruction to the wool, the pot, the broom and the fire and never to the basket — wool first, then pot, then broom, then fire, and the basket last if at all, which was never — and told the fire it was the thaw, which it was, and would go when it would go. Keep it off his ears, she told the wool; it had come all the way down with them from the reeds and would go all the way back up, and a basket set down is a pot set down and left.

“Don’t set that near the door,” she told the pot, which was not near the door and had not been moved. “You’ll pay for it in the thaw.” The pot gave back nothing, being a pot, and the pot’s instruction was the only answer she asked for. Her hands were never still when they had nothing to hold: they went to the rim, the spoon, the back of the bench, counting what could be counted and stopping where the count would have had to include her. From the basket the boy watched the order of it rather than the words, as he had watched it all his life: the thing kept first, the person afterward, if at all. He had no standing in the order and he had learned to find that funny without laughing, which cost less and kept the same blade in it.

Above him the shelf kept its two unnamed gaps in the room the fold did not touch. There was the old space at the row’s end, which had stood there through every morning of his life and which nobody had yet decided what to call, and there was the palm’s breadth that had come in on the night of Seed 6 with no scrape, no crumb, no print and no word. Two gaps. A third would have been somebody’s doing and nobody had done it. It was said of the loaf-shelves of Low Kiln that two gaps stay two gaps until a finger lays them together, and that an account then wants a name; no finger in the district had laid these together. The saying went around the houses the way a pot goes around a table, and nobody carried it into this room and set it down.

In the other room, on the bed he was not lying on, one stiff brown-edged crust lay two inches from the place where his ear would have been, with its empty place beside it still without a name. He had wanted the crust since morning and could not reach the fold, and wanting it and not reaching it sat together in him without either cancelling the other. The two rooms of the house did not touch, and the two gaps above him and the one gap in the fold were kept apart by the whole width of a wall and by a woman who had never once carried a saying from one to the other.

Sella’s lips went on moving along the row. She stood with her back to the basket and counted the loaves the way another woman might count beads, without touching any of them and without arriving anywhere; the row kept its shape and gave back no figure. She glanced once at the clay jar standing cold under its glaze at the shelf’s far end. A jar cold under its glaze is a jar cold under its glaze and not an answer to anybody. Then, still with her back to him and still moving her lips, she reached a finger beneath the front edge of the board where the charcoal tally lay. The little-door burn on the back of her hand closed on the wood’s lip and opened again as the finger went under. She held it a breath out and a breath back, the way a woman feels along the underside of a shelf for a thing she has put there and knows is there, and brought it away. She did not look at what her finger had touched. She said nothing about it and did not wipe the finger and did not go back to the shelf.

From the basket’s low angle the boy saw the hand go under and come back and saw nothing of what the marks said. He knew the tally was there: short grouped strokes of different darkness pressed hard into the underside of the board, the ones at the row’s end faded almost into the grain, a keeping put where nobody thought to look for one. He did not count the strokes. He did not press a thumb where her thumb had been. He did not lay the underside of the board across the two gaps above it, or across the one stiff crust two inches from an ear in the other room, or across the place in the returning line the yard had gone around that morning with a look and a wide step. He made no cause of the habit. Such a hand, going under a board without looking, either knows the board by heart or has been told not to look, and the reason stood between the two and would not come down on either side, and he had no standing to step up to either one. From the floor the two hands would have looked the same: the one that knew and the one that had been warned, the knuckle going under, the lip of the wood giving, the hand coming away. He had once been able to tell the difference in a room where a man’s hand went into a field before anybody said what the field was, and the telling had bought him nothing then either. A hand’s knowing is not a thing a slate will take, and a warning is not a cause, and the space between them was a place he could hold open and nothing more.

The order of it was the thing he could read without being able to use. Her hand had gone to the record before her eyes went to the row and before anybody’s eyes went to the basket; it was the same order he had watched in a yard shoulder turning ahead of a yard gaze, in a cutter feeling the weight settle before he looked at the girl who had asked, in a clerk’s hand going to a slate before his eyes went to the stall. He had learned it in a room where a hand went toward a chart before anybody looked at the face on the table, and here it was again under a shelf, performed by a woman who would have said the house had done it. He set it on its own place beside the other reachings and did not make a row of them and call it a rule. A keeping put where nobody expects to be read tells more by the care of its hiding than the writing would: that much he had learned and could not spend. An inventory is not a key, and a man who cannot lift a thing cannot use what he has catalogued.

At the hearth she told the fire it was the thaw again, which it was, and turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and fed them. “The house will keep it,” she said where another woman would have said I. The fire gave its four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, and she did not count the pulls aloud and did not ask anybody to hear the silence, and its payer had been unnamed now for a hundred and forty-four days. The warmth came through the floor under blind ash and went into the boards and into the boy’s wrist by degrees, and no one in the house said where it had first been lit or what it had cost to bring thus far. He knew the order in which a body takes heat back — the small joint first, the sting and the itch of it, the core last — and he knew too that a warmth arriving without its being named is a warmth nobody can be thanked for. He kept the payer off the slate because there was no slate in the district that would have taken the entry and no voice under the roof that would have spoken it. She stood a moment with the back of the broken pot in her hand, counting something under her breath toward no opening. Then she pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn, the grey streak at her temple showing pale off the coals, and set the pot where it could be found in the dark, rim out and the handle toward the reaching hand — the handle standing two inches beyond the hand that would have reached for it and would not have closed on it. She did not look toward the bed. She did not say whose fire it was, or whose child lay in the basket, or whose count went on under the shelf.

the widow’s mark hung where it hung at her throat, a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and she did not touch it. She touched it before she opened the kiln and at no other hour; an afternoon under a shelf is not an opening, and neither is a basket, a pot, a broom or a fire told the weather. The clean dark line at the corner and the finer hairline of grey beside it lay where they lay, unswept and called the thaw, and the fire was told the thaw and did not ask to be told twice. The household straightened around her silences the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted, and nobody in it asked what her finger had found, and nobody in it said the room off the shed had been cold. Two silences stood under one roof without a word passing between them, as they had stood for a hundred and forty-four days.

The boy lay with the cold bracket at his ear and the brown light going off the boards. Nothing within two paces could be reached: not the pot with its handle turned out, not the broom, not the shelf above him, not the clay jar cold under its glaze, not the marks on the underside of the board where her finger had been. He had the appetite to set the room out on a towel the way a man lays instruments before a case begins, each object in its own hollow and nothing resting across anything else — the two gaps on one tray, the charcoal on another, the crust and its empty place in the room that did not touch — but an inventory is not a hand. He had no hand for it but the small one outside the wool, and that hand went up an inch toward the note of water over water that came through the finger’s-width door and came down against the fold. He did not count the strokes under the shelf. He did not lay the two gaps together. He did not make a cause of a woman’s habit, or a doctrine of a keeping, or a payer of a silence.

The going of the light came on without anybody marking it, as the month had gone out without anybody marking it. The brown sheet on the floor drew in toward the hearth and left the far corner to its own darker air; the oiled paper held its last of the afternoon and let go; somewhere along the yard a pan was set down and a voice went out with the reeds and did not come back. Under the roof the woman’s shadow went up the wall and stood there while her hands went on with the small work of the house, and the boy watched the shadow and not the face, which was the whole of the world at that height. He wanted the crust two inches from his ear and the count spoken by somebody who would have known what a count was, and he got neither and could not ask for either. The small hand outside the wool went up toward the warmth and came down, and the warmth went on coming from somewhere nobody had named and had not once been entered.

One hundred and forty-four lay where the day had left it, exact and complete and entered nowhere. The reed month was going out without a boundary having been drawn at its going, the last bundles stood grey in the ruts with nobody counting them, and the third Account-day stood six days off across a district that would read a figure into the air over the basket and go home believing it had paid something. Under the roof the instruction had gone to the wool, the pot, the broom and the fire and never to the basket, and the fire had answered four low pulls with a silence where the fifth should have been. The shelf kept its two gaps above and its charcoal marks below; the finger had come away without a word and the hand had not gone back; the two rooms of the house did not touch.

Reed 26, Y.H. 813 (Sixthday)

Before first grey the dark lay in the hollows of the Clay Yard like water held in a dented pan, and the men came up through it with their collars high and their voices low, three or four of them, giving no face to the yard and taking no name from it. They went in toward the Vail wheel-shed and through the door of the room off the shed, which stood a finger’s width open and did not shut, and the cold came out of that finger’s width in a thread and went nowhere the house could enter. Sella Vail carried the boy past at the half-pace she kept from every open slate. She turned no head toward the door. She gave the house no name for the men and did not say the room was cold, because a room a house does not enter is a room a house does not describe, and what is not described cannot be entered anywhere else either. Woodsmoke went before her and the river wind came up to meet it, and the two passed through one another about her shoulders without either giving way. Keep it off his ears, she told the wool; it had come down with them and would go back up with them, and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. The instruction went to the wool and the river wind and never to the basket. One hundred and forty-five days old. Four months and twenty-five days. Neither number was spoken under any roof that morning, and if a voice in the yard had said one aloud nobody there would have known what it counted.

He lay with his cheek to the fold and the small wet sound coming and going as his head wandered and was not set right. From that height the world was a row of hems and the low reach of a door, and a man’s collar held high was the same as a man’s face turned away: a shape with no owner, which is the safest shape to give a slate. He had known men who held their faces like that in a room with better light, and knew the shape here without needing the word for it in Grathic. The low voices went on behind the finger’s width of door and came to nothing in the yard, and the yard went on without them the way a floor goes on under a bucket somebody will wipe on the morrow. The small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the low note of water over water that came through the open door and came down against the fold; nothing within two paces could be reached, not the door, not the thread of cold, not the hem of a coat going in. The arm that carried him was a record in muscle that told exactly what had been carried and never what it cost: level, half-paced, going wide around every slate. He read it once and set it down. One hundred and forty-five he kept where he kept it, exact and complete and entered nowhere, carried past the door as a woman carries a pot filled to the brim and will not have it slopped.

Jorra Vail came through the finger’s width of door ahead of them with the morning’s dust still on him, and the dust was not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel. It was fine grey slate dust ground small as meal, worked into both forearms past the bent little finger and under the nails, the creases of the wrists full of it and the little finger’s bend holding a pale line of it where nothing else reached. He kept dusty forearms and clean ones on one tray as two states of the same morning, side by side and not across, and asked neither which slate had been broken nor whether it was a birth-slate, because a question put to that dust would have had to be put to the room it came out of, and the room was a place a house did not enter. Under the lowest rack the broken birth-slates lay where the yard went around them and entered them into nothing, and no hand that morning went in among them. His eyes did not finish sentences; they went to the door, to the wheel, to the fire and came back without reporting, and the arranged face he turned toward the bench told the boy as little as a chart turned down tells a man standing at its foot. He had learned that a record kept in a material no slate will spell is still a record, and that the care of its keeping tells more than the reading would; but the boy could not take the telling to any door, and kept it where it stood.

Jorra went to the wheel before he went to anyone and spoke to it first. “It doesn’t care whose hands,” he said to the wheel. “It cares if they’re steady.” The wheel took it the way a wheel takes anything and went on turning. He set a pot on the bench with the flat of his hand and told it, “You’ll do,” in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either. The boy heard the note in the pot before it left the wheel. It was a full note, whole and clear under the knuckle of the world, the sound a vessel makes when nothing in it has given way; he had known that sound in a room across a birth-room wall and under this very roof through a wall of clay, and had never once been able to put a hand on the vessel that made it. Jorra knew it too. The boy knew that he knew it, knew it three turns before the pot left the wheel, and kept the knowing where questions are allowed to last for years. “He’s not Vail” stayed where it had been sealed on the hour it was spoken, ungiven and not withdrawn, and the boy, who was not Vail and had never been entered as one, went on being carried under the roof without a name.

A kiln-master come up from High Kiln stood at the edge of the shed with Ash on his cuffs and said the pot was singing before its time, and that a pot which sings early generally sings itself out early. Jorra did not answer him. He did not say the note was full, or that he had heard it before it left the wheel, or that the man’s ear and his own ear had heard the same thing and read it differently. He let the knowing stay where it was, advanced and not paid, and the boy filed it beside the three near-full answers on their own tray — a cup across a birth room, a pot under the wheel-shed wall, a rack pot lifted a hand’s breadth too high and set down again — and not among the hearings. Six hearings of the full note stood beside and never among, five from four sources and the sixth, the small flat knock out of a woman as the men lifted, from a person; no seventh had come and he had not made one to fill the empty place. A knowing withheld is not a hearing given, and a man who keeps an answer by him is not a man who has paid it. He set the withheld knowing on its place and did not let it stand up beside the six. A clerk would have called the knowing an entry and the entry a debt; here it was a thing in a potter’s head that no slate would take, and it went on gathering nothing.

Sella had not finished telling Jorra the fire was low. “The house keeps it low overnight. Feed it before —” The bent finger went into the cold iron of the damper ahead of the sentence, going in ahead of his hand the way it always went, and the iron took the cold out of the fingertip and kept it, giving a little back as the finger warmed. He moved the tongue of iron a hair’s breadth and left it standing there. The end of her instruction arrived at the pot instead of at him. The household straightened around his silence the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted: Sella’s hand finished its half-circle with the broken pot, Tarn’s broom found its line, the wheel went on turning, and the low voices in the room off the shed went lower. Nobody ordered the straightening and nobody observed it. Obedience that arrives ahead of the instruction is obedience and a correction at once, and both went past the basket without a word. The hand had gone to the iron before the sentence had got to the fire, before anybody’s eyes had got to the basket: the same order he had watched in a yard shoulder turning ahead of a yard gaze, in a cutter feeling the weight settle before he looked at the girl who had asked, in a clerk’s hand going to a slate before his eyes went to the stall. He set it on its place beside the other reachings and did not make a row of them and call it a rule.

From the low angle the iron was the only thing in the shed worth reading twice. It was one piece of iron with two bites. One bite closed across a pot and held the whole vessel in the fire; the other, set the wrong way or set too far, could take the hand off a man and leave the stump to learn what the fire had been doing all along. He had known that iron in a life where it was called by another name and set across a forearm to stop the Warm going up it, and there too it had been mercy and mutilation in the same bite, a clamp and a tourniquet with no seam between them. There was no word in Grathic for the two mercies at once; the language had a word for holding and a word for taking and kept them apart the way a shelf keeps two gaps apart. He filed the iron as one object joined to no kiln, worth nothing and complete, with the two bites on one tray and no third bite invented to make the pair into a rule. Beside it the two tellings of the counting room shut in the war stayed together with no third: breath through the nose and breath in the throat, both saying it was not the war’s hand, each with its date and each kept on a place of its own, and the room whose door stood a finger’s width open giving up nothing to join them. When she took it away her hand went once into her bodice to the little tin and came away without opening it. The grey in her hair was worked in among the black at the temple, and the grey wool at her shoulders smelled of the same lye as the room below.

Then she crossed two fingers at the red edge of the birth-flower at his wrist before she knew she had done it. The sign came first and the knowing came after, as it had on the night of Stone 7 and on every morning since: two fingers laid across the branching red where the wool had ridden back, a small closed gesture made in front of a basket that could not hand it back. She covered it at once with the date she had given three doors down and years ago, laying the words over the mark the way a cloth is laid over a field before the instrument comes down. Stone seven. The account went up on the eighth, before the paper went out.

The boy knew the shape of that covering better than she knew she had made it. He had watched a hand lift out of a field before the dressing went on, leaving the field as it had been found and the account standing in its neat margin; here the covering was done with the same care and the field was never opened, and the date stood over the sign where nobody had asked for a date, which was the whole labour of it. He did not make a cause of the two gestures. He kept the sign on one place and the date on another and the small hand that made them on a third, because a likeness is not a cause and a covering is not the thing covered, and a man who lays them together before he has a right to will find he has written an account where there was only a woman being careful.

“He lies quiet,” Sella said from the half-pace, and the words went to the basket as a pot might be addressed, not to the woman bending over it.

Warm and breathing, that’s all I’ve set down, Alda said. It was exact and it was not true, and it had been exact and not true for more mornings than any living person in Low Kiln kept count of. A woman lay three doors down who had believed it once and was past correcting it now; more mornings dead than anyone would count, uncollected, with the lie standing over her in a date no living person could take off. The mercy had curdled into a fact and then into a piece of the house’s furniture, something the room went around the way it went around the Ash ridge, and there was no hand alive that could lift it and set it right.

She went on with the dates after the question had stopped, because dates are a handle on a thing too large to carry. A cooper’s seventh in Mud End. A potter’s third child in Stonemouth. The last high water, which had carried a woman away and was called weather in the telling. Each date went down into the room and lay there with the others, exact, complete and answering nothing, the way an armful of reeds is entered in the muscle that carried it and in no slate at the watch. The neighbour in the back room pushed the broom or the brush along a board and the song went on above her with its words worn smooth.

Then Alda took up a narrow slate and wrote what she would not leave lying, in the second hand that no Court physician had ever seen, and turned it face-down into her shawl toward a floorboard in a house the boy could not name. Six pages lay under it in that second hand, all unread, the sixth written long ago and never paid again; no seventh had been written and none was owed to anyone who could spell it. Above the board-bed the round pewter tin kept its pale fold and thumb-bright rub with the lid shut, and below it the grey wool kept the hollow where the small pale stone had lain, and nobody said either was gone. Two private records in two materials stood apart in one room, read by nobody and carried to no door: one under a floorboard in a house he could not enter, one on a shelf above a bed that had been abandoned before he was taken from it.

The boy could not read Grathic in speech or writing. He could not ask which house held the floorboard or hand the gesture back to the woman who made it. He kept the sign, the turned slate and the covered flower on places of their own and made no cause of them, and no name and no number out of the woman three doors down was spoken in the room all morning. The order of reaching he could read without a word: the slates to the bench, the hand to the tin, the fingers to the flower, the date over all of it like a lid laid on a pot that was not going to be opened today. He put the shape of it on the tray where the things he could not enter went, beside the charcoal under another roof’s shelf and the grey cord in another man’s coat, and wrote nothing across any of them.

In the afternoon Sella carried him down the river road to the Weir at the half-pace, the instruction going to the wool and the running water and never to the basket. The reed work was going out of the month with nobody drawing a boundary around it; the last bundles stood grey in the ruts with their tips pale where the frost had bitten them, and a woman who asked what the hands would be paid was answered with the weather and the river, never a figure. The last market of Reed stood five days off and the third Account-day six, and neither was spoken of where the basket lay. The boy went along with his hand outside the fold, hearing the note of water over water come up off the Ilm before he could see the posts.

The the plank walk stood on its posts with the gaps between them showing the brown water going, and the flat blessing stone lay where it had always lain with its face rubbed smooth by ten thousand bundles set down and lifted up. A reed-cutter came along the boards with a bundle on his shoulder. He set it on the flat blessing stone, straightened the short length of grey wool or reed-fibre cord gone grey with the oil of a working thumb, turned it once so the knots stood in the small light, tied one low with his teeth and a pull of the thumb, bit the end off and put the cord back inside his coat, drawing the coat closed over it with the flat of his hand. To a woman who asked what it held he said You can ask the cord, and to I can’t read a cord, No more can the Court. He gave no figure, no name and no joining, and went up the boards to lift a trap with the question going down into the water behind him. The tally was carried and not paid, a private record in a material no Court thumb would spell.

Orra Weir stood at the lower boards with her board on its side and a grey cord at her waist. She turned the board with the wrist that had turned it ten hundred times, her hands like old rope, the pale eel scars uncovered across both forearms and salt dried white at her wrists; the grey streak showed first through the black hair when she moved her head, and the nasal half-song of the Fen came into her voice where she was tired. She pulled one knot down with her thumb and set it among the others with the small flat knock a knot makes when it is set on wood that knows it. When a weir woman asked what the levy would take now the hands were out in the reeds, Orra gave the date as she always gave it, the ninth of Ilmen, the collector’s boat coming round the bend on the flood with six oars and one man at the stern who never touched them, and to the woman who said that was not the levy she said it was a date, that the river took what the Court did not, that a person could shout at either one, and that she had tried. No figure went into the afternoon. Her thumb went to the cord again at the end of the sentence, and the boy could not tell whether that was a lie or the habit of counting and made no cause of it.

The last knot on her cord showed its double turn, tail tucked, tied left over right with the small extra weight of a thumb pressed in, and the word that would have named it stayed unspoken. The forty-first double turn was legible without its name, and he did not spend it. The knot-string went back upriver to the Eel House where no clerk could spell it, and the reed-cutter’s cord went back inside his coat with its knots unread.

The boy kept the two records on the boards apart: the grey cord in a working man’s coat with its knots unread, the knot-string at a trap-keeper’s waist with its last double turn legible and unnamed. Two private accounts within a dozen paces of the basket and no finger of his long enough to lay one across the other, and no standing to ask either of them what they kept. Side by side and not across, as they had stood on these boards through all the days of Reed, gathering toward a hand that was not his and a day that had not come.

Three doors up the yard six pages lay turned toward a floorboard with both tins shut and nobody reading either, and here on the boards the reed-cutter’s cord went back inside his coat with its knots unread. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down against the fold. One hundred and forty-six went on exact and unspent with no thumb upon it.

The afternoon gave out along the river road without a boundary drawn anywhere, the way the reed month itself was giving out without a boundary, and Sella Vail took the basket up from the edge of the work at the going of the light. She went at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, woodsmoke going before her up the clay road and the river wind coming up to meet it, the two passing in the dark between the houses the way two people pass on a stair without speaking. Keep it off his ears, she told the wool; it had come all the way down with them in the morning and would go all the way back up before the night was out; and then she gave nothing more to the wind, having said it once, because a wind takes an instruction and carries it away and there is an end of it. The instruction went to the wool and never to the basket. The boy lay with his cheek to the fold and the small wet sound coming and going as his head wandered and was not set right, and his hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water and came down against the cloth.

Halfway up she went around a pan set out to dry, tilting her shoulder and the basket with it so the pan stayed where the pan stood and nothing knocked against it; and at the well she went around the trough with its skin of ice, thin as a skim of fat set aside in a dish, and did not break the skin to get at the water underneath. Weather, the neighbours had called it all through Reed, and it would go when it would go. She gave the instruction to the bucket, which stood where a bucket stands and wanted nothing from her. He had learned before he could hold up his own head that a thing called weather is a thing nobody has agreed to count, and that a man who wants a figure from weather is a man who will be given a direction instead.

At the yard she went wide around the space beneath the lowest rack, where the broken birth-slates lay, as wide as a woman goes around a pail of hot water set down where a child might knock it — a step taken for the thing’s sake and not for her own, and not explained to anybody, least of all to the child in the basket, who could not have knocked over a cup if he had been set beside one. The Ash in the corner lay where it had lain: the clean dark line where the ridge had turned and turned again, and beside it the new finer hairline of grey gathered since, both called the thaw all through the last week and neither swept, and the name had not made either go. Beyond the ruts the last bundles stood grey in the dark where the afternoon had left them, green at the broken ends and pale where the frost had bitten the tips, and nobody had counted them since the light went. The mud kept the print of the boot that had set them down and gave it back slowly, and from the low angle he read the print the way a man reads a bone that has been set and is healing: what was done, and in what order, and never what it cost.

Under the Vail roof she lifted him out of the basket without once looking into it, the way a woman lifts a pot by the handle and does not look inside because looking will not change what is in it. She set him on the bed and not in the Debt Loft, setting him down as one sets a pot down where it will not be slopped — the base first, the weight settled, the hand kept where it is until the thing is sure of its place. Then she put the fold right at his ear and under his chin, and did not once look at his face. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the cold and she left it frozen and unwiped, because nobody under any roof in Low Kiln had been given an instruction about the thread, and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. Her hand rested a half-beat longer than the setting needed before it went away. She had learned that half-beat in the winter she had sat up three nights with a child of her own who cooled while everybody was kind, and had never told anybody what she had learned, and the house had straightened around the silence every time since. Then the hand went away and she did not go back to it.

She told the fire it was the thaw, which it was, and would go when it would go. She turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and fed them and said “The house will keep it” where another woman would have said I. The fire gave its four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, and she did not count the pulls aloud and did not ask anybody to hear the silence, and its payer had been unnamed now for a hundred and forty-six days. She stood a moment with the back of the broken pot in her hand, counting something under her breath toward no opening. She pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn, the grey streak at her temple showing pale off the coals, and set the pot where it could be found in the dark, rim out and the handle toward the reaching hand — the handle standing two inches beyond the hand that would have reached for it and would not close on it — and did not look toward the bed.

The widow’s mark hung where it hung at her throat, a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and she did not touch it. She touched it before she opened the kiln and at no other hour, and a dusk is not an opening. She went to her own bed without explaining the count and without looking toward the bed where the boy lay, and the two rooms of the house did not touch.

In the small hours he turned out his file the way a man lays instruments on a towel before a case begins, each thing going into its own hollow and nothing resting across anything else. He had kept it a hundred and forty-six days in a body that could not have lifted a spoon from the towel, and the keeping of the things apart was the only work in the room that no slate would enter.

Six hearings of the full note lay beside one another and never among them: a cup across a birth room, a pot under the wheel-shed wall, the district’s slate twice at the Cross, a man giving at the Drawing, and the small flat knock out of a woman as the men lifted, that last from a person and filed beside the five and never counted with them. No seventh had come in three days. The bell was a told bell and a heard bell, kept in two places; the wheel and the pots under the roof were notes he had left where they sounded; the clerk’s slate was a figure he could not read. He had not invented one to fill the empty place. An empty place kept honestly is a record of a kind, and a man who fills one to make a row come out even is a man with a reason, and he had learned two lives ago to be afraid of the shape of a reason in a man with clean hands.

Three near-full answers stood on their own tray, advanced and not paid: a cup across a birth room, a pot under the wheel-shed wall, a rack pot lifted a hand’s breadth too high and set down again. Each had wanted a following stroke that nobody had made, and he had not made it for any of them; the wheel-keeper’s knowledge of a pot’s note had gone to the same tray and not to the hearings, because a thing withheld is not a thing said and a knowing is not a hearing.

The older intervals lay where they had always lain: the half-beat behind a standing finger, the stroke that had gone on over the flagstones without the check, his own held breath from the market, the unchecking stroke come up beside the half-beat with no cause written across them.

And on the same tray, in the place he had made for the stoppings and had widened by one, lay eight of them — Reed 5, Reed 8, Reed 11, Reed 14, Reed 17, Reed 20, Reed 23 and now Reed 26 — eight objects and not a rule, eight intervals and not eight payments. The eighth had come on the night before at this hearth, brought all the way to the word that would call the loan by a man telling the kindling that six were counted and the next he would not count; the sentence about an ear under this roof being worth more than the debt that holds it had gone to the threshold of the word and stopped there, with no figure and no name, and the breath that should have gone in at the top of the reach had stayed where it was. He had filed it on the tray and not among the hearings, because a stopping is not a hearing and a held word is not a paid one. He held all eight apart by hand, because he had no other hand to hold them with. Eight likenesses standing in a row will stand up and call themselves a doctrine if nobody keeps a finger between them, and a doctrine is an account that has found a payer; no payer had been named to any of the eight. The seventh year stood one tend from its end and had not given. The loan stood uncalled.

Beside these lay the pieces that had no tray of their own and would not be put on one: the damper as one iron with two bites, the bite that holds a vessel whole and the bite that can take the hand off a man, filed joined to no kiln; the two tellings of the counting room shut in the war, side by side with no third, and nobody under this roof having gone into a shut room to make one; the told bell of the Long Night beside the heard bell out of the yard, two sounds of the same shape and not the same sound. He laid them out and looked at them and did not let the look become a joining. An inventory is not a key and a list is not a hand. He had catalogued a hundred and forty-six days of other people’s keeping and could not carry one object of it to any table.

Through the floor under blind ash the carried fire gave four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for a hundred and forty-six days. Overhead the boards kept four nails, four, and a fifth that was a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts in three places, and none of them became one: a note that was not counted, a fire that missed a pull, a fifth that was a knot and not a nail. He had learned early that three likenesses in a row want to stand up as a rule, and that the only honest work was to keep a hand between them; and here were three counts, and eight stoppings, and a hundred and forty-six days, and not one of them was an account anybody had asked him to enter.

Cold came in its known order: cheek, temple, wrist, the wool riding back from the small bare joint. One hundred and forty-six days old and four months and twenty-six days, told by nobody, and if somebody under that roof had said the number nobody would have known what it counted, and there was no word in Grathic that would have held it long enough for a woman passing on the clay to set it down. He wanted the crust two inches from his ear, and he wanted the number spoken by somebody who would have known what a number was, and he got neither and could not ask for either.
