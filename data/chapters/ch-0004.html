4

The Hearth Drawing

Days 5–6 · Stone 11–12, Y.H. 812 · The Vail kiln-house, Low Kiln

At the Cross a figure is read and the market goes home. In the room with the iron tube the same figure is taken, and nobody goes home.

— A hearth-maid’s proverb, Low Kiln

— Day 5 —

Stone 11, Y.H. 812 (Firstday)

The coals had held. All night the ash had lain over them in a grey skin the colour of the sky before snow, level as water left to settle, and under the skin the small red lives had gone on the way a pulse goes on under a blanket: not loudly, not for anyone’s hearing, but with the stubborn regularity of a thing that has been told to last and has decided to last. First light came into the Vail kiln-house through the oiled paper at the window and found the room as the night had left it — the bench, the hollow seat, the salt-stiff coat on its peg keeping a broader man’s shape than anyone living under the roof could fill, and the ridge of the Ash along the skirting board running to the corner and turning it without a break. The ash was grey at the edges and the coals kept a red heart in the middle, and the red heart had not gone out in the night, which was the whole of the house’s wealth and the whole of its condition. Behind the house the Slip Brook went over its stones with the small dirty sound it had kept all winter. The door at the threshold stood the width of a finger open, as it had stood for a year, letting in the dry cold that lifted the paper and would not let it settle.

Sella Vail came in before the yard was properly awake. She went to the mark before she went to the kiln. The widow’s mark hung on its cord at her throat, a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and she took it between finger and thumb with the burn on the back of her hand half-closing as she did it — two straight sides and a lintel, the skin inside puckered and pale, the little door shutting on nothing because the hand held nothing but the cord. She held it a moment longer than the touch required, longer than opening a kiln had ever required of her, and then she let it go and lifted the lid and fed the coals. The heat came up with the smell of clay and old smoke, and the room took it in the way a cup takes water, slowly, at the edges first. She stood a half-pace off while it drew, counting something under her breath that might have been breaths and might have been spoons. She did not say why she had touched the mark, and there was nobody in the room to ask.

The kiln took its first breath of the day and went on taking it in fours: four, and four, and a gap where the fifth should have been. She put a hand near the damper and did not move it yet. “Feed the fire before you feed yourself,” she said to the fire. “It’ll still be there in the morning. You won’t.” The proverb had been Garl Vail’s once, improved by nine years of use, and she said it to the fire the way the house said everything that mattered — to the thing that could not answer, and never to the person who was listening.

Corin lay on his bed under the roof with his fifth morning coming in at his ear and no way to close it out. He was five days old and awake, and the fold had ridden back off the ear, and the scrap of wool laid over his chin had gone the way all such scraps went — up toward the eye or down into the collar — and lay now across his cheek doing no work at all. He knew the shape of the cold before it arrived: the way it came in at cheek and temple and worked inward, a slow level rising, and what it would do if he let it rise. He had spent five days learning that the body he was in kept its own ledger and would not be argued out of it. It wanted the breast. It wanted the wool up. It wanted the head to stay where it was put, and the head went where it wanted, and the small wet sound came up in his throat when he turned it past a point. The man inside it knew the order in which a body gives up its heat and the exact feel of a patient going down that road, and could do nothing with the knowledge but recognise it. Recognition was the whole of his instrument now. He had once had a tray of instruments and a room with white light; now he had an ear and a count and a hand that went up an inch. That hand went up an inch toward the wool and came down again on the straw with a small sound that was not a word.

She put the fold right without looking at him. The hand came across, found the edge of the wool, tucked it under the chin and along the ear, and went back to the kiln; at no point did her eyes leave the fire. “There’s a draught under that door,” she said to the fire. “Keep it off the boy.” The instruction went to the fire, which would not answer, and not to the boy, who lay under the wool and heard every word and was the person it was about and could not say so.

❦

The household went about its order. Tarn had been sweeping since before the light, a bent man of twenty-two with the Ash in the creases of his knuckles that no scrubbing lifted and a pale ring round his left wrist where an iron clip had sat six years ago. He moved quickly, the way men move who expect to be told to stop, and he swept the yard as if the day’s account depended on the broom going over the same boards in the same order. He called everyone house, including Sella, and said I owe where another man said I am, and the boy had already learned to hear the difference between the deference and the nail in it. Six years of a seven lay behind him and he would not count the seventh, and the not-counting was the one thing in the house nobody could check. Halfway through the sweeping his hand went to the small bench knife on the sill and put it in his pocket, and came back a moment later without it, and set it down again where it could be checked.

“I took the small knife,” he said, in the direction of the bench. “I put it back. You can check.”

Nobody had asked him to check it. He said it the way another man might have said the weather was holding — as a fact one person had established and another might verify at leisure — and went on sweeping. The broom went over the boards. His hands knew where the knife was without looking, which was the whole of the joke and not a funny one.

In the Vail wheel-shed Jorra Vail was at the wheel with slip dried to the colour of old bone up both forearms, the left little finger bent permanently inward from a splinter taken at eleven. He did not look toward the house when Sella spoke, and he obeyed her before she had finished speaking, which was how the house knew what he would not say. He had not looked at her since the boy came under the roof, and the household had learned to read the not-looking the way another house reads a door slammed hard. He turned the wheel with his weight in the hollow his father had worn on the left and spoke to the pots as other men speak to animals.

“You’ll do,” he said to a jar still soft on the wheel.

He said it with no welcome in it and no unkindness either, and said nothing at all to the boy, who lay three paces away under a roof that did not divide itself for him. He shifted the damper before Sella had finished telling him to, which was his way of agreeing with her and refusing her in the same movement, and went back to the wheel with his eyes on the clay.

Then the pot was set down. It went onto the bench in the shed carefully, by a man who knew what the thing would bear, and it gave its small answer through the wall: a flat knock with no body behind it, the shadow of the full note stopping at the surface of the clay and going no further. It was not the noise of the pot he heard — the wood of the bench, the drag of a sleeve — but the small dry answer underneath it, the note that stopped at the surface of the thing instead of going through it. A sound vessel answered full and went on answering after the hand had gone away; a cracked one gave this, a knock with nothing behind it. He knew the difference the way he knew the difference between pulsing blood and welling blood, and the knowing had never once been of use to him here. Daniel Marchetti had heard that sound from a cup across a birth room, from a pot under this same roof, and twice from the district’s own slate in a market two days gone. He set this hearing beside the others the way he set his numbers down, and did not make them agree. There was no word in Grathic for the sound and no need of one, and nobody in the house tapped a pot for him. The knuckle that would have done it lay outside the fold with milky nails on it and went up an inch and came down, and there was no surface within two paces of it that it could have reached.

The broom stopped. It stopped for two strokes — not a full stop, only the space between one stroke and the next held a fraction longer, as if the man holding it had heard something in the shed and was deciding whether the turning of his head was worth the trouble. He did not turn it. He did not name the sound, or say that it was a sound at all. Then the broom went on, and the two strokes went under the ordinary noise of the house, and there was nobody under the roof who had seen anything in the stopping to remark on. The boy lay with the wall between him and the shed and the fold over his ear, and heard the broom go on, and kept the small interval where it had been put, without a word to put with it and without anyone to say the word to.

❦

Food came out of the house’s own dish. Sella fed whoever was hungry from it, the boy included, and did not call the feeding by any finer name. Barley gruel, bread with the crust left on, a scrap of something salted from yesterday — these went around the room in the order of hunger rather than the order of claim, and when a neighbour’s child came in at the door the dish was handed over without a word about whose it was. At the threshold a woman from two houses down put her head in and asked after the boy without asking after him, the way the district did everything.

“And how’s the little one?” she said to the room at large.

“Warm,” Sella said to the fire. “That’s all he is.”

The woman went away with the answer she had come for, which was none, and the house went on with its spoonfuls. “The house keeps a fire,” Sella said, when the question of the boy’s keep hung in the room without anybody putting it into words. “That’s the whole of what I know.” Faith was done in the same domestic way. A cup sat sunk in a little shelf by the door that nobody used and nobody moved, and when the gruel was served Sella’s thumb touched its rim for half a beat; a half-sentence went to the pot before the first spoonful, worn by use from whatever it had once been into an instruction about the draught. It was not prayer and it was not not-prayer, and the boy had already learned that in this house the two were the same gesture done quickly and not discussed afterward.

Money was not spoken of directly, which did not mean it was absent. The levy went around the room the way a hole in the wall goes around a poor house — spoken around rather than named, with a lowering of the voice and a glance at the door. Bits were owed and put off to the thaw. A firing of tiles was mentioned and then not mentioned, and the space where it had been was left with the same care Sella used when she set a cup down near the edge of a table. The district was saying three days to the Five, and the boy had the date of the Hearth Drawing by heart the way he had the shape of the door, and nobody under the roof had said what either number was for, or what would be drawn, or from whom.

Weather was the thing everybody agreed about because it was the only thing that cost nobody anything to name. The dry cold got under the oiled paper and lifted it; the Ilm lay under its skin of ice a short walk south and the district said without saying that the ice was holding; Low Kiln went on with its fires and its rolls the way a pot goes on holding water with a crack in it that nobody mentions until the thaw. The Return was present in the house as a habit and nothing more — a fire banked at night, a roll kept in a careful hand, a coat left on its peg — and nobody explained it, because nobody in a clay house explains the thing that keeps it warm. The boy, who could not have found the Clay Yard without being carried, had the whole of the district’s arithmetic by heart and no way to spend a single figure of it.

Garl’s objects kept their places. The wheel-seat stayed hollow on the left and Sella would not let anyone fill it. The coat kept the salt stiffness in it and the broad shape on the peg, and Jorra had twice reached for it in the dark and stopped. The Ash ridge ran along the skirting board with its sour mineral edge, and the house had not swept it in a year and would not sweep it before the Last Day. In the kiln ash the charred edge of a slate lay black on one side and eaten into the shape of a strip of bark, the writing gone off the end, and the house had swept around it for a year without picking it up. “Leave the crack showing,” the house said to the pot, in the dead man’s improved voice. “People trust a thing they can see has been handled.”

The arithmetic was performed again without anyone performing it. The foundling had the bed under the roof, warm and watched and fed from the house’s own dish; the clay-bound hand had the Debt Loft above the shed and a broom and the small knife back where it could be checked. The two facts sat side by side in the room like two cups set down at the same distance from the fire, one of which would be warmer before the morning was out. Nobody called the arrangement a decision and nobody called it kindness, and the two words would have described the same act equally badly. The part of him that had once stood in a bay and sorted twenty bodies into three piles read it in a glance and found the ordinary shape of it, and the part of him that wanted the breast read it as cold and hunger, and neither part had a sentence that could be said in that room. The house straightened around a silence, and the boy was inside the silence, and the warmth went where it had been decided warmth should go, and no entry was made of it anywhere.

The pot’s note went into the house’s noise and was gone. The coals held under their ash. The boy’s hand outside the fold went up an inch toward the wheel-shed and came down, and the door at the threshold stood its finger’s width open, and the kiln breathed four, four, and a gap, and the count stayed where it had been put.

❦

Corin’s hand stayed where it had come down. It lay in the fold by his ear with the milky nails turned up to the oiled paper, and the morning went on over it the way dust goes on settling on a shelf nobody has thought to wipe. The door kept its finger’s width open, and the dry cold came in at that width the way a thread comes through a hole in a wall, fine and persistent and patient enough to have its way. He knew how far the light had moved because he had watched it cross a hand’s breadth of the paper since the kiln’s first breath; nobody else under the roof marked its travel, and he had no way to tell anybody he had marked it. The body wanted the breast. The man wanted the morning entered somewhere, in some hand, against some account. Neither got what it wanted. The fold rode back off the ear again, and he waited for a hand that did not come.

Alda Reed came into the Vail kiln-house in the middle of the morning, when the gruel things were put away and the day had settled into its work. She had come from the east, from the direction of House Reed, past the small niche of the birth-flower shrine where the birth-slates of the district were left overnight; she did not come from either place, and neither place was spoken of. She wore the grey wool shawl of every season, and the smell of her went ahead of her through the door: soap, warm milk, tallow, and under those the faint mineral tang she promised herself every day she would sweep tomorrow. Clay lay under her scrubbed nails. Her hands were steady in the way that makes a mother grab the wrist and not the arm when the pain comes.

The first thing she did was reach for a slate.

It lay on the bench by the door, turned writing-down, and her hand went to it before it went to the child — not to lift it, only to set its edge square, the way a woman straightens a thing she has long since given up leaving crooked. Then, and only then, she came to the bed.

Under the kilnhouse Sella Vail was at the damper and did not turn. The two women had an arrangement that was a silence with a door in it; the door had been in it for three years and neither had put a hand to the latch. Alda had delivered Enna and had sat with Sella through a night in which nothing she did mattered, and had crossed herself over the second child and told nobody what she had seen, and had lied to the second child’s mother about where he had gone; Sella had taken the child and had never asked a word about any of it. The arrangement held in the ordinary way: by both women standing a pace apart in the same room and giving all their attention to the fire, the bench, the wool. There was more understanding in the space between them than in most conversations, and none of it could have been said aloud without putting a figure to it.

Alda put two fingers to the fold at the boy’s shoulder and pressed it flat. The pressing went on a moment longer than a fold needed, the way this house pressed things — as if a hand could keep warmth in by holding it down. She did not look at his face. The wool had ridden back at the wrist, and the red edge of the birth-flower showed there, a branching line the colour of a coal that has not quite gone out, running from the third rib down the forearm and stopping short of the wrist. She crossed two fingers over it. She did it the way a woman brushes a crumb from a table, without looking and without knowing the hand had gone, and then she drew the fold over the place and said a date to nobody in particular.

He watched the order in which her hand went. Slate, fold, wrist, face: she had reached in that order for four mornings now, and every time she had covered the third reach with a date before anyone could ask her what it had been. He could have told her the order. He could have told her what the two fingers meant, if the sign had meant what he thought it meant, which he did not know, and if there had been a word in the room that would have let him ask. The body in which he knew all this wanted to be picked up. Both hungers were in him at once and the louder one was the smaller.

❦

The neighbour woman came in from two houses down with a child bundled against her coat. She did not knock: the door stood its finger’s width open, and knocking at a door already open was not a thing done in Low Kiln. She crossed to the bench with the child held the way a pot is held that might slop, and she said the permitted question, which was not the question she had come in with.

“What should I do with him?” she said. “Should I keep him by the fire?”

She did not ask whether he would live. That was not a question the district allowed a mother to ask out loud, and she had learned the shape of the one it allowed instead: a practical question about the fire, the pot, the door, anything that could be answered with an instruction and a date.

The child was three days out of a return that had been thin from the beginning. It had been born small, kept warm, given back on the third day because the third day was the day the district gave children back if it gave them back at all, and it was worse now than it had been when it went. Its fingertips had gone the colour of the Ash on the bench — grey at the ends, paler at the base, the colour of a thing that has been left out of a fire — and the skin at its mouth was drawn back a little at the corners, which on a warm child would have been a smile and on this one was the cold pulling the face into the shape of one. Its breathing came in shallow and went out shallow, with a small catch in the middle that the mother had stopped hearing because she had been listening to it for three days.

Alda’s hand went inside her bodice to the little tin on its cord and stayed there for the length of one breath, and came out empty. She did not open it in company. She had not opened it in front of a mother in thirty years, and there was no question in the air that would have made her.

“Stone seven,” she said. “The account went up on the eighth, before the paper went out. What I set down was warm and breathing. There’s nothing in the slate beyond that to trouble you.”

The dates arrived in the order she had said them before: the seventh, the eighth, the paper going out on the ninth, the midwife’s entry in its proper hand. She did not say them the way a woman remembers; she said them the way a woman reads from a thing in front of her, each one exact, each one squared off at the corners, one after another with the small stop between them that he had heard twice before in a life that was not this one. The mother had stopped listening for an answer after the second date and was rocking the child against her coat, and still the dates came, the way a hand goes on pouring after the cup is full.

“A graft is settling,” she said at the end of them. “That’s the second chill. So did the one before him. That’s all I’ve written.”

The two women who had come in behind the mother took the phrase up and passed it along the room the way a pot is passed from hand to hand in a kitchen where everybody has done it a hundred times. One of them said it came on the third day and went off by the fifth if it went off at all; the older one said her mother had called it the same thing and her mother’s mother before that, and that it was the order’s word and had always been the order’s word. They knew it from their mothers and from the order, and the order had had it longer than any of them, and none of them knew that a physician at Greybank had taken a different word out of his own report in Y.H. 651, long before any of them were born, and had paid the maid who wrote it for her trouble. The word had gone into a tin instead of a slate and had come down by hands that did not ask what it was. In the room it was a graft settling, and that was the whole of what anybody needed to say over a child who had begun to go grey at the fingertips.

❦

He knew what was happening to the child. That was the humiliation of it, and it had nothing to do with being clever.

Daniel Marchetti had stood at the foot of a table in a white room and watched a body come back from the cold and go wrong on the way. The Afterdrop had a shape: the patient worsens at the instant of rescue, the blood coming back from the extremities carrying the cold with it, the core still low, the heart given a reason to stop that nobody had given it while the patient was simply left alone. The cold patient cannot clot. The grimace of severe cold puts a smile on a face that is not smiling, and strangers thank God for it, and mothers stop being afraid for an hour. You do not rub a frozen foot. Rewarming has its own treachery, and the treachery arrives wearing the face of recovery, so that a room full of people who love a child will read the worst sign in it as the best and go on being kind.

He knew all of it the way he knew the back of his own hand. He had never once been allowed to say any of it in a room like this one, and if he had been allowed, there was no mouth in the room it could have come out of. There was no sentence in Grathic that would have carried it, and if there had been a sentence, the infant body lying under the wool would have made of it a small wet sound and nothing more.

The women corrected him without knowing he had spoken. The first crossed to the bench and moved the child out of the draught from the door, and said to the pot, “Don’t set him where the wind finds him. He’ll pay for it in the thaw.” She did not look at the basket or the bed while she did it. The older one put her hand on the fold at the child’s chin and pressed it down with the same flat, certain pressure Alda had used at the boy’s shoulder, and said, “Warmth goes in slow. You can’t hurry a graft. My mother knew it and her mother knew it.” And Alda, who had already said the dates, said the dates again, slower, because the mother had begun to cry without making any sound and a date was the only comfort the district had taught her to give.

None of them raised a voice. This was ordinary work, done in the ordinary way, by women who knew what this was and what to do about it, and the kindness of it was exact and older than any of them and wrong. The room arranged itself around the wrong answer the way a pot is seated on a level place: without effort, without a word about it, with a hand that knows just how much pressure the thing will bear.

He had signed a chart once without reading the note underneath it, and the note had said the woman had been vomiting, and four hours later she had come back in cardiac arrest with a spleen the size of a fist, and the institution had called what happened a failure of the system and given him back his day and his licence and his good name. He had carried that shape in him for twenty years without once finding a word for it, and here it was in a room of soap and warm milk and tallow, done in softer hands and a kinder voice, with a tin on a cord where the chart had been. He recognised the shape so completely that there was nothing to add to it. The recognition stood in him without a word, because there was nobody in the room he could have said it to and no way to make the air move into the shape of a sentence.

❦

Alda took a slate from under her shawl — her own, not the house’s, smaller than the birth-slates, the edge worn round by thirty years of being carried — and wrote on it with a stub of chalk. The writing went down fast. It went down faster than a mother’s question had any business making it go, faster than any hand should write while it is also holding a child’s wrist between two fingers and reading the colour of the fingertips. When she had done, she turned the slate over and set it writing-down on the bench, where an officer might have picked it up and found nothing, and a mother might have picked it up and found nothing, and both would have been right to.

“Keep the pot off the floor,” she said to the pot. “And don’t set him in the door. You’ll pay for it come thaw.”

“Stone seven,” she went on, to the mother, to the room, to no one. “The account went up on the eighth, before the paper went out. Warm and breathing. That’s what’s set down, and that’s the whole of it. The eighth, before the paper. You can take that as said.”

The mother rocked the child. The other women took up their things. The dates went on coming after the question had stopped, as they always did, as they had done in a birth room and in a yard and at the edge of a market where nobody had asked her to give them. Alda’s hand went inside her bodice again, and the tin settled back against warm milk and tallow on its cord, and her hand came out with nothing in it, and the cord did not show above the wool.

The boy’s hand went to the place where a pocket should have been.

It was a habit, not a thought. The hand went in toward a coat that was not there, toward a small cold rectangle of light with a name on it that was not there to be looked at or refused, toward nine promises of a boat on a lake in a country that did not exist under this roof. The hand found no coat and no pocket and no light and no name. There was warmth at his belly from the house’s own dish and the house’s own fire, unentered, and there was the fold of wool under his fingers, and the hand closed on the fold and stayed there. The gesture went the way all his gestures went in this house: into the wool, with nobody seeing it and nobody to have seen it, and no entry made of it anywhere.

The neighbour wrapped the child again in the fold she had brought and carried it out into the yard. The door shut behind her as far as this door ever shut, which was not far, and the dry cold came in at the width of a finger and went on coming. Alda’s hand closed the tin away inside her bodice and her other hand turned the slate’s writing down on the bench; the boy’s hand, having reached a place where no pocket was, closed on the fold of wool and stayed there.

— Day 6 —

Stone 12, Y.H. 812 (Secondday, the Hearth Drawing)

Stone 12 came in on a dry cold that lifted the oiled paper at the windows, and before the kiln had drawn its first breath Sella Vail lifted Corin out of his bed and put him into the basket. She lifted him the way she lifted a pot that is filled and must not be slopped: one hand under the small of his back, the other at his head, the burn shaped like a little door closing first on nothing and then on his weight. The fold had ridden back off his ear in the night; she put it right without looking at him and said something to the wool about the river wind. There was no wind at the door at that hour and none in the room at all, and he knew she was not speaking to the wool. She was speaking the sentence she had said for six days to whatever in the house would hold a sentence and not ask for anything back. The boy wanted the breast. The woman wanted the day kept in its order. Neither got what it wanted, and the basket went out under her arm into the yard.

The year’s first collection had come into the district. He had had the date by heart since the Cross: the Hearth Drawing, Stone 12 of the twelfth month, with the Ilm under ice and the Five spoken of as three days off by people who had counted what they had and did not care to say it. Nobody in the yard had said collection or Drawing to him; those words were not spoken to baskets. He lay under the fold and carried the date the way he carried the count of the floorboards, without use and without letting go. The Clay Yard was the colour of weak tea under its rime, and the brook behind the kiln had a skin on it that would not bear a stone thrown from the bank. Sella walked with her eyes on the ground ahead of her feet and did not look down into the basket once, the way a woman carrying a full pot does not look into the pot while she is crossing a threshold.

She carried him two doors down the yard to a neighbour’s house and went in without knocking, because the door stood its finger’s width open and knocking at a door already open was not done in Low Kiln. The cold came in behind her at that width, went to the floor and stayed there, as cold does in a room that has been warm for a long time and has stopped noticing where the warmth is going. Just inside the door she stopped, turned the basket with her knee, and set it at the edge of the light.

She did not set it where a slate lay open.

There was a slate on the bench by the door, turned writing-down, and she carried the basket past it with her elbow held close and set it down beyond, against the wall, where the boy could see the length of the room and the room, if it looked at all, would see only a bundle of wool and the toe of a woman’s boot. He could see the work. He could not be part of it. That was the whole of the placing, made in one movement without a word, the way a woman sets a cup down where a sleeve will not catch it.

“A pot filled is a pot carried,” she said to the girl at the hearth. “Keep the draught off the bench.” She did not say whose pot or whose draught. She pulled the fold over the boy’s ear and went to stand where the other women were standing, a half-pace from the bench and a half-pace from the bed, with her hands folded and her little-door burn half-closed on nothing. She had set him where she had set him and she would not move him again, whatever happened in the room.

He knew the room before he had seen it. It was the wrong familiarity that had followed him since the first day: a clay-daubed room with a trodden-ash floor, a bench by the door, a pot on the bench, a draught under the door moving the hem of a woman’s skirt with patient, unhurried persistence, the smell of tallow and wet wool and a sour mineral tang he had learned to place in a corner without being told which corner. The grey of the Ash lay in that corner. He noticed it and made nothing of it, because nobody in the room had made anything of it either. It was another house and it was the same room he had been born into and the same room he had been kept in, with the bench in the same place relative to the door and the cold in the same place relative to the fire.

The body did what bodies do to him. His head went where it wanted and stayed; a thread of drool gathered at the corner of his mouth and the wool would not take it; the hand outside the fold went up toward his chin an inch and came down. He had spent twenty years telling other people’s bodies what to do and could not keep his own face dry. The joke of it had gone flat on the third day and the exactness had not. He counted the nails in the bench: four on the near board, four on the next, and a knot the width of a thumb where a fifth nail should have been. He had counted that shape under another roof and did not make anything of the repetition either. Two rooms with the same gap. A house that keeps a fire and a house that keeps a dying man, both with the empty place left in the count and nobody asked to fill it.

❦

The man lay on a board-bed against the far wall. He was a neighbour, a man whose age the grey had taken off him, and he lay with his hands crossed on his belly and his mouth a little open, breathing in a way that used the whole chest and got nothing from it. His skin had the colour of a pot that has stood too long away from the fire, and the nails of one hand showed blue at the base. Two women sat with him and a third stood at the hearth with her back to the room, and none of them was crying, because this was ordinary work done in the ordinary way. One of them had her fingers on the man’s wrist and was counting something there; her lips moved with the count, and she did not look up when the collector came in. The other had a fold of wool in her hands and was pressing it flat over the man’s chest with the same certainty Alda Reed had used at a boy’s shoulder three mornings ago, as if a hand could keep the warmth in by holding it down.

He did not come with a procession. He came in out of the cold with a flat leather satchel under one arm, set it on the bench without looking at the bed, and the room made room for him the way a room makes room for a man who has been in a hundred rooms like this one and will be in a hundred more. His coat was the colour of wet wool; his cuff was soft at the wrist from much washing and folded back twice so the forearms lay bare. The fingers of his right hand were grey at the tips, the colour of skimmed milk gone off, and he kept that hand closed until he needed it. Sella did not step forward and did not step back. She had placed the basket and would keep the placing, and the collector passed her without appearing to see either the basket or the woman.

He set his hand on the slate before he looked at the man on the bed.

The boy watched the order of it: satchel down, hand to slate, then face. He had watched that order at the edge of a market, where a clerk in a washed cuff had laid his hand on the top slate before he looked at the woman waiting, and he had watched the same order at the edge of a flare light, where a woman in a coat the colour of wet ash had written before she looked up. The hand under his eye now was a man’s hand and belonged to neither of them. It was the grammar he had come to recognise before the face: settle the thing first, look at the person after. He had no word for the grammar in Grathic and had never heard anybody in a room like this one give the order a name.

From the satchel the collector took a roll of cloth, and from the cloth a tube of cold iron the length of a finger, with a cloth stopper in one end and a narrow mouth at the other. The iron had been cold in the cloth and it was colder on the bench; the girl at the hearth moved her cup away from it without being told. Nobody said anything over the tube. Nobody crossed himself. A collector setting a tube on a bench in Low Kiln was an ordinary thing, like a man setting down a tool he means to use and has used before, and the room went on with what it had been doing. The woman at the wrist finished her count and began it again from the start.

❦

Daniel Marchetti had stood at the foot of tables in a white room and known the order of an operation whether the surgeon said it aloud or not: the field, the way in, the instrument, the response of the thing underneath, the complication, the cost. He had learned that order standing in blood that was not his, and had never once thought to look for it in a room that smelled of tallow. He looked for it now because there was nothing else in this room that belonged to him, and because his mind had nothing better to do with itself than find the shape of a thing he could not touch.

The collector found the field with two fingers and not with his eyes. He turned the man’s arm and felt along the inside of the forearm toward the elbow, and stopped where something under the skin could be felt moving against pressure. Dan knew the place without being told. He had found it on a thousand arms in a life where the thing under the skin was blood; here the man was not bleeding and would not bleed, and the collector was not feeling for blood at all. He pushed the cuff further up and wiped the place with a rag from the satchel, unhurried, not clean in the way Dan would have called clean, but careful in the way a man is careful who has done a thing four thousand times and has never once been permitted to hurry. The rag was folded into a square before it was used and folded again when it was done, and the woman at the hearth did not have to tell him where to put it.

He set the tube against the place and held it.

His fingers did not press. They set and held, and the holding was the whole of the work. Dan had seen hands like that in the old life: the hands of a man who has reduced a shoulder a hundred times and knows that the moment of force is the moment you stop using it. The cuff was soft at the wrist, the fingertips were grey, the hand was exact, and exact was not cruel. That was the worst of it. There was nothing in the hand to object to and no part of the room objecting to it, and the room was right to, because the man on the bed had been dying before the collector came in and would have gone on dying if the collector had stayed away. The only thing in the room that objected was a bundle of wool against the wall, and it had no voice and no hand that could reach the length of the bench.

Under the tube the man changed. His breathing went shallow and then deep, the way a sleeper’s goes when a cold hand is laid on him; the fingers of his free hand opened; a small sound came out of his mouth that was not a word. The woman at the wrist went on with her count. The girl at the hearth moved the pot an inch further from the draught. The man’s eyes were open now and looking at the ceiling, and whatever they saw there they did not ask anybody to explain. Then the man gave.

He gave with the small flat knock.

Dan had heard it four times. He had heard it from a cup across a birth room in House Silt, and from a pot set down in the Vail wheel-shed, and twice from the district’s own slate at the Cross: once in the morning under the notice, while two hundred people leaned in to hear a figure, and once in the afternoon when the market was coming apart and the barrow of grey was going out toward the north gate. Four hearings from three things, and nobody had tapped any of them for him, and there was no word in Grathic for the sound, and now a fifth, and this one had come out of a man. He knew the full note from its flat shadow and had no word for either in this language or in any other he could reach.

It was not a crash. A dropped pot says crash and everybody turns; this said knock, small and flat, and stopped at the surface, as though the thing that had been holding had only been set down and not emptied. He knew what it was without anybody telling him, the way he had known the shape of a chest going under a spreader and the particular quiet that follows a clamp coming off a vessel that has stopped. The walls going. The thing held going out. The sound finishing where it began. He had known it since he was twenty-four years old and had never once been able to do anything about it, and now he was six days old and lying in a basket against a wall with wool under his chin. The fifth hearing stood with the other four and was not like them, because the other four had come out of clay and stone and this one had come out of flesh that had been warm this morning.

Nobody in the room looked up at the sound. The woman at the wrist went on with her count; the collector kept the tube where it was for the space of two breaths, the way a surgeon holds pressure in a cavity after the bleeding has stopped, because stopping is not the same as finished; then he took the tube away and wrapped it in the cloth. The man’s arm lay on the folded rag with a pale round place on it where the iron had been, not a wound, no blood, only a place where the colour had gone out of the skin. The woman pressing the fold flat at the man’s chest stopped pressing and set her hands in her lap. The room went on being ordinary with a concentration that Dan recognised: this was how a room carried on when a thing had happened that nobody intended to make into a thing.

Dan knew what had happened better than anybody in that room. He had spent his life knowing it and there was no part of him that could move toward it. His hand outside the fold went up an inch toward the bench, toward the tube in its cloth, toward the slate where the number was going, and came down. The wool closed over it. Nobody saw the hand. Nobody had said a word to the basket since the door had let him in. Correctness was the whole humiliation, and the man with the soft cuff went on being reasonable.

The collector set the slate square on the bench and wrote. The chalk went down in a neat, ordinary hand, small and upright, the hand of a man who has written the same line ten thousand times and has never needed to improve it. It was not the other hand. There was no pause in it to set beside the three he carried, nothing between the clusters that would have let him put this writing with the writing at the flare and the writing on the birth-slate and the writing at the Cross, and he made nothing of it. He had learned to leave the three where they lay. When the figure was down he passed his finger over it once, the way a man checks a measure he has taken twice and does not expect to find wrong, and the slate lay flat under his palm while the chalk rested in its groove.

He spoke once, and he spoke softly, to the slate and not to the room, and not to the man on the bed, and least of all to the basket. “The measure is banked,” he said. “The account stands. The return is recorded.” He said it in the voice the clerk at the Cross had used, a voice with the verbs taken out of it and nobody left inside the sentence to have done anything. There was a reason in the man’s face, and Dan could read a reason in a face the way he read a hand-grip: the collector believed he was keeping the world alive by doing what he had done, and had done it every winter of his working life, and would go home to a warm room when the day’s names were all entered. The reason was not cruelty. The reason was the danger of him, which was a smoother thing to live beside than a cruelty would have been, and so more useful to everybody in the room except the man on the bed.

The figure on the slate was the figure the market had leaned in to hear two days ago: one in twelve, printed on a notice soft at one corner, read aloud over the heads of two hundred people who went home with it to their pots and their suppers. Here it went down in chalk on a slab of stone beside a bed, with nobody leaning in. At the Cross the number had been a thing people could shout about or agree to or stand side by side with another number and leave disagreeing; the pigs went over the boards and the number went over the heads of the crowd and neither was asked to account for the other. Here it lay under a man’s hand with a neat edge to it, and the man it had come out of was breathing in another way now than he had been breathing a little while ago, and no woman in the room straightened the fold at his chin.

He could not reach the tube. He could not read what was written. He could not lift a knuckle toward the bench where the chalk lay in the groove of the slate’s frame. He could not have said the word for any part of it in Grathic, and if he had had the word the body under the wool would have made of it a small wet sound and nothing more. The year’s first account had been taken and entered where nobody had addressed him; the house that had kept him warm since the third day had carried him to see it done and had not once told him what it was, and the woman who carried him stood a half-pace off and would not be the one to tell him now.

His hand went up an inch outside the fold and came down. It did not reach the bench. It did not reach his own chin.

The women went back to their work. The girl at the hearth set the pot a little further out of the draught and said something to it about the thaw. The collector closed the satchel and laid the slate flat under his hand, the measure entered in a neat, ordinary hand that any officer would have been glad to sign. The flat knock had gone into the room’s ordinary noise, with the pot and the draught and the women’s low talk. Nobody was tapping anything. The boy lay with his knuckle outside the wool, going up an inch and coming down, and listened to the sound finish.

❦

The flat knock finished in the room’s ordinary noise the way a stone dropped into a full pail finishes: no ring, only a small displacement, and then the surface going still. The women went back to their work. The girl at the hearth moved the pot another inch out of the draught and said something to it about the thaw; the woman at the wrist took up her count again from the beginning, as if a count interrupted were a count spoiled. Sella Vail stood where she had set herself, a half-pace from the bench and a half-pace from the bed, with her hands folded and the burn shaped like a little door shut tight on nothing. She did not look toward the basket. She had placed Corin where he could see the work and not be part of it, and she would keep that placing until she lifted him out of it, whatever the room did next.

The collector took his hand off the slate and the slate went with him. He went out as he had come in, without looking at the bed, the bench, or the basket, and the door stood its finger’s width open behind him, and the cold came in at that width and went to the floor and stayed there, as cold does in a room that has been warm a long time and has stopped noticing where its warmth is going. The collection was done and the room had turned back to what it had been doing before the man came in, which was the work of keeping a child warm.

On a folded quilt at the other side of the hearth lay a child three days returned from a thin account. He had been carried into the Vail house the morning before under a shawl that smelled of smoke and milk, and carried out again in the afternoon, and now he lay here with his head back and his mouth a little open, breathing in a way that used his whole chest and got nothing from it. He was smaller than the boy in the basket, and the grey had come into his fingers from the edges inward the way it comes into clay before the firing. It had come into the nails first and then into the fingers, and the woman at the wrist kept folding the fingers into the wool and uncovering them again to look, and folding them back.

The mother sat on a stool with her hands in her lap and did not hold him. The women had told her that holding a child in this state took warmth out of her and put none into him, and she believed them because she wanted to believe them, and because the alternative was to sit with nothing to do. There was a cup of warm milk on the bench beside her and she had not drunk from it. “Will he know me, if he comes through?” she said, and she said it to her own hands, not to the room. The woman at the hearth told her that a child knows warmth before it knows a face, and that she should keep her voice low and her hands to herself, and went on stirring nothing in the cup. It was an answer to a different question, and it was the answer the room had for all of them. The room smelled of soap and tallow and warm milk, the same smells as a birth room, the same smells as the Vail house, and under them the sour mineral edge that had a corner all to itself.

Two women worked over the child with the concentration of people shelling barley: quick, exact, unhurried, with no wasted reaching. One had her fingers at his wrist and her lips moving; the other pressed a fold of wool flat across his chest with the same certainty the hearth-maid had used at a boy’s shoulder three mornings ago, as though a hand could keep the warmth in by holding it down. A third stood at the hearth and did not bring the cup to the child’s mouth, because a child in that state must not be woken to drink. They did not raise their voices. This was ordinary work, and the ordinary way of doing it did not include raising voices over a child who might yet come through.

“A graft is settling,” the woman at the wrist said to the quilt. “That’s all it is. It comes to some and they come through it and some do not. You wrap the feet and you wait.”

It was the second chill, and it had a name in the room and an order in the room and a thing to be done about it, and the women had known all three before they had ever thought to question the order of it.

From the basket, the boy watched the child the way he had once watched a chart go past him in a bay: not for the story anybody told over the bed, but for the small evidence a body carried and nobody had asked to read. The breath was shallow at the top, then deep, then shallow again, with a little pause at the end that was longer than the breath itself. The fingers were grey from the edges inward. The child’s mouth had relaxed into something that looked like contentment, and the corners of it were set in a way he had learned to distrust: the face that smiles when the body is too cold to move, because the muscles have set that way and no one has thought to look beneath the expression.

❦

Alda came across the room when the cup had been set down. Alda Reed smelled of soap and warm milk and the faint mineral tang of the Ash she had promised herself she would sweep tomorrow; the clay under her nails was scrubbed to the quick and still there. She did not go to the child first. She looked for a flat place for her slate, found the bench by the door, set the slate down with the writing turned under, and only then took the child’s wrist between two fingers. It was the order he had watched in three rooms now: the slate before the face, the instrument before the person, the thing settled before anybody looked at the thing that could not settle itself.

The mother asked three things in one breath: whether to wrap him, whether to warm the stone, whether to wake him and try the milk. Alda’s hand went inside her bodice to the cord and the little tin. Her thumb rested on it for the space of a breath and came out with a date. “Stone eight the account went up,” she said. “Before the paper went out. Don’t rub the feet; you’ll pay for it in the thaw.” She said it to the quilt, not to the mother. It answered none of the three questions and it closed all of them. The mother wrapped the feet. The room went on with its work, and the exactness of the date went into the air of it the way chalk dust goes into a room and settles on every flat surface. He had heard that exactness before in another mouth, over a chart that said one thing and a note under the chart that said another, and in this room it was the kindest voice present and it was closing a question nobody had opened.

Alda turned the child’s wrist over and felt along the inside of the forearm toward the elbow, as though looking for a way, and found what she was looking for and let it go. She took the chalk from behind her ear and wrote on the flat of a second slate two lines in a small upright hand, and when the lines were down she turned the slate writing-down and slid it under the milk cup where no mother would think to pick it up. The boy in the basket knew a hand that turned its own writing down when he saw one; he had watched it across a birth room and at the edge of a market and now beside a death-bed, and he had never once been allowed to see what lay on the other side.

The child had a red mark at the edge of the hairline, no bigger than a thumb-nail, branching where the fair skin showed through the stubble. Alda’s two fingers went to its edge and crossed, and went back to the wrist, and she did not know she had done it in front of a room full of women and a basket against the wall. She covered it with the fold. “Warm the stone first,” she said to the pot. “A cold stone is a stone, not a kindness.” Nobody asked her what sign she had made, and nobody asked why the child’s wrist had been turned over, and the room went on being ordinary.

Daniel Marchetti knew the Afterdrop without being told the word for it in this country. The patient gets worse at the moment of rescue, when the cold blood comes back from the limbs and the heart must deal with what it brings; the cold patient cannot clot, so a cut that should seal goes on welling; you do not rub a frozen foot, because the ice in the cells has been moved and not melted; and a face that looks peaceful is often a face that has given up. Rewarming has its own treachery, and he had spent twelve years being paid to know its shape.

The white light of Cook County Hospital in Chicago came back for two sentences and took the room with it. The light there has no shadow and the body on the table is a girl who was walking an hour ago, and a nurse is holding pressure in a cavity with both hands while the resident waits for the sentence that opens the chest. The chart is on its stand with the nurse’s note turned face-down under the clip, and his own hand is going past it toward the signature while the patient warms and the patient worsens and the room wants him to decide.

Then it was the death-house in Low Kiln again, and the clay yard outside under its rime, and a child on a quilt whose breath did not finish. He had stood at the foot of that table and decided in under a minute, hundreds of times, and carried the decision out of the room behind him; here he was six days old in a basket, the only adult in the room and the least use in it. He knew what was happening to the child better than any woman there knew it, and there was no sentence in the room that would take the knowledge from him. He said nothing. The body opened and shut his mouth for him.

The body wanted the breast and wanted it now, wanted the drool wiped off its chin and a hand to turn it out of the draught; the man inside it wanted ten seconds with the child’s wrist and a light he could point where he pleased. Both wants were in the same small chest and neither had any standing in that room. The first was louder and had no words, and won nothing, because nobody had been sent to feed a boy in a basket. He lay and counted the child’s breathing on the quilt: shallow, deep, shallow, pause, with the pause the longest of the four. He had made that count in a bay without ever deciding to. Here it was the only thing in the room that was his, and it was no more use than chalk dust.

Every woman corrected him without knowing he had spoken. The one at the wrist drew the grey fingers back inside the wool and told the quilt that a babe mustn’t be let to claw at himself. The one at the hearth turned the cup of milk and said that warmth out was warmth lost and a body must be coaxed, not hurried, and that the child had been warm in the morning and would be warm again by morning. A third came from the door with a fresh fold and tucked it under the child’s chin, saying that the cold goes in by the mouth and the feet and nowhere else, which was a saying and not an answer. The mother got up, put a clean fold over the child’s feet, and sat down again with her hands in her lap. None of them raised a voice. There was nothing to raise a voice about: the room knew what this was and what to do about it, and had known it longer than any of them had been alive, and the only thing in it that disagreed lay under a fold of wool with a thread of drool at the corner of his mouth.

The boy’s hand went to the place where a pocket should have been. There was no coat there, no cold rectangle of light with a name on it, no nine summers of a boat promised and never bought. There was only the fold of wool closed over his chest and a small soft hand inside it with milky nails. He did not look at the name. He had not looked at it on the expressway and he would not look at it here, and the absence of it was the one part of him that had not changed in the crossing.

While the women worked he let his eye go along the skirting board. The grey lay there in a ridge, thicker by a finger’s width than the ridge under the skirting board at the Vail kiln-house, and it turned the corner by the door without a break and went on into the next room the way water goes on in a channel and does not stop at a stone. Nobody had swept it. Nobody had said what it was. He put it with the ridge at Vail and with the charred edge of the slate in the Vail kiln ash and with the grey barrow that had gone out through the north gate on the day of the reading, and kept the three side by side without joining them, the way he kept the kiln’s four, four and a gap. Nobody had explained any of the three, and nobody had asked him to understand them.

The child’s breath went shallow and came back shallow, and the women went on with their work. The mother held the cup of milk and did not drink from it. Alda wrote another word on her turned slate and put the chalk back behind her ear. The man on the board-bed lay with his hands crossed on his belly and the pale round place on his arm where the iron had been, and no woman straightened the fold at his chin. Sella Vail had not moved from her half-pace; when the mother looked toward the basket, Sella did not look back, and the mother looked away.

The year’s first measure had gone out of the dying in the morning. Before the day was done the newly born in the same room gave as well, and the room called it a graft settling and went on calling the day ordinary. Nobody entered either thing in the account the district kept, and nobody asked the boy why he lay with his hand closed on nothing.

In the corner the grey ridge turned the door’s corner without a break, a finger’s width thicker than the ridge under the Vail skirting board. The boy’s hand lay closed on the fold where a phone would have been, and the count of the day went into the place nobody could tax.

Dusk came down into the Clay Yard not as a fall of dark but as a thinning of light through wet wool and woodsmoke, until the rims of the pots on the drying rack went soft and the trodden ash underfoot took the colour of skimmed milk. Behind the house the Slip Brook went on talking to itself in the dark, and beyond it the Ilm lay under its skin of ice. Corin came back under the Vail roof in the basket the way he had gone out of it — carried, not consulted — with his chin bad again and a thread of drool at the corner of his mouth that had frozen in the cold and would not wipe itself away. The door did not quite shut behind them. It never did. He had learned to measure the width of its failure by the sound of the wind getting in, and tonight the wind got in at a finger’s width and went along the floor to the skirting board, where the grey lay waiting in the corner as if it had been poured there and left to settle.

Sella Vail set the basket down where the fire could reach it and nowhere near a slate, though no slate was out. She put her little-door hand into the fold and set the fold right at his ear and under his chin without once looking at his face, the way a potter straightens a rim by touch in the dark; then the hand went back to the coals. It did not go to the widow’s mark, the thumb-length of lead-glazed clay on its cord at her throat. The mark was for the kiln-mouth and the hour before it opened, and the kiln had been shut all day and would stay shut until morning, so the hand stayed where it was. With the back of a broken pot she drew the ash over the coals in a slow sweep, pressing the edges down with the flat of her palm, making a house for the heat to sleep in.

“There,” she said to the fire. “That’ll hold.” She did not say where they had been. She did not say what had been taken in the room they had been in, or from whom, or why the boy had come home with a fold that smelled of soap and tallow and the sour edge of another house’s corner. She said nothing to the basket at all, because the basket was not a person and had not asked her anything.

He had the rule of the mark now, as he had the rule of the kiln’s breathing: touched before the opening, at no other time, not even to answer a man who had spoken her name in a market and called the answering a kindness. He did not know what the rule was for. He knew only that it held, the way a seam holds when the heat is right, and that the household went on keeping its fire inside the rule and would not step outside it to explain anything. A man who had spent his life making people hold still while he counted could respect a rule he could not read. He lay and listened to the coals take.

Outside, Tarn swept the yard a second time. He had swept it before anyone woke and he was sweeping it again now that the light had gone, because a yard swept twice is a yard nobody can say was left untidy, and a debt who leaves a thing untidy gives the house a handle on him. The boy watched him through the door’s finger’s width of gap. Tarn moved fast and stopped altogether whenever he was told he was doing well, and no one had told him anything today. Grey Ash sat in the creases of his knuckles and the pale ring showed at his left wrist where the iron clip had sat six years ago.

He set the broom against the wall and took the small bench knife out of his pocket and put it back on the bench where a man could count it, and he did this unasked, as he had done it on the morning the boy came under the roof. “I took the small knife. I put it back. You can check.” He said it to the yard. Nobody had asked him to check anything. The boy knew the shape of a sentence said before anyone had asked the question, and had heard it in another life from men who wanted an account settled before it was opened.

Then the broom went back to work, and the boy waited for it to stop. He had learned that Tarn’s broom stopped for two strokes when a pot set down in the shed gave its small flat note, and that Tarn never turned his head and never named the sound. It had stopped that morning. It might stop again. The pot on the bench gave nothing now; the note was gone into the house’s ordinary noise, and the yard had only the rasp of the bristles and the wind at the door. The broom did not stop. The boy kept the count where it was and did not add to it. There was no word in this country for the note and no breath in him to push a word out, and if he had had both, the sentence would not have been the one Tarn was waiting to hear. A man could keep the full note for six years without ever saying it aloud. The boy understood keeping. He had no idea what the keeping cost.

“Yes, house. I’ll sweep it. I swept it this morning. I’ll sweep it again.” Tarn said it to the ground and went on sweeping, in the tone of a man stating the weather. He had put the knife back where it could be checked and he kept his head down over the broom, and the boy read the lowered head as a man keeping the one thing he owned out of the house’s reach. Tarn had said nothing about the note. The boy had said nothing about it either. That made two of them, which was the first thing about this house that he could have called an equality, if he had had a word for it.

Jorra Vail came in from the back room while Tarn was still sweeping. There was slate dust on his forearms, grey and fine, worked into the creases of the wrist and up past the bend of the little finger, and it was not the bone-coloured slip he wore at the wheel. The boy had learned to tell the two dusts apart the way he had learned to tell one breath of the kiln from the next. Slate dust meant the back room. It meant the low talk and the men whose names were not spoken and the door that was kept closed. Slate dust meant a thing the house did not enter in any roll it showed.

Nobody asked him where he had been. That was the arrangement, and Jorra kept his end of it. He went through into the Vail wheel-shed and drew his hand over the rim of a pot as if checking a thing he already knew, and said to the pot, “You’ll do.” Then, to the room in general and to Sella in particular, without looking at her, “The back room’s cold.” It was not an answer. It was not a question. It was a sentence shaped so that nobody could take it further, and Sella did not take it further. She shifted the damper instead, and he shifted it again before she had finished. Garl Vail was not in the room. His coat kept its salt-stiff shape on its peg and his seat kept its hollow on the left, and the two of them stood for a moment in the silence that had stood there since the boy came under the roof.

The boy watched the dust on the forearms and wanted to know which slate had been broken, and whether it had been a birth-slate, and who had brought it into the back room. He had seen the broken edge of a slate in the kiln ash every day of his life and nobody had ever picked it up. He could not ask Jorra anything, and if he could have asked, Jorra would have answered the pot. So he kept the questions where he kept the rest of it, and the questions went on being questions, which in this house was a thing that could last for years.

❦

When Sella lifted the basket again to carry him from the door to his bed under the roof, the boy’s angle cleared the doorway and the yard opened out beyond it, three doors down to the threshold of House Silt. Dessa Silt stood there with her shoulder against the jamb.

The cough came up out of her as it came up every winter, from the pits and the water in the pits, and she put her hand over her mouth and said to the yard, “Weather.” She did not call it anything else. Her thumb went to the pale scar on her left forearm, the long one where sieve-wire had opened her, and stayed there while she decided something, and when she took it away her face arranged itself into the smile her sister called the door shut: nothing further would be said here, by her, to anybody. She was a thin woman worked older than her years, with slip-grey nails and wet clay at the hem, and she stood at the end of a short walk she had taken a thousand times and had not taken today.

The murmur came across the yard on the cold air, too thin for words and shaped like counting all the same. Nineteen steps from the pit to the drying rack, nineteen back, every day for six years, and now the count had gone out of her feet and into her thumb and was running there without her legs. She counted because the count kept a larger thing out of the room. The boy knew a count used as a door when he saw one; he had kept a few of his own. He could not see what lay behind hers.

She did not look into the basket. That was the whole of it, and the boy lay under the fold and took the not-looking in with the cold air. It was the kind that keeps, not the kind that prices; he had learned the two apart without either woman explaining them. The woman at the threshold had taken the figure that was read aloud in the Cross and had made it a person and laid that person down somewhere out of reach, and she had done it so long ago that the laying down felt like a fact and not a thing she had done. She would not say the number, and she would not say the name, and she had not said either for three years. The boy did not know the number or the name. He knew the shape of an account closed by somebody who had never been allowed to open it.

He wanted something from her that he could not have named and she could not have given if he had. His mouth worked under the wool. A small animal sound came out, and Sella shifted the basket up against her hip and went on toward the bed, and the woman at the threshold went on counting into her thumb, and the yard went on being a yard with two houses in it and a broom at the third.

❦

Night came on and the house drew in around its fire. The boy lay on his bed under the roof with the fold at his chin and the cold coming in at the door’s finger’s width, and he set the day’s facts side by side the way he set nails in a board: four on the near board, four on the next, and a gap. The Ash ridge along the skirting board of the death-house, thicker by a finger’s width than the ridge under this roof, turning the corner without a break. The ridge under this roof, near two thumbs wide, which Sella had not swept in two years and would not let Jorra sweep. The charred edge of a slate lying in the kiln ash, black on one side and eaten into the shape of a strip of bark, with the writing gone off the end. And with them the grey barrow that had gone out through the north gate on the day of the reading, heaped a little above the sides where the load had shifted, and nobody in the square looking at it twice. He kept them side by side and did not join them, because nobody had explained any of them and nobody had asked him to understand them, and joining them would be a kind of deciding that he had no standing to make.

The figure that had been read aloud in the square — one in twelve, as the notice had it — had gone out of a person in a room with a pot on a bench and a draught under the door, and the room had gone back to keeping a child warm. The year’s first measure had been taken at the Hearth Drawing, and the Drawing was behind him now, and the district would go on calling the day ordinary. The boy knew the two things were the same number. He knew it the way he knew a full note from a cracked one, without being told and without any use for the knowing. The household had not changed its arithmetic. The fire was banked. The fold was put right. The debt against the tiles lay where it had been left, uncalled and therefore unpaid and therefore never done with. Nobody in the yard had said what the measure was, and nobody had said where it went. Low Kiln would say three days to the Five when it said anything at all, and would not say what it counted.

He kept the count the way he kept his breath, because there was nothing else in the world that was his to enter. Four, four and a gap. The kiln’s four, four and the breath it missed; the floorboards’ four and four and the knot the width of a thumb that was not a nail; the bell’s four, four and the pause where the fifth stroke should have been. The gap was the part nobody had thought to enter. It lay in the kiln and in the floor and in the bell and in the space between a woman’s thumb and her scar, and it was the one place in this house where nothing had been written down. He could not write there either. He could only lie with his hand outside the wool and let the gap be the part he kept.

Sella fed him from the house’s own dish, barley gruel thinned with milk and warmed at the fire, and she fed him with a spoon she had wiped on her sleeve and did not enter the spoon or the gruel or the boy in anything. She held his head with two fingers behind the ear because he could not hold it himself, and the warmth went into him through his mouth and belly and stayed, and no slate in the district had a line for it. The house had paid twice for him and had now taken a measure out of the district and had not said so, and it went on keeping him the way it kept the fire: by doing the thing every day and leaving no entry where an officer might look for one.

The coals held under their ash. The little-door burn closed on nothing as Sella put the fold right at his chin and went on to the next thing without looking. Along the skirting board the grey ridge turned the corner without a break. His hand outside the wool went up an inch and came down. Four, four and a gap, and Low Kiln saying three days to the Five without saying what it counted, and the door not quite shutting.

The Chronicle of Chapter 4

On the eleventh stone the coals held under their ash, and Sella Vail touched the widow’s mark at her throat before she opened the kiln and at no other time. The boy was five days old and could not keep the wool on his chin; she put the fold right without looking. Tarn took the small bench knife, set it back where it could be checked, and said so unasked; his broom stopped two strokes when a pot in the shed gave its flat note, and he did not turn his head. Jorra spoke to the pots, not the boy.

Alda Reed came to see the child she had delivered, reaching for a slate before him. A neighbour’s child, three days returned, lay grey at the fingertips; Alda touched the tin inside her bodice, gave exact dates where comfort was asked for, and called it a graft settling. Dan knew the afterdrop: the cold that worsens at rescue, the face that smiles when it has given up. The women corrected him without knowing he had spoken. He reached for a pocket with no phone. Her slate went writing-down.

On the twelfth, the Hearth Drawing, Sella set the basket at the edge of the light in a neighbour’s house, where he could see the work and not join it. The collector laid his hand on the slate before he looked at the man, took a cold iron tube from its cloth and held it without pressing; his fingers were exact and not cruel. The man gave with the small flat knock --- the fifth hearing, the first from a person. The measure was banked, the account stood, the return was recorded. The boy’s knuckle went up an inch and came down.

Later the rekindled child grew worse, and the women worked with the certain kindness of a thing they had known all their lives. Alda turned a second note down under a milk cup. For two sentences the white light of another life took the room --- a nurse holding pressure, a hand going past a turned note toward a signature. He knew what the child was doing and had no sentence for the room. The grey ridge along the skirting board was thicker by a finger’s width than the ridge at Vail, and turned the corner without a break.

At dusk the coals were banked without a word, and the fold put right without a look. Tarn swept again and set the knife back; Jorra came in from the back room with slate dust on his forearms and answered nothing. Dessa Silt stood at her threshold three doors down, called her cough weather, and counted nineteen steps into her thumb without looking into the basket. The boy kept four, four and a gap beside the two ridges, the charred slate and the barrow of grey. Sella fed him from the house’s own dish, entered nowhere; Low Kiln said three days to the Five without saying what it counted.
