6

Counting What There Is

Days 10–14 · Stone 16–20, Y.H. 812 · The Vail kiln-house and the Cross, Sill

Count the coal, count the crust, count the days the fire is kept. What you cannot count is who paid for it.

— A Low Kiln saying, Sill

— Day 10 —

Stone 16, Y.H. 812 (Sixthday)

The coals had held through the night under their smooth blind layer of ash, and first light came into the Vail kiln-house at the door’s finger’s width of failure, a thin grey thread that went along the floor to the corner where the Ash lay and stopped there as if it had been told to stop. It was the same light that had come yesterday and would come tomorrow, and Corin did not spend anything on surprise. Ten days old. He lay on his bed under the roof with the crust from the market gone cold in the fold at his side, stiff now at the edges and smelling faintly of smoke and barley, and he could feel its cold through the wool as exactly as he had felt its warmth three nights ago. The cold came in at cheek, then temple, then wrist, in the order he knew and could not argue with. Nobody had spoken to the basket.

Sella Vail came in from the Clay Yard with the cold still on her sleeves and went first to the fire, as she went first to the fire in every hour anyone under this roof had observed. She smelled of tallow and wet wool and, under them, the faint sour mineral tang of the corner she had not swept in two years. She took the back of the broken pot from the ash heap and lifted the edge of the ash layer with it, and the cold ash breathed up under her wrist in a small grey puff that carried the smell of everything the house had ever burned. Under it the coals lay close and red as a thing asleep. She did not open the kiln yet. Before she lifted the lid her hand went up to the widow’s mark at her throat, the thumb-length of lead-glazed clay on its cord with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and held it a moment longer than the touch required, and let it go. That was the order of it: the mark before the lid, and no other hour for the mark, not at the bank at dusk and not when a woman stood at the door with a question. The burn across the back of her left hand, shaped like a little door with its two straight sides and its lintel and its threshold, closed on nothing as the hand came down, and the pale inside of it showed for a moment in the low light and was gone.

“There’s your air,” she said to the coals, and lifted the lid.

The kiln took its breath in fours with one missing. Four, and four, and a gap where the fifth should have been. He had counted it through a wall and across a market and in a room with a dying man in it, and he counted it now with no wall between, and the gap was still the part nobody had thought to enter. The pot stood on the bench where it had stood on Stone 13, turned a little toward the light, its belly whole and its lip unchipped. He had learned in these ten days to turn his head toward that place before anybody set a thing down, and had learned also that the turning was expectation and not hearing, and that a thing which did not happen could leave a space in a room with the shape of a sound in it. He kept that space beside the bench from the death-house with its four nails and four and a knot the width of a thumb, and beside the boards under his bed with the same count, and beside the two ridges of the Ash in two houses that ran to their corners and turned them without a break. He kept them side by side in the order they had arrived and joined none of them.

Tarn came up from the the Debt Loft with the broom under his arm and went to the stack of kindling by the wall before he went to the threshold. He did not touch the stack. He measured it with his eyes the way a man measures a debt, taking the top layer and then the layer under it and then the place where the next layer would lie if there were one, and the boy watched the measuring from under the fold and understood what the man was doing because he had once stood in a supply room at four in the morning and read what was left on a shelf the same way, four there and four there and a place where the third count should have been. The stack stood four bundles along the near row and four along the row behind, and a space at the end where a fifth should have been, with the straw kicked aside and the bare boards showing through. Tarn’s eyes went to the space and came back, and he put nothing into it, and he did not say that anything was missing.

He had six years of the seven behind him and had counted every one, and the seventh he held back, the one entry in this house that the house could not check. He said it to the stack because the stack was a thing that would hold a sentence and ask nothing back. “Six counted,” he said. “The next I don’t count.”

Then he swept the threshold, and swept it again, and set the broom against the wall.

Across the room Sella moved her lips along the shelf where the loaves stood, and the boy watched the moving the way he had once watched a nurse run a finger down a record she did not mean to read aloud: no sound, only the lips arriving and returning. She had arranged the loaves so that the days to Kindling were not in them but the shape of them was — three here and two there and one set a little apart with its brown edge turned up, and then the arrangement again from the other end, coming back to the same place twice. It was enough for a woman who had kept one house through one Long Cold and meant to keep this one through another. Low Kiln went around the district with a figure closed in a fist and let it out under this roof only as an absence, and there was no number in the loaves for a collector to take up and enter, only the shape of one, which was the whole of the art. He had once counted twenty injured people in ninety seconds and sent some to a warm bed and some to a cold one. Here he could not lift his hand to the dish.

Jorra Vail came in from the shed with bone-coloured slip dried to old bone up both forearms and the little finger of his left hand bent the way it had been since he was eleven. There was no slate dust on him, and the boy looked at the clean creases above the bend of the finger and could not ask which the absence meant, any more than he could have asked about the presence of it on the days when it was there. Jorra went to the wheel and then to the bench with a pot in his hands, and said to the pot what he did not say to any person under the roof.

“You’ll do,” he said. “The shoulder’s thick. I’ll thin it.”

Sella had started to tell him the fire was low. He moved the damper before she had finished the sentence, and the fire took the instruction and went on, and she left the end of it unsaid, which was an obedience and a correction in the same movement and neither of them called it either. Garl Vail’s coat kept its salt-stiff shape on its peg behind the door, and the seat in the shed kept the hollow of twenty years on the left, and his proverb went to the pot and not to the basket: leave the crack showing; people trust a thing they can see has been handled. Nobody had filled the seat. Nobody had taken the coat down. Nobody said what the uncalled debt against a firing of tiles was, or where it lay, or how long it had lain there where Berr Clay had left it.

Alda Reed came at mid-morning, when the light on the oiled paper had gone from white to the colour of skimmed milk, and the boy knew her before the door had opened wide enough to show a shoulder. Soap and warm milk came off her, and under them tallow and the faint sour tang of a room nobody had swept, which was the smell of a woman who had spent forty years lifting other people’s children and had never once set one down without first reaching for the thing it was written on. She had her birth-slates under her arm and a narrow slate of her own in the fold of her shawl.

She reached for the slate before she reached for the child.

That was the whole of it, and he had known it would be so before she crossed the floor. He had once stood at the foot of a table in a white room and watched a senior man lay his hand on the chart before he looked at the patient lying under it, and had learned from that what the order of reaching meant: the record first, because the record was the thing that would still be there when the person was carried out. He had learned it again in this house from a woman who kept a roll she did not show and stood a half-pace from every open slate. He had no word in Grathic for the order of it and no standing to correct it, and he had the order itself laid out in front of him as plainly as a chart laid open on a table.

Alda laid the birth-slates flat on the bench with the writing turned down, and only then came to the basket. She put two fingers at the fold by his shoulder and pressed it flat, and held them there longer than a fold required, long enough that the pressure became a different thing from the straightening of wool — a hand saying what a mouth had arranged not to say. She touched the little tin on its cord once inside her bodice, a small movement of the cloth and nothing shown, and went on pressing the fold flat.

The wool had ridden back at his wrist and the red edge of the branching mark showed there, the fire-coloured edge of the thing that ran from his third rib down the forearm and stopped short of the wrist. Alda’s two fingers went to the corner of it and lifted, crossed, and came away. She did not know she had done it. She had done it now in front of witnesses four times and had never once known she did it in front of them, and the boy watched his own wrist from under the fold and put the gesture where he put the others, without a name and without a use. It lay beside the two ridges and the charred slate in the kiln ash and the bench with its knot and the interval where a pot had not answered, and it was the first of those things that had ever been done to him while he lay awake under it.

“Stone seven,” she said to the bench. “The account went up on the eighth, before the paper went out.”

Nobody had asked her for a date. There was no question in the room that the date could answer, and she had answered it anyway, the way a woman closes a door that nobody has opened. She went on speaking after the question had stopped, because there had never been a question and so there was no stopping her: “The cooper’s seventh in Mud End. The fourth day of Stone in eight hundred and six. A potter’s third child, now a carter in Stonemouth.” The dates came faster than dates had any business arriving, each one exact and each one laid over the small thing her fingers had just done at a red edge under the wool.

She took up the narrow slate from her shawl and wrote on it with a charcoal stub what she did not leave lying about. The boy could not read a word of it, could not have read Grathic on a stone a foot from his face, and knew the shape of the act all the same: the head bent, the left hand holding the slate steady, the short strokes of a woman who writes a thing down before she lets herself feel it. When she had done she turned the slate writing-down and put it back into the shawl, not under the birth-slates on the bench and not where any mother or officer would think to pick it up. There was a floorboard somewhere in her house that the note would go to, and he did not know the house and did not know the board, and knew only that a truth had been written down and turned face-down in the same movement that had pressed the wool flat at his shoulder. The two gestures were one gesture. He had spent twelve years in rooms where the same hand did both things, and had never once found a way to make the writing and the warming agree.

Sella came away from the fire with the pot-shovel in her hand and stood a half-pace from the bench. “He lies quiet,” she said, which was not a question and not an answer and was the nearest this house came to telling a visitor that the boy was still under the roof.

“Warm and breathing,” Alda said. “That’s all I’ve set down.”

She said it the way she said everything that was not true, which was exactly, and went on giving the dates after even that had stopped, because there was nobody in the room whose business it was to tell her to stop. The milk and soap went out of the room with her when she went, and the sour tang of the corner came back in its place, and the morning went on being an ordinary morning in which a woman had written a second note and turned it down and warmed a child with the same hand.

Alda’s two fingers were lifted at the red edge of his wrist and covered by a date spoken to nobody, and Sella’s hand was away from the mark again and back at the fire where it belonged, and the stack of kindling by the wall stood four, four, a place where the fifth should be. Nobody had spoken to the basket all morning. The boy kept the gap where he had kept it under the boards and beside the bench and in the space where a pot had not answered, and he could not write it down and had nobody to give it to, and it was the only place in the room where nothing had been entered.

— Day 11 —

Stone 17, Y.H. 812 (Seventhday)

Tarn came up from the Debt Loft before light on Stone 17 with the broom under his arm, and swept. The bristles went over the trodden ash with a dry, dragging sound, back and back to the same board, and the Vail kiln-house had not stirred around him. The coals held under their blind layer from the night as they had held the night before, and the kiln had not been opened, and the crust lay stiff in the fold at the boy’s side, where it had been put two days ago and where it would stay. Corin kept his own count of the strokes and felt the cold come in at cheek, then temple, then wrist, in the order he knew and could not argue with. Nobody had spoken to the basket.

Grey the Ash showed in the creases of Tarn’s knuckles, the grey that no scrubbing lifted, and the pale ring at his left wrist showed where an iron clip had sat in eight hundred and six, a circle of skin that had never gone back to the colour around it. He was twenty-two and bent at the shoulders from six years in the slip pit, which made him look older than his term. Six years of the seven were behind him and he had counted every one, and the year that was left he would not count at all, not even to the ash, because it was the one entry in this house the house could not check. Before he swept the threshold he took the small bench knife from his pocket and set it on the sill where it could be checked, and told the yard what he had done, though there was nobody in the yard to hear it. “I took the small knife,” he said to the trodden ash. “I put it back. You can check.” The mark worn into the handle was almost flat, rubbed away by six years of scraping grey from his knuckles, and he did not look at it when he said it.

Sella Vail came in from the Clay Yard with the cold on her sleeves and went first to the fire, as she went first to the fire in every hour anyone under this roof had observed. Before she lifted the lid her hand went up to the widow’s mark at her throat, the thumb-length of lead-glazed clay on its cord with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and held it a moment longer than the touch required, and let it go. That was the order of it and no other hour for it, not at the bank and not when a woman stood at the door with a question. The burn shaped like a little door across the back of her left hand closed on nothing as the hand came down, and the pale inside of it showed for a moment in the low light and was gone. “There’s your air,” she said to the coals, and lifted the lid.

The kiln took its breath in fours with one missing. Four, and four, and a gap where the fifth should have been. He had counted it through a wall and across a market and in a room with a dying man in it, and he counted it now with no wall between, and the gap was still the part nobody had thought to enter.

Sella looked at the threshold where Tarn’s broom had been. “That’s a clean sweep,” she said. “You’ve done well.”

The broom stopped. It did not stop for a stroke or a half; it stopped altogether, the man behind it going still in the way a man goes still when a door he had been holding shut is opened without warning. Two breaths went by. He did not turn his head. On the second breath he said, “Yes, house,” in the flat voice that agreed so completely there was nowhere for an argument to put its foot, and began sweeping again where he had left off.

The boy had seen the shape of it once before under this roof, on Stone 13, and had kept it without a word. He understood what it cost: that a man who owned nothing could be stopped for two breaths by the one thing he could never enter in any account, which was a kindness from the woman who held his debt. He lay with the fold at his chin and the drool freezing at his mouth and did not look at either of them, because the body has its own humiliations and the surgeon’s eye was not the only thing awake in the basket.

Jorra’s pot stood on the bench where it had stood through the day before, turned a little toward the light. Sella wiped the bench with her sleeve as she passed and turned the pot further toward the thin grey coming in at the door, and set it down softly, the way a woman sets down a vessel she has no need to scold. The boy turned his head before the set-down.

It was not a hearing. He knew that now, and knew it more surely each time; it was the turning of a head toward a place where a thing had once happened, and the body did it before the mind could catch it, the way a nurse’s hand goes to a rail in the dark. The pot gave nothing. There was no small flat knock, no answer from a sound vessel under a knuckle, nothing to put a name to in Grathic even if he had possessed the word, which he did not. There was only the space where the note should have been, and it had the shape of a sound in it.

At the bench Tarn’s broom stopped half a stroke. It checked and went on, and he kept his eyes on the ash under the bristles.

Neither of them said what had not come. The boy put the space at the bench where he put the one from Stone 13, the first of its kind under this roof, and left the count where it had stood, five hearings from four sources. An interval is not a sixth. He had learned to keep the distinction the way a man keeps two similar bones separate on a tray, because a thing that did not sound was not the same thing as a thing that did, and the cost of confusing them was a wrong answer written down in a neat hand. He kept the two intervals side by side and the five where they were, and joined nothing.

Sella gave the instruction to the fire and to the room, and not to the basket. “Don’t sleep in the draught,” she said to the kiln mouth, to the gap of cold that went along the floor to the corner where the Ash lay and stopped there as if it had been told to stop. “The breath takes a man in his sleep.”

“There was a man in Mud End,” Tarn said to the ash. He said it in the tone of a man stating the weather, which was the only tone he had for a thing he had no business knowing. “Slept in front of a kiln with the damper shut. Three leagues downriver. The district called it a merciful death.”

The boy filed that too: the breath that took a man in his sleep, and the word *merciful* laid over it like a lid. He put it with the gruel that came from the house’s dish and was entered nowhere, and the fire that was kept and entered nowhere, and the crust in the fold with a payer who could be named and a recipient who could not refuse it. Under this roof the things that were dangerous were the things that had no entry, and the things that had no entry were the things the house called by gentle names. He laid them out side by side in the order they had arrived, which was the only honest arrangement he had found in this house, and it bought him nothing.

Tarn swept on toward the wall. The boy watched the narrow back, the bent shoulders, the pale circle at the wrist, and understood with the part of him that had once sorted twenty injured people in ninety seconds that the man was keeping something the house had not given him and could not take back, and that it was the same thing he himself was keeping. Not the same sound: Tarn had heard the full note of a pot through the wall, and he had heard a cup across a birth room and the district’s slate twice at the Cross and a man giving under a cold iron tube, four sources and five hearings with one flat answer running through them, and Tarn’s hearing was separate from those and stayed in its own place. The same keeping. Each man held his from the other side of a silence. Neither had a word for it in the language of the house, and neither could hand it over.

He knew the shape of it from the inside, having lived in it for eleven days. A thing you keep that nobody has entered is a thing you cannot spend, cannot refuse, cannot set down. Tarn kept his the way a man keeps a tool he has no right to own: close to him, and announced when he set it on a sill so that nobody could say afterward he had stolen it. The boy kept his the way a man keeps a chart he signed without reading the nurse’s note: close to him, and never handed over, because to hand it over would be to let somebody enter it. The two keepings faced each other across the trodden ash with a broom between them.

And the man’s ear was worth more to this house than the man’s debt, and that made the keeping valuable in a way that was also dangerous, because a thing worth more than the debt that holds a man is a thing a house might one day learn to count. He saw it the way he had once seen a supply shelf at four in the morning: what could be counted on it was not what mattered, and what mattered was the thing nobody had written down. He had no word to say any of it, and no hand that could reach two paces past the fold, and no standing in this house to say a thing even if he had both. The red edge of the birth-flower showed at his wrist where the wool had ridden back, and he did not have a hand to pull the wool down over it. He was eleven days old and the only person in the room who could read the account, and the account went on without him.

Later, when the light on the oiled paper had gone from white to the colour of skimmed milk, Tarn took the small knife out again to scrape the grey from his knuckles. He worked the edge under the Ash in the creases carefully, the way a man cleans a thing he will have to show somebody, and when the creases were as clean as they ever got he held up his left wrist to the light and turned it, so the pale ring showed all the way round, and looked at it for a moment. Then he put the knife back on the sill where it could be checked. This time he did not tell the yard he had done so.

The boy’s hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the broom leaning against the wall and came down. He wanted the handle under his fingers, the weight and the length of it, the way he had once wanted a clamp put into his hand in a room where somebody was bleeding out on the floor. Tarn did not look. His eyes were on the sill, on the mark in the knife handle worn almost flat, and he was already reaching for the broom with his own hand.

Jorra Vail came in from the shed with bone-coloured slip dried to old bone up both forearms and the little finger of his left hand bent the way it had been since he was eleven. There was no slate dust on him. He brought the shed in with him: damp clay and tallow and the sweetish edge of lead glaze that came up from High Kiln on a bad wind, a smell that this district had made into a trade and called skill. He went to the wheel and then to the bench with a pot in his hands, and said to the pot what he did not say to any person under the roof.

“You’ll do,” he said. “The shoulder’s thick. I’ll thin it.”

Sella had started to tell him the fire was low. He shifted the damper before she had finished the sentence, the bent finger going in ahead of the rest of his hand, and the fire took the instruction and went on, and she left the end of it unsaid, which was an obedience and a correction in the same movement and neither of them called it either. Garl Vail’s coat kept its salt-stiff shape on its peg behind the door and his seat in the shed kept the hollow of twenty years on the left, and his proverb went to the pot and not to the basket: leave the crack showing; people trust a thing they can see has been handled. Jorra did not say the back room was cold. He did not look toward the basket.

Dusk came in at the door’s finger’s width, the same thin grey thread that went along the floor and stopped at the corner where the Ash lay. Sella banked the coals with the back of the broken pot and said that’ll hold to the fire, and her hand did not go to the mark, because that was for the hour before an opening and no other. The loaves on the shelf stood in their arrangement of days and bits, three here and two there and one set a little apart with its brown edge turned up, a shape that was not a number and could not be taken up and entered.

Tarn swept the last of the ash back toward the threshold and brought the broom past the bench once more. It stopped half a stroke at the place where the pot had been set down and where no note had come, and went on, and he set the broom against the wall. The small knife lay on its sill with its mark worn almost flat, where a man could check it in the morning. The kiln took its breath in fours with one missing: four, and four, and a gap.

The boy lay with the crust cold in the fold at his side and the second interval filed beside the first, and his hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth and came down. The house went on keeping what it could not count, and he went on keeping the count.

— Day 12 —

Stone 18, Y.H. 812 (Eighthday)

The number went around the yards of Low Kiln before the kiln was opened. It went from the drying racks in the Clay Yard to the well and back along the trodden ash, carried in the mouths of women who asked each other how long it was to Kindling and how long to the Five, and answered with a finger shut into a fist so that the days might be counted without ever being said. It came to the Vail door as a question and went away again as an absence, because this house did not put its number into the yard. Corin lay in the basket under the fold in the Vail kiln-house and felt the cold arrive in the old order, cheek, then temple, then wrist, and heard the figure go past him the way a man hears a cart go past a wall he will not climb. Nothing had been spoken to the basket.

A neighbour stood at the threshold with a slate held flat against her apron, the open writing turned toward her own chest. Mud had dried pale on the hem of her skirt and her hands were red from the tub, and she did not come in. Sella Vail stood a half-pace short of the slate all the while the woman was there, the little-door burn across the back of her left hand shut on nothing, and would not be drawn the half-pace nearer by anything the neighbour said.

“How long, then,” the neighbour said, not to the basket or to Sella but to the slate. “There’s the levy to think of, and what the Thane left lying where it lay—”

The end of the sentence was not said. The debt against the firing of tiles lay in the space where the end should have been, and the space had the shape of a hollow seat, and both women looked at the ground between them rather than put a name on it. In this district a debt never called was a debt that could never be paid, and a thing that could never be paid was spoken around as carefully as the Ash was swept around in the corner.

“A body might want a number to put by,” the neighbour said. “It’s a kindness, the sharing, and it’s entered where it’s entered. That’s the whole of it.”

“The house keeps what the house keeps,” Sella said. She said the house where another woman would have said I, and added, “You can ask the pot. It’s more use.” The neighbour had come for a number and got a house and a pot, and she went away with her slate still turned to her own chest and her fist still shut.

The boy filed the order of it: hand to the slate, then the sentence about the kindness, all in the time it takes a kettle to come to the boil. He had heard that grammar before he had a word of Grathic to put to it, and the grammar had the shape of a chart whose top line and bottom line agreed because somebody had turned the middle one down.

Then Sella went to the fire. Before she lifted the lid her hand went up to the widow’s mark at her throat, the thumb-length of lead-glazed clay on its cord with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and held it a moment longer than the touch required and let it go. That was its hour, the hour before an opening, and she had no other hour for it. “There’s your air,” she said to the coals, and lifted the lid.

The kiln took its breath in fours with one missing. Four, and four, and a gap where the fifth should have been. He had counted it in a room with a dying man in it and through a wall and across a market, and he counted it now with no wall between, and the gap was still the part nobody had thought to enter. He had five hearings of the full note from four sources and had added nothing to them; a thing that did not sound was not a hearing, and the two intervals under this roof lay on a separate tray, as a man keeps two similar bones separate on a tray, because a wrong answer written down in a neat hand cost more than the trouble of keeping them apart.

Tarn had swept before light and the small bench knife was back on the sill where it could be checked. He was twenty-two and bent at the shoulders from six years in the slip pit, with grey Ash in the creases of his knuckles and the pale ring at his left wrist where the iron clip had sat. Six years of the seven were behind him and he had counted every one; the seventh he held back, the one entry in this house the house could not check. “I’ll sweep it again after,” he said to the trodden ash. “I swept it this morning. You can check.” He did not look up. The broom went back and back to the same board.

Jorra Vail came in from the shed with bone-coloured slip dried to old bone up both forearms and the little finger of his left hand bent from a splinter he said had come from a fight. There was no slate dust on him. He went to the wheel and then to the bench with a pot in his hands. “You’ll do,” he said to the pot. “The shoulder’s thick. The wheel doesn’t care whose hands, only if they’re steady.” Sella had begun to tell him the fire was low; he shifted the damper before she had finished, the bent finger going in ahead of his hand, and she left the end of the sentence where it was. Garl Vail’s coat kept its salt-stiff shape on its peg behind the door and his seat kept the hollow of twenty years on the left, and his proverb went to the pot and not to the basket: leave the crack showing; people trust a thing they can see has been handled. Nobody said the back room was cold. Nobody looked into the basket.

By mid-morning Sella had carried the basket down to the yard well to draw water and set it on the shaded side of the stone lip, where a little roof dripped onto the stones in a slow, regular fall. the Slip Brook went over its stones behind the drying racks, and the wind off it came down the yard with clay in it. The cold came up through the basket and through the wool, and the boy lay with the crust stiff in the fold at his side and the drool freezing at his mouth, and counted the drips from the little roof. Four, and four, and a gap where the water fell on a stone that did not ring. He could not lift a hand to his own chin, and the well was a short walk from the racks and a long one from anything anyone would call his.

A neighbour woman came to draw with a slate tucked under her arm and a child at her skirts, a child of four or five with a running nose and the red chafe at its wrist where the wool had ridden back. She put the slate down on the stone lip first, turned so that the writing faced her, and only then put her hand out to the child to keep it from the well. “Keep off the lip,” she said. “It’ll go in.” It was the order of the reaching that caught him, not the words: the hand to the record before the hand to the person, every time, the way a nurse’s hand goes to the chart before it goes to the patient.

The ceiling light is on at four in the morning, a white hard circle over the hospital, and under it a chart lies at the foot of the bed with its note turned face-down and a hand already going past it toward the signature line. The hum runs under the light and he does not read the note and his hand goes past it.

Then it was the well again, the drip from the little roof, the river wind putting its cold hand between the woman and her child. The boy’s hand outside the wool went to the place where a pocket should have been. A pocket held wool where a lit phone with a name had lain, and the name under the lit rectangle was not looked at then and was not looked at now. He had reached that pocket in a market under a collector’s soft cuff and at a threshold and in a death-house, and each time the hand had come back with nothing but woodsmoke in the wool. Nine promises of a boat on a lake he had never seen crossed with the reaching and went out. The hand came down.

The woman drew and went, the child’s nose wiped on a sleeve, the slate turned down against her apron again as if a thing turned down were a thing put right. She had not looked into the basket. She had reached for the slate first. He filed the reaching hand where he filed the others: the midwife’s two fingers crossed at the red edge of a wrist and covered with a date nobody had asked for; the clerk’s hand laid on the top slate before his eyes went to the woman waiting; the man who had priced him with a finger passed over a figure; the woman who had kept him with a fold of wool and not a word. They lay in a row on the tray, and one grammar ran through the row, and it was the grammar of a chart that made what had happened agree with what was written down.

❦

Back under the Vail roof the light on the oiled paper went from white to the colour of skimmed milk, and the boy lay on his bed with the crust cold at his side and the fold put right at his chin.

He had signed a chart once over a note he had not read, and the hospital had called it a failure of the system and let him keep his job and his licence, and he had carried the shape of it for the rest of a life. He knew what a note turned face-down was for. It was so that the line at the bottom could agree with the line at the top, and so that nobody would have to say which of the two was a lie. Here the district did the same thing in plain market speech and called it by its ordinary names: a kindness entered, a debt left lying, a number kept in a fist, a mark confirmed and entered as a kindness. The grammar was the same grammar. What made it dangerous was not the Thane in his apron, because the Thane believed his own sentences; it was that the grammar was reasonable, and a man could go home to a warm room beside it, and the boy had lived inside it for twelve days without once being asked whether he agreed to any part of it.

The kindness that could not be answered and the account that could not be closed were the same shape seen from two sides. The bed under the roof was a kindness he had no word to refuse, and the debt in the Debt Loft was an entry he had no hand to enter, and the two faced each other across the trodden ash with a broom between them. He had more of the account in his head than anyone in the house. He could not write it, speak it, spend it, or reach the place where it was kept. He was twelve days old, the only adult in every room and the least use in any of them, and the correctness of it was the whole humiliation.

Sella came in and stood a moment with her little-door burn shut on nothing and looked at the place where the boy lay, and put the fold right without looking at his face. “The house brings what it brings,” she said to the pot, and did not finish the sentence. She moved her lips along the loaf shelf, three here and two there and one set a little apart with its brown edge turned up, and came back to the same place twice, keeping the days to Kindling as a shape and not a number. She did not say the days to the Five at all, and the closed fist went around the yard and arrived in this room as nothing anyone would say.

At the bank she did not touch the mark, because that was for the hour before an opening and no other. The coals went under the back of a broken pot and hissed once and held. Garl’s proverb went to the fire and not to the basket: feed the fire before you feed yourself; it’ll still be there in the morning, you won’t. The ridge of Ash ran to the corner and turned it without a break, near two thumbs wide, and would not be swept before the Last Day. Tarn set the small knife on the sill where it could be checked and told the yard he had done so, unasked, and went on sweeping. Jorra went to the wheel and did not say the back room was cold. The kiln took its breath in fours and missed one. Four, and four, and a gap.

The boy’s hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the pocket-place. It found wool, and woodsmoke in the wool, and the cold crust in the fold, and nothing that would ring clean under a knuckle and nothing with a name he could look at. The hand came down. The yard’s closed fist of a number went past him, and Sella’s little-door burn shut on nothing, and the house went on keeping what it could not count while he went on keeping the count.

— Day 13 —

Stone 19, Y.H. 812 (Ninthday)

The light on the oiled paper went the colour of skimmed milk and then past it, and the yard came in through the door that stood a finger’s width open. Corin lay in the basket under the fold in the Vail kiln-house with the crust cold at his side and the drool freezing at his mouth, and felt the cold arrive in the old order, cheek, then temple, then wrist. He was thirteen days old. No one had spoken to the basket all day.

Sella Vail crouched at the coals with the back of a broken cup and turned them over without lifting them, the way a woman turns a sleeping child and puts the blanket back. The little-door burn across her hand shut on nothing. Dusk was not the hour for the widow’s mark; the cord lay still against her throat while she worked, and the kiln breathed through the evening in fours and missed one. Four, and four, and a gap. “That’ll hold,” she said to the coals, and left the cup where it lay.

A man came up the yard from the Ilm with water on his legs. It had frozen pale where it met his boots. The trodden ash took his weight in four prints and four and a place where his foot had slid, and the boy counted them without meaning to, the way every count in this yard began. The man held something cupped in his hand and would not set it on the bench. A thing out of the river was not laid on a bench in a kiln-house where the fire had to be kept.

“There’s a hair in it,” the man said. He held out a palm’s breadth of ice, grey as milk gone off, with a dark line in it that ran from one edge and stopped a thumb’s breadth short of the other. “Hold it to the light. You’ll see it then.”

The woman from High Kiln came down the path with the sweetish lead edge of the glaze room on her coat and a smear of it at one corner of her mouth, and she looked at the shard as a woman looks at a pot she has been asked to price. “It’ll go early,” she said. “Ice with a hair in it goes before its time. I’ve seen it.”

“It’ll hold,” said a neighbour who had come to the door with a slate held flat against her apron. “It always holds. There’s the levy in the thaw, and a late thaw puts it off. A body wants its weeks.”

“A late thaw’s a longer week before the paying,” Tarn said to the ash at his broom’s edge. He had swept before light and the small knife was on the sill where it could be checked, and he had announced it to the yard unasked, as he did every day. “I’ll take the late. Yes, house; I’ve said it.” He did not look up. Six years of the seven were behind him and the seventh he held back, the one entry in this house the house could not check.

The man with the wet legs turned the shard in his fingers. “The water under it’s gone different. I could hear it from the bank.”

“Then it’s weather,” Sella said to the pot on the bench, and went on turning the coals. “The fire doesn’t know what the river does. Feed the fire.” Garl’s proverb went to the coals and not to the yard: leave the crack showing; people trust a thing they can see has been handled. His coat kept its salt shape on the peg and his seat kept the hollow of twenty years on the left, and the house went on holding its heat the way a cup holds water, with one crack in it nobody would name.

The boy kept the line in the ice as he kept the other objects, on the tray where he laid things in a row before anyone wrote them down. The dark hair ran and stopped short; it did not finish, and what did not finish had the shape of the space between one count and the next. He had known a pot that answered nearly full and a pot that answered flat, and a bench with four nails and a knot where the fifth should have been, and a landing on the Fishstairs where a step should have been. He did not set the ice among them as a cause. He set it among them as a shape, which is what a man does with a thing he cannot touch and has not been told the name of. A crack that stops short of the edge is a vessel that has not given yet. He had opened enough of them to know the difference between a vessel that gives and a vessel that has not, and here he could not even lift a hand to the cold.

❦

A neighbour came along the yard path from Slate Row, where she had been since morning, and stood in the open finger’s width of the door with her shawl held across her chest. She had been in the Court’s room and had come back with the room still on her, the way a woman comes home from the pigs with the smell of them in her skirt. She did not come in. Sella stood a half-pace short of her because the neighbour’s slate was open against her apron with the writing turned inward, and the half-pace held all the while the woman spoke.

“There’s a bench by the door,” the neighbour said. “The slate’s read from it. There’s a pot set on the bench, and a draught goes under it and along the floor to the corner. You feel it at your ankles before you feel the cold.” She stopped, not for breath but to find the shape of the thing she wanted to say next. “And there’s a place in the figures where a line should stand and doesn’t. It’s the same every time I’ve been.”

“A draught under a bench is a draught anywhere,” Sella said to the fire. “It’ll take a man in his sleep if he lets it. Feed the fire.”

The boy listened with the part of him that had once stood in rooms and noted where the air moved. A bench by the door. A pot on the bench. A draught under it going along the floor to the corner. A place in the count where a line should stand. He had lain in House Silt with the birth-slate propped on the bench within a woman’s reach and a cup across the room that gave its small flat note to an empty bed; the floor there had four nails on the near board, four on the next, and a knot the width of a thumb. He had lain under this roof with a pot on this bench and a gap in the kiln’s four, four. He had been carried two doors down to the death-house, where the bench stood in the same place relative to the door and the ridge was thicker by a finger’s width and the same cold went along the floor. He put what the neighbour said beside each of these and kept them in four separate places on the tray. A room was a room. The fact that four rooms had the same furniture in them was no more a thing to decide than the fact that four vessels answered in the same flat way; he had learned not to write a cause where he had only noticed a likeness. Somebody had turned a note down in a chart once and called it a failure of the system, and the boy had carried that particular mistake for the rest of a life. He was not about to make it in a basket with a draught at his ankles.

“They read it twice,” the neighbour said. “Once for the moot and once for them that come late. The figure stands either way.” She turned the slate down against her apron as if turning it down put it right, and went off along the path with her shawl held across her chest.

Jorra Vail came in from the Vail wheel-shed with bone-coloured slip dried to old bone up both forearms and the bent little finger of his left hand going in ahead of his hand at the damper. Sella had said the fire was low and had not finished; he moved the damper and left the end of the sentence where it was. “You’ll do,” he said to the pot, and did not say that the back room was cold. There was no slate dust on him.

That same draught went along the floor of this room, under the bench and away to the corner where the Ash turned it without a break. Sella put her foot against the basket and slid it a hand’s breadth out of the line of it, and put the fold right at his ear without looking at his face. She could not have said what she was doing and would not have called it love. It was the thing a woman did with a cot in a draught and a fire that had to hold, and it was entered nowhere, and Tarn swept around the place where the basket had been as though nothing had been moved at all.

❦

Sella left the fire and went to the skirting board with her thumb. She laid the edge of it in the grey and drew it along a hand’s breadth, then another, and the Ash came away on her nail the colour of a bruise nearly healed. “Near two thumbs,” she said to the board. “There’s a finger more two doors down.” She meant the house where a man had given under a cold iron tube and the child on the quilt had gone grey at the fingertips before the day was out, and she said neither of those things. The Vail house kept what it kept and said the house where another woman would have said I.

“Two thumbs, house,” Tarn said to the ground. “I can check it if you want.”

“Leave it,” she said. “It’ll be swept on the Last Day.” Then she said Garl’s proverb to the board in the voice he had used for pots: leave the crack showing; people trust a thing they can see has been handled. The ridge ran to the corner and turned it without a break, near two thumbs wide here and thicker by a finger’s width where it lay under the death-house door. Both had been left where they were. Neither would be swept before the Last Day; on the morning after that they would be burnt and called dealt with, and that would be the end of the account as far as the district was concerned.

The shelf of loaves had been gone over twice since the man came up from the river. Sella set three here and two there and one a little apart with its brown edge turned up, came back to the same place, and moved them again. Tarn’s eyes went to the stack of kindling and came off it. No one gave the number of days to Kindling. The household kept it as a shape in bread and glances and as an absence in any sentence that might have held it; the yard kept it as a fist closed around whatever figure the district had decided to give out that morning. The crack in the ice was weather. The debt against the firing of tiles was weather. The grey along the skirting board was dirt to be dealt with on the Last Day. In this district a thing was called whatever would let a man go home to a warm room beside it, and the room went on being warm, and nobody in the yard had thought to ask who was keeping it that way.

The boy kept the days exactly. He had been thirteen days under this roof and the days to the Five were the days to the Five, and neither figure could be written, spoken, spent or handed to a person who would take it. He was the only one in the yard keeping the number as a number, and he had no hand to set it down and no word in Grathic to say it with. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the shard of ice and the little light in it, and came down. He could not lift it to his own chin. The drool froze at his mouth and nobody wiped it, because a basket at the edge of a board is a pot set down and left, and the crust lay cold in the fold at his side and would not be eaten tonight.

The man with the water on his legs was still in the doorway. He had not been asked to stay and had not been told to go. He held the shard up into the circle of tallow light from the oiled paper, and the hairline came out in it like a thread laid under glass, running from the edge and stopping a thumb’s breadth short of the other. Nobody crossed himself. The woman from High Kiln had gone back up the terrace with her early thaw, and the neighbours had kept their late one and their weeks, and the yard had gone on around its closed fist of a number without once opening it where the boy could see.

The Vail Ash ridge ran to the corner and turned it without a break. Sella’s loaf shelf stood three here and two there and one set a little apart, kept badly and come back to twice. The boy lay under the fold with the cold crust at his side and his hand outside the wool going up an inch toward the light held in the ice and coming down. Four, and four, and a gap. The house kept its number the way a house keeps a fire it is afraid of losing, in objects and glances and never in a figure. He kept his exactly, and it bought him nothing; and the dark hair in the held-up ice ran to a stop a thumb’s breadth short of the edge and did not finish.

— Day 14 —

Stone 20, Y.H. 812 (Tenthday, Account-day)

Corin came awake with the dark still lying on the yard and the crust cold in the fold at his side, and knew the day by its cost before he knew it by its name. It was Account-day. The word had come into the Vail kiln-house the night before and gone around the bed, the loft and the loaf shelf without anybody saying what it meant, the way a number went around this district: as a shape, and a hand shut into a fist, and an absence where the figure should have been. He was fourteen days old. The cold came in at cheek, then temple, then wrist, in the order he knew and could not argue with. Nobody had spoken to the basket.

Sella Vail lifted him before the light had cleared the sill. She did not touch the widow’s mark; that was for the hour before an opening and no other, and this was a reading, not an opening. The burn shaped like a little door across the back of her left hand closed on nothing as she took the weight and opened on the handle, and she carried him like a pot filled that must not be slopped, down the yard and into the Cross, where two hundred people were already leaning toward a slate. All the way she kept her half-pace from every open slate, as a woman keeps her skirt from a fire she has no wish to put out; a figure lying on a stone was a thing that could reach a child across a yard, in this house’s experience, and she had stopped arguing with the distance years ago.

She set the basket at the north end of the square beside the moot-post, in the finger’s width of shade the post threw, the only shade at that end, and the cold iron came through the wool against his cheek and stayed there. The notice hung from its nail with one corner gone soft, and the wind turned the row of marks over and back as if trying to read them. He lay with the shade on his face and the drool freezing at his mouth and the crust stiff against his ribs, and heard the bell go over the square.

It was longer than a church bell. A church bell lets go and leaves a man to his thoughts; this one went on after the air had taken up the first stroke, and it went four, and four, and a gap where the fifth should have been. Nobody crossed himself at it. The pigs went on being driven over the wet boards, the crowd went on leaning, and the bell went on doing a temple’s work with a Court’s hand, which nobody in Sill had thought to explain to a child. He had counted it at the fish market and through a wall and in a room where a man was giving, and the gap was still the part nobody had thought to enter.

Berr Clay came to the block with the red cord of his office folded under his coat and the green of tasted glaze at one corner of his mouth, a man smelling of hot clay over the smell of nothing. His hands were large and clean. He wiped his thumb on his apron before he touched the slate, and wiped it again, so that the hand everyone would be watching should be seen to be clean. The crowd had been shouting itself hoarse over the pigs; when he touched the stone the shouting stopped, not because he had asked for quiet but because two hundred people had to lean in to hear him.

“One in twelve, as the notice says,” he said. It came low, into the top of the crowd rather than across it. “If your household has heard otherwise, your household has heard wrong.”

The boy had heard the same grammar in a room with better light and a chart on a table: a sentence that took the speaker out of it and left an account standing in his place. Here the account wore a kiln-master’s coat and tasted its own glaze and apologised in the form of an instruction. He had no word in Grathic for the shape of it and did not need one; he had learned to recognise the order of a thing before he had learned the name of any part of it.

“The levy is called for the feast,” Berr said. “It is not called by me.” He passed a finger over the figure to make sure it was still where he had put it. “The figure stands as read. There’ll be a slate at the watch if the house wants it again.”

He spoke of the kiln-house and the mark confirmed by the factor at Greybank and the fire that was kept under this roof, and around a consignment of tiles that nobody named. There was a place in his sentence shaped like the hollow of a wheel-seat where a debt might have stood, and Berr went around it the way a woman goes around the corner of a room where the Ash has been left lying, and set the printed figure down where two hundred people could see it instead. The uncalled debt lay where it had lain for a year, neither paid nor settled nor done with, and the house up the yard went on owing a man who would not say what it owed.

Berr did not look into the basket. The figure was read over the top of it, and the boy lay in the post’s thin shade with cold iron at his cheek and heard a number go into the square that he could not spend, could not correct, could not hand back to anybody. Fourteen days old, and the only person in that square who knew exactly what the reading had left out, which was everything.

❦

When the moot had finished with the figure she lifted the basket again and carried him the length of the square to the fringe where Slate Row began. A woman stood there with a slate held against her apron, writing turned inward, and a table stood between her and the row with the Court’s business on it in the open air, to be carried inside once the day’s figures had been made to agree. The Court’s table was bare but for a slate, a charcoal stub and a cup ring gone brown into the wood. Sella set the basket where no slate lay open and stood a half-pace off, and the woman waiting drew her hand back from her own stone as though, once his hand was on it, it had ceased to be hers.

The clerk had a cuff turned back twice at the wrist from washing and ink in the creases of two otherwise ordinary-coloured fingers. He laid his hand on the top slate before he looked at the woman. It was the same order he had used ten days ago and the same order the midwife had used at a birth and the man with the cold iron tube had used at a bed: the record first, because the record would still be there when the person had been carried out. He was a soft man with a soft voice and no edge in it at all.

“It’s entered as it stands,” he said. “There’s nothing in it the household need correct.”

The woman’s mouth opened and shut. Nothing in the household needed correcting, and the slate was entered as it stood, and the matter was finished before it had properly begun, which is how matters are finished in this district. She took her stone back turned down against her apron and went away toward the row with her fist shut around whatever figure she had come with.

The boy could not read a word of the upper line, and would not have known Grathic if it had been written an inch from his eye. He knew the hand. It was the strange neat dark hand he had seen on a slate propped on a bench in a birth room, and on another slate at this square on Stone 10, when the figure was read over the top of him, and before that in the orange light of the flare, where a hand in a grey sleeve wrote and stopped between two clusters of strokes. He knew the stop as he knew the hand: a small lifting and setting-down, a place where the writing had held its breath and let it go, the same gap as between one count and the next.

The flare light is orange on wet snow. A hand in a grey sleeve writes and stops between two clusters, and the snow takes the heat of the flare in a small hiss. The hand goes on. The stop is the part that stays.

Four times. He had seen it four times now, and had no word for it, no standing to ask what it was, and no hand to point at the place where it sat. The first three had lain side by side on the tray and had not joined, and this one lay beside them as a fourth object of the same shape, unread and unjoined and no more use than the other three. A fourth sighting changed the count and changed nothing else. It gave him no power to decide and no word to speak and no way into the slate that lay under the clerk’s soft cuff. He had more of that account in his head than any person in the square and could not enter one stroke of it.

In its own place on the tray, kept carefully apart from these four, lay the hand of the man who had drawn the year’s first measure two doors down: neat too, ordinary, with no stop in it at all. The boy had noted the absence of the pause on the hour it happened and had filed it as a different object, the way he kept two similar bones separate on a tray, because a wrong answer written down in a neat hand cost more than the trouble of keeping them apart. He kept the hand without a pause where it was. He had five hearings of the full note from four sources and had added nothing to them today; a pause was not a hearing, and the count stayed where it had stood through fourteen days.

The crowd went back to the pigs. Figures stood side by side on the heap of turnips without being made to agree: one in twelve, a levy for the feast, a measure entered as it stood, and the days to the Five shut into every fist that went past him. He heard them go by and could not spend any of them. On the road out a barrow of grey went through the north gate toward the Bone Orchard, heaped a little above the sides where the load had shifted, and nobody in the square looked at it twice; it was the afternoon’s work, the same work that went out every market-day.

His hand outside the wool went to the pocket-place. It found wool, and woodsmoke in the wool, and the river wind coming over the square, and the cold crust in the fold, and no small cold rectangle with a name on it nine years old and unlooked-at. Nine promises of a boat went out with the reaching and came back with nothing, and the hand came down. The clerk’s soft sentence went into the row behind him, and the figure stood as read, and the account went on outliving the debtor up the yard.

❦

At dusk she lifted the basket off the post and carried him up the yard with the woodsmoke of the square still in the wool and the river wind caught under the fold. The doors of Low Kiln were going shut one after another, and the ash of the day went along the trodden ground in front of her and came back to the same board. He had been fourteen days under this roof and knew the walk by the places where her hip shifted the weight: the joint by the well, the step over the loose board, the turn in at the door that stood a finger’s width open.

Under the roof she put the fold right without looking at his face, the way a woman straightens a cup on a shelf without inspecting what is in it, and set the basket on his bed and not in the Debt Loft above the shed. At the bank her hand did not go to the mark, because that was for the hour before an opening and no other, and the rule held as exactly at the end of an account-day as at the hour before a kiln was lit. She banked the coals with the back of a broken pot; they hissed once under the ash and held. “That’ll hold,” she said to the fire.

The crust lay cold in the fold at his side, stiff at the edges, smelling of smoke and barley, and she neither took it out nor called it a kindness, which in this house were two ways of doing the same thing. He could feel its cold through the wool as exactly as he had felt its warmth on the night it was put there, and he could not lift it to his mouth, and had never once asked who had put it there or what it had cost.

Tarn came up from the loft with the broom under his arm and set the small bench knife on the sill where it could be checked, and told the yard he had done so, unasked, though the yard was empty. “I took the small knife. I put it back. You can check.” Six years of the seven were behind him and he had counted every one; the seventh he held back, the one entry in this house the house could not check. His broom went past the bench once and stopped half a stroke at the place where a pot had been set down and had given nothing, and went on, and he did not look at the basket.

Jorra Vail came in from the shed with bone-coloured slip dried to old bone up both forearms and the bent little finger of his left hand going in ahead of the rest of him at the damper. There was no slate dust on him. “You’ll do,” he said to the pot, and shifted the damper before Sella had finished telling him the fire was low, and left the end of her sentence where it was. He did not say the back room was cold and did not look toward the bed.

Garl Vail’s coat kept its salt-stiff shape on its peg behind the door and his seat in the shed kept the hollow of twenty years on the left, and his proverb went to the fire and not to the basket: leave the crack showing; people trust a thing they can see has been handled. The ridge of Ash ran to the corner and turned it without a break, near two thumbs wide, and would not be swept before the Last Day. The debt against the firing of tiles lay uncalled somewhere in the square and had lain there a year, spoken around all day and named by nobody, and the boy put it on the tray beside the pause he had seen four times and joined neither to the other.

The Five were eleven days off. The household kept that number badly, in loaves and glances and an arrangement of three here and two there and one set a little apart with its brown edge turned up, come back to twice and never once said; and the boy kept it exactly, as he kept the days to Kindling and the count of the floorboards and the bell and the kiln and the landing that was not a step, and none of the numbers could be written, spoken, spent or handed to a person who would take it. He had been fourteen days in a country with no word for the thing he was doing, and was the only adult in the room and the least use in it, and the correctness of the whole account was the whole humiliation.

The kiln took its breath in fours and missed one. Four, and four, and a gap. The fourth pause lay on the tray with the other three and had not joined them, and the Drawing collector’s ordinary hand stayed in its own place with no pause in it at all, and there was no sixth hearing and no word in Grathic and no hand that could reach two paces past the fold. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth and came down. The crust stayed cold where it had been put, and the house went on keeping what it could not count, and Low Kiln would not say what it counted.

The Chronicle of Chapter 6

On Stone 16 the coals held under ash and Sella Vail touched the widow’s mark before she lifted the kiln lid, at no other hour. Tarn measured the kindling: four, four, a space where the fifth should be; six years counted, the seventh held back. Alda Reed came to see him, reached for her slate before she reached for him, touched the tin inside her bodice, crossed two fingers at the red edge of his wrist without knowing it, and covered the sign with an unasked-for date. She wrote a note, turned it face-down into her shawl, and went on giving dates after the question had stopped. He could not read it or hand it back.

On Stone 17 a pot on the bench gave nothing. The boy turned before the set-down; Tarn’s broom stopped half a stroke. Neither named the interval. At you’ve done well he went still for two breaths, then agreed flatly. He held the pale ring at his wrist to the light, then put the knife back where it could be checked. Sella named the kiln’s breath as the danger; Tarn told of a man in Mud End who died of it and was called merciful. At the well on Stone 18 a neighbour reached for her slate before her child, and for two sentences a ceiling light at four, a chart with its note turned down, a pocket with wool where a phone had lain broke through.

On Stone 19 a man came up from the Ilm with a shard of ice in which a dark hair ran and stopped short of the edge. A woman from High Kiln said the thaw would come early; everyone who wanted it late contradicted her: a late thaw puts off what is payable. A neighbour described the Court’s room: a bench, a pot, a draught, a gap. The boy set it beside the birth room and death-house and made nothing of it. Sella measured the ridge: near two thumbs here, a finger thicker two doors down. Neither would be swept before the Last Day.

On Stone 20, Account-day, Sella set him in the moot-post’s shade, half a pace from open slates. The bell went four, four and a gap; nobody crossed himself. Berr Clay wiped his thumb and read one in twelve to two hundred leaning people. The levy was called for the feast, not by him. He went around Garl’s uncalled debt and did not look into the basket. A soft-cuffed clerk laid his hand on the slate before he looked at the woman and said it stood as entered. The upper line was in the neat dark hand; its small stop came a fourth time and lay beside the other three without joining. The crowd went back to the pigs. At dusk the coals were banked, the fold put right, the crust left cold. The Five were eleven days off; the household kept the number badly and the boy kept it exactly.
