The Brook Takes Her

Days 38–44 · Ilmen 9–15, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln and the Weir, Sill

The brook takes what no slate will enter. The knot keeps what no clerk can spell. The eels run whether anybody blesses them or not.

— A saying of the Weir, Sill

— Day 38 —

Ilmen 9, Y.H. 813 (Ninthday)

The clay had come up in the night and taken the woman who stood at the end of nineteen steps. It had come behind the drying racks, where the Slip Brook ran higher than a brook had any business running in Ilmen, under a film that no longer held and with the colour of slip mixed too thin going over more water in a small continuance that had sound in it. By the time there was light enough to see, the bank had changed shape. Grey silt lay in a rim along the grass the width of a thumb; a willow stake that had stood upright through the deep cold leaned out into the current; the water had taken on the clay-heavy look of the pits and carried it down toward the Ilm. Sella Vail held the basket on the higher ground with the fold tucked at the boy’s chin, and she had turned it so that his face went toward the bank and not away from it, as she would have turned a pot so that the light fell where she wanted it. She had not set him down. She stood with her feet apart on the wet clay and both hands under him, and the little-door burn on the back of her hand closed on the wicker and shut.

Corin was thirty-eight days old. He could not move. He could not speak. He could not look away.

The women at the racks had left their work and come down. Two of them were in the water to their knees and a third held a rope, and a man from the pits came down the nineteen steps at a run and went in without taking his boots off, which is a thing a man does only when there is no time to take his boots off. Nobody called out. That was the first thing the boy registered, that the yard had gone quiet in a way that was not the quiet of early morning but the quiet of people who had come to a thing they had no words for and had decided, without a word, to do it with their hands. Woodsmoke went up somewhere behind him. A pot stood on a rack out of its own shadow. The ordinary work of the yard went on at its edges, because the ordinary work of a yard does not stop for one woman, and the boy lay in the basket and took in the whole of Low Kiln with the part of him that had once stood at the edge of a different kind of room and decided, in ninety seconds, which bodies were worth opening. His head went where it wanted and made its small wet sound and came back; the cold came in its known order, cheek and temple and wrist; the thread at his mouth was not wiped, because a basket held in two hands is a pot held in two hands and cannot be set down to wipe a chin.

He knew what he was looking at before anyone lifted her. The face had gone the colour of old rope, and the clay had got into the creases beside the mouth and would not come out; there was a wedge of wet wool at the shoulder that had been a petticoat hem; the hands were small and quick and never clean, and one of them was still half shut as though it had closed on something and had not finished closing. Severe cold puts a look on a face that passes for composure, and water puts another look on it that passes for sleep, and he had learned long ago in a room with better light not to trust either. There would be no bringing this one back. He knew the order of it the way he knew the back of his own hand: the cold, the struggling, the gasping that stops, the quiet after, and then the long work of a body handed over to the water the way a vessel hands its work to the next thing and the next until the skin holds more than it was made to hold. He knew it exactly. It was the most exact knowledge he had, and it was the least use.

Then the sound came.

It was a small flat knock, no bigger than a knuckle rapped on a board. It came out of the body as the men lifted, the sound a pot makes when it goes all the way through and the two halves of the crack speak to each other at last — the note with no ring in it, the note that stops where a full note would carry on. It was not loud. A woman at the tubs said nothing; the man with the rope said nothing; the boy heard it because he heard that sound the way other men hear their own names.

It was the sixth.

He did not explain it. He had never once been able to explain the sound to anybody, and there was no word in Grathic for it and no ear in the yard that would have known what he meant if there had been. He had held five hearings of the full note from four sources across thirty-eight days: a cup set down across the birth room, a pot on a bench in the Vail wheel-shed through the wall, the district’s own slate twice at the Cross, and a man giving under a cold iron tube at the Drawing — five from four sources, one of them the first that had ever come out of a person. This was the second from a person. It went beside the five and not among them. It was not a seventh; there had never been a seventh. It was not the nearly-full answer a pot had given on Ilmen 6, which lay on its own tray as an answer to an interval carried since Stone 13, advanced and not paid. This was flatter than that and deader, and it lay beside the five from four sources without joining them, as a thing lies beside a row of things it resembles and is not. The sound had come out of a woman who had stood at the end of nineteen steps and counted her loaves into her thumb, and he had loved her without knowing her, and he could not lift a hand toward her.

The lifting was clumsy and brief and then went on being clumsy for a long time. Hands went under a body heavy with water and could not find a purchase; a forearm that should have gone where the arm told it to went somewhere else; the man from the pits swore and shifted his grip and the body slid back an inch into the silt. Somebody said hold her head and nobody held her head; the water came up out of her mouth in a thin dark thread and went back into the brook that had put it there. They dragged her up the bank by degrees, a section at a time, with the clay sucking at the hem and the petticoat dragging, and set her down badly on the grass, and then came the long undignified interval of people not knowing what to do next. A woman knelt and wiped the clay off the face with her sleeve and the face would not wipe clean; the clay had got into the creases beside the mouth and stayed there. Someone said warm, and someone else said get a stone, and nobody got a stone. The man from the pits put two fingers to the side of the neck where a man feels for a pulse and kept them there longer than a pulse would have needed, and then took them away. He did not say what he had found. There was nothing to find, and the boy knew it and watched the fingers do exactly what fingers do when they are looking for something that is not there.

That was the whole of it: not the knock, which was over in a breath, but the long dull interval afterward, with the drool at his own mouth going cold and nobody wiping it, and his hand outside the wool going up an inch and coming down.

The shape of her thumb on the wicker came back before the lifting was done.

It had been there the day before, at the end of the nineteen steps below House Silt: her hand off the jamb and onto the handle of the basket as Sella lifted it past her shade, the thumb along the edge, the small quick grip of a woman who had carried clay up nineteen steps and children down them, staying the space of one breath and letting go. Neither woman had looked at the other. He had kept the shape where he kept the things that had no hour yet, and he had not known, lying there with the drool freezing at his mouth, that it was the detail he would be given to keep and never to spend.

A hand in a grey sleeve went into a coat pocket for a small cold rectangle of light with a name on it. A sentence about a boat on cold water, nine promises old, waited in the pocket and was not said. The name under the light was not looked at. The hand came back with wool.

Nothing crossed into the basket that a baby’s hand could carry.

She did not say the number. She did not say the name. The number lay in her thumb and the name under the pale sieve-wire scar up the inside of her left forearm, and neither came up, and the door-shut smile had gone and no other smile had come in its place. To the last she believed him spent among children she had never seen, given up in the levy three winters ago for a whole return she could not have read the writing of. It had been a mercy when it was told. It was a fact now, and there was nobody left who could correct it. He had once known an institution that preferred its own version of a thing to the thing itself because the version fit on a chart and the truth did not; he had let it, and carried the letting for the rest of a life. Here the version had gone into the water with her and come out on the bank as a fact, and there was no chart and no hand to write the correction and no living person who knew there was anything to correct. She had kept him among the bits she counted, the quietest of the accounts that had entered him, because nobody — least of all her — had thought it was an account at all.

Alda Reed came after the vessel had given. She came down from the road with her shawl tight and her hands already smoothing her apron in two passes, and the soap and the warm milk that were usually on her had gone cold under the silt of the river, so that she smelt of lye and wet wool and the mineral edge of the water instead. She went to the slate before she went to the person. It was a narrow slate and she laid it writing-down on a flat stone at the edge of the bank, and only then did she turn to the body, and then to the basket. Her hand went once inside her bodice and touched the little tin and came away without opening it. She did not say the woman’s name.

She gave dates where a wonder should have stood. Stone seven, the account up on the eighth, before the paper went out; a cooper’s seventh; a potter’s third child. The dates arrived faster than any question needed them and went on arriving after there was no question left to answer. She had crossed two fingers at the red edge of the birth-flower at his wrist three doors down and covered the sign with a date nobody had asked for, and she had done it again in a market full of people and in a dark room telling the Return, and never once known she had done it. Here there was no sign to cross and no wonder to cover, and she laid the dates over the body as she would have laid them over a cradle, because dates were a handle on a thing too large to carry and a hearth-maid’s work was to carry it.

The boy lay in the basket and watched the order of her reaching and put it on the tray where he kept the hands that went to the record before the hand went to the person. A midwife, a neighbour at a well, a clerk behind a Court table, a man who had priced him, Berr passing a finger over a figure before he touched nothing — all of them had that order, and now Alda had it too. He did not call it the same hand as Berr’s thumb wiped twice on an apron, and he did not put it with the Drawing man’s hand, the collector with the grey fingertips whose hand had had no pause in it at all. Those stayed in their own places. Alda’s going to the slate first was a fact of the same shape as theirs and not the same fact, and he had learned what three likenesses laid in a row would do in a better-lit room, and the room here was a bank in the grey before the smoke had cleared and had no light to speak of, which was no reason to be careless with it. He kept the order and wrote no cause beside it. She went on giving dates after the question had stopped, and the tin stayed shut in her bodice, and the woman on the grass kept her clay in the creases beside her mouth.

Word came up with the neighbours later, in the manner word comes up in a district that has no need to write it down: the Court had set a collector’s clerk at the Fishstairs with a slate and a cuff gone soft from washing, and he had been set there for a measure nobody had called for. A woman said it to the pot she was scouring; a man said it to his boots; it went from mouth to mouth the way the number of the ice’s crack had gone from a fist to a shoulder in the week before, and nobody claimed it and nobody gave it out. He kept the shape of it on its own tray. There was a clerk at the Fishstairs with a slate, and a woman was dead in the brook behind the racks, and the district had entered neither in anything it would show him.

Sella carried him down in the middle of the morning, past the racks and along the road toward the north bridge, with the basket at her hip and the fold tucked at his chin. She kept her half-pace from every open slate and set him at the edge of the stalls in a finger’s width of shade where no slate lay, the same care she had used at the Drawing and at the market’s edge and at a hundred places besides. From there he could see the clerk. The man had a soft cuff and a slate and a row of figures on it in a hand the boy could not read, and when a woman came to stand before him the man’s hand went to the top of the slate before his eyes went to the stalls. That was the order of it, and the boy put it on its own tray — not the woman in the grey coat at the flare, whose small pause he had seen five times and could not read, not the grey-fingered man who had drawn from the dying neighbour under the Vail roof, not joined to either of them. The clerk’s hand had no pause in it that he could see, or a pause of a different shape, and he did not count it and did not make a sixth sighting of the neat dark hand. He filed the order where he filed such orders, apart from the others, and went on looking at the stalls. Nobody came to the basket with a slate. Nobody asked the house for a figure, and nobody carried him anywhere to have one written down against him, and there was nothing in the morning that anyone could have used to enter him.

Mira Silt came up from the eels while the basket was in the shade, with pale slime dried on her sleeves and river silt in the hollow of her neck and her head held a little to the right against the cold where the top of her left ear had been. She kept the small smile on her face the way another woman keeps a door on the latch. A neighbour called after her about the water and the colour of it, and Mira answered the silence with her own answer.

“Does the river know who it takes? It takes who it takes. Ask the river.” She flicked an eel-bone against her thumb and put it away.

She did not look at the water. She did not look into the basket. She brought no crust and put nothing into the fold, and no third came into it, and she went back down toward the eels with her head held to the right. The boy set this not-looking beside the others she had given him — the not-looking at her sister at the end of nineteen steps, the not-looking at the basket in a dozen yards — and kept them in their separate places, because a not-looking was a shape and shapes were where a mistake began if a man had a hand free enough to follow them. He had no such hand. His lay outside the wool and went up an inch and came down.

At dusk Sella carried him up the Clay Yard under the Vail kiln-house roof and set him on his bed, not in the Debt Loft. Garl Vail’s coat kept its salt shape on its peg behind the door and the hollow wheel-seat its leftward lean of twenty years, and the proverb went to the pot and the fire and not to the basket: leave the crack showing; people trust a thing they can see has been handled. The clean dark line where the Ash had turned the corner for a year and two days held nothing and was not swept. Tarn set the small bench knife on its sill and told the empty yard where it was, six years of the seven counted and the seventh held back, Ash worked into the creases of his knuckles that no scrubbing lifted. Jorra Vail came in from the wheel-shed with bone-coloured slip dried to old bone up both forearms and went to the damper before Sella had finished telling him the fire was low, the bent finger going in ahead of his hand. Neither looked toward the bed. They had not been told about the woman in the brook and had not asked, and the house went on around the thing it had not entered.

She turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and did not touch the widow’s mark, because a bank was not an opening. The carried fire that had come up the yard on Kindling was still under blind ash and the broken pot, warming the boards by degrees and missing one breath in every four. Four, four and a gap. The two crusts lay in the fold where they had been put — one gone past smelling of anything since Stone 15, one giving a thin breath of barley smoke when the wind turned — neither eaten, lifted or named, and no third. He wanted the second one and could not reach it, and the wanting of it was louder than the knowing.

He lay on his bed under the roof, thirty-eight days old, with the fold at his ear. The shape of a thumb on wicker lay where he kept the things that had no hour yet, which was the only place he had to keep it. The sixth hearing lay beside the five from four sources without joining them, and the count went on, four, four and a gap, into a year the district had entered and had not entered him. A woman was gone into the brook behind the racks and did not come back, and the yard went on with its work as yards do. He was the only adult in the room and the least use in it, and his hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth behind him and came down.

— Day 39 —

Ilmen 10, Y.H. 813 (Tenthday)

The small knife was on its sill when the basket was lifted. Tarn had set it there before light and told the empty yard where it lay, six years of the seven counted and the seventh held back where he always held it, and now he stood aside from the door with Ash worked into the creases of his knuckles and the pale clip-ring at his left wrist catching what light there was. He did not look into the basket. He said nothing at all, which in that house was a way of saying the whole of it: the debt kept its place, the foundling kept his, and the two were not to be put in one sentence. Sella Vail came through behind him with the basket at her hip, the little-door burn on the back of her hand shut on the wicker, and the wool folded at the boy’s chin before she had cleared the threshold. She did not touch the widow’s mark. The kiln had not been opened and the mark had its hour and no other.

Corin was thirty-nine days old. He had been thirty-eight the day before, and the district had entered neither number, which was a fact he kept without resentment and without much hope of its changing. The fold was at his chin; the two crusts lay where they had been put, one gone past smelling of anything since Stone 15, the other still giving a thin breath of barley smoke when the wind turned off the yard, neither eaten, lifted or named, and no third. His head went where it wanted and made its small wet sound and came back. The thread at his mouth froze a little in the morning and nobody wiped it, because a basket held in two hands is a pot held in two hands and cannot be set down for a chin. Cold arrived in its known order, cheek and temple and wrist. He wanted the smoky crust. He could not reach it. The wanting was louder than the knowing, as it had been every morning of his new life.

She carried him down the Clay Yard like a pot filled that must not be slopped, turning him with her knee so that his face went toward the yard and not toward the writing a neighbour had tucked under her arm at the gate. She kept a half-pace from every open slate without appearing to keep it, going around the writing the way a woman goes around a pail set in a doorway. A pot stood on a bench along the yard with its mouth to the sky, and Sella said to the pot, “Feed the fire before you feed yourself. It’ll still be there in the morning. You won’t.” The pot gave no answer; a proverb said to a pot never expects one. It was not said to the basket, and the basket was not the pot, and the boy had learned by now to keep those two facts in different places.

A woman followed them as far as the yard’s end and said, “That’s a good basket. Whose is it?”

“The house’s,” Sella said. Then, to the wool, “Keep it off his ears. It’s the river wind.” She did not say I. She did not say my. The instruction went to the wool and the wind and not to the thing under it, and she went on walking with her burn shut on the wicker.

They came down to the Cross in the middle of the morning, when the slate was being read and the pigs were going over the weighing board. The square had four ways out of it: the road down to the south bridge and Slate Row, the steep street up to High Kiln, the north bridge, and the road that went away toward the Bone Orchard. The boy counted them from the low angle of the basket because they were there and because nothing else in the square could be counted without somebody wanting a figure for it. The bell went through the morning longer than any church bell, four, four and a gap, and nobody crossed himself at it.

Sella set him in the moot-post’s finger’s width of shade, the only shade at that end of the square, choosing the place with her knee as she would choose a place for a pot, setting it level and out of the draught and not looking into it afterward. Cold iron came into his cheek through the wool. Her hand did not go to the mark. A reading was not an opening and she had carried that rule through a year and into this one without once explaining it to anybody, least of all to the child at her hip.

Account-day had come round again and the market went on as markets do, as though a woman had not gone into the brook behind the racks the day before and lain uncollected on the bank. Eight pigs went over the board with their legs tied and their small hard eyes shut; eels came up from the water in shallow trays that left a wet mark on the boards; pots stood among turnips in heaps that nobody tried to make agree. The levy was called for the thaw. The question of what the thaw would cost went around the square and would not stand still long enough to be given.

A man come back from looking at the ice held his fist shut with the thumb tucked under, which is the way a man holds a thing he has decided not to give. A woman at the turnip heap said, “Well?”

“It’s come a hair further,” the man said, and put the fist into his coat pocket.

“A hair,” the woman said.

The man said nothing. A third man going past with a yoke on his shoulders lifted one shoulder at her, and that was the whole of the figure the square was going to get. It went from a fist to a mouth to a shoulder and came to nothing. Somebody said the river only did what rivers did in Ilmen, and that let everybody go home without owning the day. The boy lay with cold iron at his cheek and kept the shape of the number exactly, which was more than the yard did with it.

Berr Clay came to the block with the red wool cord of his office folded under his coat and the green of tasted glaze at one corner of his mouth, his hands large and clean. He wiped his thumb twice on his apron before he touched his slate, twice as he had wiped it on Stone 30 and on a dozen days besides, and the crowd leaned in, because a low voice makes two hundred people quieter than a shout would.

“One in twelve, as the notice says,” he said. Then, because a household might have heard otherwise, “If your household has heard otherwise, your household has heard wrong.” The levy was called for the thaw; it was not called by him. He passed a finger over the figure to make sure it was still where he had put it. The figure stood as read. There would be a slate at the watch if a house wanted it again.

A man near the pigs, who had been told something by somebody and wanted it made public, asked what the thaw would stand at.

“A number goes into a slate, not into a well,” Berr said. “If your house wants it, there’ll be a slate at the watch.”

Your house, not my account. The speaker stepped out of his own sentence as neatly as a surgeon takes his hand out of a field before the dressing goes on, and the boy knew the movement better than he knew the face that made it. Berr put the slate down and did not look into the basket, which lay at a half-pace from his boots and under the figure. One in twelve stood printed and unchallenged. The true subtraction went over the top of the wicker and stayed unspoken, and the boy could no more hand it back or correct it than he could lift the crust in the fold to his mouth.

Somebody at the pigs said there was a firing of tiles up the terrace that had never been paid for, and somebody else said that was a year ago and more, and the man with the yoke said the pigs had come in heavy, and so it went: the debt went around the square the way Sella went around ash in a corner, neither paid nor settled nor done with, and nobody said the name that belonged to it. The proverb lived in the house’s mouth and went to the pot and the fire, never to the basket.

At the Slate Row end of the square stood a table of the Court in the open air. The clerk had a cuff gone soft from washing and ink in the creases of two fingers, and when a woman came to stand before him his hand went to the top slate before his eyes went to her. He said, “It’s entered as it stands. There’s nothing in it the household need correct,” in a soft sentence with no edge on it, and the woman went off down the row with her fist shut and nothing in it she could spend. The boy watched the order of the reaching and put it on the tray where he kept the hands that went to the record before the person: Alda Reed reaching for her slate before the fold, a neighbour at the well reaching for hers before her child, Berr’s twice-wiped thumb, this clerk with his soft cuff. He did not make one fact of the four. They stayed four facts of the same shape in four places, and he wrote no cause beside any of them. He did not see the upper line of the slate and did not count a pause in it; the clerk’s order went on its own tray, and the clerk was a clerk and nothing more.

While the yard kept its number badly, the boy kept his exactly. Four ways out of the square. The bell’s four, four and a gap with nobody crossing himself. The six hearings of the full note stood where he had put them: a cup set down across a birth room, a pot on a bench in a wheel-shed through a wall, the district’s own slate twice at the Cross, a man giving under a cold iron tube at the Drawing, and yesterday’s small flat knock out of a woman heavy with water. Six, and not seven. The sixth lay beside the five from four sources and never among them, and there had been no new hearing in the market to move it. The nearly full answer of Ilmen 6 lay on its own tray, advanced and not paid; the intervals lay apart; the told bell lay beside the heard bell and never on it. He had once stood in rooms where a thing like yesterday’s was given a number and a name and a place on a chart, and had signed his own name to a version of it that fit the chart better than the body did. Here he lay in a market shade with cold iron at his cheek and was not asked to sign anything at all, because nobody had thought to enter him.

A trauma surgeon who could not reach a cup had become a surgeon who could not reach a crust; the trade had gone down in the world and the patient had not improved. He kept the joke without laughing, which was a way of laughing that cost less and kept the same blade in it. The pigs went over the board; the number went from the fist to the mouth to the shoulder; the bell struck and missed its fifth; the crowd leaned in to hear a figure it already knew and would not repeat. He was the only adult in the square and the least use in it, and the crust lay in the fold at his side with a thin breath of barley smoke going out of it whenever the wind turned.

In the afternoon the figure was read a second time for the households that had come late, and the same sentence went over the basket a second time, and one in twelve was not challenged a second time either. Sella stood a half-pace off with her burn shut and said nothing to anybody and nothing to him. When the light went long she lifted the basket off the post, put the fold right at his ear without looking at his face, and carried him up the yard under the Vail kiln-house roof, saying nothing about where they had been and nothing about what had been read over the top of him. At the door the small knife was back on its sill and the yard was swept around the places it did not enter. The clean dark line where the Ash had turned its corner for a year and two days held nothing and was not swept.

She set him on his bed, not in the Debt Loft. The two crusts lay in the fold where they had been put, neither eaten, lifted or named, and no third. The fire that had come up the yard on Kindling was under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees and missing one breath in every four. Four, four and a gap. He lay with the fold put right at his ear and the sixth hearing lying beside the five from four sources without joining them, and the count went on, four, four and a gap, into a year the district had entered and had not entered him. His hand outside the wool, after a moment’s consideration that was not a decision, went up an inch toward the warmth behind him and came down.

— Day 40 —

Ilmen 11, Y.H. 813 (Firstday)

No collector came for the woman in the brook. It was a fact made out of nothing: no knock at the yard gate before light, no soft cuff coming up the Clay Yard with a slate under the arm, nobody sent down the road to ask for the man who took measures and said them into a book. Sella Vail went to the fire instead. She lifted the lid without putting a hand to the cord at her throat, because there had been no opening, and she fed the coals and said to the kiln what the house always said to the kiln. The kiln took its breath in fours and missed one. Corin, forty days old, lay on his bed under the Vail kiln-house roof and heard the gap as he had heard it on every morning of his life, and heard, around it and under it and after it, the thing that had not happened.

A district that collects its dead has a shape for it, and the boy knew the shape. A man comes with a slate and a soft sentence; there is a pale round place left on an arm where the iron has been; the account stands as entered and the household has nothing in its fist that it can spend. He had heard that sentence said over a neighbour in a room with a bench and a pot and a draught under the door, and he had watched the number go around the well in fists and mouths afterward. Two days ago a woman had come up out of the water behind the racks with the clay set in the creases beside her mouth, and the shape had not appeared. Nobody went to fetch it. Nobody said that it had not appeared. The yard took up its buckets where it had laid them down and went on with its slip, and the Court entered neither the woman nor the omission in anything it showed him.

Warmth could be left where it fell and the morning came anyway. That was what the day laid out, though nobody was teaching it and he did not know he was being taught. The fire held; bread was cut; the kiln took its breath in fours; no one had gone down the road with a slate. A man under this roof had been left where he fell two winters ago and the house had gone on with its fire and its roll and its hollow seat, and a woman had been left in the water two days ago and the yard went on with its buckets. He kept the two left things in two places. They had the same shape, and a same shape was where a mistake began if a man had a hand free enough to follow it, and he had made that mistake once in a better-lit room and carried it here. He wrote no cause beside either. He did not call the leaving a decision, because nobody had decided anything in front of him. He did not call it mercy, because a district that calls things mercy has a use for the word. He did not call it a refusal, because there was no word in Grathic that would hold it still long enough to look at. He called it nothing and kept it on its own tray.

He had learned early that a thing which does not happen leaves a space with the outline of a sound that did not come. He had kept such spaces before: the half-heard note that had gone past a door in the old year, the silence where this house’s dead man’s name should have stood, the fifth stroke the bell never struck. This one went on a tray of its own, beside the intervals and not among the hearings. It was not the sixth hearing of the full note, which had lain where it was put two days ago beside the five from four sources, not joined to them and not a seventh. It was not nearly full, not a note, not an answer to any interval he had carried. It was an absence with the outline of a collection, and the outline was all he had. Ilmen 15, Y.H. 813 (Fifthday)

The boards of the plank walk had been swept before light, and the salt they swept went over the edge into the Ilm without anybody watching it go. It was the fifteenth of Ilmen, the Fish Blessing, and the Weir had put on its one clean day the way a worked house puts on a shirt it will take off again by noon: the eel traps lifted and set in the gaps with their mouths upriver, the eel racks bare and salted, and the blessing stone on the north bank flat and brushed free of grit so that the cut mark showed. Sella Vail carried the boy up from the river road in the first thin light and set him where a pot would be set, out of the draught that came along the boards, and turned him with her knee until the fold lay right under his chin. Keep it off his ears. It’s the river wind. Corin, forty-four days old, could not keep it off anything. The wool did what wool could do, which was less than the wind wanted, and the cold went at his cheek and then his temple in the order it always took. He had learned that order without being told it and could not alter one step of it.

The hearth-maids came out of the Eel House with their sleeves damp and salt in the creases of their hands, and stood on the stone with the crossed mark cut into the flat face of it under their feet. The boy could not see the mark from where he lay. If he had seen it he would have put it on the tray of shapes and not on the tray of meanings, because a shape was what he was given and a meaning was what other people made of shapes when they had a slate in reach. One of the maids sprinkled salt along the weir head with a flat hand, and another said the old words in a low half-song, and the women at the racks stopped their work and stood with their hands held loose in front of them, the way people stand when something is being done to a thing they cannot thank. A man behind him said the eels would run whether anybody blessed them or not, and somebody laughed, and the laugh went off down the boards and nobody joined it.

An old weir woman with her apron wet to the knee said the saying of the place as she worked: the brook takes what no slate will enter; the knot keeps what no clerk can spell; the eels run whether anybody blesses them or not. She said it to the boards, not to the boy, and went on with her salt. He set the three clauses on three trays, because they were three objects and not one sentence, and because the districts he had lived in put figures in fists and mouths and weather and had never once handed him a figure whole. Six mornings back the Clay Yard had lifted a woman out of a clay-heavy brook and had entered her nowhere. Here a flat stone was being thanked for fish that had not asked to be thanked. He did not join the two things. He had learned what joining two things in a better-lit room could cost, and here there was nobody to be wrong in front of and no hand to be wrong with.

Orra Weir came down the boards when the blessing was done, and the boy knew she had come before he saw her, the way everybody at the Cross knew it: silt and cold fish and tallow smoke moving together along the plank walk, and under it the wet wool smell of a woman who had stood too long on a weir. The grey streak showed first through her black hair; the pale arcs of eel scars lay across both forearms and she had covered neither; her hands were like old rope that had been wet and dried and wet again for thirty years. She took the scars for granted the way another woman takes a wedding ring, and she had no slate in her hand and would not have sat down at a table with one lying open on it. When a figure came near the racks she stood up instead of answering it, and when she spoke of names she said them into the cord at her waist and not into the air.

She crouched by the basket with her rope-hands on her knees and looked at the boy the way a trap-keeper looks at a trap that has come back up full. “You’re the Vail boy,” she said, slow, and the nasal half-song came into it where she was tired. “You’ve got her eyes and somebody else’s listening. How old is it? No — don’t tell me. It’ll be the fifteenth of Ilmen, in eight hundred and thirteen. It’s always a date with you people.”

Sella set a pot down for her and went back to the eels, and the boy was handed over without a word, the way a thing is handed from one pair of hands to another when both pairs know what the thing will bear. Orra did not ask Sella anything. She told the pot instead. “An eel goes in,” she said. “It can’t see the way out. That’s all a trap is. Don’t make it more than that.” Her thumb went to the cord at her waist while she said it, and came away. He knew the shape she had described better than he knew any word in Grathic: one way in, no way back, a vessel that holds what enters it until somebody lifts it. That was anatomy. It was not a doctrine, and he wrote no doctrine over it.

The blessing went on without him in the way all work goes on around a basket set down out of the draught. Salt got into the creases of Orra’s knuckles and stayed there. The hearth-maids came down off the stone. The weir women spoke of the number of eels the traps would hold and then of the weather, which was how a number went around this district without arriving anywhere; when one of them said a figure for the blessing a man turned the conversation to the river, and the figure lay where it had fallen and nobody picked it up. The boy lay at the low angle at which a basket sees a plank walk, with the boards going out above him on their posts and a gap in one of them that a man stepped over without putting his weight down. He filed the gap with the other gaps. He had seen a likeness between a board that would not bear a man and a breath that was missing in a house’s count, and he had kept the two apart on purpose, because a likeness was a likeness and that was all he had ever been given.

The knot-string hung at Orra’s waist, grey wool gone the colour of river silt where her thumb had worried it. There were knots at the end of it enough to make a row, and she took a fresh length between her rope-hands and tied one more while she told a story sideways, the way she told everything, starting where somebody was already in trouble.

“It was the year of the flower,” she said, and the half-song went down with the words. “The night they took them, I stood at the end of the weir and watched a woman come up the Clay Yard with a bundle under her coat. Covered it was. Small. Warm — I could see the steam off it in the cold, or I told myself I could. It had a flower on it. I’ve never known what to call it, so I tied it.” She pulled the knot down with her thumb and set it among the others, and the new one did not sit like them.

Then she said the word. She said it low, almost under her breath, half-sung the way the old words on the stone had been half-sung, and she said it to the basket and not to the women at the racks. “forty.”

She watched what the boy did with it. That was the whole of it: a woman who had kept a count in wool for three years said the number aloud where a baby lay with the fold at his chin, and watched to see whether the baby would do anything at all. He did nothing. The thread of drool at his mouth froze and unstuck and froze again; his head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound it made when it went too far; his hand outside the wool lifted an inch toward the warmth that was not within two paces of him and came down. Somewhere in the fold the smoky crust gave a thin breath of barley smoke when the wind turned, and the body wanted it, and the body was louder. He wanted the crust and he wanted to lift a hand to the cord and he could do neither, and both wants were true at once and the second had no hands.

The surgeon in him took the other register. He noted the shape of the new knot — a double turn with the tail tucked under, tied left over right, which is the way a right-handed woman ties when she is looking at something else — and he noted the weight of her thumb as she set it, the small extra pressure that means a thing has been given a place it did not have before. He noted that the word had been said low and had been watched, which told him it was a count before he knew what it counted. He could not read Grathic in speech or on a slate and could not have lifted a knuckle to touch the wool. He kept the word on its own tray, unread, with the shape of the knot laid beside it and not on top of it, because a word and the thing it names are two objects and he had learned to keep such objects apart.

The ear held the full note in five hearings from four sources, and the sixth from a person had come six mornings back and lay beside the five without being among them, and no seventh had come. The word on its tray did not go near them. It was not a sound of a vessel giving way; it was a thing a woman had said into the air and watched, and he filed it as a saying, and left the question of what it counted where the question stood, which was out of reach. Nothing joined. He had no Grathic word for nearly and no Grathic word for the flat knock and now no word of his own for the thing she had put in the cord, and he had stopped a long time ago expecting the world to give him one.

Mira Silt came up from the eels at the far end of the racks with pale slime dried on her sleeves and her head held to the right where the top of her left ear had been. A weir woman asked her whether the run would come with the blessing and Mira answered the question and then her own answer. “Does it come when you bless it? It comes when it comes. Ask the river.” She flicked an eel-bone against her thumb and put it away when the deciding was done, and when somebody said a figure for the morning’s catch her thumb went under her apron to the grey cord at her waist and stayed there; shifting the weight of her basket showed the second cord twisted twice beneath it, never carried upriver to Nine Pots, never lifted at a door, the knots on neither counted. She did not look into the boy’s basket. He set her grey wool on its board and Orra’s knot-string on its board beside it, and would not let the two boards touch. Two women kept the district’s private account in a material no officer would think to read, and neither had said what she knew, and the boy had no standing to ask either of them.

Orra put the cord back under her apron and went to lift a trap, and the new knot went with it into the hut where the boy could not enter and could not read. He had been given the shape of it and the weight of a thumb and a word spoken low, and none of the three could be spent. A man who has spent his life opening vessels cannot open a knot with a baby’s hand; a man who has read a hundred charts cannot read a cord he cannot reach. He kept what he had been given. That was the whole of his work in the world, and it had never once changed a thing.

Orra tipped one and the eels went into the basket with the small slap of wet bodies against wicker, and a boy three paces away could not have lifted one of them, and the surgeon in him noted the way the eels went head-first into the mouth of the trap and could not find the mouth again once they were in. That was all a trap was, she had said. He watched her lift the next one and did not make more of it than that, though the shape of it stayed with him because shapes stayed with him, and because she had tied a knot over it. The eels would run whether anybody blessed them or not.
