The Turning into Seed

Days 85–90 · Silt 26–30, Seed 1, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, Sill

Silt goes out on mud and Seed comes in on the same ground. Move the loaves along the shelf; the space at the end has not moved. A month is a vessel turned, and what was left in it is still in it.

— A proverb of the kiln yards, Sill

— Day 85 —

Silt 26, Y.H. 813

The mud had set into a brown skin over the ruts of the Clay Yard, faithful to a thing gone, holding the print of a boot and giving it back slowly. It had taken the boot to the ankle four mornings back and let go of it in its own time, and now it kept the shape with the obstinacy of a thing that has been stood in and will not say by whom. The skin went dull where the sun found it and kept its darkness in the hollows, and the yard, which had spent a week walking around what the water left, went around it still with a broom and a glance and no figure spoken. There was a print at the rack-end where a man had stood to look at the water and then thought better of going down to it, and another at the well where a bucket had been set and lifted and set again. The mud kept both. It gave nothing back but the shape. Wet wool, woodsmoke and tallow went over it on the wind, and under those the sour mineral tang of a ridge nobody swept.

Sella Vail had given the broom its instruction before she gave it to anybody. “Go around,” she said to the bristles, and then, because a broom is a slow listener, she said it again to the yard: “It cost enough to come in. It’ll cost more to take out.” She said the house where another woman would have said I, and the house had said it through her for six years. Before she had come down she had told the fire it was the thaw and told it to be fed, and the fire had answered with four low pulls and a silence where the fifth should have been, warming the boards by degrees under blind ash and the back of a broken pot. She went wide around the clean dark line at the Vail corner where the Ash ridge had turned its own corner and stopped, and wider still around the new hairline of grey that had gathered beside it, finer than the old, which the house called the thaw and said would go when it goes. She swept neither. Under the lowest rack was the space where whole slate should have stood and where broken ones lay now, gone around by three sweepings and entered by nobody all week; she went around that too, as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and brought the broom back to the edge of the print without touching it.

The boy came down at the edge of the work in a basket that was not his, carried level as a pot filled. Corin was eighty-five days old and had been carried everywhere since the day he was born, and the age sat in him with the exactness of a thing counted at night and not entered anywhere. She kept a half-pace from every open slate and set the basket at the edge of the light where no slate lay, and went wide around a slate propped at the rack end as though it were a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. A basket set down is a pot set down and left; that was the rule of the house and he had learned to lie under it without expecting to be asked. She gave the instruction to the wool and the wind and never to the basket: keep it off his ears, it’s the river wind. The wool smelled of woodsmoke and tallow, and under those the ridge’s old sourness, the same smell that had come up from the floor of every room he had ever lain in.

The yard counted what the water had taken as it had counted it all week, in loaves pushed along a shelf, in glances at the clay jar on the wall, and in the space at the row’s end that nobody called a number. “Nine went in where eleven stood,” a rack-woman said to the shelf, not to Sella. “That’s two for the levies and one for the pot that was broken.”

“Ask the pot,” Sella said to the shelf. “It’ll tell you what a wet thing costs set down there.”

The rack-woman put her thumb to the row’s end where the gap was and took it away again without putting a figure to it, and went back to her sleeves. The boy, carried at the edge of it all, kept eighty-five exactly against the loaves and the glances and the unnamed space, and set the number down nowhere, because there was no slate in this yard that would take it and no hand among them that would have known what it counted.

The bucket-round at the yard well had begun before the light had decided what it wanted of the day, and the rope came up stiff, keeping the shape of the hand that had hauled it. A skin lay on the water where the bucket broke it, thin as the skin on milk gone cold, and the women spoke of the skin to the bucket and not to the child in the basket. Near the tubs a woman said it was a pity about that room shut up in the war, and another said that story over the bucket had a date in it and so was nobody’s business, and the yard let it lie where it lay. There was no third telling. The second man from Greybank had brought his story to this same well yesterday and gone back down the road with his mud on him and no name given, and the yard had taken it as a curiosity with a date, as it had taken the first months before; one telling sat in the boy’s keeping from Ilmen and the other had come to sit beside it, and neither had grown a third.

The boy kept the two tellings on a tray of their own, beside the told bell of the Long Night, four, four and a gap in the dark; the bell heard at the Cross, four, four and a gap with nobody crossing himself; the early note out of the stone at the Singing Sill; and the kiln’s breath under blind ash, four low pulls and a missing fifth warming the boards by degrees. Four low things in four places. The river’s own note of water going over water was a fifth thing on a place of its own and not a hearing at all. On the tray of the two tellings he kept the likeness and the difference without letting either become the other: both men had said the words not the war’s hand, and the first had made the small sound of iron shut on iron in his throat while the second drew his breath in through his nose against his teeth. A likeness laid on a likeness will grow a cause whether or not a hand puts one there, and he had learned in a better-lit room what a man can make of two tellings of the same thing; so he kept them two, and wrote no cause across either, and joined neither to the chimney across the yard standing with its breath held under blind ash.

He knew the room before either man had finished describing it, having lain awake under this roof and counted its breath in the dark, four low pulls and a silence where the fifth should have been. A vessel with no way to feed what it holds cooks its own heat out of the air; the face arranges itself in the ordinary way at the end, and the bill is paid by the bodies inside. He had once been the man who wrote down how such a room would finish, in a calm voice, and gone on to the next body. Here he had the anatomy and no word for it in the language of the yard, and no hand to open a door if there had been one. The telling had come twice into this well and had stayed a story with a date both times, and the chimney across the yard stood with its breath held and was joined to neither telling by anyone who had spoken. He did not join them either.

Six hearings of the full note stayed six — five from four sources and the sixth out of a person who had been warm that morning — beside and never among, and no seventh came to the well. Three near-full answers stood advanced and not paid on a tray of their own, neither hearings nor the third thing, and the word forty was on a place of its own with the readable shape of the double turn beside it and the weight of a thumb on wicker apart from both, and it was not spoken in this yard on this morning. He had no hand to tap the pots and ask them to say it again, and no Grathic word with which to ask.

One brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold against his ear, and the empty place beside it had crossed another night without acquiring a name. No third came. The crust was gone past the smell of barley and had never once been a sermon, and it lay where a hand had put it years ago and nobody had moved it since. He wanted it. He wanted the breast and the cold iron of the bucket’s rim two inches beyond his hand, and the three wants sat in him together with the strict equality of three tools laid out on a tray. He could reach none. A thread of drool had frozen at the corner of his mouth in the small hours and no hand came to wipe it, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and the surgeon’s register in him noted the sound and its causes and did not need to hear it. Cold arrived in its known order: cheek, temple, wrist, the wool riding back from the wrist so the wind found the bare place. The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the rim and came down. There had been a life in which reducing a shoulder was work done before breakfast and a man put his hand where he wanted it; here he was the only adult at this well and the least use in it, and the joke had never once been funny enough to say aloud.

Tarn had set the small bench knife on its sill before the light and told the empty yard where it was, so that it could be checked. Six years stood in a mark worn almost flat by his thumb and the seventh was nearly gone and still held, the one entry in this house the house could not check. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line, and no scrubbing lifted it; the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back. His broom went over the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector, over and over, without once turning along it, and no collector came. He said Yes, house to the woman who wanted the thaw early and to the man who wanted it late, agreeing so entirely that no argument could find a foot, and went on wanting the late because it puts off the paying, though the paying had stood in the ruts all week. Below the sill a child had come out of the falling water with a foot that stayed dark and cold, and a woman had wrapped it twice in wool and pressed it between her palms and called it the cold, while the yard called the water weather; the two facts went past each other again this morning and never met. The boy read the extremity going before the core and the afterdrop waiting on the far side of a warming nobody would perform, and had no hand and no standing to put on the shin. He knew the shape of it as he knew the inside of a room that had cooled from the edges inward while everybody was kind. The reading worked perfectly and bought nothing.

At the turn of the wind Sella lifted the basket level and carried him up the Clay Yard at the half-pace from every open slate, going around the space under the lowest rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and entered by nobody. Under the Vail kiln-house roof she set him on his bed and not in the Debt Loft, and put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without looking at his face. She left the thread at his mouth frozen and unwiped. She touched the widow’s mark at no hour of this day, because a well is not an opening and a basket is not a door. The door stood a finger’s width open, and through the gap came the river’s low muttered note from the Ilm, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, and through the floor came the fire’s four, four and a gap under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, the two sounds kept in two separate places as they had been for eighty-five days. The clean dark line lay where it had always lain and the new finer grey lay beside it unswept, and the boards above kept four nails and four and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts did not meet. The payer of the coal had gone unnamed all this time. Nobody had said who kept the fire giving, and the district had one word for the water and one past tense for the thaw and went home warm on both.

One brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold with the empty place beside it still unnamed. Nothing had been joined, paid or explained. The two tellings lay on their tray and the six hearings stayed six beside them and never among, the three near-full answers stood advanced and not paid, the word was not spoken and no collector came. The boy was eighty-five days old and nobody had told him so. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the water and came down. The count crossed into Silt 27 unspent.

Silt 27, Y.H. 813

Before the light had decided what it wanted of the morning, Tarn set the small bench knife on its sill and told the empty yard where it was. He told the sill first and the yard after, because the sill would still be there when he had gone back up the ladder and the yard would not: “It’s on the sill. You can check.” Six years stood in a cut on the handle that his thumb had worn almost flat, so flat that a man had to know where to put his eye before he could find it; the seventh had never been cut at all and was nearly gone and still held. It was the one entry in this house the house could not check, and he kept it where no slate would reach it, in the space between a thumb and a breath. To the kindling he said, as he had said every morning of this last year, “Six counted. The next I don’t count.”

The kindling gave no answer and wanted none. It lay four and four and a space where a fifth should have been, the same old arithmetic the boards overhead kept with their nails and the fire kept under its ash, and Tarn went on holding the breath he had decided not to spend. He had been bound in the year the clip went on and had counted every day since. A man who owns nothing should know where the small things are, and the small thing he owned was the exact account of what he owed, which the house kept badly and he kept exactly, and which the house could not check. There was a shape that came over him some nights and left him reaching for a tool he had never used; he did not name it, and the counting kept it down as a hand keeps a lid on a pot.

The yard argued the thaw as the light came, in the ordinary way: it had been coming for a month and would come early or late and nobody had owned a day of it. A woman at the tubs wanted it early and said the pits would be green by Tenthday; a man with a barrow wanted it late and said a late thaw was a longer week before the paying, and the paying had stood in the ruts since the water went out of them.

“Green by Tenthday, if it comes,” the woman said to the tubs.

“Late is better,” the man said to his barrow. “Late is longer.”

“The house wants the water down before it wants the pits green,” Sella said, and gave the rest of it to the broom: “Go around what’s left. It cost enough to come in.”

“Yes, house,” Tarn said to the woman.

“Yes, house,” he said to the man.

He agreed so entirely with both that the argument had nowhere to put its foot, and went on wanting the late one all the same, because a late thaw puts off the paying, though the paying had stood in the ruts all week and would stand there whether the pits came green or not. A man can hold two opinions as neatly as he holds two tools and use neither, and a chest held tight against a breath is legible in a worn thumb-place. He had been sent for a collector once, in the Long Cold, and come back at the frozen ford and told the house the collector had been sent for; the house had not asked whether he had gone. He kept that sentence where he kept the seventh year, and neither was entered. Below the sill a child’s foot stayed the cold to the woman who had wrapped it twice in wool and pressed it between her palms, and weather to a yard that called the water weather; the two facts went past each other again this morning and did not meet. The boy read the extremity going before the core and the afterdrop waiting on the far side of a warming nobody would perform, and had no hand and no standing to put on the shin. The reading worked perfectly and bought nothing.

His broom went over the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector, over and over, without once turning along it. The road went down toward the Fishstairs, and a man went down it when a house wanted a figure taken from the living or the dead, and Tarn had gone down it once and come back, and came back no more. He swept the road and did not look along it, and no collector came down it this morning either. The boy, carried at the edge, kept the omission on a place of its own, as he kept the told bell and the heard bell on two places and the two tellings of the shut counting room on a tray where they lay side by side and never touched, the likeness and the difference kept and neither joined to the chimney across the yard, where the kiln’s breath stood held under blind ash, four low pulls and a gap. Six hearings of the full note stayed six — five from four sources and the sixth out of a person who had been warm that morning — beside and never among; three near-full answers stood advanced and not paid; and the word forty was on a place of its own with the readable shape of the double turn beside it and the weight of a thumb on wicker apart from both. He had no hand to tap a pot and ask it to say again, and no Grathic word with which to ask. The four low things stayed in four places and the river’s note of water going over water was a fifth thing on a place of its own and not a hearing at all, and the loan that could hear stayed uncalled.

Jorra Vail came through the finger’s-width-open door of the Vail wheel-shed with fine grey slate dust worked into both forearms past the bend of his little finger, not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel. Men had come in since first light and spoken low in the back room, and he gave the house no name for them and the house asked for none. He had not said the room was cold. The yard went around the space under the lowest rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and the broken birth-slates lay there still, gone around and entered by nobody.

He spoke to the wheel before he spoke to anyone. “It doesn’t care whose hands,” he said to it, in the flat voice that went to the wheel and the pots and only then to people. “It cares if they’re steady.” He had taken the wheel at thirteen when his father died and nobody came to draw him, and the seat was worn hollow on the left by twenty years of another man’s weight; he had spent three years trying to fill that hollow with his own. The little finger had bent from a splinter off the wheel when he was eleven and he told people it was a fight, because a fight sounded more like him. A pot turned under his hands and he told it without welcome and without unkindness, “You’ll do.” He knew the full note of a Vail pot before it left the wheel — knew it in the wrist and in the thin place under the shoulder and in the air that would be under the note when a knuckle found it — and he would not say so before a kiln-master from High Kiln, who would call it early and make something of it. The boy lay at the edge of the work and knew that he knew, and kept the knowing where questions can last for years.

Sella had not finished telling him the fire was low. “The fire’s low; the pot wants —” and his bent finger went into the damper ahead of the sentence, cold iron taking the cold out of his fingertip, and moved it; so the end of her instruction arrived at the pot instead of at him and she shut her mouth on it. The household straightened around his silence the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted: Tarn’s broom found its stroke again, the fire took what it was given, and Sella carried the rest of the sentence to the pot and left it there. He had not answered her with a word and had not looked at her, which was how he obeyed her instantly. The boy read the damper as he would read any iron that stops a thing from running: the same bite that keeps a vessel whole can take the hand off a man, and there is no word in Grathic that holds both mercies in one, so he held them in one place without a name and asked nobody to call it either.

He did not say the back room was cold and did not look toward the bed or into the basket. He’s not Vail stayed sealed as tightly as a lid nobody had tried to lift in three years, and the promise of the foundling’s name to the men who burn slates was neither given nor withdrawn. He wanted the wheel to stay in the house and the boy outside the name, and had said neither, and the two wants stood in him as the two opinions stood in Tarn, each kept whole by never being spent. The boy kept dusty forearms and clean ones on one tray as two states of one man’s mornings and asked neither which slate was broken nor whether it was a birth-slate, because a question spoken would make an object of an answer and there was no answer here to carry.

Past the door a vessel gave its note: a pot set down on a bench somewhere out of sight, the small flat knock of a whole vessel or the fuller note of one with air under it. He did not turn toward it to find which. Tarn’s head did not turn and he did not name the sound or look toward the basket. The boy did not call it a seventh hearing, because a note that is not turned toward has entered nobody’s keeping, and six stayed six beside and never among. Two ears kept their equality under one roof and two silences kept theirs, of one shape with nobody putting a word between them, and the loan that could hear stayed uncalled. A loan that can hear is a loan that can be called, and the sentence that would call it lay under the roof as it had lain for six years, under the small knife on the sill and the seventh year held where the house could not check it. Something must give at the end of the seventh year, and it had not given here.

Sella Vail set the basket just inside the finger’s-width-open door of House Silt, at the edge of the light and away from every place a slate might lie. She set it down as she would set a pot down, level, the whole weight of it committed to the floor before she let go, and then stood a half-pace off from it and gave the instruction to the wool and the wind and never to the basket: keep it off his ears, it was the river wind. The little-door burn on the back of her hand shut on nothing and stayed shut. Corin was eighty-seven days old and had been carried everywhere since the day he was born, and the number sat in him with the exactness of a measure set down at night and not entered anywhere. Alda Reed came short into the room with her grey wool shawl drawn across her shoulders and the smell of soap-and-milk gone cold under river silt on her, lye sour under the soap, her nails scrubbed to the quick with clay still under them. She came as a woman comes who has been lifting other people’s children since she was nineteen, taking the shortest way between a door and a thing that must be done. She went to the bench first. The birth-slates came down off her arm and lay writing-down on the bench before she turned to the fold, one after another, in the order of a hand that reaches for the record before it reaches for the person. Sella stood a half-pace off and asked nothing about the writing, and Alda asked nothing about why the boy was under this roof, and the arrangement between the two women held as it had held since he was three days old, a door neither of them opened.

Then she turned to the fold and pressed it flat at his shoulder, and pressed it a breath longer than a fold requires. He read the length of it from the inside as he had read the full note of a vessel from the outside: a hand that knows exactly how much pressure a thing will bear, and one breath more given after the pressure is right, a small exact tenderness that had no word in this room and no place to put itself except the wool. She crossed two fingers at the red edge of the birth-flower before she knew she had done it, and covered the sign with the date she had given the woman who lay more mornings dead than any living person kept count of, the seventh of Stone, the account up on the eighth, before the paper went out. The gesture and the date were one gesture. She did not say the woman’s name. She did not say the number, and she did not say the third day.

“He lies quiet,” Sella said to the pot on the bench.

“Warm and breathing, that’s all I’ve set down,” Alda said. It was exact and it was not true, and it had been exact and not true for three years, and there was nobody living who could take the date off the sign or correct the thing it stood over.

She went on with dates after there was no question left to answer. The cooper’s seventh in Mud End. A potter’s third child in Stonemouth, the last high water. Dates are a handle on a thing too large to carry, and she carried it by the handle, going on with the names of other people’s children long after the room had stopped asking, while the neighbour’s cloth went on moving flat against the board in the next room. In the life that was not this one a man could close a chart over a thing left lying, set his name at the foot of it and go home to his supper; here a woman covered a sign with a date and went on with other dates, and both of them would have called it the correct amount of words for a room in which somebody was in pain. The surgeon’s reading worked perfectly and bought nothing, as it had bought nothing at the well and nothing below the sill. He lay with the date over him and the fold pressed too long at his shoulder and the little sound of water going over water coming up through the floor, and kept the two things on two places without letting them touch.

Her hand went once to the little tin on its cord inside her bodice and came away without opening it. It was a small, economical movement, the hand going in and out as a hand goes to a tool it has decided not to use, and she came back to the dates without a pause in them, as if the hand had never moved. The tin stayed shut.

Her sixth narrow slate stayed turned face-down toward a floorboard in a house the boy could not name. Six pages, all unread, written in a second hand no Court physician had seen, the sixth set down on Silt 5 and turned down then and never written again. No seventh page was written in this room. The bucket went down and came up and set itself on the stone with the care of a pot filled that must not be slopped. The boy, carried at the edge of the work with the cold iron two inches beyond his hand, watched the order of it and kept it — instruction to the bucket, then to the women, then nothing to the basket, and the person last if at all.

The woman went away with her sleeve still wet and no number in her hand, and the loaf row stood with its space at the end that nobody called a figure. A man with a barrow said the strips east of town would want hands by the morrow, and a woman said the weather would have the last word, and nobody put a figure to the hands or the strips. The boy set the strips on a place of their own, a direction and not a number. A month had turned on a boundary the yard would not mark, and the arithmetic went over it unchanged, the way water goes over a stone without stopping to be counted.

Corin was ninety days old, three months, carried everywhere at the edge of the work; the number sat in him with the exactness of an instrument laid out on a towel and entered nowhere. He could have told the hour of a burn and the term of a debt and the difference between a wound three days old and one four. Here he could not get anybody to say how old he was. He wanted the number of his days spoken by somebody who would know what it counted, and nobody in the yard had any use for the number, and the house kept its account in loaves and glances and a space at the end of a row. He had reduced a shoulder before breakfast in the life that was not this one and put his hand wherever he wanted it; here he lay under a fold of wool and could not lift that hand to his own chin. He had watched a hand go to a record before it went to a person in a better-lit room, and the house performed the same order with loaves and a glance at a jar, and the person came last if at all. A chart closed over a note turned down was a small thing beside a whole yard closed over a space nobody would name. Neither of them had a hand willing to lift the lid. The joke had never once been funny enough to say aloud.

The body humiliated him exactly and in its known order. A thread of drool had frozen at the corner of his mouth in the small hours and no hand came to wipe it, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and came back. Cold arrived at cheek, then temple, then wrist, the wool riding back from the wrist so the air found the bare place and stayed there. One brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold against his ear, gone past smelling of anything, and the empty place beside it had crossed the boundary into Seed without acquiring a name; no third came. He wanted the crust, wanted the breast, wanted the lip of the bucket two inches beyond his hand, and the three wants sat in him with the strict equality of three tools laid out for a procedure he would never perform. The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water going over water behind the racks and came down. He was the only adult in the yard and the least use in it.

Tarn had set the small bench knife on its sill before the light and told the empty yard where it was, six years standing in a mark his thumb had worn almost flat and the seventh nearly gone and still held, the one entry in this house the house could not check.

“Six counted,” he said to the kindling. “The next I don’t count.”

He said it as a man says the weather and went on holding the breath he had decided not to spend, though his chest was already tight with it. He had said the same thing to the same yard every morning of six years and would say it once more on the last morning, if the last morning came under this roof. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line, and no scrubbing lifted it; the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back. A woman with a barrow said the thaw would come early and be done with it, and a man at the tubs said late was better for the ruts.

“Yes, house,” Tarn said to the one. “Yes, house,” he said to the other. He agreed so entirely that no argument could find a foot, and went on wanting the late because a late thaw puts off the paying, though the paying had stood in the ruts since the water went. His broom went over the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector, over and over, without once turning along it. No collector came. From the Vail wheel-shed came the low note of the wheel and a word spoken to a pot — You’ll do — and no figure came out of the shed onto the yard, and Jorra Vail was not seen. In the afternoon a vessel gave its note past the door and Tarn’s head did not turn and he did not name the sound, and the boy did not call it a seventh hearing. Two ears and two silences kept their equality under one roof and the loan that could hear stayed uncalled.

Under the Vail roof the carried fire lay under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees and missing one breath in every four. Sella told it it was the thaw and turned the coals with the back of the broken pot and fed it, and the fire answered with four low pulls and a silence where the fifth should have been. Its payer had gone unnamed for ninety days. The coal the brine-gull took upriver weeks back was entered in no slate and missed in none; one warmth less had gone out of the world with no account the poorer, and the month had turned over it without noticing. She did not touch the widow’s mark at the turn of the month, because a month’s turning is not an opening and no opening had come into this house in six days, and she had lost a husband to the danger and had never once described it that way herself. The clean dark line lay where it had always lain and the finer grey lay beside it, and the boards overhead kept four nails and four and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts did not meet. Every warmth in the house had a payer and none of them stood in the room to be thanked; the boy had learned to ask who paid before he asked what a thing was worth, which was a surgeon’s question in a country that had no use for it.

The boy checked the file as a man checks instruments laid out on a towel before he begins, though there was no beginning and no instrument to lay hands on. Six hearings of the full note stayed six from four sources, five from vessels and the sixth from a person, filed beside and never among and never a seventh; no seventh came on the first day of Seed. Three near-full answers stood advanced and not paid, and the three intervals — Stone 13, Stone 17, Stone 25 — stayed on another tray, objects with the shape of sounds that had not been sounds. The word forty remained a saying on its own tray, unspoken at the turning of the month, with the readable shape of the double turn beside it and the weight of a thumb on wicker apart from both. Four low things stood in four places — the told bell, the heard bell, the early note of the Singing Sill, the kiln’s gap — with the river’s note of water going over water a fifth thing on a place of its own and not a hearing at all. The two tellings of the shut counting room stayed on their own tray joined to no kiln in Low Kiln, the likeness kept and the difference kept — breath through the nose against breath in the throat, both saying not the war’s hand — and neither turned into the other though two men had carried the same danger into the same yard. The loan that could hear stayed uncalled. Three doors down the pewter tin and the turned slate stood shut and unread, with no seventh page written and no record carried to any door; a woman was more mornings dead than any living person kept count of and uncollected, the number gone into the water in a thumb. No collector came for her and the morning came all the same. Below the sill a child’s cold foot was still the cold to one woman and weather to the yard, uncured and unnamed. He knew the extremity going before the core and the afterdrop waiting on the far side of a warming nobody would perform, and the look severe cold puts on a face that other people take for a smile, and had no word for any of it and no hand two paces out. The reading worked and bought nothing. He had no Grathic word for any of it and no hand to lay on any of it.

At the going of the light Sella lifted the basket level, carried him up at the half-pace from every open slate, went around the space under the lowest rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and set him on his bed under the Vail kiln-house roof and not in the Debt Loft. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without looking at his face and left the thread at his mouth frozen and unwiped, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left. She told the fire it was the thaw and fed it, and the fire answered with four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, with its payer still unnamed and the gull’s coal entered in no slate.

The door stood a finger’s width open. Through the gap came the river’s low muttered note from the Ilm, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, and through the floor came the kiln’s breath, four, four and a gap held under blind ash, the two sounds in two separate places as they had come for ninety days and as they would come tomorrow. One brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold with the empty place beside it still unnamed, and no third came. Nothing had been joined, paid, explained or ended. Six hearings stayed six beside and never among, the three near-full answers stood advanced and not paid, the word was not spoken, the two tellings stayed on their tray, the loan stayed uncalled, and no collector came. The boy was ninety days old and nobody had told him so. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the water and came down. The first work of the planting month had been sweeping; the work after it lay east of Low Kiln in strips he had not yet seen, and the keeping went on unchanged into the planting year and the count went on.

The Chronicle of Chapter 18

Silt went out on the mud it had come in on. The yard swept around what the water left and entered none of it --- the brown skin holding a boot’s print, the clean dark line, the finer grey called the thaw, the space under the rack. Sella Vail carried the boy at the half-pace from every open slate to his bed. The house kept its days in loaves and glances and a place nobody would call a number; the boy kept his count exactly and could spend none.

Tarn set his knife on its sill and told the empty yard where it was, six years worn flat by his thumb, the seventh nearly gone and still held. Six counted. The next I don’t count, he told the kindling, wanting the late thaw because it puts off the paying; his broom went over the collector’s road without turning. Jorra Vail came in with grey slate dust past his bent finger, told a pot it would do and moved the damper ahead of Sella’s sentence; he did not look toward the bed.

Three doors down the pewter tin above the empty bed kept its pale fold. Alda Reed laid her birth-slates on the bench before she turned to the fold, pressed the wool a breath too long, crossed two fingers at the birth-flower before she knew it and covered the sign with the date of a woman more mornings dead than any living person kept count of. Warm and breathing, that’s all I’ve set down, she said, exact and not true. Her hand went once to the tin in her bodice without opening it; her sixth slate lay face-down.

At the Fishstairs Mira Silt answered a question with her own --- It comes when it comes. Ask the river. When a buyer named a figure her thumb went to the grey cord, showing the second twisted twice beneath it, neither knot counted. The steps came back four, four and a landing out of line that a market walked carefully over. On the last market Berr Clay wiped his thumb twice, read one in twelve and said, The levy is called. It is not called by me; the true subtraction went unspoken and the number for the thaw arrived nowhere. A foundling turned his head toward a pot a half-beat after the boy’s and could not say why.

Seed opened on mud and the first work of it was sweeping. The carried fire kept four, four and a gap under blind ash; one crust lay stiff in the fold and the empty place beside it crossed into Seed without a name. At dusk Sella put the fold right and left the thread at his mouth frozen, the river’s note and the fire’s gap in two places as for ninety days. He was ninety days old and nobody had told him so. His hand went up an inch toward the water and came down. Nothing had been joined, paid, explained or ended.
