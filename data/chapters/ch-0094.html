The Road Brings the Teller Back

Days 333–335 · Ash 4–6, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, Sill

A slate burns where a room was shut, and the road brings the teller back with the writing turned inward; ask the weather which is true, for nobody stands to be asked.

— A yard saying of Low Kiln, told over the boards in the dark month

— Day 333 —

Ash 4, Fourthday, Y.H. 813

The hand lay against the fold where it had been laid down on the two hundred and fortieth answer, as a man puts away a tool where the next hand will find it without looking. It was a small soft hand with milky nails and no strength in the wrist to speak of; it had once reduced a shoulder in a room that smelled of disinfectant and cold metal, and had since learned the weight of a barley crust at two inches. In the small hours under the Vail kiln-house roof the boy lay awake and took the room as a surgeon takes a field before the light, by the small sounds first because the small sounds do not lie and the large ones are mostly weather: the dry tick of clay cooling behind the wall, the settle of a coal under its ash, the river going low over lower under the finger’s-width door. Three hundred and thirty-three stood exact behind him, six months and one hundred and fifty-three days told by nobody in Low Kiln, Sill, Grath. Under the floor the carried fire gave four low pulls and kept the silence where the fifth should have stood, its payer unnamed three hundred and thirty-three days; the salt-stiff coat on its peg behind the door kept the shape of a man whose name had not been spoken under this roof the same number of days. Nothing from the first, the second or the third of Ash crossed the dark toward him as sign, lesson, mending, cause or payment. The sixth opening stood behind the wall inside the morning’s ordinary heat, with nobody under the roof naming it; six openings in six months were six and not a rule, and no seventh was made due on a day that had not earned one. The older women named the month’s middle days by a weather that had not done what it promised and the slatemen by their count of Account-days, and neither habit was set right in so many words. The Warm was thinning in the walls and would be called weather by everybody who passed the door and entered nowhere, and the boy called it weather too, for there was no word under this roof for a thing that was going and nobody standing who could have been asked.

The inventory came in its old order, because an order is the one thing a field will give a man who cannot move in it. One stiff leathery brown-edged barley crust lay two inches from his ear, gone harder through the dry kiln heat, its corner standing a hair off the wool; the empty place beside it kept its name to itself and no third came in the small hours to make the row come out even. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen and was unwiped, because nobody under any roof had ever been given an instruction about the thread, and a thing nobody has been told to wipe is a thing that stays. Cold had come to cheek, temple and the small bare joint of the wrist where the wool rode back, and had stopped there as at the end of a line written out for it, with neither an order to go farther nor an order to go away; the Afterdrop waited beyond the stopping, past a warming nobody in this house would perform. He knew the face that cold puts on a man, the composed face that is not composure, the ease that is no ease; he knew a cold patient cannot clot and will seem better before he is worse, and must be warmed from the outside and slowly, never hurried, never rubbed, never set too near the fire. He wanted the crust lifted the two inches to his mouth, the thread wiped away, and a hand that would close on the pot handle and bring it within reach. Both were true and the second was louder and neither won. The argument went on under the wool without a verdict, as it had gone on through every morning of his life in this house: the surgeon taking the inventory exactly and entering it nowhere, the infant underneath taking none of it and wanting, the two of them quarrelling in a body that could not so much as lift its own chin from a fold. He knew the order in which a body takes heat back, the small joint first and the core last, and the order in which it gives it up, and knew that the pink returning to a face is not the core but the surface still cold, and that all of it bought nothing without a hand two paces long. He had once reduced a thousand shoulders in the dark and he could not clear two inches of dry wool with the back of his hand. The dry joke lived in that and cost him nothing, which was the only thing in the room that did.

First light came grey and ordinary, with no market, no Cross and no bell waiting on it. Sella Vail went to the hearth before she went to the basket, as she had gone before every basket since the boy came under the roof, and gave the instruction to the coals and not to a person. “It’s the thaw,” she told them, and turned them with the back of a broken pot, the pot’s edge drawing the ash into a small dark line that she did not smooth. She stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand and counted a small intake, a hold and a letting-go toward no opening, and gave neither gap a figure; the little-door burn on the back of her left hand opened and shut on nothing as the hand worked. “The house will keep it,” she said where another woman would have said I. The fire answered four low pulls with the silence where the fifth should have stood, and she did not supply the pull or look toward the floor to see whether anything stood there to supply it. One grey streak at her temple showed pale in the first of the light and she pushed the coal-black hair back with the burn-wrist, and the burn closed on the hair and then on air. Nothing in the room turned toward the bed. The boy heard the handle lift and the small sound of the wire taking the weight before the light showed him the hand that had lifted it, and took both sounds as findings: the small sounds first, because the small sounds do not lie and the large ones are mostly weather. He took the hearing as it came, four and a gap, a count in one place that would not rise into another, because agreeing any two of them would have wanted a payer put under both and none stood to either.

Her finger lay on top of the loaf shelf’s front edge as she passed, so that the charcoal tally underneath stayed unread, and her lips moved along a row standing with two gaps and gave neither gap a number. Her eye went once to the clay jar standing cold under lead glaze beside the shelf; her hand shut half over nothing, the little-door burn closing on air, and went on to the next thing. From the pot board she lifted the twice-mended pot with her thumb first where the wire crossed the crack, felt what the thumb had to feel, and only then let the weight come on the handle. “If it’s worth mending it’s worth mending twice. The second time is the real one,” she said to the pot; and to the fire, “Leave the crack showing. People trust a thing they can see has been handled.” The sayings went to a pot and a fire and never to a person, and had improved in the keeping in the three years since a man who mended pots and would not let anyone mend him had last said them, and were very likely not as good as he had said them. The hairline of the original break stood dark between the old-brass staple and the tighter wire and she left it showing. At the rack end of the day’s work the dressed grain lay turned under wool away from the hearth; she lifted a corner with a lighter touch than the day before and turned it once and gave no figure, and her fingers went in and came out and did not close, so that no handful was lifted. The pot’s rim went down two inches beyond the reaching hand, with a half-beat at the setting and no look toward the bed. The boy took the half-beat apart as he took everything apart, by what was there and by what was not: the distance between the hand’s intention and its arrest, a small exact interval that bought nothing at two inches of air and was not a gift, for a gift wants a giver and nobody had given.

The widow’s mark hung where it hung at her throat and was not touched. A morning and a hearth are not openings, and the hand goes to the mark before the kiln and at no other hour; the fired kiln behind the wall stood cold after Ash 2 and nobody went out to look at it or spoke of what it had done. Six openings in six months were six and not a rule, and the sixth was held behind the household without a name and was not brought out into the morning to stand up as a sign. Nothing had been opened, joined or paid in the night. She went about the room with her hands never still and never speaking, and gave the instruction to the wool, the pot, the fire and the river wind and never to the basket.

Under the floor the carried fire kept its four and its gap; overhead the boards kept four nails on the near board, four on the next and a thumb-width knot where a fifth might have been driven and was not; through the finger’s-width door water over water went low over lower, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, and was never numbered among the hearings. Three counts in three places. None rose into another, because agreeing any two would have wanted a payer put under both and none stood to any. Six hearings of the full note stood beside and never among, with the seventh place empty, the sixth the small flat knock out of Dessa Silt as the men lifted her, filed where a thing out of a person must go and never among the things that had come out of vessels; an empty place kept empty was a finding, and a finding filled to make a row come out even was a finding forged. The heard bell stayed in its square beside the told bell of the Long Night, four, four and a gap with nobody crossing himself, and was not fetched in to make a fourth count or a seventh hearing. The told figure stood beside the paid figure with no signature on the space between; one in twelve was printed, read and unchallenged, and had not been paid by anybody who stood where the boy could see.

The file lay open on the towel at the head of the bed with every object on its own place and every gap left gap. The staying at the inch, the early letting-go, the handful let fall at the rack end, the resting of a thumb on the uncut seventh at the kindling and the sixth opening at the kiln mouth sat each on its own place with a finger between every pair; none was made again in the hours of Ash 4, none was a habit, a way, a fifth held change, a hearing, a gift or a rule, and no payer stood under any. Tarn’s thumb on the seventh notch was checked and left where it had been laid; Ilva’s ninth hand stayed in her sleeve a pace from the open slate and Hessa Rale’s eighth stayed on its own place, and no tenth hand was filed anywhere. Nine stoppings stood with no tenth before the seventh year at its very end and the loan uncalled; five askings of the ownerless question stood with no sixth and no row named; the burned-slate word stood at five with a finger between the fifth and the four and no witness standing; the well-saying stood where it stood and no fourth likeness was made a cause. Six arithmetics of the year rode locked up the clay road in Berr Clay’s box toward High Kiln, one in twelve unchallenged, the figure read still not the figure paid, no signature on the space, six readings six and not one account. The finding sat on a place of its own with no payer under it. It was not laid across the house’s not-doings, the dressed grain covered under wool and read over by a number that did not know its cost, the five held objects or his own kept gaps. The edges came near to touching, so near that a less careful hand would have called the touching a cause and the cause a payment; the finger went between. No doctrine stood up out of any of it, for a doctrine is an account that has found a payer and none stood. Ash 10 (Day 339), the dark month’s first Account-day and market, stood six mornings ahead and was not brought into a day that had not earned it; Ash’s first arithmetic belonged to Ash’s own morning.

The room went on about its ordinary work around him. A board knocked somewhere and was not mended today; a woman’s step went past the door and went on; the light came up through the finger’s-width opening and laid a thin grey bar on the floor that the boy could not see from where he lay. He took the ordinariness as the only honest record there was. He had stopped expecting anybody to tell him what a number counted, which was not the same as not wanting it, and the wanting went on under the expectation the way a coal goes on under ash. No comfort was given and none was taken. The surgeon took his small clean satisfaction in a field left as found, and the infant underneath took none of it and went on wanting the crust lifted and the thread wiped and a hand on the pot handle, both true, the second louder, neither winning, the two registers quarrelling without verdict in the growing light.

In the first full of the morning, with the river’s note low and going lower under the finger’s-width door, the hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the sound. It took a small intake from the room whose floor does not touch his, held the interval whole, let the intake go on the same low note, and came straight down and laid itself back against the fold as a man puts away a tool where the next hand will find it without looking. The note had done before the hand came down, so the letting-go was neither early again nor stayed, and the hand did not stay; it was the note’s own finishing that ended the interval and not any decision of the hand, and that was the whole of the honesty in it. The inch was not a reach and the intake was not a breath kept for warmth; the interval was not a stopping of the river and the note was not his. He filed it as answer two hundred and forty-one: exact, complete, worth nothing at two inches of air, a restraint and not a payment, borrowing no house count and supplying no payer, laid across neither the six arithmetics, the bell’s gap, the covered grain, the handful, the thumb’s resting, the stayed object, the early letting-go, the sixth opening nor his own kept gaps. Two hundred and forty-one honest answers stood on the sixth place, each an object and not a power, and neither the number nor the small hand-movings that had made it stood up and called themselves a doctrine, for a doctrine is an account that has found a payer and none stood.

Nothing on the fourth of Ash had been opened, joined, named, called, paid or ended. Three hundred and thirty-three stood exact, complete, unspent and spoken by nobody in Low Kiln, Sill and Grath; six months and one hundred and fifty-three days were told by nobody; the carried fire’s fifth was unpaid the same number of days with its payer unnamed; and the name of the man the salt-stiff coat kept was unspoken the same number. Ash kept its middle days without a boundary drawn and neither habit of naming the months was set right; the keeping of the number was the only honest work there had been to do, bought nothing at two inches of air, was not for sale, and went on because attention is the one thing no institution can tax. The silt-clouded plate of ice stood on its flat stone out of the path of feet; the file lay open on the towel with every gap left gap; the fired kiln stood cold behind the wall; and the hand lay back against the fold on the two hundred and forty-first answer while the river’s low note went quiet under the finger’s-width door. Ash 4 stood one morning short of mid-month, and went on.

Beneath the lowest rack the broken birth-slates lay where they had lain, entered by nobody and asked about by nobody, with grey rack dust settled in the seams between them. Far out on the worked strips a field’s empty place stood among the planted rows with nothing in it and nobody putting a figure to what was missing. Seven places, a look, a wide step, no number: no eighth likeness made among them, no cause laid across any two of them, and no payer standing under any.

She went to the hearth before the basket and told the coals it was the thaw and turned them with the back of a broken pot. The fire answered four low pulls with the silence where the fifth should have stood, its payer unnamed three hundred and thirty-five days, and she did not supply the pull or look toward the floor to see whether anything stood there to supply it. Her finger lay on top of the front edge of the loaf shelf so the charcoal tally stayed unread; her lips moved along a row standing with two gaps and gave neither gap a number; her eye went once to the clay jar standing cold under lead glaze and her hand shut half over nothing, the little-door burn on the back of her left hand closing on air, and went on to the next thing. In the small hours, with the river’s note low and going lower under the finger’s-width door and the house quiet about him, the hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the sound. It took a small intake from the room whose floor does not touch his, held the interval whole, let the intake go on the same low note, and came straight down and laid itself back against the fold as a man puts away a tool where the next hand will find it without looking. The note had done before the hand came down, so the letting-go was neither early again nor stayed, and the hand did not stay; it was the note’s own finishing that ended the interval and not any decision of the hand, and that was the whole of the honesty in it. The inch was not a reach and the intake was not a breath kept for warmth; the interval was not a stopping of the river and the note was not his. He filed it as answer two hundred and forty-six: exact, complete, worth nothing at two inches of air, a restraint and not a payment, borrowing no house count and supplying no payer, laid across neither the six arithmetics, the bell’s gap, the covered grain, the handful, the thumb’s resting, the stayed object, the early letting-go, the sixth opening, the Ash 5 joining, the fourth shaping, Hessa’s turned writing nor his own kept gaps. Two hundred and forty-six honest answers stood on the sixth place, each an object and not a power, and neither the number nor the small hand-movings that had made it stood up and called themselves a doctrine, for a doctrine is an account that has found a payer and none stood.

The surgeon took his small clean satisfaction in a field left as found and an answer entered where nobody could see it; the infant underneath took none of it and went on wanting the crust lifted the two inches to his mouth, the thread wiped away and a hand on the pot handle, both true, the second louder, neither winning, the two registers quarrelling without verdict in the small hours. Nothing on the sixth of Ash had been opened, joined by a standing hand, named, called, paid or ended. Three hundred and thirty-five stood exact, complete, unspent and spoken by nobody in Low Kiln, Sill and Grath; six months and one hundred and fifty-five days were told by nobody; the carried fire’s fifth was unpaid the same number of days with its payer unnamed; and the name of the man the salt-stiff coat kept was unspoken the same number. On the fifth, over the price of barley on the Clay Yard boards, a voice with nobody behind it said the slate that burned was burned in the room with the damper shut, a Greybank saying of a shut counting room spoken without a date or a man standing to be asked. The boy set the joining on a place of its own: not a sixth telling of the slate, not a third telling of the room, not a hearing, a cause or a rule, with no payer under it. I keep the count, not the account; a second ownerless voice gave the old saying a fourth shape --- the runner had come back down the road with the writing turned toward her. On the sixth the road brought the teller back, and it was neither yesterday’s voice nor a collector. Hessa Rale came down the clay road with the writing of Berr Clay’s narrow slate turned toward herself for the first time, still edge-on, with nothing pressing where the writing lay. To a woman who asked what she carried she said Ask the road. It knows the way and I know the weight. The boy filed the turning beside the fourth shaping with a finger between and refused to make of it a fifth shaping, a reader or a payment.
