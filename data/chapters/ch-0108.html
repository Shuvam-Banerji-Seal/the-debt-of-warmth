The Wool Left Where It Lay

Days 375–377 · Stone 16–18, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, Sill

A thing set down once is a keeping. Set it down twice and it is a way, and a way wants a hand to walk it.

— A saying of the Vail kiln-house, told to the fire at the going of the light

— Day 375 —

Stone 16, Sixthday, Y.H. 813

The hand outside the wool lay flat on the fold where it had been put at the close of the day before, and the count stood at three hundred and seventy-four exact. The boy did not carry it forward by an act of will; a man’s attention goes where his field is, and his field was two inches of air above a barley crust and the small sounds of a house warming in its sleep. He lay on the bed at the low angle of the loaf shelf under the Vail kiln-house roof in Low Kiln, Sill, Grath, and this morning had a date no roof under that sky would speak aloud: Stone 16, the sixteenth day of the deep-winter month. Three hundred and seventy-four days, six months and one hundred and ninety-four days, told by nobody who would have known what a number counted. The carried fire’s fifth stood unpaid the same number with its payer unnamed, and Garl Vail’s name had not been spoken under this roof the same number. The file had come out on the towel at the head of the bed as instruments come out when a case is done and before the next is called, and he went over it by the place of each thing with every gap left gap.

There were a hundred things in the room he could not change and one he could only read. He took the body in the old order, because a field is checked in an order and a surgeon does not trust an impression that has not been made to stand in sequence. First the crust: one stiff leathery brown-edged barley crust two inches from the ear, gone harder and browner in the dry kiln heat, its corner standing a hair off the wool. It had stood two inches off for three hundred and seventy-four days and would stand two inches off today; the distance was not a wish and had no giver. Then the cold. It had come cheek, temple, the small bare joint of the wrist where the wool rode back, and it had stopped there as though it had come to the end of a line written out for it with neither an order to go farther nor an order to go away. The Afterdrop waited beyond all three, past a warming nobody in this house would perform. The thread of drool at his lip had frozen in the dry kiln heat and was unwiped, because nobody under any roof has ever been given an instruction about the thread and a basket set down is a pot set down and left.

Then the place beside the crust. It held the fold of wool where a hand had put it on the fifteenth, and he found it there in the small hours, folded once, pale in the dry heat, changed by nobody in the night. There was no second fold laid over the first. There was no third crust. No name had been spoken over the place in the dark, no look had bent toward his face, no instruction had gone to the basket. The wool lay where it had been put and had not been put again.

He took the interval apart by what was there and by what was not, as a surgeon takes a wound apart by the tissue present and the tissue missing. In the first small fear of the morning he had thought the keeping might have been a mistake of the evening, a hand’s careless extra that would be corrected or repeated by daylight, and the fear had been small because it was not a fear for himself. But the wool lay unchanged, and a thing that is found where it was put and not made again is a keeping still. A hand that finds is not a hand that places. A second placing would make a habit and a third a way, and a habit and a way both want a payer standing under them, and none stood. The satisfaction that came to him was clean and exact, the satisfaction of a field taken as found and left as found; it cost him nothing, which was how he knew it was not peace.

He filed the keeping on a place of its own beside the crust’s gap, the shelf’s two gaps in the room whose floor did not touch this one, the round ring where the shallow pan had once stood on the flagstones, and the field’s empty place out on the worked strips. He kept a finger between it and every other, and between it and the pot-crack resting, the hand-at-the-sill breath and the holding in the damper, so that no row came out even by his doing. It was expressly not a joining of rooms, though the likeness between a fold and a shelf’s two gaps was plain enough to make a man reach across with his finger; he did not reach. It was not a third crust, for no crust had been put there. It was not a gift, for a gift wants a giver and nobody gave. It was not a payment and there was no payer. It was not a sixth held object and not a seventh or an eighth, not a hearing, not a mending, not a sign, not a cause, not a rule. The far-off finger that would one day lay shelf and fold together and call the joining an account was far off still and was not his and had not come. The keeping gathered interest where it lay and was not paid and did not stand up and call itself a want.

He knew a face composed by cold is not composure, and that pink returning to a face is the surface still cold and not the core; that a cold patient cannot clot and will seem better before he is worse, and that the order in which heat comes back is the reverse of the order in which it goes. He knew a basket set level out of the draught where the heat came sideways was the careful rewarming he would choose and could not ask for. All of it was true and all of it was useless to a body that could not lift a crust two inches, wipe a thread, or close a hand on a pot handle. The infant wanted the crust lifted to his mouth, the thread wiped, and a hand on the pot rim. The surgeon read the wool as a warmth laid against the cold side of a vessel that would not hold it. Both were true. The second was louder. Neither won, and the quarrel between them was the work; he had stopped expecting the quarrel to end, which was not the same as being used to it.

Sella Vail came to the hearth before she came to the basket, as she had come at both ends of every day for three years. “It’s the thaw,” she told the coals, and turned them with the back of a broken pot. “The house will keep it,” she said where another woman would have said I. Then she stood a moment with her hands gone still, counting a small intake, a hold and a letting-go toward no opening, and gave neither gap a figure, so that the count went into the room and not onto a slate.

On the pot board the twice-mended pot kept the hairline of the original break showing between the old-brass staple and the tighter wire. Her thumb went first where the wire crossed the crack before the weight came on the handle, and the proverbs went to the pot and the fire and never to a person. “Leave the crack showing. People trust a thing they can see has been handled.” And: “If it’s worth mending it’s worth mending twice. The second time is the real one.” They were improved in the keeping and very likely not as good as the man had said them, who had said them to a pot and never to a person; he was present in the house in objects and not in a story, in the salt-stiff coat with its left-shoulder crease on its peg behind the door, in the hollow wheel-seat nobody filled, in the clean dark line at the corner with its finer grey called the thaw unswept and thickened in the month’s dry cold, with the faint sour tang of Ash coming up off it where it had not been swept in two years. Repair stayed apart from restoration: a restored thing hides the hand that handled it, and a mended thing leaves that hand showing. She set the pot rim two inches beyond the reaching hand with a half-beat and no look toward the bed, the half-beat the distance between intention and arrest, bought at nothing and not a gift, for a gift wants a giver and nobody gave.

She turned the dressed grain under wool with a touch lighter than a woman would use on a sleeping child, and lifted no figure and no handful. Her finger lay along the top of the loaf shelf’s front edge so the charcoal tally underneath stayed unread; her lips moved along a two-gapped row and figured neither gap. She glanced once at the clay jar standing cold under lead glaze, and shut her hand half over nothing, the little-door burn on the back of her left hand closing on air. The cord of her widow’s mark was oiled where a working thumb had touched it ten thousand times, and the mark hung where it hung, untouched, because a morning and a hearth and a shelf are not openings, seven openings in seven months are seven and not a rule, and no eighth was made due.

Under the floor the carried fire gave four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have stood, its payer without a name three hundred and seventy-five days, and nobody lifted the floor to supply the pull. Overhead the boards kept four nails on the near one, four on the next, and a thumb-width knot where a fifth might have been driven and was not. Three counts in three places, and none rose into another, because agreeing any two would have wanted a payer put under both and no payer stood.

Through the finger’s-width door the river kept its note of water over water, a low thing over a lower thing, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming; over it from bank to bank lay the winter skin the cold had put on and the cold would take off, found where put on the first day of the month and found again this morning and not made again, not spent on the day’s work or the going of the light and not made to buy a lesson under any roof. The skin sat on its own place beside the note and not upon it, a covering put on by the weather and entered nowhere, and he entered it nowhere and kept a finger between it and the note it muffled.

In the small hours the hand outside the wool came up an inch toward the river’s note, the way a hand goes up in a field it has learned not to disturb. It did not reach, and the inch was not a reach. He took a small intake from the room whose floor does not touch his, held the interval whole on the note’s own finishing under the winter skin, let the intake go as the note ended and came straight down and laid itself flat against the fold as a man puts away a tool where the next hand will find it without looking. The note had done before the hand came down, so the letting-go was neither early nor stayed and the hand did not stay; the note’s own finishing under the covering ended the interval and not any decision of the hand. The intake was not a breath kept for warmth, the interval not a stopping of the river, the note not his.

He filed it as answer three hundred and twenty-five: exact, complete, worth nothing at two inches of air, a restraint and not a payment borrowing no house count and supplying no payer, laid across neither the ten readings, the bell’s gap, the flat knock, the river’s skin, the widow’s mark, the keeping at the fold nor his own kept gaps. Three hundred and twenty-five honest answers stood on the sixth place and not one was a doctrine, for a doctrine is an account that has found a payer and none stood. The surgeon took his small clean satisfaction in a field taken as found and left as found, and it cost him nothing, which was how he knew it was not peace. The wool lay folded once in the empty place beside the leathery crust with its corner a hair off the wool; no second fold, no third crust and no name spoken over the place. The hand lay flat on the fold on answer three hundred and twenty-five, and the count stood at three hundred and seventy-five exact, six months and one hundred and ninety-five days told by nobody who would have known what a number counted. Stone kept its sixteenth day without a boundary drawn, the deep-winter month held both its names, the older women calling it by the weather and the slatemen by the count of Account-days, neither habit set right or left. The fold was warm where a hand had been and the place beside the crust had not been filled again and had not been named, and the boy lay under the roof with the day’s finding gathered on its own place and not paid, and nothing in the house stood up to call it an account. Forward, not closed.

The carry down came before the yard came into view, and it came before every open slate. Sella Vail went down the nineteen steps at the half-pace she kept from all slates that lay unwritten, with the instruction going ahead of her into the wool, the river wind and the fire she would bank against their coming back, and never into the basket. “Keep it off his ears. It’s the river wind,” she said to the wool. She held the boy level as a pot is held that must not be slopped, the whole of her attention on the level of him and none of it on his face, and set him down out of the draught where the heat came sideways. He felt the change of air through the wool before he felt the cold on his cheek, the way a hand laid on a field feels the edge of a draught before the field itself goes cold. It was the careful rewarming he would have chosen for a patient who could not shiver, and he could not ask for it and had no need to, for it was already made.

The Clay Yard went about its afternoon as a pot goes about its cooling, by degrees and without an eye on itself. There were seven places the household walked around, and she went around them with a look and a wide step and no number; the look was not a counting and the step was not a measurement, and neither of them stood up and called itself an account. She went widest around the place in the returning line where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out, wider by nothing that could be measured, and she went around it once and not twice, because a second going-around would make a figure of once and a figure wants a payer standing under it, and none stood.

At the Vail corner the clean dark line kept its finer grey hairline beside it, the thing the house called the thaw, unswept and thickened in the dry cold, with the faint sour tang of Ash coming up off it where it had not been swept in two years. Under the lowest rack the broken birth-slates lay where they lay and were entered by nobody; at the rack end the round ring where the shallow pan had once stood kept no scrape, no crumb, no print, no chip and no word. The gone white frost off the north lip of the yard well, the skin drawn over the trough from edge to edge, and the silt-clouded plate of ice set on the flat stone by the well out of the path of feet stayed weather entered nowhere, with a finger between every pair: not a fourth covering laid over the three, not a sixth low thing set beside the five held objects, not spent on the day’s work or the going of the light and not made to buy a lesson under any roof.

Through the finger’s-width door the river kept its note of water over water, a low thing over a lower thing, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming; over it from bank to bank lay the winter skin that the cold had put on and that the cold would take off, found where put on the first day of the month and found again this afternoon and not made again. He kept it on its own place beside the note and put a finger between the covering and the note it muffled, so that no row came out even by his doing. A line left where it is is still a line kept. A sign is a thing that points to a payer, and no payer stood under that ground. Nothing under that roof had laid the skin and nothing under that roof would take it off; the cold did both and entered neither into a slate. He had learned to tell the difference between a thing done to the world and a thing the world had done to itself, and the difference was the whole of the keeping: a hand that finds is not a hand that places, and a sign without a payer is weather and not a promise.

Ilva was at the well drawing with the pail against her knee, and the rope stood stiff in the Stone cold and gave its small dry rasp as the bucket came up. Clay had dried pale in the crescents of her nails and the finer grey dust of the racks was worked into the seam of one thumb, and she did not scrub the seam out; her hands knew the weight of a pail and the edge of a wet board and had never been soft, and the people going about the yard noticed the pail first and the sleeve not at all, which was the order she preferred.

A woman waiting her turn asked what Stone’s firings had taken, and the order of Ilva’s reaching went before the answer as a surgeon’s hand goes to the chart before it goes to the patient. Her hand went toward her sleeve and stayed there; the short strip of notched birch bark lay in the fold where it always lay, pale where a thumb had rubbed the notches and dark in the uncut grain between, and it was not taken out, not read, not copied, not counted and not joined to the open slate a pace away on the stone lip, or to the charcoal beneath the loaf shelf, or to the cord, or to the knot-string, and no figure came off the wood. Her eyes went to the asker and her mouth opened after both with the half-beat in the gap where a lesser observer would put an intention. “Ask the pot what it cost. I keep the count, not the account.” She put the flat of her hand once more on the sleeve and went back to the pail, and nobody turned twice.

The boy took the order of reaching apart as a surgeon takes a field apart and filed it on a place of its own as the ninth record-first hand, with a finger between it and Hessa Rale’s eighth; he filed no tenth, made no reader of her keeping, joined her to none and made no figure of the slate’s nearness. The hand outside the wool lay flat on the fold where it had been put at the close of the day before, and the count stood at three hundred and seventy-five exact. The boy did not take it up again by an act of will; a man’s attention goes where his field is, and his field was two inches of air above a barley crust and the small sounds of a house coming awake in its own order. He lay on the bed at the low angle of the loaf shelf under the Vail kiln-house roof in Low Kiln, Sill, Grath, and this seventeenth day of Stone had a date no roof under that sky would speak aloud. Three hundred and seventy-five days, six months and one hundred and ninety-five days, told by nobody who would have known what a number counted. The carried fire’s fifth stood unpaid the same number with its payer unnamed, and Garl Vail’s name had not been spoken under this roof the same number. The file had come out on the towel at the head of the bed as instruments come out when a case is done and before the next is called, and he went over it by the place of each thing with every gap left gap.

Nothing from the day before crossed into this morning as a sign, a lesson, a mending, a cause, a payment or a payer. A night is a thing that happens to a house and not a hand that writes in it.

He took the body in the old order, because a field is checked in an order and a surgeon trusts a sequence more than an impression. First the crust: one stiff leathery brown-edged barley crust two inches from the ear, gone harder and browner in the dry kiln heat, its corner standing a hair off the wool. It had stood two inches off for three hundred and seventy-five days and would stand two inches off today; the distance was not a wish and had no giver. Beside it the wool lay folded once in the empty place, pale in the dry light, exactly as a hand had left it on the fifteenth. Then the cold. It had come cheek, temple, the small bare joint of the wrist where the wool rode back, and it had stopped there as though it had come to the end of a line written out for it with neither an order to go farther nor an order to go away. The Afterdrop waited beyond all three, past a warming nobody in this house would perform. The thread of drool at his lip had frozen in the dry kiln heat and was unwiped, because nobody under any roof had ever been given an instruction about the thread and a basket set down is a pot set down and left.

He knew what the house had done without being asked and could not ask for it. A basket set level out of the draught where the heat came sideways was the careful rewarming he would have chosen for a patient who could not shiver. A cold patient cannot clot; he will seem better before he is worse; he warms at the extremities before the core, which is the wrong order and the dangerous one, and pink returning to a face is the surface still cold and not the core. The order in which heat comes back is the reverse of the order in which it goes, and it comes sooner than anybody likes. All of it was true and all of it was useless here, for the body in question was his own and the hands were the hands of an infant.

The infant wanted the crust lifted two inches, the thread wiped, and a hand on the pot handle. Both registers were true. The second was louder. Neither won, and the quarrel between them was the work; he had stopped expecting it to end, which was not the same as being used to it.

The test came with the light, as it had come on the day before. The wool was folded once in the empty place beside the crust, changed by nobody in the night. He took the interval apart by what was there and by what was not. There was no second fold, no third crust, no name spoken over the place, no gift (a gift wants a giver and nobody gave), no payment, no payer. No hand under this roof had laid the keeping across the shelf’s two gaps in the room whose floor does not touch this one, or across the gone pan’s round ring at the rack end, or across the field’s empty place out on the worked strips. He kept a finger between it and every other kept thing — between it and the pot-crack resting of Ash 9, between it and the hand-at-the-sill breath of Ash 14, between it and the holding Jorra left in the cold damper — so that no row came out even by his doing.

A small fear had come with the finding yesterday, that a second finding might make a habit. It did not stand up now. A hand that finds is not a hand that places, and a habit wants a hand that places twice. The wool had been put down once, by a hand that did not look at his face and said nothing over the place, and it had been found twice by a hand that only read it. Finding is the trade of a surgeon and placing is the trade of a payer, and no payer stood here. A second keeping would be a habit and a third a way, and both want a payer where none stands. This was neither. It sat on a place of its own beside the crust’s gap, gathered once, found where put and not made again, gathering interest against the far-off hand that was not his and had not come.

Under the boards the four nails kept their count and the thumb-width knot kept the place where a fifth might have been driven and was not; under the floor the carried fire gave four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have stood, its payer without a name three hundred and seventy-six days. Three counts in three places, and none rose into another, because agreeing any two would have wanted a payer put under both and no payer stood.

Sella Vail came to the hearth before she came to the basket, as she had at both ends of every day for three years. She told the coals it was the thaw and turned them with the back of a broken pot. “The house will keep it,” she said where another woman would have said I. She stood a moment counting a small intake, a hold and a letting-go toward no opening, and gave neither gap a figure, so that the count went into the room and not onto a slate. Her finger lay along the top of the loaf shelf’s front edge so the charcoal tally underneath stayed unread; her lips moved along a two-gapped row and figured neither gap; she glanced once at the clay jar standing cold under lead glaze and shut her hand half over nothing, the little-door burn on the back of her left hand closing on air. The cord of her widow’s mark was oiled where a working thumb had touched it ten thousand times, and the mark hung where it hung, untouched, because a morning and a hearth are not openings and seven openings in seven months are seven and not a rule and no eighth was made due.

On the pot board the twice-mended pot kept the hairline of the original break showing between the old-brass staple and the tighter wire. Her thumb went first where the wire crossed the crack before the weight came on the handle; both proverbs went to the pot and the fire and never to a person — “Leave the crack showing. People trust a thing they can see has been handled,” and “If it’s worth mending it’s worth mending twice. The second time is the real one.” They were improved in the keeping and very likely not as good as the man had said them. Repair stayed apart from restoration: a restored thing hides the hand that handled it and a mended thing leaves that hand showing. She turned the dressed grain under wool with a touch lighter than a woman would use on a sleeping child and lifted no figure and no handful, and set the pot rim two inches beyond the reaching hand with a half-beat and no look toward the bed, the half-beat the distance between intention and arrest, bought at nothing and not a gift.

The wider file was checked without standing up, as a man checks a field by its small sounds before he lets the light into it. Six hearings of the full note stood beside and never among, the sixth the small flat knock out of as the men lifted her, filed where a thing out of a person must go and never among the sources; the seventh place stayed empty and was not dug for or filled, and the flat knock of the Drawing stood on its own place outside them. The heard bell of the Long Night stayed in its square beside the told, four, four and a gap, and was not folded into any of them. Nine record-first hands kept no tenth — Hessa Rale’s eighth, Ilva’s ninth and the collector’s ordinary hand on its own tray joined to none — and nine stoppings kept no tenth before the seventh year at its very end. Ten arithmetics stood behind one unsigned figure — the three of Frost, the first, second and third of Bone, Ash’s first, second and third and Stone’s first — the figure read still not the figure paid, no signature on the space and no doctrine out of ten; their ten true subtractions rode in Berr Clay’s box up the clay road locked toward High Kiln, unread under this roof, the key gone up in a coat pocket and not coming back down. Four rooms kept the same gap in their count held apart with a finger between every pair, four likenesses and not one account, an account wanting a hand to lay them together and a payer under all four, and no such hand standing and no such payer.

No sixth hearing, seventh opening, sixth held object, tenth hand, tenth stopping, eleventh arithmetic or eighth opening was supplied to make a row come out even, for a finding filled to make a row come out even is a finding forged. The burned slate stood at five, the shut-room tellings at two, the row at five askings with no witness and the well-saying at four shapings, and none was made a sixth of on a day that had not earned one. The finding that ten are not one account sat on a place of its own with a finger between it and every other kept thing, and was not a grievance, a sign, a power or a lesson. The keeping at the fold sat beside all of these and was not laid across any of them.

Through the finger’s-width door the river kept its note of water over water, a low thing over a lower thing, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming; over it from bank to bank lay the winter skin that the cold had put on and that the cold would take off, found where put and not made again, not spent on this morning’s work and not made to buy a lesson under any roof. He kept the covering on its own place beside the note and put a finger between the covering and the note it muffled, so that no row came out even by his doing.

In the small hours the file came out on the towel at the head of the bed as instruments come out when a case is done, and the hand outside the wool came up an inch toward the low note under the door as a hand goes up in a field it has learned not to disturb. It did not reach, and the inch was not a reach. He took a small intake from the room whose floor does not touch his, held the interval whole on the note’s own finishing under the winter skin, let the intake go as the note ended and came straight down and laid itself flat against the fold as a man puts away a tool where the next hand will find it without looking. The note had done before the hand came down, so the letting-go was neither early nor stayed, and the hand did not stay.

He filed it as answer three hundred and twenty-seven: exact, complete, worth nothing at two inches of air, a restraint and not a payment borrowing no house count and supplying no payer, laid across neither the ten readings, the bell’s gap, the flat knock, the child’s grey fingertips, the mother’s cup, the covered grain, the handful, the thumb’s resting, the held breath, the two bites of iron, the river’s skin, the widow’s mark nor the keeping at the fold. Three hundred and twenty-seven honest answers stood on the sixth place and not one was a doctrine, for a doctrine is an account that has found a payer and none stood. The surgeon took his small clean satisfaction in a field taken as found and left as found, and it cost him nothing, which was how he knew it was not peace. The infant wanted the crust lifted two inches, the thread wiped and a hand on the pot handle. Both were true. The second was louder. Neither won, and the quarrel was the work.

The instruction went to the wool, to the river wind, to the fire she would bank against their coming back, and never to the basket. The boy had no use for the date and had never needed one. Three hundred and seventy-six days stood exact, six months and one hundred and ninety-six days told by nobody who would have known what a number counted, and the seventeenth of Stone went about its work in Low Kiln, Sill, Grath, under the Vail kiln-house roof and out in the Clay Yard. The carried fire’s fifth stood unpaid the same number with its payer unnamed, and Garl Vail’s name had not been spoken under that roof the same number. Nothing that had happened in the night crossed into the afternoon as a sign, a lesson, a mending, a cause, a payment or a payer. The yard went around the seven walked-around places with a look and a wide step and no number. The step was widest around the returning-line place, where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out, wider by nothing that could be measured and once and not twice, because a second going-around would have made a figure of once. At the Vail corner the clean dark line kept its finer grey hairline beside it, the thing the house called the thaw, unswept and thickened in the month’s dry cold with the faint sour tang of Ash coming up off it. Beneath the lowest rack the broken birth-slates lay entered by nobody. At the rack end the round ring where the shallow pan had once stood kept no scrape, no crumb, no print, no chip and no word.

The three coverings stayed weather entered nowhere, with a finger between every pair. The gone white frost off the north lip of the yard well, the skin drawn edge to edge across the trough, and the silt-clouded plate of ice on the flat stone by the well a little out of the path of feet were not a fourth covering and not a sixth low thing. Through the finger’s-width door the river kept its note of water over water, a low thing over a lower thing, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming; over it from bank to bank lay the winter skin that the cold had put on and that the cold would take off. It was found where put beside the note and on its own place, not spent on the afternoon’s work and not made to buy a lesson under any roof. He kept the covering on its own place and put a finger between the covering and the note it muffled, so that no row came out even by his doing.

In the afternoon Hessa Rale went up the clay road out of Low Kiln in the boy’s sight, at an hour when the road lay long ahead of her and no reading waited at its end. The small flat correction of the second strap came first, let out for the long walk, and the turn of the wrist brought the edge of the slate round; only after both did her eyes go to the yard. Her hand had gone to the slate before either. Berr Clay’s narrow slate lay edge-on to her own forearm with the writing turned toward her sleeve, the thumb along the grain of the frame and nothing pressing where the writing was. That was not concealment but the manner of carrying a slate so the writing was not rubbed and no goer-by took a figure off it without a hand to put there. Dried pale scurf of road-mud stood unbrushed on the skirt she had shortened for the road; a runner’s coat is a tool and nobody scours a tool for its own sake.

A woman waiting with a basket of kindling asked what she carried.

“A slate,” she said. “I don’t read it. I carry it. There’s a difference, and the road doesn’t care which.”

The woman wanted to know what was written on it.

“Ask the road. It knows the way and I know the weight. That’s the whole of what either of us knows.” She settled the second strap again, not for the woman and not for the yard, and went on at the pace of a person who has made a bargain with distance and keeps it without thinking. She gave no figure. She read nothing aloud. She did not look into the basket.

Sella carried him past at the half-pace and said nothing of the runner and gave the house no name for the slate.

The boy took the order of her reaching apart as a surgeon takes a field apart: hand to the record before hand to the person, eyes after the hand, mouth after the eyes, the flat correction of the strap in the gap where a lesser observer would put an intention and find none. He filed the turning of the writing toward herself on a place of its own beside the Ash 6 sighting, with a finger between. A runner going up the road with the writing turned toward herself was not a voice standing in a doorway and not a saying fulfilled. He made no fifth shaping of the Frost 14 well-saying, no witness, no reader and no payment, and joined her neither to Ilva’s strip, nor to Berr’s thumb, nor to the charcoal under the loaf shelf, nor to the soft-cuffed clerk’s inward slate, nor to any other private keeping. Her eighth record-first hand stayed eight with no tenth. She was a runner and not a reader, and the road did not care which. He did not make a reader of her early, and the far-off day she might choose to read what she had carried was not brought nearer by a single step of her walking.

At the kindling Tarn kept the account as he had kept it on the sixteenth. Six pieces stood in the worn thumb-mark where the house could check and the seventh lay at the lip beyond it, beside Garl’s unforged collector sentence, with no name on the man who had turned back at the frozen ford and no figure on the debt. The small knife lay at the very edge of its sill, where it could be checked and where a hand could also slip it off. The pale clip-ring at his left wrist caught what little light there was and gave it back. His chest had been tight since the dark, the Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line, and his stroke went over the flagstones’ shallow bright patch without the half-check.

“Six counted. The next I don’t count,” he said to the wood, and cut nothing, counted nothing and spent nothing.

The held breath he had left at the sill on Ash 14 was where it had been put, found where put and not made again; the Bone 26 thumb’s resting was likewise where it had been left. Both sat on places of their own beside the five held objects and the pot-crack resting, with a finger between every pair — not a sixth held object, not a seventh, not a tenth stopping, not the seventh year given, not the calling of the loan, and with no payer standing. To a woman with kindling asking what the seventh year would want, and to two fence-menders saying the thaw would be early and the thaw would be late, he said “Yes, house” to each and kept the late because a late thaw puts off the paying, agreeing so thoroughly that there was nowhere for an argument to find a foot. The hare-carrier down from Cold Acre answered the price with the weather that had carried the snow and could carry the price, and went off toward the clay road with the hare unweighed. The cracked-jar man in his rope sling was sent to a pot, and the pot, being a pot, said nothing.

The ear-sentence came up to the word that would call the loan and stopped there, the ninth stopping with no tenth before the seventh year at its very end, the Seed 16 breath unspent at the top of the reach and the loan uncalled. Two ears, two silences, a loan that can hear and has never been called, with nobody to enter it. He did not look along the road. A man who owns nothing keeps the exact account of what he is owed and what he owes, and the account stood where it stood without rising into a rule.

Jorra Vail came through the finger’s-width door off the Vail wheel-shed with the day’s meal-fine grey slate dust ground small as meal past the bent little finger and white in a fist-crease where a hand had opened, and it was not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel. Three or more men unnamed had gone low into the back room before first grey; he gave the house no name for them and no word that the room was cold. He kept his dusty forearms and his clean ones on one tray and parted them as clot from vessel, asking neither which slate had been broken nor whether it had been a birth-slate, while the broken ones lay under the lowest rack and were entered by nobody.

He spoke to the wheel before he spoke to any person. “It doesn’t care whose hands. It cares if they’re steady.” To a pot he said “You’ll do,” in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either, knowing the full note it would give three turns before it left his hands, with air under it and a want in it, and keeping the knowing where nobody could make a song of it, withheld from any master come up from High Kiln who would call it early and sing a pot before its time. The boy kept the knowing on a tray of its own beside the hearings and never among them, and no seventh hearing stood up in the empty place.

Sella had not finished telling him the fire was low when his bent finger went into the cold iron of the damper ahead of her sentence, took the cold out of the fingertip, moved the tongue a hair’s breadth and was left standing, so that the end of her instruction arrived at the pot and the household straightened around the waiting as cloth straightens when a weight is lifted. One piece of iron had two bites, the holding and the taking, and there was no Grathic word covering both mercies. He took the finger out without leaving the extra holding of Ash 26 a second time, gathered once on a place of its own and not made again, for a second would be a habit and a third a way and both want a payer and none stood. “He’s not Vail” stayed sealed as a lid nobody had tried to lift in three years, and the promise of the boy’s name to the men who burn slates was neither given nor withdrawn.
