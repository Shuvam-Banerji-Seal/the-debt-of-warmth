Chapter

14

Water Over Water

Days 61–67 · Silt 2–8, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, Sill

A man can count what the river leaves. No man has counted what it takes. So the yard keeps one word for both and goes home warm on it.

— A saying of the drying racks, Low Kiln

— Day 61 —

Silt 2, Y.H. 813

The brook had come up in the night behind the drying racks, and the clay came down with it heavier than it had come in Ilmen: brown water with a grit in it that set a man’s teeth on edge, a weight that dragged at the racks as though the boards themselves were being asked to carry more than they had been made for. Corin came into the Clay Yard in the basket under the fold, carried level as a pot filled is carried, with the wool at his ear and the cold already at his cheek. He was sixty-one days old. Two months gone and one day over. Nobody under the roof gave a figure for the days before the month changed its name, and he kept it exactly, as he had once counted the injured in a field before he put a hand into it. The count went on and could not be spent on a crust.

Under the work of the yard there was a sound that had not been there in Ilmen. It came from behind the racks where the brook was going over its own rise: water over water, a low continual turning with no vessel in it that he could find, the note water makes when it has more of itself than the channel will carry. It was not the early note of the Singing Sill, felt in the teeth before the ear placed it; it was not the bell told in the dark of the Long Night, nor the bell heard at the Cross, which went four, four and a gap with nobody crossing himself; it was not the breath under his own roof, which missed one in every four and kept the missing place as carefully as the breaths that came. This was the river’s own note, and the yard had no word for it that a person could go home warm on. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward it and came down. Nothing within two paces could be reached, and the sound went on without him.

Sella Vail set the basket at the rack’s edge out of the draught, the way a pot is set where the fire will not take it sideways, and gave the instruction to the wool. “Keep it off his ears,” she said. “It’s the river wind.” She did not say it to the basket. The little-door burn on the back of her hand closed on nothing and opened again as she settled the fold. A slate lay propped at the end of the rack with its writing open; she went around it at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and came back to the fire without having looked into the writing.

“The brook’s up,” a rack-woman said, with her skirts gathered and her hands wet to the wrist. “That’ll come through the yard before the month’s out.”

“It’ll go when it goes,” Sella said to the fire. “It’s the thaw.” She fed the fire from the house’s dish and did not touch the widow’s mark at her throat, because a rack was not an opening and a basket was not a door. The mark hung where it always hung on its cord, a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and her hand went past it to the pot as though it were a nail in the wall. She said the house where another woman would have said I, and gave the next instruction to the bucket and the last one to the fire.

The body went on with its small humiliations while she worked. A thread of drool froze at his mouth in the first of the day’s cold and nobody wiped it, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left; his head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far; the cold came in its known order, cheek, then temple, then wrist, and the wool did less than the wind wanted. He wanted the bucket and could not reach it. He wanted the number of days spoken aloud by somebody who would know what it counted, and nobody in the yard had any use for it.

The women worked with their sleeves pushed to the elbow, laying wet pots along the boards and turning each one a quarter turn so the slip would not run to one side; the yard smelled of wet clay, tallow smoke and the river coming up through the reeds. A rack-woman set a basket down out of the draught and went back to her tubs, and the boy, who had once stood in a bay where twenty people arrived at once and sorted them without being told, noticed that the yard sorted itself the same way: what could be worked went on being worked, what could be carried was carried, and what could only be waited on was left where it lay and called weather. He had no hands for any of the three.

A second woman came up from the tubs and said the month had come early; the first said it came as it always came. Both said it standing over the pots, and neither gave a figure for what had risen behind them, because the yard had one word for a thing a person could be gone home warm on and the word was weather. The boy laid the two sentences side by side as he had laid others in other months, and left the question of which was true where it stood. He had no Grathic word for the brown weight going over itself behind the boards. If he had had one he could not have said it.

Beyond the racks the the Ilm ran under a skin so thin that the dark current showed in veins, and the water going over water came up through the wicker of the basket and stayed there. Sixty-one days, and the only exact thing in the yard was the one he could not spend.

At the middle of the day Sella carried him down under the north bridge to the Fishstairs and set the basket in the shade at the outer edge of the trade, where a pot might be set to keep the wind off it. The steps went down four, four and a landing where a step should have been, and she went down with the basket level and came up no other way. A soft-cuffed clerk sat at the edge of the landing with a slate held to his forearm, his cuff gone grey from washing. His hand went to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls, and he did not speak all the while they were there. He was not the woman in the coat at the flare light, nor the grey-fingered man who had given at the Drawing, nor the soft cuff behind the Court’s table. The boy set him on a place of his own and let four places stay four.

Mira Silt worked the eels at the outer boards with pale slime dried on her sleeves and river silt in the hollow of her neck, her head held a little to the right where the top of her left ear had been. The small smile stayed on her face the way a door stays on the latch. A weir woman asked whether the run would come now the month had turned, and Mira answered the question and then her own: “Does it come now? It comes when it comes. Ask the river.” While she decided she moved eel-bones against her thumb, one and then another, and when the deciding was done she put one away in her pocket, brisk, without having looked into the basket once.

The bridge kept its shade on the boards past the middle of the day and the eel slime in it dried pale on Mira’s sleeves; below the landing the water slapped the stones with the same brown weight it had behind the racks, and a brine-gull cried once and went upriver without taking anything. The boy lay with the cold at his cheek and watched the order of it: the woman who kept him did not look into the basket, and the man who would price him did not look into it either, and the two not-lookings were opposite things that stood on separate places in his keeping.

A buyer said a figure for the pair. It went over the basket and into the noise of the stalls and was gone, a number no one in the yard would have carried home in a fold, and the boy did not try to keep it. Mira’s thumb went under her apron to the grey wool cord at her waist and stayed there while she decided, and the shifting of a creel showed the second cord twisted twice beneath it, never carried upriver to Nine Pots, never lifted at a door, the knots on neither counted by any thumb but her own. Her board lay beside Orra Weir’s knot-string on the boards under the bridge and did not touch it, and the shape of the double turn was legible to him without a name in any tongue he could speak.

Berr Clay came up short at the edge of the shade, as though the ground had told him to stop there. The red wool cord of his office was folded under his coat, the green of tasted glaze sat at one corner of his mouth, and hot clay came off him over the smell of nothing at all, which is the most expensive smell in Grath. He wiped his thumb twice on his apron before touching nothing and held a narrow slate unopened against his forearm, passing a finger over what was written and letting his eyes rest on it a breath longer than the writing required.

“A number goes into a slate, not into a well,” he said. “If the house wants it again, there’ll be a slate at the watch.” He took himself out of his own sentence as neatly as a hand out of a field before the dressing goes on, and went off toward High Kiln with his box still locked in his house and the true deduction inside it. He did not look into the basket.

The boy laid the figure, the cord and the unopened slate in three places that did not touch. The eel price was a sound in a market; the twist in the wool was a shape he could read and not answer; the slate was a thing a man held before he looked at a person. Three orders of reaching, none of them a cause he could write across the others. The clerk’s hand on his slate went down a fourth time that he had seen. The brook’s note went on under all of it, lower than the talking and not one of them.

❦

At dusk Sella lifted the basket level and carried him up the Clay Yard at a half-pace from every open slate. Under the Vail kiln-house roof she set him on his bed and not in the Debt Loft, and put the fold right without looking at his face. Garl Vail was kept as he had been kept for two years: by the salt-stiff coat holding a broader man’s shape on its peg, the hollow seat keeping its twenty-year lean, the clean dark line where the Ash ridge had turned its corner and been burned and called dealt with, and the new hairline of grey that had gathered beside it in the night and lay there still because the house said it was the thaw. The carried fire lay under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees, and the kiln’s breath went four, four and a gap through the floor. There had been no opening that day and the mark stayed untouched.

He put water-over-water on a place of its own. Beside it, but never on it, lay the early note from the Sill felt in the teeth, the told bell of the Long Night, the heard bell at the Cross, and the four-four-and-a-gap under the roof — four low sounds in four places, and now a fifth thing that was not a sound any vessel had given and not a hearing. Six hearings of the full note stayed six, five from four sources and the sixth out of a woman the brook had taken twenty-three mornings ago, filed beside the five and never among them; no seventh had come. The two nearly-full answers stayed advanced and not paid. The word stayed a saying on its own tray with the readable shape of a double turn beside it and the weight of a thumb on wicker apart from both. He wrote no cause across any of them. A cause written too early was a thing he had once watched a whole institution sign, with the note beside it unread.

Nothing in the room had been joined. The grey beside the clean dark line was weather; the low sound through the door was weather; the clerk at the steps was a man with a slate; the board at the Weir was a board trodden by nobody; the early note, the told bell and the heard bell were three things in three places. The district had one word that let a person go home warm, and the boy had no word at all and could not go anywhere. He kept the count as the one entry in any room that nobody else had thought to enter, and the gap stayed the part nobody had thought to look at.

One crust lay in the fold where two had lain, brown-edged and stiff past smelling, and the empty place beside it went through another day without a name. He had wanted the breast in the first days and wanted the crust now and could not reach either, and he was the only adult in every room he had ever lain in and the least use in any of them. The door stood a finger’s width open. Through the gap came the low new sound of the brook going over itself behind the racks, and through the floor came the fire’s missing breath, and the two kept their separate places as the told bell, the heard bell, the early note and the gap had kept theirs for two months. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the water and came down. The count went on.

— Day 62 —

Silt 3, Y.H. 813

Before light came up over the slip pits Tarn had set the small bench knife on its sill and told the empty yard where it lay. “It’s back. You can check.” He said it to the sill and the boards and whatever part of the yard might be thought to answer, and nothing answered, as nothing had answered for six years. The knife lay with its handle uppermost, the mark on it worn almost flat by a thumb that had scraped the Ash out of the same creases every morning of a term no man had asked him to keep. A man who owned nothing should know where the small things are, and Tarn knew where this one was even in the dark, with his eyes shut in the loft above the shed. Before he swept he told the kindling what he told it every morning: six were counted, and the next he did not count.

He held the pale clip-ring at his left wrist up to the little light there was and gave it back. The ring had sat in his skin since Y.H. 806, when the iron went on and he was bound to the house, and the place under it held what light the morning had the way a cup holds a little water. Six years. The seventh was nearly gone and he did not count it. It was the one entry in this house the house could not check, and he kept it where he kept the small knife, in a place nobody entered. He said *I owe* where another man would have said *I am*, and said it to the floor, and the floor took it as it took everything else.

Corin came into the Clay Yard in the basket under the fold at the half-pace Sella kept from every open slate, carried level as a pot filled is carried. He was sixty-two days old. Two months and two days, and nobody under the roof gave a figure for the days before the month changed its name, so he kept the count himself, as he had once counted the injured in a field before he put a hand into it. The count went on and could not be spent. Cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, and the wool did less than the wind wanted; the thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody wiped it, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left.

The yard argued the thaw as it argued most things, standing over the pots with their sleeves to the elbow. One woman wanted it early and another wanted it late, and both said it as though the weather had been asked and had given a proper answer. The first said it would break before its time, as it had the year before; the second said it would hold. Neither gave a figure for what had risen behind them, because the yard had one word for a thing a person could go home warm on, and the word was weather.

“Yes, house,” Tarn said to the first, and “Yes, house,” to the second, and agreed so entirely that there was nowhere for an argument to put its foot. He went on wanting the late one all the same. A late thaw put off the paying, and a man who has spent six years putting off a payment knows the exact weight of each day’s reprieve. The wanting had an edge to it this morning. the Slip Brook ran higher behind the drying racks than it had run yesterday, brown water gritted with clay, dragging at the racks as though the boards were being asked to carry more than they had been made for, and the water had a note in it that went over itself without stopping. The paying came with the water whether a man wanted it early or late. Tarn swept toward the racks with his head down and said nothing about the note, and when Sella told him he had done well he stopped altogether for two breaths before he said “Yes, house” and went on.

By mid-morning the yard was laying wet pots along the boards and turning each one a quarter turn so the slip would not run to one side, and the boy lay at the rack’s edge where the basket had been set out of the draught. Tarn’s broom went over the trodden ash in long level strokes. At the place where a pot had cracked all the way through on Stone 25 and been called a pot gone wrong and swept around, the broom checked half a stroke. The yard had gone around the place ever since and entered it into nothing; the small flat knock had been a sound for one breath and an empty spot in the boards afterward, and Tarn swept the empty spot as he swept the rest. It checked and went on. He did not turn his head. He did not name the small flat sound the pot had made two months before, and he did not supply a Grathic word for it, and he said nothing at all to the boards.

The boy kept that check as a fact on a place of its own. It was the third interval, and it lay on its own tray with the intervals of Stone 13 and Stone 17 — objects, not hearings, silences where a note had been expected and had not come — kept apart from the tray of near-full answers and apart again from the tray of hearings. Six hearings of the full note stayed six: five from four sources, a cup across a birth room, a pot through a shed wall, the district’s slate twice at the Cross, a man giving at the Drawing; and the sixth, the small flat knock out of a woman the brook had taken, from a person, filed beside the five and never among them. No seventh came into the yard that morning. He did not call the cracked-place check a hearing, and he did not call Tarn’s silence an answer, and he did not let either tray touch another.

A vessel gave its note past the yard door as a woman carried it by, and Tarn’s head did not turn. It was the same note he had heard through the wall on other mornings and had never called a note; the woman went on toward the racks without knowing she had carried a thing worth more than a day’s wage past the only two ears under the roof that would have known what it was. The boy watched the not-turning from under the fold. Two ears kept the same silence under one roof, and the loan that could hear stayed uncalled; the sentence that would have called it lay under the day where it had always lain, unspoken by the man who owned the ear and unspoken by the man who owned the house’s account. A loan that can hear is a loan that can be called, and neither man said it, and the yard went on turning its pots as though nothing had been carried past the door at all.

Jorra Vail came up from the wheel-shed with slip dried to the colour of old bone on both forearms and no grey worked past the bend of his little finger, and went to the damper with that bent finger ahead of his hand. Sella had not finished telling him the fire was low; the end of her sentence arrived at the pot instead of at him, and the household straightened around his silence without an order. He told the wheel it did not care whose hands worked it, only if they were steady, and told a pot it would do, in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either. He did not look toward the basket.

Under the Vail kiln-house roof the day went the way the days went. Sella fed the fire from the house’s dish and told it it was the thaw. She gave every instruction to the fire and the wool and the bucket and never to the basket, and said *the house* where another woman would have said I. Garl Vail was kept as he had been kept for two years: the salt-stiff coat holding a broader man’s shape on its peg behind the door, the hollow seat in the shed keeping its twenty-year lean that nobody filled, the clean dark line where the Ash ridge had turned its corner and been burned and called dealt with, and the new hairline of grey that had gathered beside it and lay there still, unswept, because the house said it was the thaw. A barrow of the same grey had gone out toward the Bone Orchard at the last market and nobody looked twice, and the clean dark line was not swept, because a line left in place is still a line kept. His proverb about leaving the crack showing went to a pot with no crack in it, said over the corner where the repair had been rubbed out, and cost more now than it had when he was alive to say it. The carried fire lay under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees, and the kiln’s breath went four, four and a gap through the floor, keeping the missing breath as carefully as the four that came.

The body went on with its small humiliations while the count went on in the part of him that had nothing to do with the body. A thread of drool froze at his mouth and was not wiped; his head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far; cold arrived at cheek and temple and wrist and stayed longest at the wrist. He had once reduced a thousand shoulders in a room kept warm by other people’s money, and he could not keep a blanket on his own chest. He had wanted the breast in the first days and wanted the crust now, and the two wants were the same want wearing different clothes; the adult in him called it a failing of the field and the child in him called it hunger, and the hunger was louder. He wanted the crust in the fold and could not reach it, and he wanted the number of his days spoken by somebody who would know what it counted, and nobody under the roof had any use for it. He had no word in the district’s tongue for the note in the water, and if he had had one he could not have said it.

At dusk Sella lifted the basket level and carried him up the yard at a half-pace from every open slate, and set him on his bed under the roof and not in the Debt Loft, and put the fold right without looking at his face. One brown-edged crust lay in the fold where two had lain, stiff past smelling of anything, and the empty place beside it went through another day without a name. There had been no opening that day and the widow’s mark at her throat stayed where it hung. Six hearings stayed six and no seventh had come; three near-full answers stayed advanced and not paid; the word stayed a saying on its own tray with the readable shape of the double turn beside it and the weight of a thumb on wicker apart from both; the three intervals stayed objects; and the new grey lay beside the clean dark line and was called thaw. He wrote no cause across any of them. A cause written too early was a thing he had once watched a whole institution sign, with the note beside it unread. The door stood a finger’s width open, and through the gap came the low note of the water going over itself behind the racks, and through the floor came the fire’s missing breath, and the two kept their separate places as they had kept them for two months.

Out in the yard Tarn’s broom went once more over the place where the pot had cracked and checked half a stroke at the empty air where it had stood, and then went on. Under the roof the boy’s hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth and came down. The count went on.

The wheel had been turning since before he was lifted out of the basket, and the back room off the shed had kept its finger’s width of open all morning. Jorra Vail came through that gap with fine grey slate dust worked into the creases of both forearms past the bend of his little finger, and the dust was not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel. It lay in the seams of his skin the way the Ash lay in a corner nobody swept, grey and fine and particular, and he said nothing about where he had been. Two men had come into that room since first light and spoken low; the boy had heard the sound of them through the wall and had no names to set against the voices, and the house kept no roll in which to enter them. They went out again the way such men go, without being spoken of, and the dust came back into the shed on the wheel-keeper’s forearms as though a wind had carried it there.

Corin lay at the edge of the drying racks in the basket under the fold, carried level as a pot filled is carried, and he was sixty-three days old. Nobody under the roof gave a figure for the days before the month changed its name, so he kept the count himself, as he had once counted the injured in a field before he put a hand into it, and the count went on and could not be spent. Cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, and the wool did less than the wind wanted; the thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody wiped it, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left. From the low angle he could see the two states of the same forearms: grey dust in the creases this morning, old bone on other mornings, both of them Jorra’s, both of them one man’s having been in one place or another. He set them on the same tray as two entries and did not let one become an account of the other. He had once read a resident’s hands before the resident opened his mouth — a smear at the cuff, a habit of wiping on a coat tail, the thing a man had been holding a moment before he was asked — and knew that hands told the truth about a day that a face would arrange differently. The question of which slate had been broken stayed where it was. The question of whether it had been a birth-slate stayed beside it. Neither was asked. Questions could last for years in this house; they had lasted for years already.

Under the lowest rack was the space where whole slate should have stood. The yard went around it with pots and buckets and brooms, around it as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it over, and entered it into nothing. Two years of broken slates lay buried there and no slate above ground gave them back. The boy watched the going-around from under the fold and kept the space as an empty place on its own tray, apart from the dusty forearms and apart from the clean ones, and wrote no cause between them. A repair that pretends the break never happened was the district’s ordinary kindness, and it had a shape a man could learn to see: the stool shifted an inch, the broom stroke shortened, the word that stopped before it reached the thing it named.

In the Vail wheel-shed Jorra spoke to the wheel before he spoke to anyone. “It doesn’t care whose hands,” he said, in the flat voice that went to the clay and the iron before it went to a person. “It cares if they’re steady.” He set a pot on the wheel and told it, “You’ll do,” with no welcome in the sentence and no unkindness either, the way a man might tell a tool it would serve. The wheel took up its rhythm again, damp clay and the sweetish edge of lead glaze coming off it on a bad wind from High Kiln, and the house went the way a house goes when someone who knows what he is doing has begun to do it.

The boy knew a thing about that wheel which nobody had told him and which he could not have said aloud. The full note was in a Vail pot before it left Jorra’s hands; he had heard it through the wall on mornings when no one tapped anything, and Jorra, who could feel the note in the turning clay before it was a sound, would not have said so in front of a kiln-master. A kiln-master would have called it early and made something of it, and a thing made something of in this district acquired a figure, and the figure went into a slate. So the knowing stayed shut in the wheel-keeper as it stayed shut in the boy, two keepings under one roof with no word between them. Six hearings of the full note stayed six: five from four sources and one out of a person, filed beside and never among, and no seventh came into the shed that morning. Knowing what the pot would give before it gave it had bought him nothing he could lift a hand to. He kept Jorra’s knowing beside his own on a place where neither touched the other, as he kept the dust beside the slip and the clean dark line beside the new grey.

Sella had not finished telling him the fire was low. “The fire’s low, Jorra. Don’t set that wet — ” The bent finger went in ahead of his hand at the damper and moved it, and the end of her sentence arrived at the pot instead of at him. She shut her mouth on the instruction and gave the rest of it to the clay. The household straightened around his silence without an order being given, the way a room straightens when a man who has been patient has stopped being patient and nobody wishes to find out what he will do next. Iron took the bend of the finger first, then the whole hand; the boy watched the order of it from under the fold, the way he had once watched which instrument went into a field before the hand went in after it. A man’s anger in this house did not raise its voice. It moved a damper a half-turn early and let the sentence go where it would.

Tarn came to the shed end with his broom and stood there a moment. the Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line where the ridge had turned its corner, and the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light the morning had and gave it back. He said “Yes, house” to whatever the household had said to him, and did not look at the dusty forearms and did not look into the basket, and went back to his sweeping. He had once been sent for a collector and had come back without one, and the thing he carried for two years was the sentence Sella had not said. Two ears kept the same silence under the same roof. The loan that could hear was still a loan that could be called, and the sentence that would call it lay where it had always lain, unspoken by the man who owned the ear and unspoken by the man who owned the house’s account. The boy set Tarn’s not-looking on a tray of its own beside Jorra’s not-saying and let the two lie near without touching.

Jorra did not say the back room was cold. He had said it once, years ago, to nobody in particular, shaped so that nobody could take it further, and Sella had shifted the damper instead of answering, and the house had learned from that hour what it was allowed to hear. He did not look toward the bed where the foundling lay and did not look toward the basket. *He’s not Vail* stayed sealed as tightly as a lid; it had been sealed on the hour it was spoken and nothing in three years had lifted it, not the taking in, not the bed under the roof, not the wheel turning with a boy who was not the wheel-keeper’s to keep. The promise of a slate with the foundling’s name on it, made to the men who came into the room and spoke low, stayed where it had always stayed, ungiven and not withdrawn. A boy with no house-name was a coat anybody could wear; he had said as much in another room and had not said it here. The nearer the wheel’s hollow seat came to being filled by his own weight, the tighter the sentence about the foundling closed, until it held more than a name could have held and would not let go. The carried boy went on under the roof unwritten, and the house went on around him as though that were the whole of the arrangement, which it was, and as though it cost nothing, which it did.

The carried fire lay under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees, and the kiln’s breath went four, four and a gap through the floor, keeping the missing breath as carefully as the four that came.

At dusk Sella carried him up the yard at a half-pace from every open slate and set him on his bed and not in the Debt Loft. She put the fold right without looking at his face. One brown-edged crust lay where two had lain, stiff past smelling of anything, and the empty place beside it went through another day without a name. He wanted the crust and wanted the breast and got neither; the adult in him called the wanting a failing of the field and the child in him called it hunger, and the hunger was louder. The wool had ridden back from his wrist and the cold found the place, and he wanted to pull it up and could not. The dry crust pressed at his ear with the stubbornness of a thing put there by somebody else and left to stay until somebody else decided otherwise. He had once stood at the head of a table and decided which bodies had already gone beyond help. Here he lay under a roof and could not lift a hand to the one thing in the world that was two inches from his ear.

The door stood a finger’s width open. Through the gap came the low note of the river moving under the ice, and through the floor came the fire, four, four and a gap, and the two kept their separate places as they had kept them for two months. In the corner the clean dark line where the Ash ridge had turned its corner lay where it had lain for a year, and the new hairline of grey beside it lay there still, unswept, because the house said it was the thaw and would go when it goes.

The low note came up through the boards beneath his basket, the same low thing the boards had kept all year; and the boards kept a count of their own above it, four nails and four and a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts did not meet, coming as they did from two different places under one roof. He called the note nothing. To call it anything would be to write a cause across four separate places: the told bell in the dark, the heard bell in the square, the kiln’s gap through the floor, the early note out of the stone under the falls. Four, in four places, with no cause written across any of them, and the word that would have joined them left where it lay. The count went on and could not be spent. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the sound and came down.

First light came thin and the colour of dishwater over the yard well, and the stone lip of it was wet where the day before it had been dry. Mud had got into the rope. It was ground into the twist of it, the colour of old rope gone dark, and it came up under the fingernails of every woman who drew and went back down again with the bucket, so that the well drew mud out of itself and drank it again in the same motion. Corin was carried down in the basket under the fold and set in the little roof’s thin shade, level as a pot filled that must not be slopped, where the shadow of the well-house lay across him and the draught went the other way. Sella Vail turned the basket with her knee until his face was out of the wind, then gave every instruction to the bucket and the wool and the fire at the far end of the Clay Yard, and never once to the basket. “There’s mud in it,” she said to the bucket. “Rinse it. Keep it off him,” she said to the wool, “it’s the river wind.” There had been no opening that morning, and the widow’s mark at her throat hung where it hung and was not touched. She said the house where another woman would have said I, and went to the bucket-round with her burn shut on nothing.

The women spoke of the skin on the water to the water and to the bucket and never to the boy under the fold. One of them, letting the bucket down on a rope that had mud in it to the last strand, said the skin had thickened overnight and would hold a poured spoon. Another said it would not hold a spoon and came up anyway, and the two of them went on with the drawing as if the thing had been settled. A man come back from looking at the ice stood with his hands in his armpits and told it to the boards and to the well-roof and to whoever stood within hearing, the way a man says a thing he does not want owned.

“It’s honeyed all through,” he said. “Not just at the edge. All through. You could cut it and find it yellow in the middle.”

“Weather,” a woman said from the tubs, and the man nodded as though she had given him an answer, and went off toward the road.

Nobody joined it to any house. The boy lay under the fold and took the words in as he took everything: as objects to be set down, not as a cause to be written across anything. Honeyed was a word the district had had for a month for the taste in the water and the colour at the edge of the ice, and now the same word had gone all the way through the sheet and meant the same thing it had meant before, which was nothing a person had to own. A man had said it to the boards. The boards had no house in them. He set the saying down on the place where he kept the early note and the told bell and the heard bell, and he wrote no cause across any of them, and the word bought nobody a figure.

The argument went the way it always went. A man come up from the brook said the month had come early, meaning the thaw, meaning the levy that fell in it, meaning the figure that nobody said aloud; and a woman at the tubs said it came as it always came. Neither gave a number. The man said the ice had gone soft before its hour and the woman said it had not, and both of them wanted the late one in the end, because a late thaw puts off the paying, and they went home over it warm. The boy had learned what that warmth was for. It was not agreement. It was permission to stop looking.

He lay in the well’s thin shade and kept the count. Sixty-seven days. The number sat in him with the weight of a small stone he could neither set down nor lift, and he had no one to give it to and no word in Grathic in which to give it even if he had. Cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, and the wool did less than the wind wanted. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody wiped it, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far. The bucket stood on the wall of the well within a hand’s reach of him — a thing with water in it, a thing with a lip and a rope — and he wanted the bucket and wanted the brown crust in the fold beside his ear, and he could reach neither. He had once stood at the head of a table and decided which bodies had already gone beyond help. Here he lay in the shade of a well and could not lift a hand two inches to the water.

The adult in him called the wanting a failing of the field and read the cold in his own wrists the way he had once read it in somebody else’s, the small vessel going at the extremities before the core admitted to it, the afterdrop waiting on the far side of any warming. The child in him was hungry and cold and wanted the breast. Both were true and the second was louder.

Later Sella carried him out beyond the drying racks to the edge of the Ilm and set the basket where the boards gave a lee out of the draught, turning it with her knee the way she would turn a pot so the light fell where she wanted it. The racks stood behind him with slip drying on them in the colours of old bone, and the Slip Brook went clay-heavy somewhere out of sight, and the river was in front of him under a skin so thin that the dark current showed through it in veins. He had never seen the current show through before except as a rumour in words. Now it lay under him in long dark lines that moved without any wind moving them, the way a bruise moves under thin skin when the light changes, and the ice above it was not white any more but the colour of grey slip-water standing in a shallow dish. The man’s word came back to him with the sight of it. Honeyed all through. Not just at the edge. There was no edge to be seen on the water in front of him, only the veins going under and the skin over them, and the skin was thinner here than it had been three doors down at the well.

More hairlines had run into the ice since the last time he was set here. They came in from the bank and went a little way and stopped a thumb’s breadth short of the open water, every one of them stopping at the same distance as though a hand had drawn them and lifted before the last stroke. He kept them where he kept the other stopping hairlines, on a place of their own, and did not let them touch the widening of the day before or the honeyed-all-through saying or the early note out of the stone under the falls. Six hearings of the full note stayed six: five from four sources and one out of a person, filed beside the five and never among them, and no seventh had come. Three answers stood on the near-full tray with air under the note and a want in it, advanced and not paid, and nobody had tapped a pot to ask any of them to speak again. The clerk was still set at the Fishstairs with a slate for a measure nobody had called, and the weak board in the plank walk was still there and trodden by nobody. A weak board was a gap in a place that would one day have to carry weight; a missing step was a gap in a flight of stairs; a hairline stopping short was a gap in a sheet of ice. Three gaps in three places, kept apart. He set the widening hairlines beside them and still let nothing touch. A likeness was not a cause. He knew what a cause written too early would do to four honest witnesses, and he wrote none across any of them.

The Chronicle of Chapter 14

For seven days the Slip Brook went over itself behind the racks with a weight no one could name, and the boy carried under the fold heard the river’s own note and could not reach it. He was sixty-one days old on the first morning and sixty-seven on the last; nobody gave a figure, so he kept it himself. The yard gave the rising water one word --- weather --- and went home warm on it. Sella Vail said it was the thaw, gave every instruction to the fire, the wool and the bucket and never to the basket, and touched the widow’s mark only before an opening.

At the Fishstairs Mira Silt answered a question with her own answer, moved eel-bones while deciding and put her thumb to the grey cord when a figure was spoken, showing a second cord twisted twice beneath it, never to Nine Pots or a door, neither knot counted. Berr Clay wiped his thumb twice, said a number goes into a slate, not into a well, and took himself out of his own sentence; the soft-cuffed clerk put hand to slate before eyes to the stalls, silent. Tarn set his knife on its sill, six years counted and the seventh held back, wanting the late thaw, because it puts off the paying; his broom checked where a pot had cracked, and his head did not turn at a vessel’s note.

Jorra Vail came from the room off the wheel-shed with slate dust past his bent finger, moved the damper ahead of Sella’s sentence and did not look toward the bed. Three doors down Alda Reed laid her birth-slates writing-down before turning to the fold, touched the tin in her bodice unopened, crossed two fingers at the flower and covered the sign with the date she had given Dessa, saying warm and breathing, exact and not true; then she wrote what she would not leave lying and turned the slate toward a floorboard in a house the boy could not name. Five pages became six, read by nobody.

A pot in the yard answered nearly full under nobody’s knuckle, a third answer beside Ilmen 6 and Ilmen 23; six hearings stayed six, five from four sources and one from a person, no seventh. The Singing Sill sang early; a woman who said water ran fast under the ice said weather and went home. Word came that the weak board was trodden by nobody and the clerk still set. On the last day the ice was honeyed all through, the current showed in veins, mud in the rope; he wanted the bucket and crust and could reach neither. At dusk Sella set him on his bed, one crust in the fold and the empty place unnamed; the door stood a finger’s width open, the river’s note through the gap and the fire’s gap through the floor kept apart. Nothing joined, paid or explained. His hand went up an inch toward the water and came down.
