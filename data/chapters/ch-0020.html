The Shelf Keeps Nothing

Days 94–100 · Seed 5–11, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, Sill

Two gaps on a shelf are two gaps till a finger lays them together. After that they are an account, and an account wants a name. Keep your finger off the shelf.

— A saying of the loaf-shelves of Low Kiln

— Day 94 —

Seed 5, Y.H. 813

The broom went around the stone and not over it, the bristles setting into the brown skin the water had left and coming up with a small dry rasp, the way a hand goes round a sleeping animal and does not wake it. The stone was the size of a man’s fist and had been trodden into the mud before the mud set, so that one side of it showed and the rest lay buried, and the woman with the broom pushed the bristles around it twice and then stood a moment longer than the stone required, looking at the ground where it sat. The light came up thin through the barley dust out on the barley fields east of Low Kiln, and every stalk that had been beaten down stood at its own angle, and the strips went away in long brown ranks with the seed already in them under a skin of silt. Nothing in the field was counted aloud. A woman’s fist opened over a strip and let seed go, closed on the empty air above it, and came back with chaff in the creases.

Sella Vail gave the broom its instruction before anybody spoke. “Round it,” she said to the bristles, to the stone, to the brown skin holding the boot-print. “It cost enough to come in. It’ll cost more to take out.” She said it low, without looking up, the way another woman might have said a grace over a table, and the woman with the broom went on around the stone and did not answer, because the instruction had been given to the broom and not to her and she had not taken it. Sella carried Corin at the edge of the work in a basket under a fold of wool, going along the strip at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, and she gave the basket no instruction at all. A basket set down is a pot set down and left, and a pot set down is not told what it is holding. When a woman at the next strip asked whose child it was, Sella said the house had brought him, which was an answer and not an answer; the little-door burn on the back of her hand closed on the basket’s edge and then on nothing, because her hands had already gone back to the work.

The man with the barrow came up the thin track between the strips with the barrow empty and one wheel running askew, and said to nobody in particular that the strips would want hands before the week was out. The woman with the broom, not looking up from the stone, said the weather would have the last word. Between the two sayings lay the whole field, worked and waiting; hands went around as a word goes around, strips lay where they lay, and the seed had gone out of the fists into the ground without becoming a number by being covered. The boy kept the count because it was the only work available to him. He had no voice to spend on it and nobody had asked him for it.

From the low angle of the basket he watched the woman’s fist come up empty. There had been a figure in it a moment before, a small measured amount the hand had known without counting, and now there was chaff and the shape of the giving left in the creases. He read the emptied fist the way he had once read a chart closed with a neat line drawn across the figures and a signature at the foot to make the two agree: a thing entered, a thing paid, both of them accounted for, and nobody in the room the wiser for what the line had covered. It was a clean reading. It was worth nothing. He had once reduced a shoulder in the dark and now could not lift his own hand off the wool; here was a whole worked strip accounted for in fists and looks, and the account would never be asked of him, and if it were he would have no word in Grathic to give it and no hand with which to set it down.

The body interrupted the reading in its known order. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had been given an instruction about the thread, so it lay stiff against his chin and did not go away. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and the woman at the strip did not look up, because a head that goes where it wants is a thing a basket is used to. Cold came at cheek and then temple and then the bare place at the wrist where the wool rode back; the hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the low note of water beyond the fields and came down again. He was the only adult in the field and the least use in it. He knew the order in which warmth is given out and the order in which a body loses it, and both orders ran perfectly in a body he could not turn over.

The field went on around the basket without asking anything of it. The woman with the fist moved down the strip and the chaff in her creases brushed off against her skirt, and the bristles went over the last of the seed and covered it, so that the ground looked as though nothing had been done there all morning except a day’s work done and gone. He set ninety-four edge to edge against the arithmetic he knew was waiting under the roof: loaves pushed along a shelf, a glance at the clay jar, the space at the row’s end that nobody called a figure. None of those things would give him the number. They were the only arithmetic in Low Kiln and they kept nothing exactly. He kept ninety-four exactly and had no place to put it, which was the whole of his dignity and the whole of his use.

By midday the light had thickened and they had come back into the Clay Yard, and Sella set the basket at the edge of the yard well with the cold iron of the handle two inches beyond his hand. She drew with her free hand and gave the bucket its instruction before she gave one to the women standing round: go down easy, come up level, leave the lip as you found it. The women went on with their talk. One of them, with wet sleeves pushed above the elbow and her hair plastered at the temple, asked what the planting would cost now that the seed was in the ground, as though a figure might rise out of the mud if it were asked for in the right place.

Sella moved her lips along the loaf shelf in the kiln-house doorway, counting what was there in the only arithmetic the house kept, and glanced at the clay jar on the wall shelf. The space at the end of the row she left where it was, with no figure put to it and no name. Then she looked at the woman. “Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there,” she said. “The house has said it.”

The woman looked at the pot, which had nothing to say, and went back to her bucket. No figure had passed between them. The question had gone into the yard and come back out as weather and a proverb, and the seed stood in the strips east of the town with its cost still unspoken. The boy lay in the fold at the edge of the well with the iron two inches from his knuckles and wanted the number of his days said aloud by somebody who would know what it counted. Nobody in the yard knew he had a number, and he could not have asked for it in a language anybody there would have understood him to be speaking.

Tarn took the broom only after Sella had given it its instruction, and went around the clean dark line at the Vail corner, and the new hairline of grey finer than the old lying beside it, and the space under the lowest of the drying racks, going as wide around the last as around a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. He swept none of the three. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the line he would not cross, and no scrubbing lifted it; the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back. When he had finished the yard he took the broom over the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector, over and over, without once turning to look along it. He had been sent down that road once in the Long Cold and had come back at the frozen ford and told the house it had been done, and he kept that sentence where he kept the seventh year and entered neither.

The small bench knife lay on its sill with six years standing in a mark his thumb had worn almost flat and the seventh nearly gone and still held. To the kindling he said, “Six counted. The next I don’t count,” and went on holding the breath he had decided not to spend. He agreed with the ones who wanted the thaw early and the ones who wanted it late, saying Yes, house to both, and went on wanting the late one because it put off the paying, though the paying had stood in the ruts since the water went. A vessel gave its note somewhere past the door and his head did not turn and he did not name the sound, and the boy did not call it a seventh hearing.

At the going of the light Sella carried him up the yard at the half-pace and set him on his bed under the Vail kiln-house roof, not in the Debt Loft. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without looking at his face and left the thread of drool frozen and unwiped, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left. Under the roof the private file was checked the way a man checks instruments laid out on a towel, and it was unchanged. Six hearings of the full note stayed six beside and never among, five of them from four sources and the sixth, the small flat knock out of a woman who had been warm that morning, from a person; no seventh had come. Three near-full answers stood on their tray, advanced and not paid. The three intervals stayed on another. The word forty was unspoken, and the readable shape of the double turn stood beside it on its own place, and the weight of a thumb on wicker stood apart from both. Four low things kept their four places, with the river’s note of water going over water a fifth thing and not a hearing. The two tellings of the room shut in the war stayed joined to no kiln, likeness kept and difference kept, breath through the nose against breath made in the throat, both men saying not the war’s hand. The loan that could hear stayed uncalled.

Sella told the fire it was the thaw and turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and fed it, and the fire answered with four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been. Its payer had gone unnamed for ninety-four days, and the coal that went up with the brine-gull was entered in no slate and missed in none. She did not touch the widow’s mark, because a strip, a shelf, a basket, a well and a bed are not openings and no opening had come into this house on Seed 5; she had lost a husband to that danger and had never once described it that way herself. In the corner the clean dark line lay where it had always lain and the new hairline of grey, finer than the old, lay beside it unswept and was called the thaw and would go when it went. Ash was worked into a man’s knuckles in the colour of that line, and nobody fetched a broom for either.

One brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold with the empty place beside it still unnamed, and no third came. The place at the end of the loaf row in the kiln-house and the place at his ear lay in two rooms and did not touch. The boards overhead kept four nails and four and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts did not meet.

The door stood a finger’s width open. Through the gap came the river’s low muttered note from the Ilm, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, and through the floor came the kiln’s breath, four, four and a gap under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, the two sounds in two separate places as they had come for ninety-four days. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water going over water and came down. Nothing had been joined, paid, explained or ended. The boy was ninety-four days old and nobody had told him so, and the count stood at ninety-four, unspent, with the seed in the ground and no figure put to it and the fist that had let it out already forgotten by everybody but him.

The loaf shelf had a pale oval worn into its board where a loaf had stood long enough for the wood to take the bread’s shape and keep it. The oval was the width of a man’s palm and lighter than the dark around it, with a small hard ridge at one end where a crust had caught and held through many bakings. Sella Vail stood before the loaf shelf in the doorway of the Vail kiln-house and moved her lips along the row the way a woman counts spoons into a drawer — one, two, three — without ever letting the counting become a figure in the mouth. She had done it every morning of the boy’s life and had never once said the number aloud. Today the row answered her differently.

There were two gaps in it now. The old one stood at the end of the rank where a loaf had once stood and nobody called it a number, a space the house kept the way it kept Garl’s hollow seat and the salt-stiff coat on its peg, going around it and leaving it standing. The other was new. It sat within the rank, not at the end, a palm’s breadth of bare board where the pale oval showed and the bread did not, and beside it the next loaf had settled a finger’s breadth toward the empty place, the way a stack leans when the sheaf under it is taken away. Sella’s thumb went over the new gap, felt the little ridge at its end, and came back. She said nothing about either.

Another loaf’s worth had gone off the board since the going of the light yesterday and nobody had marked it. There was no scrape on the shelf, no crumb on the floor, no print in the grey, no word said over the row. A loaf had stood here at dusk on Seed 5 and did not stand here at the coming of the light on Seed 6, and the house had gone on around the absence the way water goes around a stone and leaves it where it lies. Carried at the edge in a basket under a fold of wool, Corin read the two gaps as he had once read a column of figures with a neat line drawn through the middle of it: a thing entered, a thing taken away, both of them accounted for, and nobody in the room any the wiser for what the line had covered. It was a clean reading. It was worth nothing.

A neighbour came to the shelf with her basket on her arm and reached for a loaf. Her sleeve was pushed above one elbow and her hands were red from the tubs. Her hand went out at the level of her own hunger and passed over the new gap without stopping, the fingers opening for the half-beat it takes to find what is not there, and then closing on the loaf beyond it. She did not look at the place her hand had crossed. She said something to Sella about the pot she had come to fetch, and Sella told the pot to come up level and leave its lip as it found it, and the woman went on with the pot, and the gap went on standing in the row. It was ordinary work. Nothing had happened. No figure passed between them and no hand was laid on the bare place to make it mean anything.

Sella gave the instruction to the pot and to the fire and never to the basket. She said the house where another woman would have said I, and when she spoke of the day’s baking she spoke of what the house had said and not of what she had decided. The little-door burn on the back of her hand closed on the basket’s edge and then on nothing, because her hands had already gone back to the work. Once, in the middle of the telling, her eyes went to the clay jar on the wall shelf and rested there a moment, and came back. The jar was cold under its glaze, the colour of skimmed milk gone off, and it held nothing the boy could see from where he lay. She did not touch it and did not say what it was for.

Under the fold the boy set the shelf’s new gap down on one place and the empty place beside the crust at his ear on another, and kept the two places apart. He had learned, in a better-lit room than any in Low Kiln, what three likenesses in a row will do to a man who wants an answer: lay like on like on like and a cause grows up between them that no one of the three would have given by itself, and the man goes home satisfied with the thing he has made. He had watched a man do it with three reports and be paid for the doing, and the reports had not been lying. Here were two absences in two rooms, with nobody putting a hand to either and nobody asking what had gone. He wrote no cause across the space between them. The keeping of the two places apart was the whole of the work available to him, and it bought him nothing.

He wanted the number of his days said aloud by somebody who would know what it counted. Ninety-five was exact in him and had been exact on every morning since the first, and there was nobody under this roof who knew he had a number, and nobody who could have been told what it counted in Grathic that would have understood him to be speaking of a life. He could not ask. The pot got its instruction and the fire got its instruction and the broom got its instruction and the basket got none, and the boy got none either. He was the only adult at the shelf and the least use in the room.

The wheel took his weight and gave its low rhythm back, the note it had given since Garl’s seat went hollow three years ago, and the floor of the shed answered in the same key. He set a pot up and drew the shoulder thinner with two fingers and told it, “You’ll do,” in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either. the full note was in the pot already, whole before it left the wheel, and he knew it as certainly as he knew the bend in his little finger. The kiln-master stood in the doorway with his glazed thumb held away from his coat and looked at the pot turning on the wheel. “That one’s singing before it’s fired,” he said. “Somebody’ll make something of that.”

He moved it a finger’s breadth and held it there. From the height of the basket the boy watched the bent finger on the iron. He had read the thing in another life with his hands full and the field already lost: any iron that stops a thing from running is the same iron whatever it is that is stopped. A clamp closes a vessel and a clamp takes a hand. A damper holds the heat in a room that must keep it and shuts the breath off in a room that cannot spare it. The bite that kept the pot whole and the bite that would take a man’s finger at the knuckle were one bite, and no word in Grathic held both mercies, and nobody under this roof could have been made to understand him if he had found one. He kept the reading where he kept the rest, an anatomy of the house’s danger filed as an object: exact, and worth nothing. Three people in this house could read that iron and kept it to themselves for three different reasons — Sella because a holding is not an opening and she had lost a husband to that danger, Jorra because a name is a thing a kiln-master can carry up the terrace and make something of, Tarn because a loan that can hear is a loan that can be called — and the house went around all three and called the holding a holding and never a taking.

Two men, two reaches for a record, and the difference between them was a thing he could read without a word in Grathic for it and could not carry to any door. The soft hands went back into their coat and the man waited in the doorway of the afternoon as if waiting were a trade he had worked at for years. Nothing passed that any slate could have called a sale.

On the short turn down to the landing later, when Sella carried him to the edge of the fish-trade shade where no slate lay, the soft-cuffed clerk was still set at the steps with his cuff gone grey from washing and his slate held to his forearm for a measure nobody had called. The hundredth morning came up over the Clay Yard the colour of wet slate, with the mud the water had left set into a brown skin that held the print of a boot and gave it back slowly as the light went across it. There was no frost on the racks. Out east of Low Kiln the seed stood in the barley fields where the fists had let it out, not green yet, only worked ground with the earth drawn over it in a line that stopped where the next strip began, and the women who had put it there went around it with their brooms as though a line drawn in dirt were a thing a man might step over and spoil. Nothing in the ground said a figure. A fist had opened and a fist had closed, and the strip had taken what it was given without a mark to show whose hand had paid it. The morning went on in the small sounds of bristles and boots, and a man at the edge of the strips said the work would want hands before the week was out, and a woman at the racks said the weather would have the last word, and the two sayings passed one another in the yard without either putting a number to the hands or the strips, the way two carts pass on a road and neither leaves a mark of how far the other has gone.

Sella Vail had set Corin at the edge of the work with the wool at his ears and had given the broom its instruction before she gave it to anybody. “Go around,” she said to the bristles. “It cost enough to come in. It’ll cost more to take out.” The women took it up as though it had been said to them and went on with their sweeping; the basket got no instruction at all. The little-door burn across the back of her hand closed on the wicker’s edge and then on nothing. He lay with cold iron at his cheek where an old bracket had been bolted and left, and the cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, with the wool riding back from the wrist so the cold found the bare place. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had been told to wipe it. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the light and came down. Nothing within two paces could be reached.

He was one hundred days old. Three months and ten days. The count had gone edge to edge and come to a round place where a man might have set a mark and shut a column, if there had been a column, or a slate, or anybody who knew what the column counted. A round number is a handsome thing at the foot of an account; it asks to be entered and asks it in a clean hand. The house kept its days in loaves pushed along a shelf, in a glance at the clay jar on the wall, in the space at the row’s end that nobody called a figure. Nobody entered his hundred. Nobody said three months and ten days in the yard, or over the strips, or anywhere within the sound of his ear. He kept it where he kept the rest of it, edge to edge and exact, and the exactness was the whole of what he had and bought nothing at all.

Under the drying racks the clean dark line where the Ash ridge had turned lay where it had always lain, and beside it the new hairline of grey, finer than the old, lay unswept and was called the thaw that would go when it went. Sella stood over both with the broom in her hand and swept neither, and went around the space under the lowest rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. The broken slates lay beneath that space and were gone around by every foot in the yard and entered by none. A woman pushing her bristles around a stone that had come up with the flood stopped and looked at the ground a moment longer than the stone required, then went on with her sweeping and did not say why she had looked. He kept the boot-print, the clean line, the finer grey and the empty space on four places that did not touch, because a likeness laid on a likeness grows a cause unless a hand holds them apart. The boards overhead kept four nails and four and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb, and that count met none of the others either.

Tarn took the broom only after Sella had given it its instruction, and went over the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector, over and over, without once turning to look along it. The small knife lay on its sill with six years standing in a mark his thumb had worn almost flat and the seventh nearly gone and still held, the one entry in this house the house could not check. To the kindling he said, “Six counted. The next I don’t count.” To a woman who wanted the thaw early and a man who wanted it late he said, “Yes, house,” to each, agreeing so entirely that no argument could find a foot anywhere in the yard, and went on wanting the late one because a late thaw puts off the paying, though the paying had stood in the ruts since the water went. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line and no scrubbing lifted it; the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back. He had been sent for a collector once in the Long Cold, had come back at the frozen ford and told the house it had been done, and kept that sentence where he kept the seventh year and entered neither. A loan that can hear is a loan that can be called. The sentence had never been said under the roof and lay under it all the same. Something must give at the end of the seventh year. It did not give on the hundredth morning.

From the Vail wheel-shed came the wheel’s low rhythm and a word given to a pot and not to a person. “You’ll do,” said Jorra Vail, and the boy heard him and did not see him. No figure came out of the shed onto the yard; the back room was not mentioned, and the men who had come in before the light were not named. Jorra knew the full note of a Vail pot before it left the wheel and would not say so before a kiln-master from High Kiln, who would call it early and make something of it; the boy kept the knowing where questions are allowed to last for years. The bent finger went to the damper ahead of a sentence nobody had finished, the cold iron taking the cold out of the fingertip, and the household straightened around the silence the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted. He did not look toward the basket, and “he’s not Vail” stayed sealed as tightly as a lid nobody had tried to lift in three years. The promise of the foundling’s name to the men who burn slates was neither given nor withdrawn. He was the only adult at the edge of that yard and the least use in it: a surgeon who could read the order of a failing body and could not keep the wool on his own wrist, a man of forty who could not lift his hand to his own chin.
