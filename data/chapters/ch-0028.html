The Word Held at the Teeth

Days 123–125 · Reed 4–6, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln and the Weir, Sill

A breath held at the iron is spent whether it goes in or not. A word held at the teeth is kept, and a kept thing is owed.

— A saying of the clay-bound, Low Kiln

— Day 123 —

Reed 4, Fourthday, Y.H. 813

The light came up off the river with mud in it on the fourth day of Reed, and the yard at Low Kiln took the colour of a thing long handled and set down wet. The wind had come round in the night and brought the smell of cut grass downriver over the sill, and under it the thin mineral edge the Ilm leaves on everything it touches, so that the ruts and the upturned barrow and the backs of the drying racks all wore the same brown skin. The fourth day had come on without a boundary drawn anywhere in the yard, and the work went into the beds the way the seed had gone into the strips: carried in armfuls, laid in the ruts where the barley had stood, judged by the arm that brought it and by nothing that could be set on a slate. Sella Vail carried the boy up from the rack end at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, the basket held level the way a pot is held that must not be slopped, and gave the instruction to the wool and the river wind and never to the basket. “Keep it off his ears. It’s the river wind.” The instruction went into the wool and into the wind and nowhere near the child inside it. He was one hundred and twenty-three days old and four months and three days, carried where he could not have crawled, and nobody in the yard or under the roof had said so, or would have known what it counted if they had. Cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, the wet wool riding back from the bare joint so the cold found the place it had come for.

Men down from the reed beds came across the yard with cut reed on their shoulders, green at the broken ends and pale grey at the frost-bitten tips, and laid the armfuls in the ruts. They laid them down and straightened and looked at what they had laid, the way a man looks at a thing he has carried and is willing to have judged by the carrying. A cutter wiped his palms down his thighs before he took up another bundle, and the mud came away in two dark streaks that he did not notice. “A bundle’s counted in the arm that carries it,” he said, and it was a statement of the arithmetic the whole yard was using and he made no move to put any part of it into writing.

“And the hands that cut it?” a woman at the racks said.

The cutter took up another armful and went past with the question still in the air behind him, going down the yard the way a thrown thing goes that nobody means to pick up. He did not say whose hands, or how many, or what they had been paid; he did not turn his head. The woman held her gaze a moment on his going and then went back to the pot she had shifted an inch so the light fell where she wanted it, and the question lay where he had left it, unanswered and entered nowhere.

A man come up from the sluice with his sleeves wet to the elbow stood a moment over the laid bundles and asked what the beds would want before the week was out. He asked it in the form of a direction, because that was the form the work had taken since the hands came out of the strips: a going-downriver in the morning and a coming-back at dusk, and a day’s cutting measured by how far a man had walked to get it and how far he walked home with it.

“Ask it,” Sella said. “It’ll tell you when it’s had enough. The house has said it.”

The man turned the answer over, found no figure in it, and went off satisfied, because a direction was what he had asked for and a direction was what he had been given. He had asked what the beds would want and been told to ask the beds; he had asked the cost in the form of a direction and been answered with the weather and the river. Nothing had been priced and nothing had been refused. The cost had been asked in a shape that had no room for a figure in it, and so no figure had come. The boy, carried past them at the half-pace, heard the order of it exactly: the question first, and the answer given to the thing the question was about, and the woman standing between the two with her burn shut on the basket handle and her face turned nowhere. He had spent two lives learning how a person takes himself out of his own sentence; here a whole district took itself out of its own cost.

The yard went around what it would not enter. The clean dark line lay at the Vail corner where Ash ridge had turned, and beside it the new finer hairline of grey that the yard had called the thaw after the fact, both unswept since the water went and crossed by no broom in four days. Sella stood over them with the basket steady and swept neither. The space beneath the lowest rack held its broken birth-slates where whole slate should have stood, and the going-around widened there by the width of a hand and came back, the way a woman goes wide around a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. Out in the worked strips east of the houses the returning line held its gap. One hand that had gone in on Seed 15 had not come out with the rest on Seed 27, and the yard went around the place where it should have stood with a look and a wide step and no number. No body had come up out of the brown ground. No cause had been said over it, no accident named, no hand identified and no figure set at the foot of what the month had cost. A slate would be found and a figure made if a hand came up in a month or a year, and nothing had come up. The place was held and not entered, carried into Reed the way it had been carried through the end of Seed, and the reed work going-downriver arranged itself around it as the planting had.

The boy read the going-around as a surgeon reads a chart laid face down on a table: a neat line drawn to close a note that nobody had opened. It was exact. It was complete. It was worth nothing. Two lives had taught him the difference between a record that tells what happened and a record that tells only where to stop reading, and the yard’s empty place was the second kind, closed as neatly as any slate and with as little inside it. Here the chart was a field with a hole in a line and no signature at all, and the not-reading was done in the open air before a whole yard that leaned toward it the way a crowd leans toward a man about to speak low. He set it on a place of its own where he kept such things and laid nothing down beside it.

A woman come up from the strips with dry mud worked into the creases of her wrists stopped at the rack end and asked what the reeds would cost now the hands were out of them. It was the question of the month, asked plainly and in a form that wanted a figure, and the yard went quiet in the small way a yard goes quiet when a thing everybody has been going around is said aloud. Sella glanced once at the clay jar cold under its glaze on the wall shelf and sent the answer to the pot.

“Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there. The house has said it.”

The woman stood a moment with her question still held out, then took it back the way a woman takes back a cup that has not been filled, and carried it away held level so that nothing slopped. She did not ask again and nobody asked for her; the cost went off across the yard in her two hands as carefully as a full pot goes off a shelf, and arrived where it had been asked to arrive, at a pot that had no answer in it. The boy watched the level carry of it and set that on a place of its own too, a keeping set apart and not a price, with no figure in it and no hand laid across it.

❦

Sella carried him the length of the yard at the half-pace she kept from every open slate. She went around the space beneath the lowest rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, the step widening by the width of a hand and coming back, and around the two unswept lines without crossing either. The instruction went to the wool and to the river wind: keep it off his ears, it is the river wind; keep it dry, it is running water. She said it twice, because the wool had not moved and the wind had not listened, and never once said it to the child in the basket. She said the house where another woman would have said I, and the boy, listening from inside the basket at the half-pace, heard the taking happen and did not ask for the answer that never came.

Midday came on over the yard without anything marking where the morning ended and the other half of the day began. The reeds went on coming down from the beds in armfuls and standing grey in the ruts; the hands that had carried seed in shut fists now carried reed in armfuls, and nobody put a figure to either. The district’s withholding was not kept by one woman in a doorway but performed in front of everybody, in a question sent to a pot and a direction answered with the weather and an empty place gone around with a look; it was the most ordinary thing in the yard, and the boy was the only one in it who could read it as a closed account. Competence without a hand to lay it down was only a more exact kind of helplessness. He had the whole shape of it in his head, the chart closed with its neat line, the note unopened under it, the arithmetic of the arm that carried the bundle and told what had been carried and never what it had cost, and he had no hand to set it down and no voice with which to offer it, and if he had had both he would not have offered it, because an account laid on a table in this district is an account that wants a payer.

He turned the keeping over the way a man lays instruments on a towel and found nothing new to add. the loaf shelf kept its two unnamed gaps in the next room, through a wall this yard did not reach: the old space at the row’s end that had been named as a thing the planting brought in, and the newer palm’s breadth that had come in on the night of Seed 6 with no scrape, crumb, print or word. In the fold at his ear one stiff brown-edged crust lay with the empty place beside it still unnamed, and no third had come into the fold in a hundred and twenty-three days, and no third gap stood on the shelf. Two rooms did not touch. He laid no likeness across them, and none across the field’s empty place, and none across the place where the hand had gone in. A likeness laid on a likeness grows a cause, and a cause in this district always arrived with a clean hand at the foot of it, and the clean hand was the one object he had spent two lives declining to become.

His small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the worked strips and came down. It had gone up an inch toward the loaf shelf and toward a pot two paces off and toward the note of water through the finger’s-width gap, and it came down; nothing within two paces could be reached. In the other life it had closed on clamps and on a phone with a name he would not look at, and had found a vessel in the dark by the note it gave under a knuckle. That hand was gone. This one went up an inch and came down, and the count went on with it, one hundred and twenty-three exact and unspent and spoken by nobody who would have known what it counted when they said it.

Out in the worked strip the yard went around the empty place with a look and a wide step, and the armfuls of reed passed it on their way downriver and would pass it again coming back at dusk, and there was no number anywhere in the line.

❦

Back under the Vail kiln-house roof the light went out of the oiled paper by degrees, the squares on the floor losing their corners first and their middles last, as though the room were being closed by a hand. Sella Vail carried the boy in at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, the basket level under her burn-shut hand, and set it down where no slate lay, against the wall and out of the door’s draught, the way a woman sets a pot where no passing hip will find it. She did not look into it and she did not speak to it. The instructions went to the things that could not answer, and went first.

“Settle,” she told the bucket. “You’ve come up out of a dark place. Don’t bring it in with you.”

“Go around,” she told the broom. “It cost enough to come in. It’ll cost more to take out.”

“Not by the door,” she told the pot. “You’ll pay for it in the thaw.”

The fire got its instruction last and kept it longest: feed me before anybody is fed. The words went into the bucket, the broom, the pot and the fire, and nowhere near the basket; the basket kept its level carry, and the fold at his ear was put right without a look at his face. She said the house where another woman would have said I, and the boy, who had spent two lives learning the grammar of a person taking himself out of his own sentence, heard the taking happen and let it lie where it fell. He was one hundred and twenty-three days old and four months and three days, and nobody under this roof had said so, or would have known what it counted if they had said it.

Between the rack end and the wall there was a space where whole slate should have stood. Sella went around it with a step that widened by the width of a hand and came back, the basket held as level as a pail of hot water carried past a child who might knock it. She did not look at the space and she did not sweep it. The broken birth-slates lay where they lay under the lowest rack, entered by nobody in four days, and the going-around had the care of a thing seen without being entered in any account.

In the next room the loaf shelf kept its days the way this house kept most of its days: badly, and in materials no Court thumb would read. Loaves pushed along the board, a glance at the clay jar cold under its glaze on the wall, and spaces left where no figure stood. She moved her lips along the row without a figure, her hand going past the old empty place at the row’s end and past the newer palm’s breadth that had come in on the night of Seed 6 with no scrape, crumb, print or word, and closed on a loaf beyond both. Two gaps; no third. In the fold at his ear one stiff brown-edged crust lay two inches from him with the empty place beside it still unnamed, and the two rooms did not touch. Two neighbours had laid the old gap and the empty place at his ear under one cause in a single breath and gone off with their pots, and she had heard the sayings and made neither his and made neither hers; a shelf was not an opening, and a likeness laid on a likeness in this district grew a cause with a clean hand at the foot of it.

The widow’s mark hung at her throat, a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it. She did not touch it. It was touched before an opening and at no other hour, and a shelf was not an opening, nor a strip, a rack, a well, a market, a road, a basket or a bed. Her hand went instead to the strand of coal-black hair at her temple that had come loose in the carrying, and she pushed it back with the wrist that carried the little-door burn, the skin inside the door puckering as the wrist turned. The grey streak showed pale in the dying light off the coals. She had said once that it came in the winter Garl died and had not said it again.

At the fire she told it what it was. “It was the thaw.” The words went down into the coals as instructions went into the bucket, and the coals answered nothing. She turned them with the back of a broken pot, the broken edge lifting and settling, and fed them, and the house kept its warmth the way a cup keeps water with a crack in it nobody will mention.

“The house will keep it,” she said, where another woman would have said I.

Then she stood with the back of the broken pot in her hand and counted something under her breath toward no opening. The lips moved; the count did not arrive anywhere; there was no slate, no door, no crossing of a threshold for it to be entered at. The boy, carried past at the half-pace and set where he could see and not join, read the stopping of the count as a surgeon reads a chart closed over a note nobody has opened: exact, complete, worth nothing. He had learned in two lives to tell a record that says what happened from a record that says only where to stop reading, and this was the second kind, kept in the mouth instead of on slate and more carefully for that.

She set the pot where it could be found in the dark, rim out and handle toward the reaching hand, the way a woman sets a cup for a man who will come in late and not look where he puts his fingers. She did not look toward the bed. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen white in the small hours and nobody under this roof had been given an instruction about the thread, so it stayed where it had been put, a thing kept and not entered.

The going of the light brought the yard in through the oiled paper as a sound before it brought it as a sight: a broom, a foot, a voice saying something about the weather that arrived at the door as a direction. Ash lay in the Vail corner where the old ridge had turned, a clean dark line with a new finer hairline of grey beside it, both unswept in four days and both called the thaw. There was no sweeping of either, only the going-around, which was the sort of care that looks like not seeing. Sella stood over them with the pot still in her hand and went on not sweeping them.

Outside at the edge of the yard Tarn took up the broom only after the broom had been given its instruction; he did not put his hand to a tool that had not been named. He went around the two lines and the space beneath the rack with the stroke that went around and entered nothing, and then he went over the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector, over and over, without once turning his head to look along it. No collector had come down it for a woman the brook took, and he had never said that the thing which had not happened had a name. The small bench knife lay at the very edge of its sill with six years standing in a thumb-mark his thumb had worn almost flat and the seventh nearly gone and still held, the one entry in this house the house could not check. He had told the sill where it was, unasked, and the unforged collector sentence lay beside it where he kept it, neither entered. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line and no scrubbing lifted it; the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back.

His chest had been tight since the dark. The breath he had decided not to spend on Seed 16 was still unspent, and there was a small interval at the top of each reach where it should have gone in, an empty place in the order of a thing a man does without thinking. A woman going past with a pot said the thaw would come early. A man by the upturned barrow said it would come late.

“Yes, house,” Tarn said to the one, and “Yes, house,” to the other, agreeing so entirely that no argument could find a foot to stand on. He kept wanting the late, because a late thaw puts off the paying, though the paying had stood in the ruts since the water went and would stand there when the reeds came in. When he reached the top of the yard the breath that should have gone in did not go in, and he went on with the broom; this was the shape his help took when he was frightened. The tongue of the damper stood where it had been left that morning, moved a hair’s breadth and standing there still, and he did not ask about the ear and she did not ask about the room. Two silences stood under one roof with nobody putting a word between them, and the loan that could hear was not called. Something must give at the end of the seventh year; it had not given yet.

❦

On his bed under the roof the boy turned out the file as a man lays instruments on a towel, each thing in the place he had put it, nothing added and nothing taken away. Six hearings of the full note stood beside and never among: five from four sources — a cup across a birth room, a pot through the wheel-shed wall, the district’s slate twice at the Cross, and a man giving at the Drawing — and the sixth, the small flat knock out of Dessa Silt as the men lifted, which came from a person and was filed beside the five and never counted with them. No seventh had come today. The bell that had gone across the square was a told bell, and he put it where it belonged, in its own place and not among the hearings; the pots under the roof gave notes he had learned to leave where they sounded. Three near-full answers stood advanced and not paid, three intervals lay on another tray, and the word forty stayed unspoken with its readable double turn kept without a name. He wrote no cause across any of them. A likeness laid on a likeness grows a cause, and a cause in this district always arrived with a clean hand at the foot of it, and the clean hand was the one object he had spent two lives declining to become.

Through the finger’s-width gap came the river’s note of water over water, beyond the reed beds and the Weir, a fifth low thing kept on a place of its own since he was six days old, never a hearing and never among the six. Through the floor under blind ash the carried fire took its coal and gave back four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for a hundred and twenty-three days. Above him the boards kept four nails, four, and a fifth that was a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts sat in three places and did not become one. A man with an ear for vessels might have laid one gap across another and called them the same absence; he had spent two lives practising the refusal, and the refusal itself was worth nothing to anybody under this roof.

Nobody said he was a hundred and twenty-three days old. He had stopped expecting to be told, which was not the same as not wanting it. The count went on edge to edge against the house’s loaves and glances and unnamed spaces, exact and unspent and spoken by nobody who would have known what it counted if they had said it. The cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, the wool riding back from the bare joint so the cold found the place it had come for and went under the fold at his ear.

His small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down. Nothing within two paces could be reached; not the crust, not the pot set rim out for a reaching hand, not the fold, not the bucket, not a slate, not a hand. The hand was his and small, and the count went on with it. River, fire and floorboards kept their three counts in three places. The water went over water beyond the gap, the fire missed its fifth breath under the floor, and one hundred and twenty-three stood where it stood, unspent and spoken by nobody.

— Day 124 —

Reed 5, Fifthday, Y.H. 813

Before first grey the Clay Yard had the look of a vessel that has been set down in the cold and left, and left so often that the leaving has become its use. The racks stood a row of darker uprights against a sky the colour of wet ash, and the light came up off the river with the thin mineral edge the Ilm leaves on everything it touches, so that the flagstones and the upturned barrow and the shoulders of the men wore one grey skin. The men came up the yard with their collars high and their heads bent together, as though they carried between them something that would break if the wind got under it, and they went low-voiced into the room off the wheel-shed, which stood a finger’s width open and did not shut. Jorra Vail gave the house no name for them and did not say that the room was cold. Sella Vail set the boy at the edge of the work in the Vail wheel-shed where no slate lay, the basket level on the boards and his cheek turned to the cold of them, and stood a half-pace from every open slate. She gave the instruction to the wool before she gave it to anybody. “Keep it off his ears. It’s the river wind.” The instruction went into the wool and into the wind and nowhere near the child inside it. He was one hundred and twenty-four days old and four months and four days, and nobody in the shed or the yard had said so, or would have known what it counted if they had. Cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist; the wool had ridden back from the bare joint in the grey and the cold found the place it had come for and went under the fold at his ear. The thread at his mouth had frozen where nobody under this roof had been given an instruction about the thread. His head wandered, made the small wet sound, and came back. He did not follow the men. The room was not entered.

Jorra came through the finger’s-width door with fine grey slate dust ground small as meal into both forearms past the bend of the little finger, and it was not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel. Slip sits on the hair and dries in pale plates; this had gone into the creases and stayed there, a grey so fine it might have been swept from a slate and breathed in. He kept the dusty forearms and the clean ones on one tray of the morning, the way a man sets two states of the same vessel side by side on a bench and does not mix them, and he asked neither which slate had been broken nor whether it had been a birth-slate. The broken birth-slates lay under the lowest rack where the yard went around the space above them as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and entered them into nothing. Nobody went under the rack. The dust came out of the room and the room kept whatever it kept, and the house gave the men no name and the boy took down none.

A woman come up from the racks with a pot in her hands and reed-fibre grey at her wrists stopped at the edge of the boards and asked who had come up the yard before the light. It was a plain question and she asked it plainly, with her eyes on the pot and not on his face, which was the way this yard asked a thing it did not expect to be answered. Jorra did not give her a name. He took the pot from her and set it where the light fell on the rim, and told her the slip at its shoulder was too thick and would want thinning before it went near a firing. The woman took the pot back and went off with her question still in her hands, carried level the way Sella carried a basket, and the boy, who had learned to read a deflected question the way he had once read a chart with the note turned down, put the unanswered thing on a place of its own and wrote no cause under it. No name had been given and no name had been taken.

He spoke to the wheel before he spoke to anyone. “It doesn’t care whose hands. It cares if they’re steady.” It was the same sentence the boy had heard from the same mouth through the whole length of the planting month, given to the wheel as a man gives a beast its handful, and nothing in it asked who had been in the room or what had been ground into the creases past the little finger. A pot came up on the wheel and Jorra looked at its wall a moment longer than the wall required and said, “You’ll do,” in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either. He knew the the full note that was in that pot before it ever left the wheel; he had known such notes in pots of this house since he was eleven, and he would not say so in front of a kiln-master come up from High Kiln, who would call it early and make something of it. Garl Vail’s proverb had gone to a pot with no crack long ago and cost the more now that there was nobody to say it in his own voice, and the boy, who had spent two lives learning the exact weight of a note before a thing breaks, kept the knowing where questions could last for years. The knowing was worth nothing that anyone could take. It could not be sold, entered, or carried up the yard; it belonged to a boy who could not say it and a wheel-keeper who would not, and the pot went on turning with the note still inside it, uncalled.

A kiln-master come up from High Kiln went along the row of pots with his hands behind his back, looking at the rims and not at the boy, and said nothing that any slate could carry. He had the sweetish edge of glaze on his coat from the terrace, a smell the boy had learned to separate from the damp clay and tallow of the shed, and he stopped at a pot that had a shoulder a hair too thick and passed his eye over it without putting a finger to it. Jorra said nothing either. The note was in the Vail pot before it left the wheel and he had known it there, and a thing said in front of a kiln-master becomes a thing the kiln-master has heard, and a thing heard is a thing entered in some book the boy would never see; the house had taught him that much without ever saying it. The kiln-master went off up the yard with his hands still behind him, and the wheel took up its low note again as though no man had stood over it. The boy set the unsaid knowing on a place of its own and did not lay it beside the dust on the forearms, though the two things had come in on the same morning and from the same door.

❦

Sella had not finished telling him the fire was low. “The fire’s low, Jorra, and the—” The sentence never got to the end of itself, because his bent finger went into the cold iron of the damper ahead of it, took the cold out of the fingertip, moved the tongue a hair’s breadth and left it standing there. The household straightened around his silence the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted from it: the pot went where the pot was told, the broom stayed where the broom had been put, and the end of the instruction arrived at the pot instead of at the man. He had not answered her with a word and had not looked at her, which was how he obeyed her instantly, and the correction in it was the same correction he had made every morning of the planting month and never once named. She shut her mouth on the rest of the sentence and gave it to the pot, and the yard went on as though a weight had been lifted and the cloth had settled and nobody had seen the hand that did it.

The boy read the iron once more. It was one piece of cold iron with two uses in it, and he had spent two lives learning to tell them apart and had never found a word in Grathic that held both mercies at once. The bite that closes on a vessel and holds it whole, so that the warmth stays where the warmth is wanted, is the same bite that can take the hand off a man if the man is slow or careless or told to be quick; the iron does not choose between them and has no need to. Here it was a damper, a tongue of iron moved a hair’s breadth by a bent finger, and the reading of it was exact and worth nothing and joined to no kiln. Two men had carried a story into this yard of a counting room shut in the war with the damper closed and the men inside dead of the breath, eighteen leagues east and dated when they told it, and a third voice had only mentioned such a room, with a date in the mention and none of the weight. The boy set the two tellings on their own tray, kept the third mention where it belonged, and set the tray beside the iron without laying one across the other. A likeness laid on a likeness grows a cause, and there was no cause in this yard that any man had earned the right to name; besides, the men had gone home with their bags and their mud, and the kiln had gone on holding its warmth in its own damaged way. Nothing had been said that a slate could carry, and no third telling came to make a rule of the two.

Outside at the edge of the yard Tarn took up the broom only after the broom had been given its instruction. Go around, it cost enough to come in, it will cost more to take out; the instruction went into the broom and the flagstones and never into the man, and he took up the tool as a man takes up a thing already named. “Yes, house,” he said to a woman who wanted the thaw early, and “Yes, house,” to a man who wanted it late, agreeing so entirely that no argument could find a foot to stand on. He kept wanting the late, because a late thaw puts off the paying, though the paying had stood in the ruts since the water went and would stand there when the reeds came in and after they were cut. His chest had been tight since the dark. The breath he had decided not to spend on Seed 16 was still unspent, and there was a small interval at the top of each reach where it should have gone in, an empty place in the order of a thing a man does without thinking, a breath stopped against iron and never let through.

His seventh year was pressed past the end of the third tend of the planting and still held. The small bench knife lay at the very edge of its sill with six years standing in a mark his thumb had worn almost flat and the seventh nearly gone and never named, the one entry in this house the house could not check; the unforged collector sentence lay beside it where he had kept it since the frozen ford, and neither was entered. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line and no scrubbing lifted it, and the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back. Before he swept he put his hand to the small knife on its sill, moved it a hair’s breadth and set it back where it could be checked, and told the sill where it was, though the sill had not asked and the yard had been told a hundred mornings running. Six years stood in the worn mark and the seventh was where the house could not check it. The boy watched the hand go to the tool and come away and set the checking where he set the other record-first hands, kept and not entered; a record kept where no thumb would read it was still a record, and the house’s arithmetic had no place in it for the one entry that mattered. To the kindling, later, the account would stay as it had stayed through the planting month: six counted, and the next he did not count. He went around the two unswept lines and the space beneath the lowest rack with the stroke that went around and entered nothing, and then he went out toward the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector, and no collector came, and he did not say that the thing which had not happened had a name.

His stroke went on over the flagstones without the half-check. Behind Jorra’s standing finger the bristles passed the place where the stroke had stopped on Seed 22 and did not stop there now, and the stones kept that place all the same, a shallow bright patch in the pattern of the paving that no foot would enter and no broom would call by its name. On Seed 25 the change had been held and not repeated; on Seed 28 and on Reed 2 it had been held again; on this morning of Reed it went on as though the half-beat had never been made, and the two things lay on one tray in the boy’s keeping as two objects and not a rule. He set the unchecking stroke beside the earlier half-beat and laid no cause across them.

Jorra’s bent finger stood in the cold iron of the damper, and Tarn’s bristles went on over the flagstones without the check, the stones keeping the shallow bright patch where the stroke had stopped. Neither man asked about the other’s silence. Jorra did not ask about the ear and Tarn did not ask about the room; neither named the other’s silence, and the loan that could hear was not called. A loan that can hear is a loan that can be called, and the sentence that would call it had lain under this roof for six years without being said. An ear worth more than a debt is an unsafe thing to own, and the boy, who had spent two lives learning what an uncalled account costs, kept the two silences apart on the tray where they had lain since the night he came, with no finger laid across the space between them. The equality under the roof held at the place it had moved to, neither rising nor falling: one man’s obedience arriving before the instruction that called it and being also a correction nobody would name, the other’s stroke going on without the check it had once made. Two kept things stood in one room with no hand joining them. Nothing was paid, named, or ended. One hundred and twenty-four stood where one hundred and twenty-three had stood, exact and unspent and spoken by nobody who would have known what it counted, while the wheel kept its low note and the iron kept its bite and the reed work went on downriver without a figure.

The light went out of the yard in the order it always went, off the tops of the racks first and off the flagstones last, so that for a while the sky held a dull pewter over a floor already dark. Under the Vail kiln-house roof the dark came in through the door standing a finger’s width open and lay along the boards in the low places, the way water lies where it is let. the boy had been carried in from the edge of the work and set on his bed and not in the Debt Loft, and the fold was right at his ear and under his chin, and the thread at his mouth had frozen where nobody under this roof had been given an instruction about the thread. He was awake. One hundred and twenty-four days stood in him, four months and four days, exact as a set of instruments laid out on a towel and spoken by nobody who would have known what it counted. He had stopped expecting to be told, which was not the same as not wanting it.

Sella Vail was at the fire. She had given the pot its instruction and the pot had taken it, and now she gave the end of the same sentence to the coals, because a sentence begun to a thing must be finished to something or it stands about the room all night with nowhere to go. “It was the thaw,” she told the fire, and turned the coals with the back of a broken pot, and stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand counting toward no opening. The little-door burn on the back of her hand was shut on nothing. She pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carried it, the grey streak at her temple pale in the dying light off the coals, and set the pot where it could be found in the dark, rim out and handle toward the reaching hand.

Tarn was at the hearth with the coals already banked. He had done it without looking at the fire, the way he had banked it for six years, pressing the blind ash over the red with the back of his hand and leaving one small place where the heat came through slowly enough to be trusted. His chest was tight. The breath he had decided not to spend on Seed 16 was still unspent, and there was a small interval at the top of each reach where it should have gone in, an empty place in the order of a thing a man does without thinking, a breath stopped against iron and never let through. He finished the bank, wiped his knuckles on his thigh without lifting the Ash worked into the creases, and put his hand out on the sill for the small bench knife.

The knife lay at the very edge, where a man could check it and where a man could also take it, and he moved it a hair’s breadth and set it back where it could be checked. Six years stood in a mark his thumb had worn almost flat, and the seventh nearly gone and never named, the one entry in this house the house could not check. The unforged collector sentence lay beside it where he had kept it since the frozen ford, and he did not enter either. The pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what little light there was and gave it back. “It’s back,” he told the sill, though the sill had not asked and the yard had been told a hundred mornings running. “You can check.” Then he told the sill something else, lower, in the tone a man uses to state the weather: “Six counted. The next I don’t count.”

The kindling stack stood where it had stood through the planting month, and Tarn crouched to it as he crouched to all his tools. It smelled of straw and the tallow that had dripped from the house’s own dish onto a split end, a dry, honest smell that asked nothing of a man. He laid a stick across and took the measure of the stack with his eyes as he had taken it a hundred evenings, four, four, and a space where the fifth should have been. The space was older than the boy. It had been kept as carefully as the breaths that came, and no hand had ever filled it and no hand had ever called it by a name.

He spoke to the wood, because the wood was what he spoke to when a thing had to be said and a person would have made of it a question. “Six counted,” he said. “The next I don’t count.” The stick lay where he put it. He took up another, and the sentence went on past the counting the way a man walks on past a gate he has left open, not meaning to go far and going all the same. “An ear under this roof that can hear,” he said to the kindling, “is worth more than the debt that holds it.” The wood took it as wood takes everything, without answer and without keeping. He laid the second stick. “And if a man said whose ear it was, and set a figure to it—”

The sentence came up to the word and stood there.

He had it at his teeth. It was a short word, a plain word, a word that would have called the loan and set the whole house to answering it; he had carried it in his mouth for six years without once letting it out, and now it had come up through the counting as water comes up through a crack and stood on the threshold of his teeth with no figure under it and no name on it. He did not say it. The kindling kept its arithmetic. The dark under the roof kept whatever it kept. Somewhere beyond the finger’s-width gap the river went over water in its low muttered note, and under the floor the carried fire took its coal and gave back four low pulls and a silence, and neither of them said the word for him.

The boy, on his bed with the fold at his ear, knew the shape of a stopped sentence the way he knew a breath stopped at the top of a reach — the small empty place where the thing should have gone in. He had spent two lives learning what a word does when it is held at the teeth. In the one life a word spoken into a chart could call a number out of a man and set a hand writing; in this one a word held at the teeth was kept, and a kept thing was owed. The word that would call the loan was the same piece of iron as the damper: the bite that holds a vessel whole and the bite that can take a hand off a man, with no word in Grathic for both mercies. Here it had a figure cut into it that nobody had read, and where there was a figure there was a payer, and nobody under this roof had named one. He set the stopped sentence on a place of its own. He wrote no cause across it.

Tarn drew the breath that should have gone in at the top of the reach and did not spend it. It stood where it had stood since Seed 16, a clamp stopped against the Warm and never let through, and his chest kept the shape of it. He put the last stick on the stack, set the space where the fifth should have been where it had always been, and took up the broom. He had taken it only after it had been given its instruction, and he went around the two unswept lines and the space under the lowest rack with the stroke that went around and entered nothing. “Yes, house,” he said to the fire, and to the pot, and to nobody who had asked him a thing. When he was frightened he became helpful, and in a kiln-house that meant doing three things at once and quietly putting the fourth right; the household straightened around him the way cloth straightens when a weight has been lifted, and nobody said what had lifted it.

Sella gave the end of her instruction to the pot. “There,” she said to it, and left the sentence where a pot would understand it, and asked Tarn nothing about the ear. She had been within hearing of the wood and the stopped sentence and the word that had come up to the teeth and gone back; she had heard it the way she heard the fire’s fourth pull and the silence after it, and she asked nothing, because a question said aloud becomes a thing a house must answer, and a thing answered is a thing entered. Jorra Vail was gone from the shed. The bent finger stood in the cold iron of the damper where he had left it, and the fine grey dust was already ground into his forearms past the little finger and filed where the house kept such things, and the back room off the shed stood a finger’s width open with whatever it kept still inside it. He did not ask about the ear. Tarn did not ask about the room. The two silences stayed where they had stayed since the night the boy came, two shapes under one roof with no hand joining them, and the equality held at the place it had moved to, neither rising nor falling. Nothing was paid, named, or ended.

The boy turned out his file as a man lays instruments on a towel, in the dark, with the count standing at one hundred and twenty-four where one hundred and twenty-three had stood the day before. Every object was where he had left it. Six hearings of the full note stood beside and never among, five from four sources and the sixth, the small flat knock out of a woman as the men lifted, beside them and never counted with them; no seventh had come. Three near-full answers stood advanced and not paid. On another tray lay the intervals: the half-beat of Tarn’s check behind the standing finger, the stroke that had gone on over the flagstones without the check, his own held breath from the market, and now this — a sentence stopped at the threshold of the word, two objects and a stopped sentence on one tray and not a rule. An interval was not a payment and a near-saying was not a hearing, and neither was a seventh. He wrote no cause across any of them. A likeness laid on a likeness grows a cause unless a hand holds them apart, and he had no hand that could reach two inches and no voice to say the thing that would make the holding plain. He kept them apart by knowing what each one was, which was the only instrument he had ever had and the only one he had never been able to lay down.

The kindling kept its old arithmetic at the hearth, four, four, and an empty fifth, and the knife kept its six in the worn mark with the seventh at the very edge and still held. The breath in Tarn’s chest stayed unspent, the near-saying stayed at the teeth, and something must give at the end of the seventh year and had not given yet. Under the floor the carried fire lay under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, taking its coal and giving back four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for a hundred and twenty-four days. Through the gap came the river’s note of water over water, a fifth low thing on a place of its own, never a hearing and never among the six. Above the bed the boards kept four nails, four, and a fifth that was a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts in three places, and none became one.

The boy’s small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water and came down. He lay with one stiff brown-edged crust two inches from his ear and its empty place beside it unnamed, in a room that did not touch the shelf in the next room with its two gaps, and in a house that had never once said how old he was. The sentence stood at the threshold of the word with no figure under it and no hand laying a line across it. The loan that could hear was uncalled. The seventh year was held. The dark went on keeping what it kept, exact, complete, and spoken by nobody, while the woodsmoke and the wet wool and the straw smell of the kindling went up together under the roof and the count went on into the next day unspent.

— Day 125 —

Reed 6, Sixthday, Y.H. 813

The morning came up off the Ilm with mud in it and cut grass, the same thin mineral edge the river left on everything it touched, and Sella Vail carried the boy down the road to the Weir at the half-pace she kept from every open slate. He was one hundred and twenty-five days old, four months and five days, and nobody who carried him had any use for the number. The reed work had come downriver in the night the way it always came, not as a figure but as a direction: bundles going out and bundles coming back, judged by the arm that brought them and by nothing else. At the edge of the work she set him where no slate lay, gave the instruction to the wool and the river wind and never to the basket, and went a half-pace away to stand where she could see the boards and the posts and the men upon them. The basket sat level. Nothing in it slopped. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the low mutter of the water and came down, because two inches was the whole of his reach and the water was a league away.

the reed beds went on behind the yard in long grey ranks, green at the broken ends where the knife had been and pale at the frost-bitten tips, the colour of old bone laid out in ruts. The traps stood in the gaps of the weir with their mouths upriver, and the plank walk went out over the water on posts with salt dried white where the flood had touched them, a dry, honest line of a thing that would bear a man’s weight until the day it would not and would give no notice first. A girl with a creel went along it ahead of the others, her step quick and her eyes on the boards. The boy watched the boards as a man watches a floor he has not yet trusted, but he had no hand to put on one and no voice to say which would hold.

A reed-cutter came along the planks with a bundle on his shoulder, the broken ends wet and dark against his coat, and set the bundle down on the flat stone where the hearth-maids had stood in Ilmen to bless the traps. He took a short length of wool or reed-fibre from inside his coat, gone grey with handling, and straightened it on his thumb the way a man straightens a thing he means to trust. The knots stood at the lengths a man’s arm could judge. He turned the cord once so they stood in the small light off the water, and the boy saw the shape of them without reading them, which was the whole of what the cord was for. The reed-cutter tied one low down with his teeth and a pull of the thumb, bit the end level, and put the cord back inside his coat. He did not show it to anyone and he did not count it where anyone could hear him count.

A weir woman with salt dried white at her wrists came up the boards and asked how many had gone up yesterday.

“You can ask the cord,” the reed-cutter said.

“I can’t read a cord.”

“No more can the Court.” He took up his bundle and went to lift a trap, and the question went along with him and down into the water and did not come up again with the eels.

No figure came out of it. No knot was counted and no name was given, and the boy did not lay the cord across the place in the worked strips where a hand had gone in with the seed and had not come out with the rest. A man’s tally of reeds was a man’s tally of reeds; an absent hand had no figure even in a cord that no Court thumb would read. He saw the cord as he had seen a chart closed with a neat line over a note nobody had opened — exact, complete, worth nothing to anyone and most of all to him, who could not have closed two inches on it or told a soul what it kept — and set it on a place of its own.

It was the third such place he kept. A woman at the eel boards had a grey wool cord at her waist and a second twist twisted twice beneath it, and when a figure came near her thumb went under her apron to the first and stayed longer than the figure required; neither knot had ever been counted by any thumb but her own, and neither cord had ever been carried upriver to Nine Pots or lifted at a door. Another woman’s knot-string lay on a board beside hers and did not touch it. Six pages in a little tin, written down in a second hand, lay turned writing-down toward a floorboard in a house he could not enter, and a round pewter tin above a bed nobody used kept its pale fold and its thumb-bright lid shut through more mornings than anybody kept count of. No tin was opened. No box was opened. Every record in the district was kept in a material no Court thumb could spell, and none of them touched another.

Orra Weir stood at the lower boards with her board on its side and a cord at her waist, pulling one knot down with her thumb and setting it among the others. Her hands were like old rope and the pale eel scars lay uncovered across both forearms, and the grey streak showed first through the black hair when she turned her head, the way it had shown first at the blessing and every morning since. The nasal half-song of the Fen came into her voice where she was tired, and she had never entirely lost it. The weir woman with the salt-white wrists asked what the levy would take now the hands were out of the reeds.

“The ninth of Ilmen,” Orra said. “The collector’s boat came round the bend on the flood and had six oars. One man stood at the stern and never touched them.”

“That isn’t the levy.”

“It’s a date.” She pulled another knot down. “The river takes what the Court doesn’t. You can shout at either one. I’ve tried.”

A date given where a figure was asked for was an answer with the question taken out of it, a chart with the finding left off and the neat margin left in; the boy had learned to read the shape in a woman’s face before he could read the words on a slate, and he set her saying where it belonged and made no cause of it. Her thumb went to the cord again as the sentence ended, and whether that was a lie or only the habit of counting he could not tell, and he did not make a cause of that either.

When the reed-cutter lifted his bundle off the stone she watched what there was to watch at the edge of the shade and got no answer from the basket. Nobody asked her and she did not ask. The shape of the last knot on her cord was legible without its name — double turn, tail tucked, left over right, the small extra weight of a thumb — and the boy did not spend it. The word forty stayed where it had stayed since the year of the flower, unspoken in a morning that had a great many figures in it and not one he could enter. Her board lay beside the eel board and did not touch it.

Mira Silt worked the eels at the outer boards with pale slime dried in long grey strokes on her sleeves and river silt dried pale in the hollow of her neck, her head held a little to the right where the top of her left ear had been, her small smile kept on her face the way another woman keeps a door on the latch. A weir woman asked whether the run would be thin now that the hands were in the reeds.

“Thin? Who’s to say thin?” Mira said. “It comes when it comes. Ask the river.”

She flicked a string of eel-bones against her thumb while deciding whether to lie, moved two of them from hand to hand, and put one away.

A buyer in a coat with the river still in its hem named a figure for two pair. Mira’s thumb went under her apron to the grey cord at her waist and stayed there longer than the figure required, and when she shifted a creel to reach into a basket the second cord twisted twice beneath it showed, and neither knot was counted and the knot for the thin child upriver was not touched. “Two marks the pair and two short,” she said. “You’ve said it twice and I’ve counted it twice, and I’ll count it again after I’ve eaten. There’s nothing in the sentence that wants an answer.” She did not look into the basket. Her not-looking at the boards was a keeping, set on a place of its own and joined to no pricing; her board lay beside Orra’s and did not touch it.

Beyond the Cross the stalls had come up in the ordinary way, reeds laid over eels in one long noisy row where a buyer could not tell which hand had cut what, and the market sold over the top of a man with a slate as though a man with a slate were a thing every landing had. Below the north bridge at the Fishstairs, on the out-of-line landing where a step should have been, the soft-cuffed clerk was still set. His cuff was grey from washing and grey again by midday. He held his slate to his forearm for a measure nobody had called, and his hand went to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls, every time, so that the reaching came before the looking and the looking never changed the slate. Nobody stepped around him. Nobody asked what he was set there for.

A girl with a creel went over the landing sideways, caught the rail with the hand that was not carrying, and went on selling. Nobody made a fall of it. The boy put the order of the clerk’s hand on its own place, apart from the women’s hands at the boards and apart from the man who would come up the road with a clean thumb, and wrote no cause across any of them. A measure set down before anybody called it was a measure set down ahead of something; he had kept that as a shape for a hundred and twenty-five days and had never made a rule of it. The market’s noise went over the slate in a sheet, the way water went over a stone and left it where it was.

Berr Clay came up short off the clay road a little after midday with the red wool cord of his office folded under his coat and the green of tasted glaze at one corner of his mouth, hot clay over the smell of nothing at all, the most expensive smell in Grath. His hands were large and clean. He wiped his thumb twice on his apron before touching nothing and held a narrow slate unopened against his forearm, passed a finger over the figure and let his eyes rest on the writing a breath longer than the writing required, the way a man checks a dish he has set down to be sure it will not slop.

“A number goes into a slate, not into a bundle,” he said. “If your house wants it again, there’ll be a slate at the watch.”

He read one in twelve, as the notice says, low into the two hundred, called the levy and said it was not called by him. A man brought a number up out of a fist and asked what the reeds would cost now the hands were out of them. Berr told him there would be a slate at the watch — your house, never I — and took himself out of his own sentence as neatly as a surgeon takes a hand out of a field before the dressing goes on. The number went from the fist to his mouth to a shoulder going up the road and arrived nowhere. He did not look into the basket. He went off up the clay road toward High Kiln with the box in his house still locked and the true subtraction inside, believing he had done the district a kindness.

A figure read aloud was not the figure paid. A number put into a slate was not a number put into a strip. The boy kept the three things on three places — the cord in the man’s coat, the tally in the slate, the figure that went nowhere — and no hand of his could have laid one across another if his life had depended on it, which it did, though nobody under any roof in Sill would have known what the dependence was.

No slate was opened within reach of the basket. No sixth sighting of the neat dark hand came; the foundling was nowhere at the market’s edge, Sill held no collector anybody could have mistaken for a market woman, and the woman more mornings dead than any living person keeps count of was not named by any person in the morning. The reed-cutter took up his bundle and went along the boards, the clerk’s hand went to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls, and the market sold its reeds and its eels over the top of him as though a man with a slate were a thing a landing always had. Through the floor under blind ash the carried fire took its coal and gave back four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for a hundred and twenty-five days. Above him the boards kept four nails, four, and a fifth that was a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts in three places, and none became one. A man with an ear for vessels might have laid one gap across another and called them the same absence; he had spent two lives practising the refusal, and the refusal itself was worth nothing to anybody under this roof.

He turned out his file as a man lays instruments on a towel, in the dark, with the count standing at one hundred and twenty-five where one hundred and twenty-four had stood the day before. Every object was where he had left it. Six hearings of the full note stood beside and never among: five from four sources — a cup across a birth room, a pot through a wall, the district’s slate twice at the Cross, and a man giving at the Drawing — and the sixth, the small flat knock out of a woman as the men lifted, which came from a person and was filed beside the five and never counted with them. No seventh had come. He had not made one to fill the empty place. The bell that had gone across the square in the morning was a told bell, and it sat in its own place with the heard bell in another, and the two places did not touch.

Three near-full answers stood advanced and not paid: a cup across a birth room, a pot under a wheel-shed wall, a rack pot lifted a hand’s breadth too high and set down again. On another tray lay the intervals, three made in the yard and one made under this roof last night: the half-beat of a check that came behind a standing finger, the stroke that had gone on over the flagstones without the check, his own held breath from the market, and now the near-saying stopped at the threshold of the word — two objects and a stopped sentence on one tray and not a rule. An interval was not a payment and a near-saying was not a hearing, and neither was a seventh. The word that would call the loan had come up through the counting as water comes up through a crack and had stood on the threshold of the teeth with no figure under it and no name on it, and had gone back where it came from; here it lay as an object among objects, the same iron as the standing tongue of the damper, the bite that holds a vessel whole and the bite that can take a hand off a man, with no word in Grathic for both mercies. He wrote no cause across any of them. A likeness laid on a likeness grows a cause unless a hand holds them apart, and he had no hand that could reach two inches and no voice to say the thing that would make the holding plain. He kept them apart by knowing what each one was, which was the only instrument he had ever had and the only one he had never been able to lay down. The word forty stayed unspoken with its readable double turn kept without a name, a shape on a place of its own in a night that had a great many figures in it and not one he could enter.

The knife kept its six in a worn thumb-mark with the seventh at the very edge and still held; the breath in a man’s chest stayed unspent with the unforged collector sentence beside it; the near-saying stayed at the threshold and something must give at the end of the seventh year and had not given yet. He did not join them. He had spent two lives learning that a held thing is a kept thing and a kept thing is owed, and that the owing does not go away because nobody has said what it is.

One stiff brown-edged crust lay two inches from his ear in the fold, past smelling of anything, uneaten, unlifted and unnamed, with its empty place beside it still without a name and no third come to lie between them. The loaf shelf in the next room kept its two gaps in a room that did not touch this one, and the empty place at his ear was not the old gap and not the new one and was not laid across either by any finger of his. Two neighbours had laid the old gap and the empty place under one cause in a single breath and gone off with their pots, one naming the planting and one a child’s appetite, and he had set their sayings on places of their own and made neither his own. No fourth likeness had made a cause. No finger of his had drawn the line. The line was a thing a Court hand draws at the foot of a slate, and he had declined to become that hand in two lives and would decline it again in the dark.

The hand that had gone in with the seed held its place with no name, no cause, no body and no figure, and a slate would be found and a figure made if a hand came up in a month or a year, and nothing had come up. The reed-cutter’s cord stayed inside a man’s coat with its knots unread, a tally in wool or reed-fibre gone grey with handling that no Court thumb would spell, and it lay on a place of its own beside the grey cord at a woman’s waist and the second twist twisted twice beneath it, her board beside another woman’s knot-string and not touching it, six pages turned writing-down toward a floorboard, a round pewter tin above a bed nobody used with its thumb-bright lid shut. No tin was opened. No box was opened. The cold foot below the sill stayed the cold to one woman and weather to the yard, uncured, uncaused and unnamed, and the boy did not lay it across the other absences, because a likeness laid on a likeness grows a cause and there was no hand in this house to hold them apart but his, and his could not reach two inches.

Nobody under this roof said he was a hundred and twenty-five days old, four months and five days. He had stopped expecting to be told, which was not the same as not wanting it. The count went on edge to edge against the house’s loaves and glances and unnamed spaces, exact and unspent and spoken by nobody who would have known what it counted if they had said it, and went on into the day that would come after this one. The near-saying stayed at the threshold. The seventh year stayed held. Something must give at its end and had not given yet, and the loan that could hear was uncalled.

His small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down. In the other life it had closed on clamps and on a phone with a name he would not look at; here it could close on nothing at two inches, not on the crust, not on the pot set rim out and handle toward the reaching hand, not on the fold, not on a slate, not on a hand. The hand was his and small, and the count went on with it. The water went over water beyond the gap, the fire missed its fifth breath under the floor, the boards kept their knot where a fifth nail should have stood, and one hundred and twenty-five stood where one hundred and twenty-four had stood the night before, exact, complete, unspent and spoken by nobody. The dark went on keeping what it kept, and the count went on into Sill’s next morning with him.

The Chronicle of Chapter 28

Reed came on and the reeds went into the beds as the seed had, judged by the arm, never by a figure. The hands that cut them went uncounted. The yard went around the clean dark line, the grey named the thaw, and the place where a hand went in on Seed 15 and did not come back: a look, a wide step, no number. Sella Vail sent the woman who asked what the reeds cost to the pot --- the house has said it --- and carried the boy at the half-pace. One hundred and twenty-three days old, he read the going-around as a chart closed over a note nobody had opened: exact, complete, worth nothing.

Under the roof the shelf kept its two unnamed gaps in a room the fold did not touch, its one crust and its empty place, and no third came. Sella told the fire it was the thaw and said The house will keep it. Men came before first grey into the back room; Jorra Vail gave them no name, came through with grey dust ground into both forearms and told a pot You’ll do. His bent finger went into the cold damper ahead of Sella’s unfinished sentence. Tarn took the broom only after it was instructed; his seventh year stood held, six years in a worn thumb-mark and the seventh where the house could not check it, and his stroke went on without the half-check.

That evening Tarn brought the sentence to the threshold of the word that would call the loan --- that an ear is worth more than a debt --- and stopped there: no figure, no name, the loan uncalled. His held breath stayed unspent. The boy filed the stopped sentence on the interval tray, an interval and not a payment. A reed-cutter’s tally went past in a grey cord --- You can ask the cord; No more can the Court --- its knots unread. Orra Weir gave a date for a figure, Mira Silt answered a thin run with Ask the river, and the clerk sat for a measure nobody called while the market sold over him. Berr Clay read one in twelve and took himself out of his sentence with your house, never I.

That evening Sella set him on his bed, put the fold right, left the thread frozen, told the fire it was the thaw and went to her own bed. The door stood a finger’s width open: the river’s note came through the gap, the fire missed its fifth breath, and the boards kept a knot. Three counts in three places; none became one. He turned out his file and found every object in its place --- six hearings with no seventh, three answers unpaid, the near-saying on the tray. Nobody said he was a hundred and twenty-five days old. His small hand went up an inch toward the water and came down; the count went on, unspent and spoken by nobody.
