The Sweeping Around

Days 75–77 · Silt 16–18, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, Sill

A boy turns his head toward a pot before the knuckle falls, and another turns it a breath later and calls it weather. The loaf is warm. Nobody says who paid for the flour.

— A saying of the well-lip, Low Kiln

— Day 75 —

Silt 16, Y.H. 813

The mud of the Clay Yard held the print of every boot that crossed it and kept a little of each before letting go, the way a closed hand keeps the shape of a thing it has held and is slow to open. On Silt 16 the water was out of the ruts and the shallow pans the slip pits had worn, and what it left was this: a brown skin over the trodden ash that took a boot to the ankle and gave it back with a small wet sound, reluctant, as though the ground had reasons of its own for keeping a foot. The smell of the place was wet wool and river silt and, under it, the sour mineral tang of the corner nobody swept. Corin was carried out before the light had settled which way the day would go, level in the basket as a pot filled that must not be slopped, with Sella Vail’s knee turned against the wicker to keep the draught off his ears. She gave the broom its instruction before she gave it to anybody. “Go around,” she said to the broom. “It cost enough to come in. It’ll cost more to take out.” She said the house where another woman would have said I, kept her half-pace from every open slate, and did not touch the widow’s mark at her throat, because a yard is not an opening and a basket is not a door.

The yard was counting what the water had taken, and it did not count in figures. A woman at the tubs moved a loaf along a shelf and left a space at the row’s end where another loaf had stood, and she did not call the space a number, because a number was the one thing the yard would not give. A man come up from the lower houses said the flood had taken three days’ baking and a row of pots and the board off the weir-steps, and the woman with the loaf said it had taken a good deal, and that was the whole of the account. Tarn, going over the ground with his broom, said yes to the good deal and yes to the three days’ baking, and agreed with both so entirely that no argument could find a foot in it. The number went from a fist to a mouth to a shoulder and arrived nowhere, as it had arrived nowhere all through the flood, and the boy, seventy-five days old and carried at the half-pace, kept it exactly on the tray where the house kept it badly. He had the printed figure and the figure nobody spoke and the figure the water itself would have written if a hand had been there to hold the slate, and he let the three lie where they lay. He had learned in a better-lit room what a man can do with a figure he wants too much.

The mud was what recovery looked like here: not a thing mended, not a thing entered, but a thing gone around. In the rooms he had once known a chart closed with a neat line and a signature at the foot, and the ward went on. This yard had closed its chart the same way, in loaves and glances and the space at the row’s end, and gone back to its buckets. He had watched a quarter of a town held near its capacity and let down again, and nothing that had been unsettled before the water came had been settled since; the flood had only moved the things already lying about into new places in the mud. He set the thought down as an object and did not make a rule of it.

To the yard the going of the water was the end of a nuisance. To the boy it was the reading of a vessel that had been held near its capacity for eight days and had not, in the end, gone over. He had heard the note of the whole district under strain — a low turning of many small failures inside one slow certain one — and now the note was going out of the mud as quietly as it had come up through it, and nobody would say what it had been. There was no word in Grathic for it and no hand to point at the place where it had been, so he filed the going beside the coming and wrote no cause across either. The work of deciding which bodies are worked on and which are waited on had once been done around him; here it was done for a whole quarter, and the waiting had a note, and the note had no name.

Below the sill the thing the water left was going past him in the mud and not stopping. A woman come up from the bank had wrapped a child’s foot twice in wool the day the water fell, crossed the fold over the instep, pressed it flat between her palms the way a woman presses a dressing she means to hold, and called it the cold. A woman passing with a bucket had called the water weather, and the two facts had gone past each other that morning and gone past each other again now, in the same mud, and never met. The first woman had gone back to her tub without looking at the foot again. The boy read the extremity going before the core, the blood going back too fast and too cold, the afterdrop waiting on the far side of a warming nobody would perform, and knew what to do and had no word and no hand and no standing to put on the shin. He carried the wool and the dark foot on a place of their own with no name, no cause and no cure, and did not lay the woman’s not-going under the roof beside it as a cause. Reading it was worth nothing, and it was the whole of what he could do.

At the Vail corner the Ash ridge had turned its corner for a year and two days and left a clean dark line that the flood had stood a hand’s breadth short of and gone around, and the line lay there still. Beside it a new hairline of grey, finer than the old, had gathered in the week of the water and lay unswept because the house said it was the thaw and it would go when it went. Sella stood over the two lines with the broom in her hand and swept neither. She said the proverb to a pot with no crack in it — leave the crack showing; people trust a thing they can see has been handled — and the saying had outlived the thing it was true about and cost more now that there was nobody to say it in his own voice. Garl Vail’s debt against a firing of tiles lay where it lay, neither paid nor settled nor done with, and his salt-stiff coat held a broader man’s shape on its peg inside the Vail kiln-house door and his wheel-seat in the Vail wheel-shed kept the hollow on the left that nobody filled. Under the lowest of the drying racks was the space where whole slate should have stood, and the yard went around it as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and entered it by nobody. The boy set the standing line, the finer grey, the debt and the space under the rack in four places that did not touch, and made no doctrine of a thing that had simply not been done.

❦

At the yard well the rope had mud ground into it to the last strand and the stone lip was wet where it had been dry two days ago. A woman letting the bucket down spoke to the bucket about the skin on the water and not to the boy, and another said the skin had thickened in the night and would hold a poured spoon, and the first said it would not, and both went on drawing. A third said the rope had mud enough in it to scour a body’s hands and let the bucket go anyway. Sella set the basket down out of the draught and turned it with her knee so the light fell where she wanted it. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. The instruction went to the wool, the fire and the bucket, and never to the basket. She said the house, and went wide around a slate laid out to dry at the well’s edge, as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and did not touch the widow’s mark at her throat, because a well is not an opening.

Two men come up from the lower houses stood with their fists and gave the number of loaves the water had taken, and a third man with a mouth for it passed it on, and a woman with a shoulder for it carried it away toward the racks, and it arrived nowhere. A woman come back from the Fishstairs said the soft-cuffed clerk was still set there with his slate for a measure nobody called, his hand to his slate before his eyes to the stalls, and nobody had taken him off; and that was answered with the weather and gone into it. The yard had one word that let a person go home warm, and the word had done more work in eight days than the brooms had.

Cold came at cheek, temple, wrist, in that order, and the wool rode back from his wrist so the cold found the place it wanted. He wanted the crust at his ear and the breast that was not given and the bucket two inches from his hand, and could reach none of them. The adult in him read the small vessel going at its extremities before the core admitted to it, the afterdrop waiting on the far side of any warming he could not perform; the child in him was hungry, and the child, as usual, was louder. He put the argument where he put everything he could not spend, and kept the two registers apart as he kept the river’s note apart from the fire’s gap. One brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold at his ear with the empty place beside it still unnamed, and had gone past smelling of anything weeks ago and was no longer food and had never been a sermon.

❦

Under the Vail roof the carried fire lay under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees and missing one breath in every four. Four, four and a gap. Above it the boards kept four nails on the near board and four on the next and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts did not meet, and never had, and nobody had thought to enter the gap in either. He could not tell from the basket whether the fire had missed its breath since morning, but he carried the shape of the missing breath the way he had once known a pulse that stopped between the second beat and the third: not the absence of a sound but the presence of a place where a sound should have stood.

The wheel in the shed turned under a hand the boy could not see and gave its note once past the door, and Tarn’s broom went over the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector without once turning along it, and his head did not turn at the note. The small knife was on its sill with six years standing in a mark worn almost flat by his thumb and the seventh nearly gone and still held back, the one entry in this house the house could not check. He told the empty yard where the knife was and did not tell it what he was counting, and the loan that can hear stayed uncalled. Two silences under one roof kept their equality without a word between them, and the boy kept Tarn’s beside his own and did not let the two touch.

Six hearings stayed six, five from four sources and one from a person, and no seventh came; three near-full answers stood on their own tray, advanced and not paid; the word stood on a place of its own with the readable shape of the double turn beside it and the weight of a thumb on wicker apart from both, and was not spoken. The district’s note, the falling water’s drag, the cold foot and the space under the rack lay each on its own place, and no cause was written across any of them.

When the water came the file had gained the district’s note and nothing else; the days since had added a cold foot that was called weather, a heresy nobody had spoken, a space under a rack entered by nobody, and a line left standing and called kept. None of it could be spent and none of it had been cured. The yard had turned eight days of water into loaves and glances and a word that let a person go home warm; the boy’s private account had grown by four objects and a number he could not give to anybody. He kept them as he kept the crust in the fold, without a name and without a hand to put them on a slate.

Midday came on without a figure. Sella went back to the two lines in the corner with the broom, and the bristles went around the clean dark line and around the finer grey beside it and took up neither, and she swept the mud on either side of them instead. The boy was carried past at the half-pace with one crust in his fold and the empty place beside it. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the mud and came down. The count stood at seventy-five and was not spent, and the yard went on around the things it had left where they lay.

❦

The afternoon came on in the mud and did not improve it. Water stood in the shallow pans the slip pits had worn and in the prints of boots, and the yard went about its work with its skirts gathered and its mind on other things, the way a woman steps over a puddle she has already decided not to mention. Corin was carried back to the yard well in the basket with the fold at his ear and one brown-edged crust in it and the empty place beside the crust still carrying no name. Sella Vail had turned the basket with her knee so the light fell where she wanted it, and she kept the half-pace from every open slate as she had kept it for seventy-five days, and said the house where another woman would have said I.

At the well the rope had mud ground into it to the last strand. A kiln-hand with slip dried pale in the creases of his wrists let the bucket down and said to the bucket, “They say the water took three days’ baking and a row of pots and a—”

“—and a good deal,” said a voice at his elbow, quick and warm and affectionate. “That’s the whole of the account. Everyone knows it. I’m only saying.”

The yard laughed. The kiln-hand laughed too and went on letting the bucket down, and the woman at the rope said something about the mud scouring a body’s hands clean and did not finish it either, because the voice at her elbow had finished it for her before she got to the end, in the same warm tone, and they laughed again.

It was a small child in clothes that had belonged to somebody else and had been made to fit by being put on: a coat with the shoulders of a man and the sleeves turned back twice, breeches cut off at the knee with the raw edge left showing, worn like a uniform by a boy who had never been measured for one. There was a gap where a front tooth had been and he grinned through it, and the grin went into every circle of the yard ahead of him and came out with permission to say anything. He smelled of other people’s hearths and small ale, of river silt and the warm stone of kitchens where he was always welcome and never at home. On a string at his neck he carried a sliver of green lead-glaze, and his thumb went to it and rubbed it while he decided what a person wanted to hear, which was usually what the person had already said they wanted and a little more. He said what everybody was thinking and said it in a voice that made it harmless, the way a child repeats a hard word in a kitchen before he knows it is a hard word. He never said I want. He said they say, and everyone knows, and your house wouldn’t mind, so that a refusal could be given to nobody in particular.

The boy in the basket watched him the way he watched everything he could not reach, with the attention of a man who had once stood at the edge of a crowd and sorted what was useful from what was only noise. He had known people who could say a hard thing first and smile, so that the thing went down easy, and he had learned in a better-lit room what three likenesses laid in a row will do to a man who wants an answer. He set the likeness on a tray of its own and did not write a cause across it. The child was quick; that was all, and quickness was not a key that opened anything.

A woman came to the well-lip with a pot in her hands and set it down. The boy’s head went toward it while it was still in her hands, before her knuckle came down on the wet stone, before the note; his head went where the sound would be, as it had gone for seventy-five days, and the small animal sound came in his throat when he went too far and he did not care. The pot gave its full note under the knuckle, clear and whole, and the woman said something to it about not leaving it near the edge and went back to her bucket.

The child’s head went toward the pot too. It went a half-beat after the boy’s, after the knuckle and after the note, and then it stopped and came back, and his thumb went to the green sliver at his neck and rubbed it, and he grinned through the gap in his teeth. He could not have said why he had turned; there was nothing at the well that wanted his attention, and nobody had called, and the pot had already given and gone quiet. He was as surprised as anybody. He rubbed the sliver once more, put the grin away, and went back to watching the grown faces.

For the boy in the basket the half-beat lay where the intervals lay, on a place of its own with no cause written across it. It was not a hearing: the child had turned after the note, not before it, and a thing that comes after the knock is a different object from a thing that comes before it, even when the heads that turn are the same shape. It was not a seventh hearing, and he did not set it among the six or let it stand in a row with the other likenesses. He kept his own turning and the child’s turning side by side on the tray and did not let the two touch. The child had turned after him. That was all it was. It was not nothing, and he left it where it lay.

❦

A man came down from Slate Row and stood at the edge of the well with his hands loose at his sides, and nobody asked him what he wanted and he did not say. His name was on no roll the boy had ever heard read, and there was no mark on him that any yard would have entered: no cord of office at his wrist, no slate under his arm, no smell of the pits or the fish-trade. He had the soft hands of a man who sits at a table and the patient face of a man who can wait in a doorway for as long as it takes. The boy had seen that face across a counter in a better-lit room, waiting while somebody else decided what a person was worth, and he set it beside the likeness of the quick child and did not bring the two together. The man held out a loaf.

It was warm. The boy knew it before a hand touched it, from the small breath of steam going up where the man’s thumb had pressed the crust and from the smell that came off it under the river smell of the well: barley and a fire that somebody else had fed. The crust had a brown edge and a split along the side where it had been pulled apart, and the man held it easily, the way a man holds a thing he has handled often and expects to be taken.

The child saw the loaf and his mouth opened on a sentence. It had the shape all his sentences had — everyone, or your house — and then it stopped where a want would have stood, because he had no form of words for a want that was his own and he was too quick a child to say a thing that could be refused to him by name. The man waited. The yard went on drawing water. The sentence did not finish itself, and the man put the loaf into the child’s hand.

There was no slate between them. No mark was laid on the stone and no writing was shown and no figure passed from hand to hand; a keeping changed hands with nothing to enter it, and the loaf went into the pocket of the cast-off coat, which held a bit of string and nothing that any clerk could read. The man said something low that did not carry, and the child nodded as though he had understood it, and the man went away up the yard with his soft hands loose at his sides. The child went back to the well with the warmth against his ribs and the gap in his grin showing, and rubbed the green sliver with his thumb, and rubbed it again.

The boy in the basket kept the shape of it. He had been entered in three accounts that had never asked him, and here was a fourth thing that went the other way: value had gone from a man to a child and no slate had stood in the middle of it, and nobody in the yard had looked toward the basket to see whether it had been read. He had once watched a hand go to a record before it went to a person, and had learned to know the order of a room by it. This was a different order, and he set it on a place of its own beside the half-beat, and entered it nowhere. He did not call it a sale, because there was no Grathic word he had for the shape of a keeping that changed hands without a slate, and because a name laid on a thing too early is a way of closing the chart on it. The loaf was warm; the man’s name was on no roll; the child could not say why he had turned his head. He kept the three objects separate and spent none of them.

Sella turned the basket with her knee out of the draught off the brook, and the boy’s head came back from the pot. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. She gave the instruction to the wool and the bucket and the fire and never to the basket, and she said the house where another woman would have said I. At the well’s edge lay a slate set out to dry, and she went around it as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it over, and her hand did not go to the widow’s mark at her throat, because a well is not an opening and a basket is not a door. She did not look at the child, and did not look at the loaf in the child’s pocket, and did not connect the man from Slate Row with either one. If she saw the passing of the loaf she put it where she put the other things she did not enter, in the private roll nobody was shown, and she carried the basket up the yard at the half-pace without a word about it.

Under the Vail kiln-house roof Tarn had set the small knife on its sill before the light went, and told the empty yard where it was, as he had told it every morning of six years. Six years stood in a mark worn almost flat by his thumb and the seventh was nearly gone and still held back, the one entry in this house that the house could not check. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line in the corner, and no scrubbing lifted it, and the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back. His broom went over the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector, over and over, without once turning along it. A vessel gave its note past the door and his head did not turn, and he did not name the sound, and the boy did not call it a seventh hearing. The two ears kept their equality under the roof; the two silences were of one shape and nobody put a word between them. A loan that can hear is a loan that can be called, and the sentence had never been said in the house where it was truest, and the seventh year pressed at the edge of the sill and was held.

❦

Dusk came onto the Clay Yard without a figure, the way the flood had gone out of it. Sella carried him up at the half-pace and set him on his bed under the roof and not in the Debt Loft. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without looking at his face, and the thread at his mouth stayed frozen where it had frozen in the small hours and nobody wiped it, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left.

Under the floor the carried fire lay under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees and missing one breath in every four. Four, four and a gap. The boards above it kept four nails on the near plank and four on the next and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts did not meet and nobody had thought to enter the gap in either. The door stood a finger’s width open and the river’s low muttered note came through the gap, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, and he kept it where he had kept it for seventy-five days, apart from the fire’s gap and apart from the six hearings and apart from the half-beat that had come into the yard that afternoon.

He had wanted the crust and the breast and the bucket, and got none of them, and the child’s warm loaf went into a pocket that held no slate while he was carried up the yard with one crust in his fold and the empty place beside it still unnamed. He had seen a head turn toward a pot half a beat after another head turned, and a mouth open on a sentence that never finished, and a man whose name was on no roll give warmth to a child who could not say why he wanted it. He set the half-beat and the loaf and the unfinished sentence on their separate places and wrote no cause across any of them. The count stood at seventy-five, and there was no word for what he had seen, and no hand to put it on a slate.

Silt 17, Y.H. 813

The steps had come back in the night the way a mouth comes back after a blow, four and four and a landing where a step should have been, and the landing stood a hand’s breadth out of line with the rest. On Silt 17 the mud line the Ilm had left lay along the risers where the water had stood and gone, a brown edge the width of two fingers, and the market went up and down over it the way it had gone up and down before the river came, lifting skirts, setting pots on flat stones, making nothing of the gap. Nobody made a fall of it. A fall wanted a body on the stones and a name for the thing that had put it there, and the district had neither, so the landing stayed where it was and the feet that went over it went carefully and without comment, as feet go over a board they have been told not to trust. Corin, seventy-six days old, was carried down to the Fishstairs and into the shade under the north bridge in the basket, level as a pot filled that must not be slopped, with Sella Vail’s knee turned against the wicker to keep the draught off his ears. She gave every instruction to the wool and the fire and the bucket and never once to the basket. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool, and she said the house where another woman would have said I, and kept the half-pace from every open slate as she had kept it for seventy-six days.

The soft-cuffed clerk sat at the landing with his slate held to his forearm and his cuff gone the colour of river water from washing. He had been set there for a measure nobody had called and nobody had taken him off, and the eels went on being sold over the top of him. His hand went to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls, the order of a man whose record is waiting whether or not anybody comes to claim it. He did not speak; nothing in the morning wanted his voice. A woman came and laid a mark down on the stall in front of him and waited, shifting her weight from one foot to the other, and he did not lift his eyes from the slate, and she took the mark away again and went back to her eels. The measure he kept his place for was not called that morning either. The boy filed the order of reaching on the place where he kept all record-first hands: the woman in the grey coat writing at the flare, the man who had priced him from the block, the clerk behind the Court’s table, the Drawing collector with his ordinary hand and no pause in it. He kept the five apart and made no sixth of them, and he did not set the woman in the grey coat down into this landing, because a likeness is not a person and a hand going to a slate first is the commonest hand in Sill. The neat dark hand with the stop in its stroke had been seen five times and belonged to nobody he could name, and it had not come to the Fishstairs. He had learned in a better-lit room what a row of likenesses laid too close together will do to a man who wants an answer, and he did not lay them close.

Below the bridge Mira Silt worked the eels with pale slime dried on her sleeves and river silt in the hollow of her neck, her head carried a little to the right against the cold in the place where the top of her left ear had been. The small smile sat on her face the way a door sits on the latch, shut or open without anybody seeing the hand that moved it. She had a knife in one hand and an eel in the other, and neither waited for the market to decide anything. The eel slapped once on the board and lay there with its mouth working, and she turned it without looking. Cold fish and salt and tallow went ahead of her and stayed behind her; the boy took the smell in before he took the woman in, because that was the order of the world from the height of a basket.

A weir woman with salt white at her wrists came down the last step and asked whether the run would be thin now the water had gone down.

“Thin?” said Mira. “Who’s to say thin? It comes when it comes. Ask the river.”

She did not look up from the eel. She had answered the question and then her own answer, which left her the choice of which one the woman got to keep, and the woman went back up the steps with an answer that had cost her a question and gained her nothing. While she decided, Mira moved two eel-bones from hand to hand against her thumb, the little string of them clicking like dry seeds in a pod, and when the deciding was done she put one away in her pocket. The eel in her hand was cold and alive and did not care what had been decided over it.

A buyer in a coat that had dried into the shape of a wet man said a figure for a pair, and then said it again a little lower, as though a second saying might make it true.

“Two marks the pair,” the buyer said. “Nobody’s asked a pair of eels to carry a number all the way from the Weir.”

“Two marks the pair and two short,” said Mira, brisk, not looking at him. “You’ve said it twice and I’ve counted it twice, and I’ll count it again after I’ve eaten. Don’t trouble yourself. There’s nothing in the sentence that wants an answer.”

The buyer went away with the pair and the figure he had not won, and Mira went on with her knife. Her thumb went under her apron to the grey wool cord at her waist and stayed there while she decided whether the morning’s deciding was done. Shifting a creel to reach another eel showed the second cord twisted twice beneath the grey, and the boy saw the twist the way he saw the small wet sound his head made when it went too far — before he could place it and without any word in Grathic for what it was. That second cord had never gone upriver to Nine Pots and had never been lifted at a door. The knots on neither had been counted by any thumb but her own, and the knot for the thin child upriver was not touched now. Her board stood beside Orra Weir’s knot-string and did not touch it; the shape of the forty-first knot was legible from the basket without its name, and no number was spoken over the eels that morning.

The boy read the two cords as he would read two lines on a chart that had been turned face down: one kept what had been taken from a house, and one kept what had been kept back from a house, and which was which he could not have said, and nobody had written the legend. A Court’s slate could be taken up and read aloud and entered in a figure; wool could not be confiscated, because no officer would know to look under an apron for the second twist of a cord, and a knot held its entry without any ink to go wet in the rain. He had spent his life in rooms where the hand went to the record before it went to the person, and here was a woman who kept the most important account in the district in a material no clerk would think to call a record at all. He had no standing to ask her what either cord stood for, and no word in Grathic in which to ask it, and the not knowing did not make the reading less exact. It only made it worth nothing to anybody, least of all to him.

Nobody in the market spoke of the thin child upriver, and the knot that stood for that child was not touched, so the keeping went on in wool where no Court’s thumb would think to look for it. The boy set the not-speaking beside two others he had kept: Mira’s not-looking into the basket, which was a keeping, and Berr Clay’s taking himself out of his own sentence over the standing water and the figure, which was a different thing again. One omission left a thing out to save it; one left a thing out to hide it; one left the speaker out of his own account so that the account would look cleaner. Three omissions of one shape stood in three places and did not touch. He had no word in Grathic for a record kept by leaving something out of it, and the want of the word did not stop him reading the shape of it, and the reading bought him nothing he could spend.

Cold came in its known order — cheek, temple, wrist — and the wool did less than the wind wanted, riding back from his wrist so the cold found the place. He wanted the crust in the fold and the breast and the bucket, and could reach none of the three. One brown-edged crust lay stiff at his ear; the empty place beside it still had no name, and it had crossed the breaking and the flood without acquiring one. The market bell went back toward the Cross, four, four and a gap, and nobody crossed himself, and he kept the heard bell in the place where he always kept it and did not set it among the hearings.

Sella set the basket in the bridge’s shade where no slate lay and turned it with her knee so the light fell where she wanted it. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. She gave the instruction to the wool and never to the basket, said the house where another woman would have said I, bought eels and counted them with her eyes, and did not touch the widow’s mark at her throat, because a bridge’s shade is not an opening and a basket is not a door. She carried him home at dusk without a word about what had been said over the eels. That was the order of her reaching, and the boy had watched it in four rooms and knew it the way he knew the order of cold: the record before the person, always, even in a room where the person was a child who could not answer. Her nails were scrubbed to the quick and still had clay under them. She stood over the fold and pressed it flat at his shoulder with two fingers, and pressed it too long. A fold does not need that much pressing. Silt 18, Y.H. 813

The broken birth-slates lay under that space and were gone around with it, entered into nothing. A yard is not an opening. A rack is not a door. A house does not enter such a room in any roll it shows, and a basket is not set down in it. He kept the slit of floor on a place of its own and wrote no cause across it, because he had learned that a cause written across a room is the quickest way of never seeing the room again.

The dust had come back. It was worked into both forearms past the bend of his little finger, fine and grey, ground small enough to sit in the creases and stay there; it was not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel, which dried to the colour of old bone and cracked when it was rubbed. Two men had come in since first light and spoken low in the back room, and he gave the house no name for them, and the house asked for none. The boy held the dusty forearms and the clean ones of the day before on one tray as two states of one week, and did not ask which slate had been broken, or whether it was a birth-slate. He had learned in a better-lit room what three likenesses in a row will do to a man who wants an answer; he kept the two forearms as two and let them lie. Dust in the creases of a thrower’s arms is a record no officer would think to take up, and a record that lasts only as long as the arms carry it, which is the kind the district keeps best.

Jorra spoke to the wheel before he spoke to anyone. “It doesn’t care whose hands,” he said to the wheel. “It cares if they’re steady.” He set a pot up and told it, “You’ll do,” in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either, and the wheel took the weight of his hands the way a gate takes a hinge. He knew the full note of a Vail pot before it left the wheel — knew it in the wrist and not in the ear, the way a man knows his own weight in the dark — and would not say so before a kiln-master come up from High Kiln, who would call it early and make something of it. The boy kept the knowing beside the bent finger and the dust, where questions can last for years. He had no Grathic for the note and no standing to ask, and the not-asking did not make the knowing less exact. It only made it worth nothing to anybody, least of all to him.

She did not say the back room was cold. She did not ask about the dust. The little-door burn on the back of her hand closed on the empty air and opened again, and she turned the basket with her knee a hand’s breadth out of the draught without looking down into it.

He did not look toward the bed or into the basket. It lay where it lay, which in this house is a way of keeping a thing: not spoken, not entered, not carried to any door. Two ears under one roof heard what they heard, and two silences of the same shape kept their equality without a word between them. A loan that can hear is a loan that can be called. The river’s low muttered note was a fifth thing and not a hearing, and he kept it where he kept it, apart from the four, apart from the six, and apart from the fire’s gap that would come later through the floor. One thing in one place and another in another, for seventy-seven days, with no word in Grathic that would let him lay them side by side and call them one.

Into the file with these went a new shape that was not a hearing and not a number: a head turned toward a pot half a beat after the basket turned, a green sliver rubbed with a thumb, a warm loaf going into a pocket that held no slate. The shape of a keeping that changes hands with no slate. He entered it nowhere and wrote no cause across it, because the shape of a sale is not the sale, and a better-lit room had taught him the cost of calling a likeness a cause. It lay beside his own turning toward the pot and did not touch it.

One brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold at his ear and the empty place beside it was still unnamed. No third had come in three days of weather, and there was no word in Grathic for an absence that could not be answered. He wanted the crust and wanted the breast and wanted the bucket two inches beyond the reach of his hand, and the wanting was a thing the body did without asking him. The adult who had once sorted twenty injured people in ninety seconds and chosen who would be worked on and who would be waited on lay in a basket at the edge of a well and could not lift a hand to any of the three.

Sella’s hand, Alda Reed’s hand and Mira’s hand stayed on their tray and did not become a cause. A kindness that could not be answered was still an absence that could not be answered; the smoky crust had gone out of the fold on a day nobody would name and nobody said it was gone, and the hand that took it was entered nowhere. He kept the three hands as three hands. He had learned what a better-lit room does with three hands in a row, and he would not give the room the satisfaction of a fourth.

Near the well the argument about the skin on the water ended where it had begun, in weather, and a woman went off with her bucket as though she had been told something. Tarn went over the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector, over and over, without once turning along it, and told the empty yard where the small knife was, the way he had told it every morning of six years. Six years stood in a mark worn almost flat by his thumb, and the seventh was nearly gone and still held. Something must give at the end of it; the end was not written here, and he went on holding it as a man holds a breath he has decided not to spend yet, though his chest is already tight. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line, and no scrubbing lifted it, and he did not scrub at it this afternoon. A loan that can hear is a loan that can be called; the sentence had never been said under this roof and lay under it all the same, and the two silences under one roof kept their equality without a word between them. He did not name the sound when a vessel gave its note past the door, and his head did not turn.

Under the roof the carried fire lay where it had lain all week, under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees and missing one breath in every four. Four, four and a gap. The boards above kept four nails and four and a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts did not meet and nobody had thought to enter the gap in either. Nobody had said who had paid for the coal that kept the fire, and the payer went unnamed as the payer had gone unnamed for seventy-seven days; warmth came into the house from somewhere the boy could not see or reach, and he set the payer on a place of its own and wrote no cause across it. The fire took what it was given and gave back what it could, and the gap fell in the same place in the fourth breath, as regular as a fault in a stone, and nobody in the house had thought to count the one thing the boards had that the fire did not.

Garl Vail’s proverb about the crack went to a pot with no crack in it. The salt-stiff coat kept a broader man’s shape on its peg, the hollow seat kept its twenty-year lean, the clean dark line lay where it had lain and the new grey lay finer beside it, unswept and called thaw and left to go when it went. The debt against the firing of tiles remained neither paid nor settled nor done with, and went around another year as Sella went around Ash. The boy kept the coat, the seat, the line, the grey and the uncalled debt in five places that did not touch, and did not make a sixth of them.

Dusk came on without a bell for it. Sella carried him up at the half-pace from every open slate, set him on his bed under the Vail kiln-house roof and not in the Debt Loft, and put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without looking at his face. She left the thread at his mouth frozen and unwiped, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool, and the wool did less than the wind wanted, riding back from his wrist so the cold found the place.

The door stood a finger’s width open. Through the gap came the river’s low muttered note, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, and through the floor came the fire, four, four and a gap. The boy kept the two sounds in two places as he had kept them for seventy-seven days: the river’s note in its place and the fire’s gap in its place, and the six hearings in theirs, and the told bell beside the heard bell and both beside the early note and the kiln’s gap and the district’s new note, and the half-beat and the warm loaf on a place of their own, and the cold foot with no name, and the crust stiff in the fold with the empty place beside it still unnamed. Nothing had joined. Nothing had been paid. Nothing had been explained.

His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the water and came down. The count crossed into Silt 19 unspent.

The Chronicle of Chapter 16

The flood went out of the yard and left mud that took a boot and gave it back slowly. The sweeping went around the clean dark line, the finer grey and the space under the lowest rack. Sella Vail told the broom to go around before she told anybody and said the house where another woman would say I. The yard counted its losses in loaves, glances and a space at the row’s end nobody called a number; the figure went from a fist to a mouth to a shoulder and arrived nowhere. The boy kept it exactly.

At the well a small child in cast-offs finished a kiln-hand’s sentence and made the yard laugh. When a pot was set down the boy’s head went toward it before the knuckle; the child’s went too, a half-beat late, and he rubbed the green sliver at his neck and could not say why. A man from Slate Row, on no roll, held out a warm loaf. The child’s mouth opened on a sentence and did not finish it, and the loaf went into a pocket that held no slate. The boy kept the half-beat and the loaf on separate places and wrote no cause.

At the Fishstairs the steps came back four, four and a landing nobody made a fall of; the soft-cuffed clerk sat for an uncalled measure. Mira Silt answered a question with her own answer; when a buyer named a figure her thumb went under her apron to the grey cord, showing the second twisted twice beneath it, neither knot counted. Three doors down the tin above the bed kept its pale fold; the grey wool below held the stone’s hollow. Alda Reed laid her birth-slates down before turning to the fold, crossed two fingers at the flower unknowing and covered the sign with a date given the woman thirty-eight mornings dead. Warm and breathing, exact and not true; her sixth slate lay turned toward a floorboard.

Jorra Vail came through the wheel-shed door with grey slate dust past his little finger and named neither of the two men in the back room. He spoke to the wheel first and put his bent finger to the damper ahead of Sella’s sentence. Tarn’s knife lay on its sill, six years counted and the seventh held; his broom went over the collector’s road without turning. The new grey was not swept, called thaw.

At dusk Sella set him on his bed under the roof, put the fold right without looking at his face and left the thread at his mouth frozen and unwiped. The door stood a finger’s width open; the river’s note came through the gap and the fire’s four, four and a gap through the floor, kept apart. One crust lay stiff in the fold with the empty place beside it unnamed, and no third came. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the water and came down. The count crossed into Silt 19 unspent.
