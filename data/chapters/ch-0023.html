The Year He Does Not Count

Days 107–109 · Seed 18–20, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, Sill

A slate counts what a house is owed. A man counts what he owes the house. The two counts never meet, and the year between them is the one he cannot spend.

— A saying of the clay-bound, Low Kiln

— Day 107 —

Seed 18, Y.H. 813

Before first grey the small bench knife lay on its sill in the Vail wheel-shed, blade to the wall and handle out, the order Tarn kept even in the dark, and six years stood in a mark a thumb had worn almost flat while the seventh was nearly gone and still held. Sella Vail had carried the boy down before the yard stirred and set him at the edge of the light with the fold tucked under his ear and the wool riding back from one wrist, the way a woman sets a pot where it may be seen and not slopped. The house had not yet decided whether the day would be cold. Behind him the carried fire kept its own quiet account, four low pulls and the space where the fifth should have been, and nobody had yet told it the morning was come. On the sill the knife held the one entry in this house the house could not check.

Tarn came in with the dark still on his shoulders. He took the small knife up, turned it in his fingers and put it back where it could be found, telling the empty shed where it was as a man states the weather; a small tool went into his pocket and came out again, because a man who owns nothing should know where the small things are. Then he bent to the kindling and set his thumb along the worn mark the way a man sets a finger on a pulse to count it, and said to the wood, “Six counted. The next I don’t count.” The words went into the kindling and stayed there. His chest had been tight since the dark. When he reached up to the stack the breath did not go in at the top of the reach; there was a small interval there, the width of a breath, and he went on through it without spending the breath he had decided not to spend. The boy watched the interval come and go the way he watched a pot’s note come up to the rim of hearing and go back down, and put it on the place where such things were put.

Presently a vessel went past the shed door in somebody’s hands and gave its flat note — a whole pot, air under the note and nothing cracked in it — and Tarn’s head did not turn and he did not name the sound, any more than he had named it on the mornings when it came through the wall. The boy had once turned his own head before a pot was set down, before the note and before the knuckle that gave it; he had learned since to stop the turn where it started. He kept his stopping on one place and Tarn’s not-turning on another and wrote no word across the space between them. Two ears kept their silence under one roof, and an ear that can hear is worth more than the debt that holds a man, which is why the debt keeps it and why nobody under the roof says so.

Ash was worked into the creases of Tarn’s knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line out at the corner, and no scrubbing lifted it. The pale clip-ring at his left wrist, in place since Y.H. 806, held what light there was and gave it back, so that the wrist looked warmer than the hand. He was narrow and bent from six years in the slip pit, and people noticed first how quickly he moved and last that he stopped moving altogether when he was told he was doing well. He called Sella house and the yard house and the kindling house, and said I owe where another man would have said I am. When a woman at the yard well asked how long he had been with the house he said, “I owe six,” and shut his mouth on the seventh as a man shuts a fist on seed.

Sella did not give the broom to anybody until she had given the broom its instruction. “Over the road first,” she said to the broom, “then the Clay Yard, and let the line lie where it lies.” Only then did she let Tarn have it in his hands, and he took it as a man takes an instrument that has already been named. The yard had two voices on the thaw that morning. “It’ll come,” a man said, “and the strips want the sun on them before the tend’s out.” A woman at the racks said, “Let it come late. There’s a week yet before the paying, and a late thaw’s a longer week before it.” “Yes, house,” Tarn said to the early one, and “Yes, house,” to the late one, and agreed so entirely that the argument had nowhere to put its foot. He did not take either side; he stood with the broom in his hands while the two voices went past him the way weather goes past a wall, and the only thing that moved in him was the wanting of the late, which stayed where it was, because a late thaw puts off the paying, though the paying had stood in the ruts since the water went and had not stirred out of them.

The bristles went over the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector, over and over, and Tarn went over it with the broom and did not once turn along it. The ruts held the old water in their hollows and gave it back muddy at the edges. The road led down toward the Fishstairs and the north bridge and the houses where a man with a slate and a cold tube would come from; the bristles crossed the mouth of it again and again and went back across, and no collector came. He had been sent once in the Long Cold, had come back at the frozen ford and told the house it had been done, and kept that sentence where he kept the seventh year, entering neither. A loan that can hear is a loan that can be called. The sentence that would call it had not been said under this roof and lay under it all the same, like a tool set down in a dark room against a morning a man means to take it up.

Word came up from the worked strips east of Low Kiln that the planting went on: hands in the brown ground again, counted in glances and handfuls and never in a figure. A woman come up from the strips had silt dried pale on her shins and held up two fingers with chaff in the creases to show the seed was in. “They went in yesterday,” she said, and would not say what the fingers counted. “The strips want another tend,” the man beside her said, and looked at the ground a moment longer than the ground required, and the woman said the weather would have the last word. Neither put a figure to a hand or a strip, and the word went around the well as a direction goes, arriving at every ear and at no slate. Sella went at a half-pace from every open slate, gave the instruction to the bucket and the broom before she gave it to anybody and never to the basket, and went wide around the space under the lowest rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. She said the house where another woman would have said I, and did not take a broom to the two lines at the corner, because a strip and a well and a rack and a basket are not openings.

The basket sat at the edge of the work with the fold over him and the river wind off the Ilm coming under the wool. He could see the road and the yard and the strip beyond it as a direction carried and not yet a number, and kept them on the places he had made for them and did not lay one across another. A woman drawing at the well let the bucket down and said something about the cost of a wet thing set down in the wrong place. “Stand there,” Sella said to the bucket, “and come up quiet.” She gave the woman no figure. “The house has said it,” she said, which was true and was not a number. In a house that kept its days in loaves pushed along a shelf and in glances at a clay jar on a wall, the boy had learned to tell when a sentence had been given an instruction and when it had been given an answer. Nobody that morning gave either to the basket. His head went where it wanted under the fold and made the small wet sound, and the thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and no hand had been instructed about the thread.

At the going of the light Sella carried him up at the half-pace from every open slate and set him on his bed under the Vail kiln-house roof, not in the Debt Loft, and put the fold right at his ear without looking at his face. She told the fire it was the thaw, turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and fed it, and the fire took the coal and gave back four low pulls with the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for a hundred and seven days. She said the house where another woman would have said I and did not look toward the bed.

Tarn’s chest came back to him as an object laid on its own place: tight since the dark, with a breath held behind it and the small interval at the top of each reach where the breath should have gone in. He read it as a breath held against a clamp, which is an exact thing and worth nothing — the same iron that holds a vessel whole and will take the hand off a man, one bite with no word in Grathic for both mercies. He had spent his life reading what a held thing costs: a breath stopped behind a clamp is a breath spent whether it goes in or not, and the price is in the hand that holds the iron, not in the vessel it holds. He had known the order of a dozen such holds and had never once been the man who paid for the iron. Knowing the shape of the thing bought him nothing here. A loan that can hear is a loan that can be called, and the breath behind the tight chest was kept where the seventh year was kept, and neither was spent under this roof.

Somewhere off the bed the small bench knife lay on its sill with six years in a mark worn almost flat and the seventh nearly gone and still held, the one entry in this house the house could not check. The broom was set away after going over the collector’s road without once turning along it. Something must give at the end of the seventh year; it did not give here, and the breath pressed to its edge.

The door stood a finger’s width open. The river’s low muttered note came through the gap from the Ilm, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, and through the floor came the kiln’s breath, four, four and a gap under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, the two sounds in two separate places as for a hundred and seven days. Cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, and the wool rode back from the wrist so the cold found the bare place. He wanted the crust two inches from his ear, the breast, the bucket’s lip two paces off and the number of his days said aloud by somebody who would know what it counted, and could reach or ask for none of them. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down. He added one hundred and seven to the count and spent none of it, and the dark under the roof kept it without a figure.

Midday came into House Silt sideways, through a door that stood a finger’s width open, and Sella Vail set the basket just inside it at the edge of the light and away from every open slate. She set it down as a woman sets a pot where it will not be slopped and will not be stepped in, and then took her hands off it. She opened no slate. The errand that had carried her three doors down the Clay Yard was an errand to a room, and she did not turn it into a walk to Slate Row, though Slate Row lay up the road and most of what this room held would have read better there. She stood a half-pace off with her little-door burn shut on nothing, and gave the instruction to the wool. “Keep it off his ears. It’s the river wind.” The instruction went into the wool and not into the basket and not into the boy. He lay where a pot is laid, within reach of no hand.

The room had a bench under the door, a chipped cup on the shelf by the wall, and a grey ridge of Ash running along the skirting into the next room. Above the abandoned board-bed a round pewter tin kept its place, with a pale fold showing at the seam of the lid and a thumb’s bright rub worn into it. Below it the fold of grey wool lay as it had been laid and held only the hollow where the small pale river stone had once lain. Nobody in the room said that either thing was gone. In the back room a neighbour scrubbed at a board with lye water, and a low song came up through the floor with its words flattened out by work. From the height of a basket the shelf is the highest thing in a house, and the boy had entered the tin nowhere in three years.

Then Alda came.

She came short, the way a woman comes who has been called out of a room where somebody else has just been fed: soap-and-milk gone cold under river silt, lye sour under the soap, her nails scrubbed to the quick with clay still under them. Alda Reed had her birth-slates under her arm and a narrow slate of her own in the fold of her shawl. She crossed to the bench and laid the writing-down flat before she turned toward the fold. It was the order of a hand that reaches for the record before the person, and it did not vary; he had watched it from a basket at a market’s edge and from a bed, and had never once seen her reverse it. He had watched a senior man lay his hand on a chart before he looked at the patient under it, and had learned there what the order meant: the record was the thing that would still be there when the person was carried out. There was no word in Grathic for the order, and he had no voice to spend on it, and he saw it performed again now in a room where the person was not going to be carried out at all.

She put her hand to the fold at his shoulder and pressed it flat a breath longer than a fold requires. Not tucking it, not smoothing it: pressing it, the way a hand holds pressure on a graft until the vessel takes. The small weight of her palm came through the wool and stayed and went away. His head went where it wanted under the fold and made the small wet sound it made when it went too far, and she did not put it right, because the slate was already on the bench and the fold was already pressed and there was no instruction left over for a head.

Her hand went to the red edge of the birth-flower at his wrist. Two fingers crossed there, at the corner where the branching mark nearly reached the joint, before she knew she had done it. The sign was not in her face; it was in the hand, and the hand went on. Then, as a shutter comes down over a window, she covered the sign with a date. “Stone seven,” she said, to the bench, or to the fold, or to the room. “The account went up on the eighth, before the paper went out.”

The date laid itself over the sign the way a slate laid over a crack lays itself flat. It was the date she had given the woman who bore him, and it now stood over a death more mornings gone than any living person kept count of, and a woman uncollected whom no living person could correct. Where a name should have stood there stood a date. She had put it there for mercy three years ago and had gone on putting it there ever since, and it had curdled through to a fact that nobody alive could take off. The boy lay under the wool with the birth-flower going down his forearm and the date standing over it where he could not reach either, and he kept his finger off both.

“Warm and breathing,” she said. “That’s all I’ve set down.” It was exact. It was not true. He had no way to tell her so and no standing if he had, and she did not wait for an answer; she went on with dates after the question had stopped, because dates are a handle on a thing too large to carry: a cooper’s seventh in Mud End, a potter’s third child in Stonemouth, the last high water, the exact day of a birth in a house nobody in this room would ever enter. The dates came faster than dates had any business coming, and the room arranged itself around them the way a market arranges itself around a reading it has not understood.

Her hand went once inside her bodice to the little tin on its cord and came away without opening it. She took up the narrow slate from her shawl and wrote on it with a charcoal stub what she did not leave lying about, in the second hand no Court physician had ever seen, and turned it writing-down into the shawl toward a floorboard in a house the boy could not name. Six pages, all unread, written one at a time over years and never carried to a door; no seventh had been added and nothing was paid again today. Above the board-bed the pewter tin kept its pale fold and thumb-bright lid shut, and below it the hollow in the wool kept the shape of a stone nobody mentioned. Two private records in two materials stood in one room on two separate places, and a third thing lay at his wrist under a date, and the boy set all three apart and wrote no cause across the space between them. A likeness laid on a likeness will grow a cause whether or not anybody put one there. He had learned that in a better-lit room and had spent three years in this one keeping his finger off the places where it would grow.

She went out as she had come in, short, with the birth-slates under her arm, and Sella did not ask what had been written. A half-pace and a turned slate stood between the two women for the whole of the visit, which was the arrangement, and neither had put a word into it in three years. Sella lifted the basket level and carried him back out at the half-pace from every open slate, and did not look into it, and did not turn the errand into a walk to Slate Row on the way back either.

The afternoon went on at the loaf shelf under the Vail kiln-house roof, where the house kept its days badly: loaves pushed along a bare board, a glance at the clay jar on the wall, and a space at the row’s end that nobody called a number. A neighbour came in with a pot in her hand and reached past the board for a loaf. Her hand went to the empty place first and opened for the half-beat it takes to find what is not there, and then closed on the loaf beyond it. Only after that did her eyes go to the boy.

“That old place at the end came in with the planting,” she said, to the board, or to her pot, or to nobody. “A row comes up short and a body gets used to it.” She said it in one breath, the planting and the short row, and went off with her pot and never saw the empty place at his ear.

The boy filed the saying beside the one already waiting there. A neighbour had stood at this same board and named the old gap as a thing the planting brought in, and had gone off with her pot without seeing the place at his ear; this one said it again with a short row to finish it, and went off the same way. Two tellings of one absence, laid on two places and kept from touching, and no third came and no finger in Low Kiln had laid them together. There was a saying of the loaf-shelves that two gaps stay two gaps until a finger lays them together and an account then wants a name. No finger had laid these together. He had spent his whole life declining to be the clean hand at the foot of a figure, and he was not going to begin at a loaf shelf with a neighbour’s pot in the room.

The shelf kept its two unnamed gaps — the old space at the row’s end that came in with the planting, and the new palm’s breadth that had come in the night of Seed 6 with no scrape, crumb, print or word — in a room that did not touch the fold’s one stiff brown-edged crust and its empty place. Three absences in three places, and if a line were drawn through them it would be a cause with a signature at the foot of it. He kept his finger off the shelf and off the fold and the line was not drawn.

Sella moved her lips along the loaf row and gave nobody a figure. She glanced at the clay jar on the wall shelf, cold under its glaze, and did not touch it or explain it, and moved her lips back over the same row and came to the same place twice. “The house has said it,” she said to a woman who had asked, which was true and was not a number. She did not touch the widow’s mark at any hour of the afternoon, because a shelf and a strip and a bed are not openings, and she had lost a husband to that danger and had never once described it that way herself. Her private roll stayed unshown.

She told the fire it was the thaw and turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and fed it. The fire took the coal under blind ash and gave back four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, as it had for a hundred and seven days, its payer unnamed. Every warmth in the room had a payer and none of them stood in it to be thanked. She said the house where another woman would have said I and did not look toward the basket.

At the going of the light she carried him up at the half-pace from every open slate, going around the space under the lowest rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and set him on his bed under the roof and not in the Debt Loft. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without looking at his face and left the thread of drool at his mouth frozen and unwiped, because nobody had been given an instruction about the thread and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. The crust in the fold stayed stiff with its empty place unnamed and no third.

The keeping bought him nothing and kept the places clean, which was his exact and unspendable dignity. The date over the sign and the neighbour’s planting were two sayings on two places, and the two records in tin and slate stayed shut in a room he could not enter. There was no reach in the hand outside the wool for the crust two inches from his ear, no voice to ask for the number of his days, and no tool in the world that would have taken any of it out of a neighbour’s mouth and set it right.

The door stood a finger’s width open. The river’s low muttered note came through the gap from the Ilm, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, and through the floor came the kiln’s breath, four, four and a gap under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, the two sounds in two separate places as for a hundred and seven days. The boards overhead kept four nails and four and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts did not meet. Cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, and the wool rode back from the wrist so the cold found the bare place. He wanted the crust, the breast, the bucket’s lip two paces off, and the number of his days said aloud by somebody who would know what it counted, and could reach or ask for none of them. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down. The count stood at one hundred and seven, unspent, and the dark under the roof kept it without a figure.

Seed 19, Y.H. 813

Before first grey the room off the Vail wheel-shed gave back its dust the way a shut hand gives back nothing, and the door of it kept the finger’s width of open it had kept since the year before this one. Men had come up the Clay Yard in the dark with their collars high and gone in, and the low voices that came out of the room did not carry as far as the edge of the light where Sella Vail had set the boy in his basket. She set him the way a woman sets a pot where it may be seen and not slopped, the fold tucked under his ear and the wool ridden back from one wrist, and took her hands off him. The instruction went to the wool. “Keep it off his ears. It’s the river wind.” None went to the basket. None went to the men, who were not called men or anything else under this roof.

Jorra Vail gave the house no name for them. He did not say the room was cold. He came through the shed door with his eyes going where they always went — to the door, to the fire, to the wheel, and back without reporting — and the boy read the not-saying the way he had once read a chart turned face-down at the foot, where the note lay under the signature and the signature covered it. There was a shape to a keeping like that: it admitted no question because it had answered none. No word in Grathic held that particular silence, and he had no voice in any language to spend on it. He kept it as he kept the rest, on a place of its own, exact and worth nothing.

The dust came in on Jorra’s forearms. It was fine and grey and worked into the creases past the bend of his little finger, ground small as meal and rubbed in with the sweat of a hand that had been splitting slate; it was not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel. Slip dried pale and sat on top of the skin where any man could see it. This had gone into the skin and stayed. The boy kept the dusty forearms and the clean forearms of other mornings on one tray together, two states of one man’s working life, and asked neither which slate had been broken nor whether it had been a birth-slate. He had learned early in this house that a question spoken would travel further than the answer, and that the answer, if it came, would arrive in a hand he could not read and over a name he could not see. There was no reach in the small hand outside the wool for the wheel, no voice to put the question into the yard, and no standing from which to put it. He sorted the two states and filed them without a cause, the way he had once sorted twenty injured people by the single thing each of them would not say for himself.

Under the lowest rack the broken birth-slates lay where they had been laid, and the yard went around the space above them as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. Nobody entered the space. Nobody lifted the slates. Sella, carrying him down at the half-pace from every open slate, went around it without looking and gave the broom its instruction before she gave it to anybody, and the broom went where it was told and not into that space, and the household kept the place by leaving it alone, which is a kind of keeping no slate can read. It cost the yard a step each time and nobody counted the steps. The boy counted them and put them on the same tray as the dust.

Then Jorra spoke to the wheel before he spoke to anyone. “It doesn’t care whose hands. It cares if they’re steady.” He said it as he had said it a hundred mornings, to the wood and the clay and not to any person who might have used it, and the wheel gave back its low note under his hands and went on taking clay up out of the basket. To a pot going past on a bench he said, “You’ll do,” flat, with no welcome in it and no unkindness either. He knew the full note of a Vail pot before it left the wheel and would not say so before a kiln-master come up from High Kiln, who would call it early and make something of it. The shed smelled of damp clay and tallow and, on a bad wind off the kilns, the sweetish edge of lead glaze. The seat at the wheel kept its hollow on the left where Garl Vail had leaned for twenty years, and Jorra’s weight had not filled it in three, and nobody called attention to the difference. The boy knew the knowing in the young man’s hands the way he knew the bent finger: a thrower could hear a sound vessel before it left the wheel, and this one kept the hearing to himself because a man who said such a thing before a kiln-master would have it entered against him as a claim. He kept the knowing where questions could last for years, which in this house was most of them.

Sella had not finished telling him the fire was low. “The fire’s low. It wants — ” She did not get the rest out, because his bent finger had gone into the cold iron of the damper ahead of it, ahead of his hand and ahead of the word, and the iron took the cold out of his fingertip and gave it back a little warmer. By the time she could have finished, the instruction had arrived where it was going, at the pot and at the fire, and she gave the end of it there instead of to him. “Feed it,” she told the fire, and that was the whole of the sentence that had begun with her son’s name.

The household straightened around his silence the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted. Nobody said the room off the shed was cold. Nobody said the fire had been low. The correction had been given and taken in one movement with no word in it, an obedience that was also an answer neither of them would ever name, and the yard went on with its work as if nothing had been said, because nothing had. He had not answered her with a word and had not looked at her. The boy watched the order of it from the edge of the light: the instruction to the fire first, then to the pot, and the son last, and the son moving before the instruction ever reached him, so that the order was kept whole and overturned at the same time without a word spent on it. He had once watched a senior man do something like it with a chart and a resident, and had never seen it done more cleanly.

His head went where it wanted under the fold and made the small wet sound it made when it went too far, and nobody put it right, because the fold was already right and the wool was already off the ear and there was no instruction left over for a head. Cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, and the bare place at the wrist found the wind before the wool could be pulled back over it. He wanted the crust two inches from his ear and the breast and the bucket’s lip, and got the note of the wheel instead, which was worth nothing and went in anyway.

The boy read the iron once more as any iron that stops a thing from running. It was the same bite in the damper as in a clamp set across a vessel in a lit room: the bite that holds a thing whole is the bite that can take the hand off a man, and one piece of iron carried both mercies while the language he heard every day had no word to hold them together. Here they called it the damper and left it at that, which was not a poverty of the shed but of every mouth in it. He filed the reading as an object, exact and worth nothing, joined to no kiln. A man could know what the bite was and still not be able to spend the knowing, and there was nobody under this roof who would have paid him for it if he could have handed it over.

The promise of the foundling’s name to the men who burned slates was neither given nor withdrawn. It had stood in this house since the boy came under the roof and Jorra said the one sentence that sealed it: He’s not Vail. He had not looked toward the bed or into the basket this morning, and the sentence stayed where it had been put, as tightly as a lid nobody had tried to lift in three years. Two things lived in the same young man without either troubling the other — the most careful keeper of the Vail fire in four generations, and a boy who let the men who wanted every slate in Sill burn come into his mother’s back room and speak low. He had never once thought they could not live together. The boy, lying where a pot is laid and not addressed, kept the two facts on separate places and wrote no cause across them, because a cause drawn here would be a clean hand at the foot of a figure, and he had spent his whole life declining to be that hand.

Midday went over the yard without any change in its arithmetic. The clean dark line where the Ash ridge had turned lay where it had always lain, and the new finer grey beside it, finer than the old, lay unswept and was called the thaw and would go when it went. Sella stood over both lines with a broom in her hand and swept neither. She gave the instruction to the broom and the pot and the fire and never to the basket, and said the house where another woman would have said I. She did not touch the widow’s mark at any hour, because a wheel-shed and a rack and a yard and a basket are not openings, and she had lost a husband to that danger and had never once described it that way herself. Her private roll stayed unshown. The yard went around the space under the rack again, and the steps were not counted by anybody else.

At the going of the light she carried him up at the half-pace from every open slate, going around the space under the rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and set him on his bed under the Vail kiln-house roof and not in the Debt Loft. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without looking at his face and left the thread of drool at his mouth frozen and unwiped, because nobody had been given an instruction about the thread and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. The one crust in the fold stayed stiff with its empty place unnamed and no third, and the new grey in the corner stayed where it lay, unswept and called thaw.

He turned out the private file the way a man turns out instruments on a towel and found it unchanged. Six hearings of the full note stood beside and never among, five from four sources and the sixth out of a person the yard had lifted, and no seventh came on Seed 19; three near-full answers stood on a tray of their own, advanced and not paid; three intervals lay on another; the word forty remained a saying with the readable double turn of a cord twisted twice beneath a grey one beside it and the weight of a thumb on wicker apart from both; four low things kept their four places and the river’s note of water over water was a fifth on a place of its own and not a hearing; the two tellings of the shut counting room, nose-breath against throat-breath, both saying not the war’s hand, stayed joined to no kiln with no third; the damper’s double bite lay filed as an object; and the loan that can hear was still uncalled. He added one hundred and eight to the count and spent none of it.

Account-day came up over the Cross without anybody calling it, the tenth day of the tend and a market-day as all the tenth days were, and the square had been wet since first grey with the mud the pigs had carried in. Sella Vail brought the boy down at the half-pace from every open slate, going around the boards where a clerk had set a figure out to dry as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and set the basket in the moot-post’s thin shade before the crowd thickened. She gave the instruction to the wool and to the wind and never to the basket. “Keep it off his ears. It’s the river wind.” Then she took her hands off it and stood a half-pace away with the little-door burn shut on nothing, the stance of a woman who has set a pot down where it may be seen and not slopped and will not be the one to say what is in it. Beyond the post the barley fields lay open to the morning, the brown worked skin of the strips east of Low Kiln holding the boot-print and the stone and the new finer grey, and the hands that had gone into it on Seed 15 were gone now, leaving the ground with whatever figure the ground had kept.

The reading block stood dark along its lower edges where eight pigs had gone over it since first grey, and the straw lay trampled into the seams behind them. The pigs had been driven off into the noise beyond the post, but their going had left figures standing the way a cart leaves a rut, and the men who had driven them stood about with their hands on their knees and said nothing about which eight they meant. At the Slate Row fringe of the square a Court table had been set out in the open air, and a clerk wrote a figure down in a second hand, his hand going to the slate before his eyes went to the rail and then to his mouth. Two hundred people leaned toward the block in the top of the morning, and the market went on behind them with its own figures going from fist to mouth to shoulder and arriving nowhere. There were four ways out of the square: the road down to the south bridge and Slate Row, the steep street up to High Kiln, the north bridge, and the road toward the Bone Orchard, and on Account-day every one of them had somebody standing in it with a number he would not give. Corin lay at the edge of the shade with cold iron at his cheek where the post’s iron bracket had been bolted and left, and the cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, with the wool riding back from the wrist so the cold found the bare place. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had been told to wipe it. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the noise and came down.

He had learned to read a figure before he could read a word, and what he read now was not on the block. It was in the order of the day: the tenth of the tend, the date on the notice, a number that had been written down somewhere before anyone in the square had opened his mouth, and a crowd leaning in to hear a man read it low. The district had withheld its number in fistfuls all through Seed. A woman at the head of the first strip had let seed out a little at a time and would not say what the fist held, and had closed the fist again on nothing with chaff in its creases; a man with a barrow had shaped the number of hands, looked at the strip, thought better of it and shut his mouth, so that the figure went from the strip to his teeth and back to the strip and arrived nowhere. The boy had read the emptied fist as a chart closed with a neat line and a signature at the foot over a note that had not been read, exact, complete and worth nothing. Now the number was coming at them from the other direction, from a slate at a date, and nobody in the square thought to set the two beside each other and ask why a thing the ground would not give had been given so neatly from a piece of stone. A figure let fall from a fist into a strip was entered nowhere; a figure read from a slate at a date was entered everywhere, and the two were the same missing thing wearing different clothes.

Berr Clay came up to the block short, with the red wool cord of his office folded under his coat instead of worn across it and the green of tasted glaze at one corner of his mouth. He smelled of hot clay and, under it, nothing at all, which is the most expensive smell in Grath. His hands were large and clean. He wiped his thumb twice on his apron before he touched nothing, and held a narrow slate unopened against his forearm with the figure turned away, passing a finger over it and letting his eyes rest a breath longer than the writing required, the way a man checks a dish he has already set down to be sure it will not slop. He did not look toward the basket. He did not raise his voice.

“One in twelve, as the notice says,” he said low into the top of the crowd, and the crowd leaned harder to hear him, so that a man who might have shouted and been heard at the far end of the square was heard instead by everybody at the near end, which is a better kind of hearing and costs nothing. He read it twice, once for the moot and once for the households that had come late, and laid the figure where everyone could see it and nobody could challenge it. “The levy is called. It is not called by me.”

The sentence went around the square like a bucket passed from hand to hand, and nobody in it thought to ask who was carrying it. A woman near the post said one in twelve the way another woman might say the weather, and two men feeding the pigs said it after her, and by the time the boy had counted the sayings the figure had stopped being a number anybody owed and had become a thing the morning had always been. That grammar was the whole of his Account-day. A man takes his own hand out of his sentence as neatly as a surgeon takes his hand out of a field before the dressing goes on, and the sentence closes around the space he left without a seam showing. The boy had watched a midwife reach for the record before the person, a neighbour reach for a shelf before she looked at him, a clerk behind a Court table and a clerk set at the Fishstairs for a measure nobody called; here was the same order with the speaker taken out of it as well, and no word in Grathic for the two absences at once. He set the grammar on a tray of its own and did not join it to any of the others.

A man at the edge of the crowd brought a number up out of a fist and asked what the planting had cost now that hands had been in it. The fist had carried the number all the way from the strips and had let nothing out of it on the road; the man opened it as a man opens a hand to show a thing he has been keeping warm, and the thing was a figure nobody had said aloud. The crowd went quiet in the small way a market goes quiet when a man asks a figure in earshot of the man who has it. Berr did not look into the fist. He wiped his thumb once more, though it had been clean a moment before, and said, “A number goes into a slate, not into a strip. If the house wants it again, there’ll be a slate at the watch.”

Your house, he had said. Never I. He apologised in the form of an instruction, which is the only apology a Thane need make, and the man with the fist had nothing to answer because there was no speaker in the sentence to answer him.

The man shut his fist and went off with the number still in it, and the boy watched the order of it: strip, fist, mouth, slate, and the speaker gone out of the middle of it before the dressing went on. The number went up the road with Berr and arrived at the watch, which was a place and not a person and could not be asked why. Nothing in the worked strips changed its figure because a man had looked at it. The brown ground east of Low Kiln kept its boot-print and its stone and its finer grey, and the hands that had gone in by the look, in glances and handfuls and never in a figure, did not become a count because a slate had a date on it. A chart signed at the foot does not make the note above it true; it only makes the chart difficult to argue with. The boy knew there was a figure read aloud and a figure paid and that they were not the same; he knew the true subtraction was going over the top of the crowd unspoken and sitting in the locked box in Berr’s house up the road, and that the box had a key and the key had a pocket and the pocket had an owner who believed his own sentences. He could not read either figure and could not carry the box, and the knowing bought him exactly what the knowing had bought him on every Account-day of his life, which was nothing he could spend and one more thing he could not forget.

Garl Vail’s uncalled debt against the firing of tiles went around the space where a name might have stood and was not called. Garl’s salt-stiff coat kept his broader shape on its peg up the yard and his hollow seat kept its twenty-year lean, and neither of them had anything to say to the figure read over the ground where his son’s hands had once gone.

He was the only adult in the room and the least use in it. A man lays his instruments out in order because he means to put his hands into a body; the order is a promise of use, and the promise is most of the discipline. He had laid out a whole district’s kept things in order and there was no field to open, no clamp to set, no person who would let him carry any of it to a door. He knew what each thing was and what it would cost if a hand ever moved it, which was the whole of his usefulness and the whole of his punishment. The file was exact, complete, and worth nothing.

There was no word in Grathic in which anybody under this roof could say what a life would count. The house kept its days in loaves pushed along a board and in glances at a jar and in a space at the row’s end, and a boy carried under the roof for one hundred and nine mornings had no name for the thing he kept and no person to keep it with. He had learned long ago, in a room whose light was better than this, that a figure at the foot makes the note above it difficult to argue with and no truer. Here there was no chart and no foot and no signature, and the count went on being exact for nobody. Exactness kept in a room with no use for it is not knowledge. It is a habit with nowhere to put its hands.

In the fold one brown-edged crust lay stiff with its empty place unnamed and no third beside it. It had gone past smelling of barley and had not gone out of the fold; nobody had lifted it and nobody had said it was there. On the loaf shelf in the next room the row kept its two unnamed gaps, the old space at the row’s end that had come in with the planting and the new palm’s breadth that had come in the night of Seed 6 with no scrape, crumb, print or word, in a room that did not touch this one. Three absences, three places, no line drawn through them. A line drawn through three absences is a cause with a clean hand at the foot of it, and the clean hand was the thing he had spent two lives declining to become. He kept his finger off all three without thinking about it now, the way a man keeps his finger off a wound he has decided not to open.

Below the sill the child’s foot stayed dark inside three turns of wool. The cold was the cold to one woman and weather to the yard, uncured, uncaused and unnamed, and the two words had never once been let into the same sentence. He read the extremity going before the core as cleanly as he had ever read it across a table: the dark flesh, the almost-smile that is not peace, the afterdrop waiting on the far side of a warming nobody would perform. The reading was perfect and bought nothing. There was no hand in the room long enough to wind the wool tighter, and no word he could make that would tell one woman the thing she already knew and had chosen not to call by its name.

The clean dark line where Ash turned its corner lay where it had always lain, and the new finer grey beside it lay unswept and was called the thaw that would go when it went. He had watched a woman stand over the two lines with a broom in her hand and sweep neither; the broom went around them the way water goes around a stone set in a shallow pan. The space under the lowest rack had been gone around again that morning as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and the broken birth-slates beneath it were entered by nobody. No opening had come in three days, so the widow’s mark hung at her throat untouched and her private roll stayed unshown. A strip, a shelf, a basket, a road, a door and a bed are not openings; the rule held on Account-day as exactly as it held on any morning, and the mark was not for a reading, a carrying or a silence.

Before she went to the fire Sella moved her lips along the loaf shelf in the next room and gave nobody a figure. The clay jar stood cold under its glaze on the wall shelf and she glanced at it once, the way a woman checks a pot set down where it may be seen, and took nothing from it and said nothing about it. Her hand went over the bare board where the row had lost its two places and came away with no loaf and no number, and she went around the clean dark line and the finer grey on her way back with a care that looked like not seeing them at all. From the bed the boy watched the order of it: the hand to the shelf, the eye to the jar, the broom to the corner, and the fire last of all. It was the order he had watched all day in other rooms, from a man with a fist at the Cross to a clerk with a slate at his forearm, and here it ended in a woman counting what the house was owed in loaves and glances and a space nobody called a figure. She gave the instruction to the wool and the pot and the fire and never to the basket, and the basket lay under the roof with its fold put right and took no instruction at all.

Sella went to the fire. “It was the thaw,” she told it, and turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and fed them the way a woman answers a child who has asked the same question a third time. The fire took the coal under blind ash and gave back four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, as it had given for a hundred and nine days. Its payer had no name in this house and no figure on any slate she had ever shown him. “The house will keep it,” she said where another woman would have said I, and did not look toward the bed. The little-door burn shut on nothing as her hand came away from the pot, and the household, which was only a bed and a fire and a shelf in two rooms that did not touch, went on being kept.

She stood a moment with her hand on the back of the broken pot, counting something under her breath that no man could have taken up as a figure, and then set the pot where it could be found in the dark. The grey streak at her temple showed pale in the dying light off the coals and she pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carried the burn, and went on counting. Three nights she had once sat up with a child of her own who cooled while everybody was kind; the story had come into this yard from other mouths and never from hers, and she had never once told it near a bed. Tonight she told the fire the thaw and nothing else, and did not look toward the bed, and the room went on being a room in which nothing was said.

The door stood a finger’s width open. Through the gap came the river’s low muttered note from the Ilm, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, water over water with no vessel in it and no crack to find; and through the floor came the kiln’s breath, four, four and a gap under blind ash and the back of a broken pot. The two sounds came in two separate places and had come so for a hundred and nine days, and he kept them apart without effort now, the way a man keeps two charts on two tables and will not let the corners touch. The boards overhead kept four nails and four and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts did not meet and never had.

Cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, and the wool rode back from the wrist so the cold found the bare place. The small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down. He wanted the crust two inches from his ear, the breast, the bucket’s lip two paces off, and the number of his days said aloud by somebody who would know what it counted; he could reach none of them and ask for none of them, and the wanting was exact and went nowhere the way a figure written on a slate goes nowhere toward the strip it was taken from. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and nobody under this roof had been given an instruction about that sound either.

He had watched a man reach for a figure and take himself out of his sentence, and a woman reach for a slate before she turned to a fold, and a neighbour reach for a shelf before her eyes went to a child, and a clerk set his slate to his forearm for a measure nobody had called. Four orders, kept apart, no two joined. He had set them all down and carried none of them to a door, and now there was no door in this room except one standing a finger’s width open for the river and no person on the other side of it who would take a figure from a child who could not speak. The wanting sat where the hand could not go, which is the ordinary condition of a man who knows the order of things and has no standing to change it. He did not try. Not trying was the last exact thing he had left.

The dark kept the count without a figure. One brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold with its empty place unnamed and no third; the shelf in the other room kept two gaps; the thread at his mouth was frozen and unwiped; the wool rode back from the bare wrist. The river went over water beyond the finger’s-width gap and the fire missed its fifth breath under the floor, and the two counts did not meet. One hundred and nine stood where one hundred and eight had stood the day before; on the morrow one hundred and ten would stand in the same place, exact, unspent, and spoken by nobody. Somewhere off the bed the small bench knife lay on its sill with six years in a mark worn almost flat and the seventh nearly gone and still held, the one entry in this house the house could not check, and the loan that could hear waited under the roof for a sentence no man here would say.

The Chronicle of Chapter 23

On the eighteenth of Seed Tarn’s knife lay on its sill with six years worn almost flat and the seventh nearly gone and still held, one entry the house could not check; to the kindling he said “Six counted. The next I don’t count.” His chest had been tight since the dark, the breath unspent. He took the broom only after Sella Vail had instructed it, said “Yes, house” to an early thaw and a late, and swept the collector’s road without once turning along it. No collector came. The planting went on in glances and handfuls, never a figure. The boy read the held breath as a clamp’s, worth nothing, and added one hundred and seven.

Three doors down Alda laid her birth-slates on the bench before the fold, pressed the wool too long, crossed two fingers at the flower unknowing, and covered the sign with a dead woman’s date. “Warm and breathing” — exact and not true. At the loaf shelf a neighbour named the old gap as a thing the planting brought in and went off without seeing the empty place at his ear. On the nineteenth men spoke low in the room off the wheel-shed and Jorra Vail gave them no name; he came through with grey dust past the bent finger, put it to the cold damper ahead of Sella’s sentence, and the household straightened around his silence. The boy read the iron as the bite that holds and the bite that takes, with no word for both in Grathic.

At the dawn market Mira answered whether the run would be thin with her own answer, put her thumb to the grey cord when a figure was named and showed a second twisted twice, no knot counted, her board beside Orra’s. A foundling turned toward a pot half a beat after the boy’s, after the note, and rubbed the green sliver. On the twentieth, Account-day, Berr Clay wiped his thumb twice and read one in twelve over the worked ground: “The levy is called. It is not called by me,” and “A number goes into a slate, not into a strip.” The figure read was not the figure paid; the subtraction sat in a locked box up the road. The bell went four, four and a gap and nobody crossed himself.

Under the roof at the going of the light the boy turned out his file and found it unchanged — six hearings and no seventh, the word forty unspoken, the loan still uncalled. One crust lay stiff in the fold beside its unnamed empty place; the thread at his mouth was frozen. He was the only adult in the room and the least use in it. The river’s note came through the door and the kiln’s missing breath through the floor, two counts that did not meet. His hand went up an inch toward the water and came down. The count stood at one hundred and nine, unspent and spoken by nobody.
