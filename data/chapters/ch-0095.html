Days 336–338 · Ash 7–9, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, Sill

— A yard saying of Low Kiln, in the last tending days of Ash

— Day 336 —

Ash 7, Seventhday, Y.H. 813

The hand lay against the fold where it had been laid down on the two hundred and forty-sixth answer, as a man puts away a tool where the next hand will find it without looking. It was a small soft hand with milky nails and no strength in the wrist worth speaking of; it had once reduced a shoulder in a room that smelled of disinfectant and cold metal and had since learned the exact weight of a barley crust at two inches. In the small hours under the Vail kiln-house roof the boy lay awake and took the room as a surgeon takes a field before the light, by the small sounds first, because the small sounds do not lie and the large ones are mostly weather: the dry tick of clay cooling behind the wall, the settle of a coal under its ash, the river going low over lower under the finger’s-width door. Three hundred and thirty-six stood exact behind him, six months and one hundred and fifty-six days told by nobody in Low Kiln, Sill, Grath. Under the floor the carried fire gave four low pulls and kept the silence where the fifth should have stood, its payer unnamed three hundred and thirty-six days; the salt-stiff coat on its peg behind the door kept the shape of a man whose name had not been spoken under this roof the same number of days. Nothing from the fourth, the fifth or the sixth of Ash crossed the dark toward him as sign, lesson, mending, cause or payment. The joining said over the boards, the fourth shaping at the well and the turned writing of the runner stood each on a place of its own with a finger between, and were not joined, spent or promoted in the night. Six openings in six months were six and not a rule, and no seventh was made due on a day that had not earned one. The older women named the month’s middle days by a weather that had not done what it promised and the slatemen by their count of Account-days, and neither habit was set right in so many words. The Warm was thinning in the walls and would be called weather by everybody who passed the door, and entered nowhere, because a thing entered is a thing that wants a payer and none stood.

The inventory came in its old order, because an order is the one thing a field will give a man who cannot move in it. One stiff leathery brown-edged barley crust lay two inches from his ear, gone harder through the dry kiln heat, its corner standing a hair off the wool; the empty place beside it kept its name to itself and no third came in the small hours to make the row come out even. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen and was unwiped, because nobody under any roof had ever been given an instruction about the thread, and a thing nobody has been told to wipe is a thing that stays. Cold had come to cheek, temple and the small bare joint of the wrist where the wool rode back, and had stopped there as at the end of a line written out for it, with neither an order to go farther nor an order to go away; the Afterdrop waited beyond the stopping, past a warming nobody in this house would perform. He knew the face that cold puts on a man, the composed face that is not composure, the ease that is no ease; he knew a cold patient cannot clot and will seem better before he is worse, and must be warmed from the outside and slowly, never hurried, never rubbed, never set too near the fire. He knew the order in which a body takes heat back, the small joint first and the core last, and the order in which it gives it up, and that the pink returning to a face is the surface still cold and not the core. He wanted the crust lifted the two inches to his mouth, the thread wiped away, and a hand that would close on the pot handle and bring it within reach. Both were true and the second was louder and neither won. The argument went on under the wool without a verdict, as it had gone on through every morning of his life in this house: the surgeon taking the inventory exactly and entering it nowhere, the infant underneath taking none of it and wanting, the two of them quarrelling in a body that could not so much as lift its own chin from a fold. He had reduced a thousand shoulders and could not keep a crust in his own mouth. The joke lived in that and cost him nothing to keep, which was why it was the only one he kept.

First light came grey and ordinary, with no market, no Cross and no bell waiting on it. Sella Vail went to the hearth before she went to the basket, as she had gone before every basket since the boy came under the roof, and gave the instruction to the coals and not to a person. “It’s the thaw,” she told them, and turned them with the back of a broken pot, the pot’s edge drawing the ash into a small dark line that she did not smooth. She stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand and counted a small intake, a hold and a letting-go toward no opening, and gave neither gap a figure; the little-door burn on the back of her left hand opened and shut on nothing as the hand worked. “The house will keep it,” she said where another woman would have said I. The fire answered four low pulls with the silence where the fifth should have stood, and she did not supply the pull or look toward the floor to see whether anything stood there to supply it. One grey streak at her temple showed pale in the first of the light and she pushed the coal-black hair back with the burn-wrist, and the burn closed on the hair and then on air. Nothing in the room turned toward the bed. The boy heard the handle lift and the small sound of the wire taking the weight before the light showed him the hand that had lifted it, and took both sounds as findings: the small sounds first, because the small sounds do not lie and the large ones are mostly weather. He took the hearing as it came, four and a gap, a count in one place that would not rise into another, because agreeing any two of them would have wanted a payer put under both and none stood to either.

Her finger lay on top of the loaf shelf’s front edge as she passed, so that the charcoal tally underneath stayed unread, and her lips moved along a row standing with two gaps and gave neither gap a number. Her eye went once to the clay jar standing cold under lead glaze beside the shelf; her hand shut half over nothing, the little-door burn closing on air, and went on to the next thing. From the pot board she lifted the twice-mended pot with her thumb first where the wire crossed the crack, felt what the thumb had to feel, and only then let the weight come on the handle. “If it’s worth mending it’s worth mending twice. The second time is the real one,” she said to the pot; and to the fire, “Leave the crack showing. People trust a thing they can see has been handled.” The sayings went to a pot and a fire and never to a person, and had improved in the keeping in the three years since a man who mended pots and would not let anyone mend him had last said them, and were very likely not as good as he had said them. The hairline of the original break stood dark between the old-brass staple and the tighter wire and she left it showing, because a restored thing hides the hand that handled it and a mended thing leaves that hand showing. At the rack end of the day’s work the dressed grain lay turned under wool away from the hearth; she lifted a corner with a lighter touch than the day before and turned it once and gave no figure, and her fingers went in and came out and did not close, so that no handful was lifted. The pot’s rim went down two inches beyond the reaching hand, with a half-beat at the setting and no look toward the bed. The boy took the half-beat apart as he took everything apart, by what was there and by what was not: the distance between the hand’s intention and its arrest, a small exact interval that bought nothing at two inches of air and was not a gift, for a gift wants a giver and nobody had given.

The room went on about its ordinary work around him. A board knocked somewhere and was not mended today; a woman’s step went past the door and went on; the light came up through the finger’s-width opening and laid a thin grey bar on the floor that he could not see from where he lay. He took the ordinariness as the only honest record there was. He had stopped expecting anybody to tell him what a number counted, which was not the same as not wanting it, and the wanting went on under the expectation the way a coal goes on under ash. No comfort was given and none was taken. The surgeon took his small clean satisfaction in a field left as found, and the infant underneath took none of it and went on wanting the crust lifted and the thread wiped and a hand on the pot handle, both true, the second louder, neither winning, the two registers quarrelling without verdict in the growing light.

In the first full of the morning, with the river’s note low and going lower under the finger’s-width door, the hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the sound. It took a small intake from the room whose floor does not touch his, held the interval whole, let the intake go on the same low note, and came straight down and laid itself back against the fold as a man puts away a tool where the next hand will find it without looking. The note had done before the hand came down, so the letting-go was neither early again nor stayed, and the hand did not stay; it was the note’s own finishing that ended the interval and not any decision of the hand, and that was the whole of the honesty in it. The inch was not a reach and the intake was not a breath kept for warmth; the interval was not a stopping of the river and the note was not his. He filed it as answer two hundred and forty-seven: exact, complete, worth nothing at two inches of air, a restraint and not a payment, borrowing no house count and supplying no payer, laid across neither the six arithmetics, the bell’s gap, the covered grain, the handful, the thumb’s resting, the stayed object, the early letting-go, the sixth opening, the joining over the boards, the fourth shaping, the turned writing nor his own kept gaps. Two hundred and forty-seven honest answers stood on the sixth place, each an object and not a power, and neither the number nor the small hand-movings that had made it stood up and called themselves a doctrine, for a doctrine is an account that has found a payer and none stood.

Three hundred and thirty-six stood exact, complete, unspent and spoken by nobody in Low Kiln, Sill and Grath; six months and one hundred and fifty-six days were told by nobody; the carried fire’s fifth was unpaid the same number of days with its payer unnamed; and the name of the man the salt-stiff coat kept was unspoken the same number. She carried him level, at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, the way a woman carries a pot filled to the brim and does not slop it. The crust lay two inches from his ear in the dry cold, stiff and leathery, its corner standing a hair off the wool and the empty place beside it keeping its name to itself; the thread at his mouth had frozen and would stay frozen, because nobody under any roof had ever been given an instruction about the thread. She went around the yard as the yard went around itself, with a look and a wide step and no number. The three coverings stayed weather and were entered nowhere. The frost-bloom had gone off the well’s north lip on Bone 3 while the yard looked elsewhere, and it was not a sign now and had not been a sign then; the skin on the trough broke under the knock of a pail and closed again over what it had covered, and nobody lifted a finger to it; the silt-clouded plate on the flat stone stood where no foot went. A covering over a depth, the same covering over another depth, a piece of the depth lifted and set where no foot would go — three things on three places with a finger between every pair, called weather, and no fourth likeness made a cause. He had learned in this yard that things were covered and the covering was called weather, and that to name the covering was to ask who had put it there, and nobody asked; he had learned it the way a man learns the shape of a wound by not opening it.

A man come down from Cold Acre shifted a hare across his shoulders at the well, and a woman with a pot asked what the carrying had cost. He judged her by the arm and not by a figure, looked at the weather, and told her to ask the weather, which had carried the snow and could carry the price. He went off toward the clay road with the hare unweighed and no slate opened, and nobody followed him with a question. Another man with a cracked jar in a sling of rope asked what the month’s mending would want, and Sella told him to ask the pot, and the pot, being a pot, said nothing. A woman with a cost-question got the weather for hers and went away with it as she had come. Each was a direction judged by the carrying arm and the weather, and the boy read each as a chart left blank where the number should stand; he entered none of the blanks and supplied no payer for any of them.

A woman with a basket of kindling asked what the seventh year would want when it came, and two men mending a strip of fence said the thaw would be early and the thaw would be late. Tarn said “Yes, house” to the woman and “Yes, house” to each of the men, and kept the late, because a late thaw put off the paying. He agreed so thoroughly that there was nowhere for an argument to find a foot. Three days of the dark month went by in the Vail house with no boundary drawn and nothing opened, named, paid or ended. On the seventh he came into his three hundred and thirty-sixth day with one crust two inches off, the thread frozen, cold stopped at cheek, temple and wrist, and the afterdrop waiting past a warming nobody would perform.
