Before the Kiln

Days 180–182 · Ember 1–3, Y.H. 813 · The Vail kiln-house, Low Kiln

No man draws a line where a month turns. The hand goes to the mark before the kiln and to nothing after; the fire keeps four and wants a fifth, and the cost goes to the pot.

— A yard saying of Low Kiln, at the first firing of Ember

— Day 180 —

Ember 1, Y.H. 813 (Firstday)

Hot clay under tallow came into the room before the grey did, and wet wool under that, and under both the sour mineral breath of the corner nobody had swept in two years. It was the smell of the firing month and the smell of the kiln month at once, for the kilns had stood at their heat through Tallow and would stand at it through Ember and no hand in the district had drawn a line where one name turned into the other. The yard called Tallow the firing month and Ember the kiln month, and neither saying was wrong and neither had been corrected; the heat went on behind the wall under both names the way a man’s breath goes on while he is deciding what to call the weather. The smell settled into the fold where the boy lay and stayed there. He was one hundred and eighty days old this morning and six months exactly, and nobody under any roof had said either number, and there was no word in this house that would have carried them.

Under the smell, under the floor, the kiln drew breath in fours with a gap. Four low pulls, then the silence where a fifth should have been, then four again. It was not a bell. A bell spent four, four and a gap and left a hole in the hearing that a man might cross himself over or might not, and the told bell kept its place beside the heard bell and the two had never become one; the kiln spent four and left the gap in its breathing and went on, and had never asked anybody to count it. Through the finger’s-width door the river kept its note of water over water, a fifth low thing he had never counted among the six hearings of the full note and had never invented to stand up as a seventh. Overhead the boards kept four nails, four, and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb. The carried fire under the floor answered four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer without a name for a hundred and eighty days.

Three counts in three places. He had spent every one of those days keeping them apart: a gap in a wall, a note in a doorway, a knot in a board, and no account standing at the bottom of them. A man who lets three counts lie together long enough will wake to find they have stood up and called themselves a truth, and a truth with a payer at the bottom of it is an account, and no payer stood to any of these. He had learned that in a better-lit room, among people who wrote things down, and had brought the habit into a house that kept its days in loaves and glances and unnamed spaces; it made him no friends and bought him nothing, and he kept it anyway, the way a man keeps a instrument clean when there is nobody to see it.

The crust lay two inches from his ear, stiff and brown-edged, gone leathery in the dry heat of the last tend, with the empty place beside it still without a name and no third. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had been given an instruction about the thread, so it stayed where it had frozen, a small bright line nobody wiped. His head went where it wanted, and when it went too far it made the wet sound that nobody under this roof had been given an instruction about either. Cold had come cheek, temple, the small bare joint at the wrist where the wool rode back, and stopped there as if it had reached the end of its own order; the afterdrop waited on the far side of a warming nobody in this house would perform.

He knew what he wanted. He wanted the crust. He wanted the breast. He wanted the pot handle standing two inches beyond his hand, close enough to see and not to close on. He wanted a number spoken by somebody who would have known what a number counted. The wanting was small and immediate and animal, and it was louder than the man who knew the order in which a body takes heat back, the small joint first and the core last, the almost-smile that cold puts on a face and that is a grimace and not peace. Both were true. The body was louder. The surgeon’s register read the room as a field laid out and covered, an instrument kept behind a screen, a vessel that would not bear the taking off; the body read it as two inches of wool between a mouth and a crust. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water through the finger’s-width door and came down against the fold.

Sella Vail was already at the hearth when the instruction went out, and it went out ahead of her hands. “Keep it off his ears. It’s the river wind,” she said to the wool, which had no ears, and to the wind, which had nothing to give back, and not to the basket. She said it to the fire and the pot and the broom and the running water before she said anything to a person, and to the basket she said nothing at all, because a basket set down is a pot set down and a pot is told nothing. “The house wants the cloth dry,” she said, where another woman would have said I. She had said the house for three years and had never once corrected it, and the boy had never once heard her correct it, which was its own kind of answer and not one he could enter anywhere.

She moved at the half-pace, the pace that keeps a filled pot level and a carried thing clear of a slate’s open edge. Her little-door burn shut on nothing as she pushed the coal-black hair back with the wrist that carried it, so that the grey streak at her temple showed pale off the coals; she said the streak had come in the winter Garl died and would not discuss it further, and nobody under the roof had asked her twice. Her eye went once to the clay jar standing cold under its glaze on the wall shelf, and her hand started toward it and shut half over nothing and came away without touching. She gave nobody a figure. At the loaf shelf, standing with her back to the basket and moving her lips along a row with two gaps, she set the pot rim out on the board with its handle standing two inches beyond the reaching hand, and let her hand rest a half-beat longer at the setting than the setting needed before it went away. Then she lifted him base first and level, the way a woman lifts a filled pot from a board, and carried him out.

Through the finger’s-width door of the Vail wheel-shed the wheel was going and a forearm grey with slip to the colour of old bone came round and went again; no word came out of the shed into the yard. Somewhere a broom went over the flagstones without checking at the shallow bright patch, and the going of it was Tarn and the going of it was all the boy saw of him. The woodsmoke went before her in long low bars out of Low Kiln and the river wind came up behind with the taste of water over water, and the two passed on the stair of the morning as they had passed every morning of his life without taking each other’s colour or spending a word on either.

At the kiln mouth she set the basket down with its back to the opening, the way a vessel is set where the heat will come at it from behind and not into a child’s face. She went around the yard the way the yard goes around what it will not name: past the clean dark line where Ash had turned the corner for two years and the new finer grey called the thaw lay beside it, neither swept and neither with an instruction; wide around the space beneath the lowest rack where the broken birth-slates lay and nobody went; past the round place on the wall where the shallow pan had stood mouth-up to dry through Reed and was standing there no longer; and wide again around the empty place in the returning line where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out. A look for each and a wide step and no number and no explanation given to the basket. The basket was a pot set down, and a pot is told nothing.

The first firing of Ember was an opening, and she performed it the way a hand performs an opening on a vessel it will not name. Before she went near the kiln she put her hand to the widow’s mark on its cord at her throat, the thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it, held it one breath, let go. The intake came and the hold came and the letting go came, and the mark went back to hanging where it had hung, and nobody in the yard praised it and nobody saw what it mended. Then the clay lid came up. She lifted it with the back of a hand and set it where it would not rock, the way a covering is lifted off a field before the instrument comes down, and tipped the carried coal under the back of a broken pot with a movement of the wrist that had done it a hundred times and would not have called it a gift. Heat was a thing kept behind a screen in this house and let through the gate of two bites — the holding and the taking, one piece of the damper iron with no word in Grathic that would carry both mercies in one mouthful — and this was the one hour in the month when the screen came up. The fire took. The carried fire under the floor answered four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, as it had answered every pull since the boy came under the roof, and no payer was named to the gap and no slate was entered and no lesson was made.

The household straightened around the thing that had been done. Sella turned the coals with the back of the broken pot, told the fire it wanted feeding, said “The house will keep it” where another woman would have said I, and stood a moment counting under her breath toward no opening, the small intake at the top, the hold, the letting go, and the intake came and the hold came and the letting go came and nothing more opened. She did not touch the mark at a pot or a basket or a morning’s work. The wheel went on behind the finger’s-width door. The broom went on over the flagstones. Two people kept two silences under one roof and neither made anything of the other’s, and the boy set the two down on separate places without drawing a line between them, because two silences standing side by side will call themselves an equality if a man leaves them standing long enough to want an answer.

Then she carried him in and set him on the bed and not in the Debt Loft, base first and the weight settled the way a woman sets a vessel that must not rock. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without once looking at his face, left the thread frozen, set the pot rim out again with its handle two inches beyond the reaching hand, and did not look toward the bed. The widow’s mark hung where it hung and was not touched at a bed or a morning’s end, for a bed is not an opening and the hand goes to the mark before the kiln and at no other hour. The two rooms do not touch.

The boy lay with his back to the opening and wanted what a body wants and what a man cannot ask for. He wanted the crust two inches from his ear. He wanted the breast. He wanted the pot handle standing two inches beyond his hand. He wanted a number spoken by somebody who would have known what a number counted, and got none of them, and had stopped expecting to be told, which was not the same as not wanting it. The register in him read the morning as an opening performed on a vessel by a hand that would not name what it mended, heat kept behind a screen, a fifth pull missing under the floor with no payer to it, an inventory exact and complete and worth nothing at two paces; he was the only adult under this roof and the least use in it. The figure read was not the figure paid; the box had gone up the road locked; the clerk stayed set at the landing for a measure nobody called. Nothing in the room had a mouth that would say what a number counted, and the one mouth that could have asked had not yet learned to make a sound anybody would answer.

Through the finger’s-width door the river went on with its note of water over water, a fifth low thing never counted among the hearings and never made to stand up as a seventh; under the floor the fire kept four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been; overhead the boards kept four nails, four, and a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts in three places, and none rose up and became the others. One hundred and eighty stood exact and unspent in the dark of the wool, spoken by nobody under any roof, while the district went on calling Tallow the firing month and Ember the kiln month and the kilns kept their heat through both names without a boundary drawn between them. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water through the finger’s-width door and came down against the fold. The one opening was done and put behind the household; nothing else was joined, named, called, spent or ended, and the count went forward.

The basket had been set down near the kindling, and the broom had been given its instruction before anybody gave it to Tarn. From the low angle of the basket the boy watched the order of it the way he watched every order in this house: the eye going to the sill first, the hand going second, and the two never swapped. Tarn took the broom up as a man takes up a tool that has been named in front of him. “Six counted,” he said to the wood. “The next I don’t count.”

He said it to the wood and not to a person, and the wood gave back nothing, which suited him. Six years stood in the worn thumb-mark of the sill where the house could put a finger and check them; the seventh stood at the lip where no thumb of the house had gone and none would go, held the way a man holds a thing he has not yet made up his mind is his to spend. The small knife lay at the very edge with Garl Vail’s unforged collector sentence beside it, the one about the man sent for a collector who turned back at the frozen ford, and neither knife nor sentence had been entered where the house could read them. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line, and no scrubbing lifted it; the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back, and his chest had been tight since the dark.

The sentence about an ear under this roof being worth more than the debt that held it came up to his teeth as he reached, the way a thing comes up in a man’s mouth before he has decided to say it. It came all the way to the word that would have called the loan and stood there. He drew the breath that should have gone in at the top of the reach and did not spend it. No figure came with it and no name; he stood no tenth stopping up before the seventh year was earned, and the nine stoppings stayed nine objects on a tray that could not be seen, with no payer standing to any of them. The loan that could hear stayed uncalled. The Seed 16 breath stood unspent at the top of every reach, with its small interval showing where a breath should have gone in and had not.

“Yes, house,” he said to an early thaw, and a moment later, “Yes, house,” to a late one, agreeing so entirely that no argument could find a foot to stand on. He went on wanting the late because a late thaw puts off the paying, though the month had turned and the kilns stood open and the paying stood where the water had left it, in the ruts. Wanting a thing and agreeing with its opposite were two rooms in one house, and he had lived in both all his life. His broom went over the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector, over and over, without once turning along it. No collector came. In the clay pot in the loft above the shed the warm stone she had given him in the winter Garl Vail died lay two years gone and cold; he had not looked at it in a year and had stopped trying to reconcile it with the debt, which is not the same as forgiving it. A kindness that cannot be entered is a kindness a man cannot pay back, and he left it lying where it lay and let it stay cold.

On the flagstones his stroke went over the shallow bright patch without the half-check. There had been a day when the wheel-keeper’s bent finger stood in the cold iron of the damper ahead of Sella’s unfinished sentence and his broom checked half a beat behind it and went on without a word or a turn of the head; that movement lay where it had been left, a place in a yard, and he did not repeat it now. The surgeon’s register read three held changes on three places and would not let them stand up together and call themselves one thing: the fraction at the iron in a wall, the beat held beneath the loaf shelf in a room that did not touch the wall, the half-check on the flagstones in a yard that touched neither. Not a pair. Not a rule. A rule is an account that has found a payer, and no payer stood to any of the three. The boy kept them on three places because that was where they were, and spent the better part of an afternoon taking them down one at a time so that three likenesses standing in a row would not stand up and call themselves a truth.

Sella Vail came through with the basket’s weight still in her shoulder and gave the half-beat to nothing. At the loaf shelf she stood with her back to him and moved her lips along a row with two gaps, the old space at the row’s end and the palm’s breadth that had come in on the night of Seed 6, and gave nobody a figure. Her eye went once to the clay jar standing cold under its glaze and her hand started toward it and shut half over nothing and came away without touching. Her finger did not go beneath the front edge where the charcoal tally lay. The beat that had stayed under the board on an older day stayed where it was left, between knowing the board by heart and being told not to look; the marks waited under the edge for a hand that was not hers and a day that had not come, and she gave the shelf no word and the boy no reason for standing as she stood.

At the going of the light she carried him home up the clay road, woodsmoke going before her in long low bars out of Low Kiln and the river wind coming up behind with the taste of water over water, the two passing on the stair of the evening as they had passed every evening of his life without taking each other’s colour or spending a word on either. The arm that had carried him a hundred and eighty mornings kept the weight in muscle and gave no account of the cost — the height of a handle, the give of a shoulder, the turn of a wrist at a step’s end, a record kept and not an entry. At the edge of the yard she went around the place on the wall where the shallow pan had stood mouth-up to dry through Reed and was standing there no longer, looking for no scrape and finding no word; around the trough at the well, bare and dark, whose water is called weather; wide around the space beneath the lowest rack where the broken birth-slates lie, entered by nobody; and around the empty place in the returning line where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out. A look for each and a wide step and no number and no explanation given to the basket. The finer grey called the thaw lay beside the clean dark line in the unswept corner and neither was swept and neither had an instruction, and the household went around both as it went around the pan’s round ring and the field’s empty place, which stayed on separate trays and did not touch.

Under the Vail kiln-house roof she set him on the bed and not in the Debt Loft, base first and the weight settled the way a woman sets a vessel that must not rock. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without once looking at his face, left the thread of drool frozen and unwiped because nobody under any roof had been given an instruction about the thread, and let her hand rest a half-beat longer at the setting than the setting needed before it went away — the half-beat learned in the winter she sat up three nights with a child of her own who cooled while everybody was kind, and never told and not to be entered now if a slate were held under it and a clean hand set to the figure. At the hearth she told the fire it was the thaw, turned the coals with the back of a broken pot, said “The house will keep it” where another woman would have said I, and stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand counting under her breath toward no opening, the small intake at the top, the hold, the letting go. She pushed coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn so the grey streak showed pale off the coals, set the pot rim out with its handle standing two inches beyond the reaching hand, and did not look toward the bed. The widow’s mark hung on its cord at her throat where it had hung since the first firing and was not touched at a shelf, a road, a dusk or a bed, because a shelf, a road, a dusk and a bed are not openings and the hand goes to the mark before the kiln and at no other hour. The one opening of the day was behind the household and nobody had praised it and nobody had seen what it mended. The two rooms do not touch.

In the small hours he turned out the file as a man lays instruments on a towel before a case begins, each thing going into its own hollow and nothing resting across anything else, the order of the laying being the whole of the discipline and the discipline older than any word in this country. He had no towel and no instruments and no case, and laid them out anyway in the dark with hands that could not lift the least of them. Six hearings of the full note lay beside and never among — five from four sources, a cup across a birth room, a pot in the Vail wheel-shed through a finger’s width of door, the district’s slate twice at the Cross, and a man giving at the Drawing — and the sixth, the small flat knock out of Dessa Silt as the men lifted, filed where a thing out of a person must go and never among the sources. No seventh came and none was invented to fill the empty place, for an empty place kept empty is a finding and a finding filled to make a row come out even is a finding forged, and a forged finding carried into a room with a signature at the bottom is a finding that will be believed. Three near-full answers stood on their own tray, advanced and not paid: a pot under the shed wall that answered nearly full with air under the note and a want in it; a rack pot lifted a hand’s breadth too high and set down again; the knowing the wheel-keeper keeps back from a kiln-master come up on the wrong wind. None was promoted into the hearings and no pot was lost in the yard.

The older intervals lay at Stone 13, Stone 17 and Stone 25, with the half-beat behind a standing finger, the unchecking stroke over the bright patch, and his own held breath from a market morning. On the widened tray nine stoppings stood — Reed 5, 8, 11, 14, 17, 20, 23, 26 and 29 — objects and not a rule, intervals and not payments, no payer named to any, held apart by hand because a doctrine is an account that has found a payer and no payer stands to any of them. A tenth had not stood up before the seventh year was earned. The damper lay beside them as one piece of iron with two bites, the holding and the taking, with no word in Grathic that would carry both mercies in one mouthful, joined to no kiln and entered in no slate. The two tellings of a counting room shut in the war stood side by side with no third and neither had produced a room; the told bell lay beside the heard, four, four and a gap with nobody crossing himself at the stopping; the sixth green was a colour joined to no ear and the foundling a shape with no sale, offstage and unspent.

The shelf’s two gaps, the fold’s crust and its empty place beside it, the shallow pan’s gone ring and the field’s empty place in the returning line lay on trays that did not touch. No third gap had come, no fourth likeness had been made a cause and no finger of his had drawn the line: three likenesses in a row will stand up and call themselves a truth if a man leaves them standing, and he had spent a hundred and eighty days taking them down one at a time, with nobody to help and nobody to see. The carried fire under the floor gave four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer without a name for a hundred and eighty days; through the finger’s-width door the river kept its note of water over water, a fifth low thing never counted among the hearings and never invented to stand up as a seventh; overhead the boards kept four nails, four, and a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts in three places, and none rose up and became the others. Ash lay thin along the skirting board in the colour of the clean dark line and the new finer grey called the thaw lay beside it, neither swept at this hour and neither with an instruction.

— Day 181 —

Ember 2, Y.H. 813 (Secondday)

Before first grey the yard had the smell of a wet thing left too near the fire: hot clay under tallow, and under that the sour mineral breath of the corner nobody had swept in two years. the boy lay with the basket set low at the edge of the Vail wheel-shed, the wool at his ear and the crust two inches from it gone leathery in the dry heat. The grey came into the yard the way water comes into a shallow pan, without a boundary anybody drew. Three or four men went up the clay road with their collars high and went low through the finger’s-width door of the back room, and the door kept its finger’s width of open after them. They were given no name. Sella Vail carried past at the half-pace, turned no head toward the door, gave the house no name for them and did not say that the room was cold. The boy did not count men who had been given no name. Two tellings of a counting room shut in the war stood side by side where he kept such things — one from a man at a well, one off the road down from Greybank — and no third came to stand beside them and neither had produced a room. Nobody followed the men in and nobody said what the room held; it kept its finger’s width of open and gave back nothing but cold.

Then Jorra Vail came through with the day’s dust ground small as meal into both forearms past the bent little finger and under the nails, white in the creases where a fist had opened. It was not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel. The surgeon’s eye in him parted the two the way it parts a clot from the vessel that holds it: one was the material of the work, the other the material of a room, and the room had got into the creases of a hand and stayed there. Slate dust ground that fine goes wherever the hand goes; it sits under the nail and shows white when the fist opens, and no washing of a working man’s hands in a yard bucket takes it all out. He kept dusty forearms and clean ones on one tray, the way another man might keep a soiled rag and a clean rag folded on the same shelf, and asked neither which slate had been broken nor whether it had been a birth-slate. Under the lowest rack the broken birth-slates lay where the yard went around them, entered by nobody and counted into nothing.

He spoke to the wheel before he spoke to anyone, as he had spoken to it a hundred mornings. “It doesn’t care whose hands. It cares if they’re steady.” He said it flat, to the wheel, and the wheel gave back the small wooden complaint that was its only answer and went on turning. A pot came up under his hands and he set the rim true with the bent finger and told it, “You’ll do,” in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either. Three turns before the pot left the wheel he knew the note it would give — knew it the way a man knows the give of a shoulder before he lays hands on it, from the wall of it, the water in it, the small unevenness at the foot — and he kept the knowing where he kept the rest of his counsel. He did not say it aloud.

A kiln-master come up from High Kiln on the wrong wind stood at the shed edge with the sweetish edge of lead glaze at one corner of his mouth, the taste of it gone green on his lip, and let his eye rest on the turning pot. Jorra’s hands went on. The kiln-master said a pot singing before its time generally sings itself out early, and said it in the tone of a man passing judgment he would not be asked to pay for; the wrong wind had brought the sweetish edge with it and took it away again when it turned, and neither the man nor his saying stayed in the yard. Jorra did not answer him and did not look at him. The knowing went to the near-full tray where the other near-full answers lay — the pot under the shed wall that answered nearly full with air under the note and a want in it, the rack pot lifted a hand’s breadth too high and set down again, and this third thing withheld from a man who would have made something of it — beside the hearings of the full note and never among them, advanced and not paid. No pot was lost in the yard. No lesson was made and no room was changed.

A finding held back is still a finding, and a chart closed over it with the neat margin showing is a chart that will be believed when it is carried into a room with a clean hand at the bottom of it. He had learned that in a country whose name he carried and did not speak, and here he had only a tray that no other eye could see. He kept the knowing on its own tray and would not promote it into a hearing, because an empty place kept empty among six is a finding and a place filled to make a row come out even is a finding forged. The sixth hearing, the small flat knock out of a woman as the men lifted her, lay where a thing out of a person must lie and never among the sources; no seventh had come and none had been invented to stand up as one. He spent the better part of the morning taking the likenesses down one at a time so that a row of them would not stand up and call itself a truth.

At the hearth Sella’s voice went low and unfinished. “The fire’s low. It wants—” she said, and the end of the sentence hung in the room the way a tool hangs from a nail when the hand that would take it has stopped short. Jorra’s bent finger went to the cold iron of the damper ahead of his hand and stood there. The iron took the warmth out of the fingertip and gave nothing back; it was a piece of iron that did what iron does and asked nothing of the room. He waited while she finished “feeding,” gave the end of the instruction to the pot, and only then moved the tongue a hair’s breadth and left the finger standing in the iron. It was a fraction held in the holding and not in a word. Nobody spoke of the waiting and no payer was named to it. The household straightened around his silence as cloth straightens when a weight is lifted, and the fraction lay where it was left — not paired with the beat held beneath the loaf shelf on an older day, not paired with the half-check a clay-bound broom had made on the flagstones on another. Three places were three places and not a rule with a payer standing to it.

From the basket’s low angle the boy read the iron again as one piece with two bites. One bite holds a vessel whole while the fire is brought up under it; the other can take the hand off a man at the wrist and leave him to bleed into the dust. There was no word in Grathic that would carry both mercies in one mouthful. He knew what a surgeon knows, that the clamp is the same instrument in the same hand whether it is set to save or set to stop, and here the instrument lay joined to no kiln and entered in no slate, an object worth nothing that could take a hand off a man without anybody asking who would pay for the hand. He kept the two bites on one piece of iron and let them lie. A thing with two uses is not therefore two things, and a thing with two uses is not therefore a doctrine; the iron had no name for either bite and went on being iron.
