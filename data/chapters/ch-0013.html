13

The Approach to Silt

Days 55–60 · Ilmen 26–30, Silt 1, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, Sill

A counting room was shut in the last war and the men in it died of the breath with the damper closed. The yard says the war did it. Silt is named for what the river leaves, and the river leaves what no slate will keep.

— A saying of Low Kiln, Sill

— Day 55 —

Ilmen 26, Y.H. 813

The bucket went over the lip of the yard well before there was light enough to see the water in it, and the rope came up cold and a little stiff, keeping the shape of the hand that had pulled it. White had written on the stone through the small hours where the Warm had lately gone out, fronds and a branching that ran to nothing; by noon it would be gone and leave no slate the poorer. frost-bloom came and went in this month the way a figure comes and goes in a clean hand — entered nowhere, paid by nobody, and gone by the time anyone thought to call a witness. Corin lay in the basket in the lee of the well’s little roof with the fold turned right under his chin, fifty-five days old, and watched the rope come up and the water lean and spill. A skin lay on the water where the bucket broke it, thin as the skin on milk gone cold, and the women at the tubs spoke of that skin to the bucket and not to him. There’s a skin on it. Keep it off his ears. The instruction went to the rope and the wool, and the boy under the wool was not part of the sentence.

Sella Vail set the basket down in the well’s shade out of the draught as she would set a pot that must not be slopped, the little-door burn on the back of her hand shutting on the wicker and then on nothing. She had carried him down before the light at a half-pace from every open slate, going around one propped at the rack end as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it over, and she had not looked into the basket since. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. “It’s the river wind.” To the fire at home, which was not here and would hear her anyway: “It’s the thaw. Feed it.” She said the house where another woman would say I, and gave every instruction to the fire, the wool and the bucket and never to the basket. A basket set down is a pot set down and left; that was the whole of the rule, and the boy had learned to lie under it without expecting to be asked.

A man came down the road from the north with road-mud dried grey on his boots to the calf and the salt-and-hot-clay smell of the east road on him, a smell that opened the yard’s nostrils the way a door opens in a warm room. He was not one of theirs. He waited his turn at the bucket with a bag across one shoulder and the patience of a man who has walked eighteen leagues and knows a queue when he sees one, and when the bucket came up he held it a moment before he drank, because a man who has come that far does not put his mouth to water without looking at it first. The yard looked at him and at his mud, and went on with its buckets.

“You’ll have come from Greybank,” a woman at the tubs said.

“From east of it,” he said. “Eighteen leagues, near enough. The road’s a pot that’s boiled over since the thaw.”

He drank, and wiped his mouth with the back of a hand that had carried a load, and then he told them, because a man who has walked eighteen leagues owes the yard a thing to say for the loan of its water. It was a story with a date in it, which is the way such stories are told so that nobody has to believe them.

“Last war,” he said. “There was a counting room in a house east of us. Shut up for the duration, they said. Men inside it keeping the accounts. They shut the damper to save the coals —” he made a small sound in his throat, iron closing on iron, the sound of a thing with no word for it in his mouth “— and the men in the room died of the breath. Not the war’s hand. Their own heat went out of the room and there was no way to feed it.”

The boy watched the man’s hands as he drank. They were the hands of a man who had carried a load and meant to carry another: the knuckles broad, a pale line where a strap had worried the palm, the nails grey with road-dust. He held the bucket with both hands and did not let it rest on the lip, as if water set down was water lost. A pulse beat in his throat, slow and steady, and the breath came short through a nose caked with clay dust. Nothing about him was dangerous. The boy had once sorted twenty bodies in ninety breaths by the exact signs such a man gives — the grip, the breath, the colour under the nails — and here he could not sort one stranger out of a queue, could not have tapped his chest and asked him to say the thing again. The skill had gone nowhere. It had simply stopped having a handle.

The yard took the story the way a yard takes a thing that has happened eighteen leagues east. A woman with her sleeves in the wash said the war did for them, which settled it. A man at the racks said a room shut up was a room nobody had counted, and the men who had died in it had died of being unentered, which was a joke that cost nothing because the dead were not there to hear it. Somebody said the frost would take the pits early at this rate, and somebody else said the pits had been worked early already and the colour of the water would tell you. Nobody said kiln. Nobody said Vail. Nobody looked toward the roof of the house behind him where the chimney stood with its breath held under blind ash. The story went around the bucket and came back as a curiosity with a date, and the yard went on with its morning.

Sella, who had heard every word because a woman who keeps a fire hears what is said about heat, did not connect it. She told the fire it was the thaw and told it to be fed. “Cold in the east,” she said to the coals that were not there, and then, to the bucket: “Easy. There’s a skin on it.” She had lost a husband to the thing the man had just described and had never once described it that way herself. The house kept its own word for it, a low word that named the danger and the mercy in the same breath, and she had said that word to a boy at a kiln mouth years ago and warned him away. She did not say it now. A room shut up eighteen leagues east was a room shut up eighteen leagues east; the mouth that told it had mud on its boots and no reason to lie, and no reason to be heard either.

But the boy in the shade heard two things that the yard let go past it. He heard the man say the breath, and he heard him say the damper closed. There was no word in Grathic that held either thing the way the man held it, not for a room, not for the stopping of air in a room, and the two phrases lay in the basket beside him like tools with no handle. He knew what the man had described. He had known it since he was old enough to lie awake under this roof and count the fire’s breathing in the dark — four low pulls and a silence where the fifth should have been, four and a gap, night after night. He knew the anatomy of it as he knew the anatomy of a wound that closes over a cavity and seals what is in it: a vessel with no way to feed what it holds, the contents cooking their own heat out of the air, the face arranging itself in the ordinary way at the end. He had once stood in a room whose door would not open and known, before anyone said it, how it would finish, and had written it down in a calm voice and gone on to the next body. The surgeon’s register came and went without a word of the other life in it. Here there was no word at all, and no room to put one, and no hand to open a door if there had been one.

He filed the Greybank saying on a tray of its own. Beside it, in four places that did not touch, he kept the bell told in the dark of the Long Night, four, four and a gap; the bell heard in the square at the Cross, four, four and a gap with nobody crossing himself; and the breath of the Vail kiln-house itself, four low draws and a missing fifth, warming the boards by degrees under blind ash. Four low things kept in four places. The early note of the Singing Sill lay with the bells and the kiln’s gap and did not join them. The stranger’s room shut up eighteen leagues east went on the newest tray, and he wrote no cause across any of them, because three likenesses laid in a row had once taught him in a better-lit room what a man can do with an answer he wants too much. The tray did not become a cause. It was the whole of the work available to him and it bought him nothing he could lift a hand to.

Six hearings of the full note stayed six, five from four sources and one out of a person who had been warm that morning, beside them and never among them, and no seventh came to the well. The two nearly-full answers stayed on their own tray, advanced and not paid, with no hand to tap the pots and ask them to say the thing again. Five unjoined pauses stayed unjoined. Word, shape, cords, crust, three hands, three near-misses — each kept its place, and the tray of the Greybank saying was set down among them without touching any. A room can be sealed and the bill paid by the bodies inside; he had seen that arithmetic done in a room with better light and had once performed it himself, with a clamp and a hand held up for silence. Here the arithmetic was done by a stranger over a bucket and called a war. He could not lift a hand to the bucket, could not say a word of it, could not have written the word on the wet stone even if a hand had been given him.

Tarn came up the yard with his broom and the small bench knife back on its sill before anyone had asked after it, six years of the seven counted and the seventh held where he always held it, the one entry in this house the house could not check. the Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles that no scrubbing lifted, the colour of the clean dark line where the ridge had turned, and the pale clip-ring at his left wrist caught what light there was and gave it back. A neighbour at the rack end said the thaw would come early and the pits would pay for it; another said a late thaw was a longer week of a man’s own fire. “Yes, house,” Tarn said to the first, and “Yes, house,” to the second. He agreed so thoroughly with the early and the late that there was nowhere for an argument to put its foot, and went on wanting the late one, because a late thaw puts off the paying. He owned no sentence a house could enter and kept the account he most exactly where nobody could check it. His broom went over the road a man takes when a house wants a collector, over and over, without once turning along it. No collector went down it for Dessa Silt, seventeen mornings dead in the clay-heavy rise behind the racks, and the omission had no word and had not made a doctrine of itself. A loan that can hear is a loan that can be called; the sentence had never been said under this roof and lay under it all the same.

The stranger finished his water and gave the bucket back with a nod that cost him nothing and was worth something, and the mud on his boots dried lighter where the sun found it. Somebody asked him what year he had put to the story, and he gave a year two wars back and a month the yard did not use, and the answer made it more true, not less, because a date is what a thing has when it has stopped happening to anybody. He shifted the bag on his shoulder and went back down the road the way he had come, with the salt-and-hot-clay smell going off him into the yard’s own smell of wet wool and woodsmoke, and the yard took up its buckets where it had left them. A woman at the tubs said the war had been a bad business and went on with her washing. That was the whole of what the story became by the second bucket: a bad business, eighteen leagues east, with a date in it. The man had carried a description of the danger under this roof into a yard that had no use for it and had left it there like a tool somebody else might want, and gone home with his bag and his mud. The boy kept it where it was set down.

The body humiliated him exactly, as it had every morning of fifty-five. A thread of drool froze at his mouth and nobody wiped it. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and cold arrived in its known order, cheek, then temple, then wrist, the wool doing less than the wind wanted. The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the bucket’s rim and came down. Reducing a shoulder had been ordinary work in the life that was not this one; keeping his own hand on a lip of cold iron was not, and the joke had not gone stale and had never once been funny enough to say aloud. He was the only adult at this well and the least use in it. He had heard a man describe the way a room kills the men inside it, and had known the description for true before the man had finished saying it, and could not have lifted a finger to write the word for it on the stone lip where the frost had already written and gone.

At the turn of the wind Sella came for him and lifted the basket level, and carried him up the Clay Yard at a half-pace from every open slate. Behind her Tarn’s broom went over the trodden clay, going and coming, going and coming, without once turning along the road a man takes when a house wants a collector. Under the Vail kiln-house roof she set him on his bed and not in the Debt Loft, and put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without looking at his face. She said nothing about the man with the mud on his boots, or the room shut up eighteen leagues east, or the damper closed. If she had connected any of it she would have put the connection where she put the rest of it, in the private roll nobody was shown; but she had not connected it, and there was nothing to put away. She touched the widow’s mark at no hour of this day, because a well is not an opening and a basket is not an opening, and a story told over a bucket is not one either.

Garl Vail was absent and kept by objects, which in this house is a way of keeping a man no Court can enter. His salt-stiff coat held a broader man’s shape on the peg behind the door and the hollow seat kept its twenty-year lean and nobody filled either. The clean dark line where the Ash ridge had turned its corner was not swept, because a line left where it is is still a line kept, and one of his proverbs went to the pot and the fire and the other to the fire again, and neither went to the basket. Behind the boy the carried fire lay under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees and missing one breath in every four. Four, four and a gap.

He lay on his bed, fifty-five days old, with one crust in the fold and an empty place beside it carried through eight days without a name. The crust from Stone fifteen lay stiff past the smell of anything where it had always lain, unlifted and unnamed, and nobody had said either crust was gone. The Greybank saying lay on its own tray with no cause written across it, and the six hearings stayed six beside it and never among, and the two nearly-full answers stayed advanced and not paid. Nothing had been joined, paid, explained or ended. Somewhere eighteen leagues east a room that had been shut in a war was still a story with a date in it, and under this roof the fire breathed four and missed one, and the two things did not touch. The count went on exactly as the house kept it badly. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth and came down.

Sella Vail carried him three doors down the Clay Yard in the flat mid-morning light, the basket held level against her hip, the little-door burn on the back of her hand shut on the wicker and staying shut. She went around a slate propped at the rack end as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it over, and kept the distance she kept from anything with writing on it, which was a half-pace and sometimes more. Corin lay under the fold with his chin tucked and his ears out of the draught, fifty-six days old, carried everywhere and asked about nothing. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. “It’s the river wind.” To the fire at home, which was not here and would hear her: “It’s the thaw. Feed it.” She did not say whose house he was, or whose child, and she did not look into the basket.

House Silt had been a birth room once and was a room kept now, which in this district are the same thing. The trodden ash floor had the spring of old bread under a bare foot; a grey ridge of the Ash ran along the skirting board from the bench by the door and went under the door into the next room, left there for the Last Day. There was the bench with its pot, the draught that went along the floor to the corner and was felt at the ankle before the cold was felt anywhere else, a stool by the wall, a hearth with a slip pot, and the board-bed with its fold of grey wool that nobody had moved since the woman who lay on it, Dessa Silt, was taken eighteen mornings ago by the clay-heavy rise behind the racks. Sella set the basket just inside the door, which stood a finger’s width open, at the edge of the light and well away from anywhere a slate lay open. She held it level while she set it down, then straightened the chipped cup on the shelf and did nothing else. There was no errand in the room that a slate could enter, and she did not turn it into a walk to Slate Row. She did not touch the widow’s mark; a room with a door a finger’s width open is not an opening. The boy was set where he could see and not join, as a pot is set where the light falls on it and the hand that set it goes away.

He set the room beside the others without writing a cause across any of them. The birth room, the kiln-house under whose roof he slept, the death-house two doors down with its ridge a finger’s width thicker, the Court’s room that had reached him only as a voice through a doorway, the Eel House upriver with salt where this room had ash: a bench, a pot, a draught and a gap in each, five likenesses kept in five places and this one the sixth. Six rooms with the same shape and no door between them that he could find, and he had learned what three likenesses laid in a row will do in a better-lit room.

The room held what it would not say in the manner of a vessel with a seam: the chipped cup straight on the shelf, the fold of wool untouched on the board-bed, a stain on the ceiling above the bed that nobody had washed or covered. The boy read these as a surgeon reads a dressing left in place after the work under it is done: evidence of a hand that had done a thing and then gone on doing it after the reason for it had gone. Nothing in the room was named. The clay jar that had come out of it stood on a shelf under another roof, and the place it had left was a space the light could lie in. Between the two houses a keeping had been divided without a word, and the boy set each half where it lay and would not make a journey of it.

From the low angle of the basket the shelf above the board-bed was a high country. On it stood a round pewter tin.

The pewter had gone dull the colour of water under river ice, all but a small oval on the lid where a thumb had rubbed it bright, a rub the size of a thumbprint and the shape of one. At the seam of the lid a pale fold showed, cloth folded over and pressed, the colour of milk gone cold. The tin still held its scrap of cloth from the birth, and had held it three years, and nobody in this room had opened it or carried it to a bank or said what it was for. The boy took it in the way he took in a seam on a pot: a wall, a closure, a place where a hand had been. He could read the thumb’s rub as evidence of a hand, and the fold as evidence of a folding, and could not read past either. The seam of the lid ran all the way round, a closed line like the line where a wound has been brought together and left to heal under a dressing, and the pale fold lay against it like a dressing not yet lifted. He had lifted many such dressings and known what he would find before he lifted them; here he could not raise the lid of a tin, or his hand the inch and a half farther that would have put his fingers on it. The bright place was where a thumb had gone back when there was nothing else to do with it. He did not know what the tin held. He did not know the fold had ever been warm, or at what fire it had been pressed. From where he lay the shelf was beyond the reach of any hand he had, and the hand outside the wool went up an inch toward it and came down.

He set the tin on a tray of its own. It lay there with the bell told in the dark and the bell heard in the square at the Cross, with the breathing of the Vail kiln-house and the saying brought downriver in a stranger’s mud, with the nearly-full answers and the two cords and the word that was a saying and not a vessel’s sound. None of them touched another. He did not write a cause across the tin, because a tin above an empty bed is a tin above an empty bed, and an answer wanted too much had once cost him a life he still carried the shape of. It bought him nothing he could lift a hand to.

The cloth under him was cold and would not be made warm by wanting. A thread of drool froze at his mouth and nobody wiped it, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and the cold arrived in its known order, cheek, then temple, then wrist, the woodsmoke in the wool doing less than the wind wanted. There was nobody in that room older than he was and he could not reach the shelf. He could not read the writing on the slates propped at the bench. He could not roll over to see the tin from another angle, or ask whose hand had rubbed the lid bright, or say any of it in Grathic. The clinical eye went on doing what it had been trained to do, and had no field and no instrument and no standing.

Alda Reed came in short, with her shawl drawn tight against the draught at the door, the smell of soap-and-milk gone cold under river silt on her and lye sour under the soap, the birth-slates under her arm. She stopped at the bench before she stopped at the child. Her nails were scrubbed to the quick and still had clay under them, and the stoop in her shoulders was the stoop of a woman who had spent her life leaning over other people’s births. She did not say please and she did not say sorry, which was not coldness in her but the correct number of words for a room where somebody had once been in pain. She laid the slates writing-down on the wood, and the soft scrape of stone went through the room, and the boy marked the order of it: the record first, then the child. She pressed the fold flat at his shoulder and kept her fingers there a breath longer than a fold required. The wool rode back from his wrist and showed the red edge of the birth-flower where it runs down the forearm and stops short of the hand. Alda crossed two fingers at that red edge before she knew she had done it, and covered the sign before it could be seen with a date nobody had asked for.

“Stone seven; the account went up on the eighth, before the paper went out,” she said.

Sella, standing a half-pace off with her burn shut on nothing, said, “He lies quiet.”

“Warm and breathing — that’s all I’ve set down.”

It was exact and it was not true. The woman eighteen mornings gone had believed it to the last, and the death stood unentered where no living person could now correct it, and Alda did not say her name or the third day. She went on with a cooper’s seventh in Mud End, a potter’s third child in Stonemouth, the last high water, going on after there was no question left in the room to stop her, because dates are a handle on a thing too large to carry. The dates went on arriving like slates laid one after another on a bench, each flat and clean and each covering the thing underneath it. The boy kept the order of it on its own tray: the writing laid down before the body, the date laid over the sign, the exact words laid over a thing that could not be said. He had seen the same order in a room with better light, where a clean page had been made to cover what nobody wanted read.

Her hand went once inside her bodice and touched the little tin there and came away without opening it. Two tins in one room, both shut, one in pewter above an empty bed and one in cloth against a woman’s body; two records in two materials, kept on two trays that did not touch. He could not ask either what the other held, or whether the two had ever been in the same hand.

He watched for the narrow slate and the turning of it down, and neither came. The hidden record stayed where it was, five pages in a second hand no Court physician had seen, face-down toward a floorboard in a house he could not name. No sixth page was added in this room or any other on this day. Sella did not ask what had been written, or what had not; a wall stood between the two women with no door in it, and her private roll stayed where it was, unshown. Nothing passed between them but the child and the weather and the exactness of dates.

Alda took up her slates and went out with the tin still shut inside her bodice, and did not look back at the bed or the shelf, and did not say the woman’s name.

At the turn of the wind Sella lifted the basket level and carried him back up the Clay Yard at the careful distance she kept from anything with writing on it. Under the Vail roof she set him on his bed and not in the Debt Loft, and put the fold right at his ear without looking at his face. Behind him the carried fire lay under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees and missing one breath in every four. Four, four and a gap. One crust lay in the fold where two had lain, stiff past smelling, and the empty place beside it was carried into a ninth day without a name.

The house kept its absent man the way it kept everything it would not enter: the salt-stiff coat holding a broader man’s shape on its peg behind the door, the hollow wheel-seat keeping its twenty-year lean that nobody filled, the clean dark line where the Ash ridge had turned staying unswept. Garl Vail was three objects and a proverb and a debt nobody called, and the boy put him where he had always put him, apart from the tin three doors down and apart from the woman under the empty bed. A house can keep a man by his things and a room by what it has not moved; he set the two keepings side by side without making either the cause of the other.

Six hearings of the full note stayed six, five from four sources and one out of a person who had been warm that morning, beside them and never among them, and no seventh came. The two nearly-full answers stayed advanced and not paid, and the word stayed a saying on its own tray, and the five unjoined pauses stayed unjoined. Three doors down the pewter tin kept its place above the empty bed, with the pale fold at its seam and the thumb’s bright rub on the lid, and nobody opened it, and the room kept the shape of a room that has been left where it is. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the shelf and came down.

Sella Vail stood at the shelf with the little-door burn shut on nothing, moving a loaf along the row the way a woman moves a thing she has already decided about and forgotten she decided. She set it down where it had stood, straightened it, and came back to the same place a second time with her lips working over the days. Under the roof of the Vail kiln-house the morning still held the yard’s cold in it, woodsmoke and wet wool and the faint sour mineral breath of a corner nobody swept. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. To the fire, which was not a person and would hear her: “It’s the thaw. Feed it.” To the bucket she gave the instruction she gave everything that had no ears of its own. The basket lay on the bed under the fold and she did not look into it. Three, her lips said, and two, and whatever the moot gives out, and never the other thing, which was the figure. Corin lay where she had set him, fifty-seven days old, carried everywhere, and heard the days go around in loaves and glances the way a man hears a slate read in a room he has not been allowed to enter.

He kept the number exactly, which was the only work in that house that needed no hands. From Ilmen 28 to Silt 1 was three days, and he counted them in the order a surgeon counts what is left before a hand goes in: the ones he can see, the ones he can reach, the one that will not be there when he gets there. Three. He set them on a tray of their own. He had once stood over a field triage and known, without laying a finger on anyone, how many of the bodies on the ground would still be warm by morning; the arithmetic had been the same then and the hands had been his. Now the arithmetic was the same and the hands were a folded inch of wool outside a blanket. He wanted the number spoken aloud by somebody who could spend it, and nobody spoke it. Knowing it bought him nothing he could lift a hand to, and the dry joke of that lay beside the count like a second loaf nobody had thought to put on the shelf.

Jorra Vail came in from the Vail wheel-shed with slip dried to the colour of old bone up both forearms, the bent little finger going in ahead of his hand the way it always went, and no grey worked past the bend of it. He spoke to the wheel before he spoke to anyone, and told it what it had been told a hundred times: it did not care whose hands were on it, only if they were steady. To a pot turning under his palm he said, “You’ll do,” in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either. He knew the full note of a Vail pot before it left the wheel — knew it in the wrist before the knuckle ever went near it — and would not say so before a kiln-master, who would call it early and make something of it. The boy under the fold kept that knowing where questions are allowed to last for years. Three doors down the Clay Yard a tin kept its shelf and its pale fold, and he did not make a journey of it; here a wheel kept a hollow seat worn by a man who was not Jorra, and the two keepings lay on one tray and did not touch.

Sella had not finished telling him the fire was low. Jorra’s bent finger went in ahead of his hand at the damper and moved it, and the end of her sentence arrived at the pot instead of at him, so she shut her mouth on it and gave it to the clay. The scoop and scrape of iron came through the room and the fire took the change without being told twice. He did not say the back room was cold. He did not look toward the bed or the basket. the Debt Loft kept its ladder and its unmarked clay pot above the shed; the room off the shed kept its finger’s width of open, and nobody pushed it wider. He’s not Vail stayed where it had been set on the hour it was spoken, sealed as tightly as a lid, and the foundling went on being carried under the roof without a name written against him. Jorra went back to the wheel. The yard straightened around his silence without an order.

Tarn had his small bench knife back on its sill with six years counted and the seventh held back — the one entry in this house the house could not check, because a man who owns nothing is the only one who knows what a debt is worth. the Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the line in the corner, and no scrubbing lifted it; the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back. To the neighbour who wanted the thaw early he said, “Yes, house,” and to the one who wanted it late he said, “Yes, house,” and agreed so thoroughly that the argument had nowhere to put its foot. He went on wanting the late one. A late thaw puts off the paying; that was all of it, and it was enough. When Sella told him he had done well he stopped altogether for two breaths, then set the knife where it could be checked and went back to his broom.

A vessel gave its note past the yard door — a small flat knock in the clay air, the sound of a pot that has handed over all it had and kept nothing in reserve. Tarn’s head did not turn. He did not name the sound, and there was no Grathic word in the yard for the thing it was, and he had spent six years not supplying one. The boy under the fold did not call it a seventh hearing. Six hearings stayed six, five from four sources and one out of a person who had been warm that morning three doors down, and this was not a sixth and not a seventh and not anything he had standing to enter. Two ears kept their equality under one roof without a word passing between them, and the loan that could hear stayed uncalled. Behind her Garl Vail’s salt-stiff coat held a broader man’s shape on its peg and the hollow seat kept its twenty-year lean that nobody filled. the kiln’s breath went four, four and a gap; the carried fire lay under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees. The coal the brine-gull took on Day 45 was fifteen days gone now and entered in no slate and missed in no account.

❦

At the yard well the stone lip was wet and the mud had got into the rope. Sella set the basket in the shade under the little roof, out of the draught, and gave the instruction to the bucket. “Easy,” she said. “There’s a skin on it yet.” She did not say it to the basket. The little-door burn shut on the rope and opened on nothing as the bucket came up, and the water inside it carried a skin that the rope disturbed and left whole again. A man waiting his turn said the month had come early; a woman at the tubs said it came as it always came; neither gave the number of days that had gone before the name changed, and the argument went down with the bucket and came up with the next one. The boy lay in the well’s thin shade and kept the number exactly, and the cold came at his cheek and then his temple and then his wrist, and the wool did less than the wind wanted. He wanted the bucket more than he wanted the argument and could not reach either.

Word came up the river road with a man who had been to the Weir and did not stop in the yard: the soft-cuffed clerk was still set at the Fishstairs with his slate for a measure nobody had called, and the weak board in the plank walk was still there and trodden by nobody. The man said it the way another man says that a step is missing, and went on toward his dinner. The boy set the clerk’s order of reaching on a place of its own, apart from the woman in the grey coat at the crash, apart from the grey-fingered man who gave at the Drawing and apart from the soft cuff behind the Court’s table; the board went on a second place, beside the missing Fishstairs step and the hairline that stopped short of the edge, and the new grey in the Vail corner went on a third, and none of the three touched another. The clerk sat for a debt the district had not entered. The board waited for a weight nobody would put on it. The hairline stopped where the ice thinned. The yard had one word for all of it and the boy had no word at all.

Beyond the drying racks the the Ilm ran under a skin so thin that the dark current showed through it in veins, and more hairlines had run into the ice in the night and stopped a thumb’s breadth short of the edge. the Singing Sill gave its low note early, felt in the teeth before it was placed in the ear, and a woman at the tubs said the water was running fast under the ice and then said weather, and went home warm on it. the Slip Brook ran clay-heavy behind the racks a little higher than it should. He kept the hairlines, the early note and the higher brook on places that did not touch the clerk’s place or the board’s place, and did not let the new grey in the corner come among them. The note came up through the boards beneath his basket and was the same low thing the boards had kept all through the year; the house called it weather and he called it nothing, because to call it anything would have been to write a cause across four separate places.

Tarn came up the yard with the small bench knife back on its sill and told the yard where it was, though the yard had not asked. Six years were counted and the seventh held back, the one entry in this house the house could not check. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles that no scrubbing lifted, and the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held the light and gave it back. He agreed with the man who wanted the thaw early and the woman who wanted it late and went on wanting the late one, because a late thaw puts off the paying. His broom went over the road to a collector over and over without once turning along it, and no collector came down it for the woman the brook took twenty-two mornings ago. A vessel gave its note past the yard door and his head did not turn, and the boy did not call it a seventh hearing. The two ears under one roof kept their equality, and the loan that can hear stayed uncalled.

Jorra Vail came in from the room off the Vail wheel-shed with slip dried to the colour of old bone up both forearms and no grey worked past the bend of his little finger. He told a pot it would do, told the wheel it did not care whose hands, only if they were steady, and went to the damper before Sella had finished telling the fire it was low, so that the end of her sentence went to the pot. He did not say the back room was cold. He did not look toward the bed or the basket. The boy had kept the dust of some mornings and the clean slip of others on separate places since the first days and had never asked which slate was broken; he kept the clean forearms on the dust’s place as a fact and nothing more.

❦

At dusk Sella lifted the basket level and carried him up the Clay Yard at a half-pace from every open slate. She set him on his bed under the roof and not in the Debt Loft, and put the fold right without looking at his face. The door of the room stood a finger’s width open. Through the gap came the low note of the river beginning to move under the ice, and through the floor came the carried fire’s four, four and a gap, and the two sounds kept their separate places as the told bell of the Long Night, the heard bell at the Cross, the early note from the Singing Sill and the gap under his own roof had kept theirs for two months. He had no Grathic word for the flat answer, none for nearly, none for a thing a house could leave lying where it lay and go home warm.

Six hearings of the full note stayed six, five from four sources and the sixth out of Dessa Silt, filed beside the five and never among them; no seventh came in the new month. The two nearly-full answers stayed advanced and not paid, third sounds on a tray of their own. The word forty stayed a saying on its tray with the readable shape of the double turn beside it and the weight of a thumb on wicker apart from both, and it had not been spoken again. Nothing had been joined, paid, explained or ended in the turning of the month; the count went on.

One crust lay in the fold where two had lain, brown-edged and stiff past smelling, and the empty place beside it was carried into Silt without a name. He was sixty days old. Two months old. He had wanted the breast in the first days and wanted the crust now and could not reach either, and the warmth he most wanted was the empty place where a hand had been. He was the only adult in every room he had ever lain in and the least use in any of them.

In the corner a new hairline of grey lay beside the old clean dark line, unswept because the house said it was the thaw. The door stood a finger’s width open. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the new grey and came down. The count went on.

The Chronicle of Chapter 13

On Ilmen 26 a man come down from Greybank told the yard how a counting room was shut in the last war, the damper closed, and the men inside died of the breath when their heat went out with no way to feed it. The yard took it as a curiosity with a date. Sella Vail, who had lost a husband to it, heard and told the fire it was the thaw; Tarn agreed with early and late and wanted the late, which puts off paying. Only the boy, fifty-five days old in the well’s shade, knew it for true, with no word to write and no hand to lift.

The next morning she carried him three doors down to House Silt and set him inside a door a finger’s width open. He saw the pewter tin above the empty bed, pale fold, thumb’s bright rub on the lid, birth-cloth kept three years. Alda Reed came short with soap-and-milk cold under river silt, laid her birth-slates writing-down before turning to the fold, pressed it too long, crossed two fingers at the birth-flower’s red edge unknowing and covered the sign with a date. She said warm and breathing, exact and not true, touched the tin in her bodice without opening it, wrote no sixth note and left without the woman’s name.

Through the days that followed the house kept its numbers badly in loaves and glances. Sella moved a loaf and came back to the same place; a space at the row’s end went uncalled, while he counted three days to Silt and could spend none. The yard prepared a sweeping already done --- grey burned on Ilmen 1, called dealt with --- and Sella laid one thumb in Vail’s clean dark line, then in the death-house ridge a finger’s width wider, and fetched no broom. Jorra’s bone-coloured slip went to the damper ahead of her instruction; Tarn swept the collector’s road without turning, and no collector came for the woman in the brook.

At the last market, Berr Clay wiped his thumb twice, read one in twelve twice over the basket and took himself out of his own sentence; Mira Silt answered a question with her own answer, put her thumb to a grey cord with a second twisted twice beneath it, knots uncounted, and brought no crust. A barrow of grey went out unread, and the river began to move under the Ilm’s skin and was called weather. Silt came in on mud. A new hairline of grey gathered in the Vail corner beside the old clean line; Sella stood over it with a broom and would not sweep. At dusk he lay sixty days old, one crust in the fold, the empty place carried into the month without a name. The clerk was still set at the Fishstairs, the board trodden by nobody, and the fire behind him kept four, four and a gap. His hand went up an inch toward the new grey and came down.
