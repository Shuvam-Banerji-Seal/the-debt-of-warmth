Nineteen Steps

Days 7–9 · Stone 13–15, Y.H. 812 · The Vail kiln-house and the Fishstairs

A crust is not a sermon; a knot is not a name. Carry both in your hands and call it weather.

— A Fishstairs proverb, Sill

— Day 7 —

Stone 13, Y.H. 812 (Thirdday)

The coals had held under their ash through the night, and first light came into the Vail kiln-house at the door’s finger’s width of failure, thin as a pulled thread, going along the floor to the corner where the grey lay. It was the same light that had come yesterday and the day before, and the boy did not spend anything on surprise. Corin lay under the fold with a thread of drool at the corner of his mouth that nobody had wiped in the night and would not be wiped now until somebody’s hand happened to pass that way. Seven days old. He had the floorboards already: four on the near board, four on the next, and a knot the width of a thumb standing in for the fifth nail. The knot had not moved. The cold had come in at cheek, then temple, then wrist, in the order he knew and could not argue with, and the house had gone on breathing around him the way a vessel breathes when it is held and not yet cracked.

Sella Vail came between the bed and the fire and put her hand into the fold without once looking at his face. The little-door burn across the back of her hand — two straight sides, a lintel and a threshold, the skin inside it puckered and pale — closed first on nothing, then on the wool, and she set the fold right at his ear and under his chin the way a potter straightens a rim by touch in the dark. She did not wipe the drool. That was not a cruelty and she would not have called it one; there was a dish and a spoon and a hundred things the house required before a corner of wool at a child’s mouth. The boy had learned the order of her hands. He had learned that what she did not do was as exact as what she did.

She went to the coals, and before she lifted the kiln lid her hand went to the widow’s mark at her throat, the thumb-length of lead-glazed clay on its cord with the Vail kiln scratched into it. She held it a moment longer than the touch required. Then she let it go and gave the instruction to the fire.

“There,” she said to the coals. “That’ll take. Don’t set that near the door. You’ll pay for it in the thaw.”

She said it to the pot, and then to the fire, and never once to the basket. The boy lay under the fold with the rule of the mark already in him as he had the rule of the kiln’s breath: touched before the opening, at no other time, not at the bank last night, not to answer a man who spoke her name in a market and called the answering a kindness. He did not know what the rule was for. He knew that it held, the way a seam holds while the heat is right, and that the household kept its fire inside the rule and would not step outside it to explain anything. A man who had spent his life making people hold still while he counted could respect a rule he could not read. It was the only kind of respect he had left in this house, and it was useless to him.

The lid came up and the kiln took its breath. Four, and four, and the breath it missed. The boy kept the count without deciding to, the way he kept the count of everything that had a rhythm and a gap in it. Garl Vail’s proverb went to the fire in a voice improved by twenty years of use: feed the fire before you feed yourself, for the fire would still be there in the morning and a man would not. Nobody said it to the boy. Nobody said it to a person at all. It went into the ash and stopped there, and the house straightened up around it the way a room straightens around a thing it has agreed not to mention.

The gruel came from the house’s own dish. Barley thinned with milk and warmed at the fire, and the spoon wiped on her sleeve before it came near him. She lifted his head with two fingers behind the ear because he could not hold it up himself, and the warmth went in through his mouth and belly and stayed. She said the house where another woman would have said I. The house fed him. The house put the fold right. The house kept the fire and let the boy lie where the fire could reach him. It was not a lie; it was the grammar she had chosen for a thing that would become enterable the moment she said it in the first person. He had heard that grammar before in a white room where a chart said one thing and a room had done another, and had recognised it then as the sound of a man making himself clean by making himself absent. Here the woman was kinder and the grammar was the same.

Between one spoonful and the next his eye went where it wanted, which was not where his head wanted. The bed was under the roof. The loft was above the wheel-shed, and there was a ladder up to it that a debt climbed and a foundling did not. The hollow seat at the wheel kept a man’s shape on its left side and nobody filled it. Three arrangements, and a fourth in the corner where the grey lay. He read them with the part of him that had once sorted twenty injured people in ninety seconds into those who would live, those who would live only if he chose them, and those who would die whatever he did. He had done it hundreds of times and had never once found a way to make it clean. Now he lay in a basket under a fold and could not lift a hand to his own chin, and the part of him that had done the sorting went on doing it with nothing to sort and no standing to act on what it saw. The household did not know that it was sorting. It knew only that the bed was under the roof and the loft was above the shed and the dish was the house’s dish.

“That’s the house’s boy,” she said to the dish, not to him. “The house will keep him.”

The spoon went back to the fire. The little-door burn closed on nothing and the hand went to the coals again, and the feeding was entered nowhere — not in the slate at the Cross, not in the private roll she kept in a careful hand, not in the district’s figures, not even in the arithmetic of the household, where a loaf was a loaf and a bed was a bed and neither had a name written on it. She had paid for him twice and had taken a measure out of the district yesterday and had not said so, and she went on keeping him the way she kept the fire: by doing the thing every day and leaving no entry where an officer might look for one. The warmth was real. The warmth was not his to refuse. He had learned in seven days that a kindness which cannot be answered cannot be refused either, and that this was not an argument he could win even if he had the words.

The pot waited on the bench in the shed. Jorra Vail had one hand under its belly and was bringing it down, slowly, the way a man sets down a thing he has just made and does not want to hear criticized. There was bone-coloured slip dried up both forearms to the creases of the wrist, and the little finger of his left hand bent where a splinter had taken it at eleven. He did not look toward the basket. He never looked toward the basket.

The boy turned his head before the pot touched the bench.

It was not a hearing. He had not heard anything. The pot had not given its sound yet, and his head went where it went without asking him, toward the bench and the soft shape of the thing above it, because in the seven days of his life a pot set down on a bench in this house had given a small flat knock and he had learned to turn toward it. That was all. It was the expectation of the sound, not the sound. He had no more choice in the turning than he had in the drool at his mouth, and no word in Grathic for the thing he expected, and no hand to lift toward the bench two paces off even if the turning had meant something he could do.

Jorra set it down softly. He set it down the way one sets down a thing that must not be startled, and it gave nothing. Not the flat knock. Not the little wooden tick of clay meeting boards. Nothing at all. The interval where the note should have been stayed open and the boy kept it.

He did not add it to the count. Five hearings from four sources, and the fifth from a person, and that was where the count stood and where it would stand. A thing that did not happen could not be a hearing; he knew that as well as he knew that a vessel which gives no sound under the knuckle has not answered you at all. He kept the interval beside the other facts he kept and put no name to it. It would have been a decision to call it anything, and he had no standing to decide. He had learned that a thing could be kept without happening, which was a new fact and a useless one, like most of what he knew.

Outside, Tarn came up the yard with his broom.

He had swept before light and he would sweep again; a yard swept twice is a yard no man can say was left untidy. Grey Ash sat in the creases of his knuckles where no scrubbing lifted it, and the pale ring showed at his left wrist where the iron clip had sat six years ago. The small bench knife was already back on the sill where it could be checked. He went through the motion of checking it with his eyes — a glance, a small nodal inclination of the head, nothing that could be called looking at the boy — and then lowered his head over the broom.

The bristles went over the trodden ash. Twice. On the second stroke, at the soft set-down of the pot in the shed, the broom stopped for half a stroke and did not go on. Tarn’s head stayed down. He did not turn it toward the shed. He listened for something that did not come, and the yard gave him only the wind at the door and the sound of his own bristles going nowhere, and after the half stroke he swept on as if nothing had interrupted him.

He had seen the boy turn his head before the pot was set down. That was the thing. He had been coming up the yard with the dawn at his back and had seen the bundle under the fold move its head toward the bench before Jorra’s hand let go, and he had known what the turning was for, because he had done the same thing himself for six years and had never once said what it was. A clay-bound hand who can hear a pot before anyone taps it is worth more than his debt, and a debt who says so is a debt the house can price. So Tarn swept and said nothing, and the boy under the fold lay with his mouth working slightly and said nothing either.

Two ears under one roof, each kept out of the other’s reach, and no count of either anywhere in the house. Nobody named the sound. Nobody named the stopping of the broom. The boy had spent seven days being the only person in a room who noticed, and now someone else under the roof had noticed him noticing and had chosen silence. He could not enter that silence and could not hand it back. It was not safety. It was not yet a threat. It was an equality he had no word for, and he lay with it against him under the wool the way another child might lie against a warm stone.

Jorra went back to the wheel and set his weight into the hollow on the left, which he had spent three years failing to fill. The wheel took under him. He drew a pot up out of the clay and spoke to it before he spoke to anyone, in the flat voice he kept for pots and tools and the damper.

“You’ll do,” he said. “The shoulder’s thick. I’ll thin it.”

Then, with his eyes on the damper and not on Sella and not on the basket, “I’ll shift that. It’s sticking.”

Sella had not finished telling him to shift it. He shifted it before she had finished and shifted it again a moment later, and she let him, and the kiln took the change in its breath without either of them saying whether it was right. He was her son and he obeyed her so quickly that there was nowhere for an argument to go, and he never looked at her while he did it, and the house had learned to read the lowering of his voice the way it read the weather.

There was no slate dust on his forearms today. That was the fact the boy kept turning over, because yesterday there had been dust grey and fine worked into the creases past the bend of the little finger, and today there was only slip the colour of old bone. Slate dust had meant the back room, and the low talk, and the men whose names were not spoken, and a door kept closed. Its absence meant either that the back room had been cold in a different way or that there had been no back room at all, or that Jorra had washed. The boy could not ask which it meant. If he could have asked, Jorra would have answered the wheel. So the difference went into the file beside the other differences and stayed a difference, which in this house was a thing that could last for years.

The household went on being ordinary around the two of them. Sella counted spoons. Tarn swept. Jorra spoke to the clay and shifted the damper and did not take up the silence about the room he had come out of yesterday. Garl Vail’s coat kept its salt-stiff shape on its peg behind the door and his seat kept its hollow on the left and his proverb kept its place in the house’s mouth, and the ridge along the skirting board turned the corner without a break. Nobody said what had been taken out of the district two doors down yesterday. Nobody said where it went. Low Kiln would say three days to the Five when it said anything at all and would not say what it counted, and the morning went on being a morning in which a woman fed a child from her own dish and a man swept a yard and a boy at a wheel told a pot it would do.

The boy lay under the fold with the drool frozen at the corner of his mouth and the warmth of the gruel going out of him by degrees into the cold. He had the rule of the mark, the rule of the fold, the rule of the dish, and now a fourth rule he had not known existed until a man with a broom chose to keep it with him. Four rules and a gap, he thought, and then corrected himself, because the gap was not a rule and the counting of it was not the same as the keeping of it. He had been the only adult in a room full of people who could not know he was there. Now there were two of them, and the other one had a broom and six years of a seven-year term and the small knife back on the sill where it could be checked, and neither of them had said one word to the other.

His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the bench and came down.

The pot on the bench gave nothing. Tarn’s head stayed lowered over the broom. The coals took under their ash, and the door stood at a finger’s width, and the wind went in along the floor to the corner where the grey lay, and nobody in the house shut it.

By mid-morning the wheel had taken the house into its rhythm and the day had a shape, the way a pot has a shape before it is fired. The wheel turned wet and steady in the shed, and Jorra Vail drew clay up out of the basket under his hands and set each pot on the rack with the same small tilt of the wrist, and the pots stood in a row with their bellies out, waiting for the fire. Slip had dried to the colour of old bone up both his forearms. He spoke to them as they came. “You’ll do,” he said to the third. “The shoulder’s thick. I’ll thin it.” He did not look toward the basket where the boy lay. Corin had spent seven days learning the order of this house and understood perfectly that he was not being addressed, and understood nothing about why that should be a relief.

To the wheel he said a thing he would not have said to a person in the room. “It doesn’t care whose hands,” he said, settling his weight into the hollow on the left. “It cares if they’re steady.” The wheel took under him and the pot came up wet and round, and the boy, who in the life before this one had worked under men who said the same sort of thing about instruments — that a tool has no interest in who holds it, only in whether the hand is steady — took the sentence in without letting anyone see he had taken it. Jorra never said it to Sella. He never said it to Tarn. He said it to the clay, which could not report him.

The kiln breathed behind the wall. Four, and four, and the breath it missed. He kept the count because there was nothing to stop him keeping it, and because the part of him that had once counted a pulse at the head of a bed while a machine wrote the number on a screen went on counting now with nothing but a kiln to count. He had the floorboards as well: four on the near board, four on the next, and a knot the width of a thumb standing in for the fifth nail. The knot had not moved since first light. That was the kind of fact a man could build a day on if he had no other work.

Sella Vail moved between the fire and the rack with her hands never still. When they had nothing to hold they counted: loaves on the shelf, bits in the jar by the door, the days. She did not say the days aloud. A man had come up from the iced river before light with water on his legs and a look in his face like a door standing open in a cold house, and somebody in the yard had asked how long it was to Kindling, and he had said, “Not so far as a man would want,” and gone into his own house. That was the whole of the answer the yard had. The number went around in the shape of a fist closed on something and came through the door as an absence, and the boy took the absence in with the rest of the morning. He could feel the shape of it. He could not have said what was inside, and it did not occur to him to be angry about that. A withheld number was a thing he had met before, in a room where a chart said one thing and the room had done another.

Tarn came in from the Clay Yard with Ash in the creases of his knuckles and the pale ring at his left wrist where the iron clip had sat six years ago. He had six bits to a term and one year left of seven, and he had counted every day of the six and would not count the seventh, and the not-counting was the one entry in this house that the house could not check. “Yes, house,” he said to nobody. “I’ll sweep it. I swept it this morning. I’ll sweep it again.” He agreed with everything so completely that an argument had nowhere to put its foot.

The bits were not in a jar and were not on a slate. They were in his head, laid out in the order he had earned them, and the seventh was not there because he would not let it be there. The boy knew a man who had kept a set of instruments the same way — every clamp in its place, one empty place in the tray where the thing he was afraid of using would have gone — and he had never once asked the man why the place was empty. He did not ask Tarn either. It was not a question a boy in a basket could ask, and it was not one a clay-bound hand could answer without being priced for it.

Sella passed him with the coal-shovel — it was the back of a broken pot, blackened along one edge — and said, “You’ve done well, Tarn.” It was not a large sentence and she said it to the floor the way she said most things. Tarn stopped altogether. The broom stood still with its bristles on the trodden ash, and the hand on it went still, and for two breaths the whole room held the position it had been in when the sentence arrived. Then he said “Yes, house” and began again.

The boy had seen that stop before, in a white room with a senior at the head of the table. A resident, asked a question he could not answer, had stopped just so — not the hesitation of a man thinking, but the stopping of the whole animal, the eyes going to a place on the wall where there was nothing to see — and he had known without being told that the man was agreeing with something he had no standing to disagree with. Here the thing agreed to was that a clay-bound hand had done well, which was a kindness and therefore not enterable. He read the stop exactly. It bought him nothing. He had a great talent for reading stops and no way to put a hand on one.

Jorra went quieter when the levy came into the room, which was not often and never by name. A woman at the drying rack said, “There’s the house’s share to think of,” and left the sentence lying where the loaves lay, and Sella answered her with the price of slip, which was not an answer and did not need to be one. She said, “That’ll go the house’s way,” to a pot, and the yard straightened up around the sentence the way a shelf straightens when a heavy jar is set upon it. Jorra’s hands went on turning the wheel, but his voice dropped out lower than hers had gone. He did not say the thing he had said three days ago and had never taken back. He did not say it now. The boy had been under this roof five days and the sentence was as sealed as it had been on the hour it was spoken, and the not-saying of it had become a part of the house like the hollow in the seat.

There was no slate dust on the forearms today. There was only slip, bone-pale, dried in the creases past the bend of the little finger. the back room was either cold in a different way or had not been opened, and the boy kept the difference the way he kept all his differences, side by side and unjoined. If he had been able to ask, Jorra would have answered the wheel.

Under the fold he made his inventory. It was not a record and it was not a decision; it was what his hands used to do at the end of a shift, laying what they had found in a row on a table so that the shape of it could be seen before anybody wrote it down. He had the two ridges of Ash: one under the Vail skirting board, near two thumbs wide, turning the corner without a break; one in the death-house two doors down, thicker by a finger’s width, turning its corner without a break as well. He had the charred edge of a slate in the kiln ash, black on one side and eaten into the shape of a strip of bark, with the writing gone off the end. He had the barrow of grey that had gone out through the north gate toward the Bone Orchard, heaped a little above the sides. He had the bench from the death-house with four nail-heads on the near edge and four on the far and a knot the width of a thumb standing in for what was missing. And he had the interval where the pot on the bench had not given its the flat note — an empty place with the shape of a sound in it, which he kept as carefully as if something had actually happened. He had known the full note from its flat shadow for seven days and had never once been thanked for knowing it. He set them all side by side and joined none of them. He had no word in Grathic for any of it and no standing to make one.

The same arrangement kept returning and he could not have said why. A bench by a door. A draught under it. A pot on the bench. Four, four, and a space where the fifth should be. It was the architecture of a room in which something had been done and nobody had entered it, and his eye, which had found the same architecture in a hundred rooms of the old life and made a living out of finding it, went on finding it here and made nothing of the finding. To join the rooms would have been to decide what had happened in them, and he had no standing to decide. He had learned that in his five days under this roof and had spent every day since proving that he had learned it.

The body went on with its humiliations without asking his opinion. The thread of drool at the corner of his mouth had frozen during the morning and stayed frozen, and no hand had happened to pass that way again. His head went where it wanted, which was toward the light at the door, and when it went too far a small wet sound came out of him that was not a word, and would have embarrassed him once, when he had been a man who could be embarrassed by such things. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the bench two paces off and came down. Two paces. He had once reduced a shoulder in a room the size of this one, with three people holding the light and nobody saying what to do. He lay now with his own chin beyond the reach of his own hand. There was a joke in it and it cost him something and he did not laugh.

Dusk came into the Vail kiln-house the way it came into it every day of the world — by degrees, through the door that would not shut, along the floor to the corner where the grey lay. Sella banked the coals with the back of a broken pot and pushed ash over them with the edge of her hand, and the little-door burn closed first on the broken edge and then on nothing. “Lie you there,” she said to the coals. She did not touch the widow’s mark at her throat; that was for the hour before an opening, and there was no opening now. The days to Kindling were still a closed fist somewhere in the yard. She had gone around them all afternoon without once putting a figure to them, passing the question from the fire to the rack and from the rack to the pot, and the boy had followed the passing the way he had once followed a case conference in which everyone agreed to call a thing by a less exact name so that nobody would have to decide what it was. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without once looking at his face, and the hand did not stay to wipe the drool, and none of it went into the arithmetic of the house, where a loaf was a loaf and a bed was a bed and neither had a name written on it.

Garl Vail’s coat kept its salt-stiff shape on its peg behind the door and his seat in the shed kept its hollow on the left, and his proverb went to a pot in the voice twenty years had made better than the man had ever said it: “Leave the crack showing. People trust a thing they can see has been handled.” Nobody said it to the boy. Nobody said it to a person at all. The debt against the tiles, which had never been called, lay where Berr Clay had left it, spoken around all day and never named, and the house went on being short of coin and warm.

Five days under this roof had taught him something he would have preferred not to learn. The whole house was an arrangement of things nobody would enter: a bed under the roof, the Debt Loft above the shed with its ladder, a seventh year a man would not count, a number a man had gone around, a ridge nobody would sweep, a slate with its writing gone off the end, a debt that could not be paid because it could not be settled and could not be done with. Everything was kept exactly. Nothing was entered. He had kept his file through five days without help and without a single thing in it that anybody could have taken from him, which was the whole of his wealth and not enough to buy a crust. His competence was useless here; he had the eye of a man who could read a room in ninety seconds and the hands of a creature that could not lift a crust, and the only possession anybody had failed to tax was the attention he could not put down. It was not despair. It was a colder attention than he had brought under this roof on the third day, and it went on taking the measure of the room after the light had gone.

Tarn swept the yard once more at dusk and put the small knife back on the sill where it could be checked, announcing it to nobody in the tone of a man stating the weather. Jorra came in with slip on his forearms and spoke to a pot and shifted the damper, and did not take up the silence about the room he had come out of yesterday. Nobody said what had been taken out of a house two doors down on Stone 12. Nobody said where it had gone. Supper was eaten over the fire; the pot was set on the bench and gave nothing; the yard went quiet by degrees. The household went on keeping its accounts in the only way it knew, which was by doing tomorrow what it had done today.

The boy lay on his bed under the roof with the fold at his chin. The coals held under their ash. The grey ridge ran to the corner and turned it without a break. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the bench and came down. Four, four and a gap. Low Kiln said three days to the Five without saying what it counted, and the wind went in at the door’s finger’s width, and nobody shut it.

— Day 8 —

Stone 14, Y.H. 812 (Fourthday)

Nothing in the house had told him they were going. Sella Vail took the basket down from its peg in the half-dark while the coals still lay under their ash, and the little-door burn closed first on the handle and then on the fold, and she said to the wool what she said to the pot: “Keep it off his ears. It’s the river wind.” Eight days old, carried because there was nobody in the yard to leave him with, he went out through the door that did not quite shut and down the Clay Yard with the cold coming up off the the Slip Brook to meet him.

She carried him the way she carried a pot filled that must not be slopped, her forearm under the basket’s belly, the burn shut around the handle. His head went where it wanted, toward the strip of sky between the roofs, and when it went too far she put it back with two fingers behind the ear without once looking at his face, and that was the whole of her attention and the whole of her refusal in one movement. The yard was trodden ash and frost, and the frost lay over the ash in a skin that broke under her heel. The drying racks stood with their empty slats up. The wheel-shed was dark behind him. The house went on breathing four, and four, and the breath it missed, long after the wall was between them, and he kept the count going after there was nothing to count, because counting was a thing a man could do with his hands full and there was no part of him that had ever been able to stop.

The walk took the time a walk always took and Sella did not hurry it. The Slip Brook went over its stones beside the path with the thin skin on it that the children of Low Kiln read as weather, and a woman up to her elbows in a tub at a door looked up as the basket passed and went back to her work without appearing to have seen it, which was the whole attention the district had to give a woman carrying a bundle. Sella kept to the middle of the path and a half-pace from every door where a slate might lie open, and the boy, who had learned the half-pace in the market at the Cross and had never once been told what it was for, counted the doors instead. He counted seven and lost the count at the eighth when his head went where it wanted and the small sound came out of him and Sella put two fingers behind the ear and set him right. Seven, and a place where the eighth should have been. It was becoming a habit of his, this finding a gap where a thing should have been, and he had no standing to call it anything.

The town woke in layers on the walk down. First the gulls, crying above the roofs before there was light enough to see them, their cry coming down thin and peeled; then the smell of wet rope and salted perch; then the small sound of water moving under ice somewhere ahead; then the cold, which came last and stayed. By the time the steps of the the Fishstairs were under Sella’s feet the whole place was made of one material, and that material was cold, and the cold was a substance a body could put a hand against. He knew that much without being told it. He had spent eight days learning that warmth in this country was always somebody’s, entering and leaving rooms the way a person enters and leaves a room, and that the cold was the part nobody entered and the part that kept whatever was taken. He lay with his cheek against the fold and felt it come in at ear and temple and wrist, in that order, which was an order he had learned to know without ever having chosen to learn it.

At the foot of the steps the market had not opened in earnest but was already full of the things that would be sold later: a man setting out a board of perch with his back to the wind, two girls turning a barrow of wet weed, a collector’s boy with a satchel waiting for nothing in particular. Rumours went around before the fish did, though they would not be priced and cried until noon; the boy heard a figure and then another and could not spend either of them, and the language they were spoken in was a row of sounds he had absorbed through his ears and had never been given a key to. It was an account read aloud in a tongue he did not have, and he was carried through it at the level of Sella’s hip with his mouth working under the wool. A brazier of coals stood by the eel stall with a coal at its edge that had fallen out and gone black on the stone, and the boy, who knew what a banked fire was worth in this district, noted that even here warmth was kept and counted and lost by inches.

The the north bridge stood over them with its shadow on the steps. The the Ilm lay under its skin of ice, a skin the colour of skimmed milk left standing too long, and the ice held, and the district said it held, and nobody on the steps looked at it twice, because there was nothing a body could do with ice. Downstream, beyond the bridge, the river went over the ledge of hard stone that had given the town its name, the the Singing Sill, and gave back a low note when the Warm rose in it; he had the name by listening and had never had it explained, and he set it down in the file he kept among the things that were named and not accounted for. A brine-gull came down over the landing with something bright in its beak, a coal not yet cold from somebody’s brazier, and went up again with the gulls crying after it, and the boy noted that warmth here was a thing that could be stolen in flight and carried off and eaten, which was not a thought he enjoyed and was nevertheless exact.

At the bottom of the steps the stall was already open and Mira Silt was in it up to the elbows. Slime had dried pale on her sleeves in stiff folds the colour of old bone, and she had the salt of the perch up to her wrist and river silt in the creases of her neck, and she smelled of silt and tallow smoke and the cold off the water all at once, which was how he had learned to smell her in two rooms before he had ever seen her behind a stall. The top of her left ear was gone. She held her head a little to the right against the cold in the place where it had been, the way a man holds his head away from a draught, and she said a hook had taken it, and the scar said a knife. He read the scar the way he had once read a wound on a man who told him he had fallen: a clean edge drawn down and across, not the ragged tear a hook would leave. The small smile sat on her face the way a door sits on the latch, able to be shut without anybody seeing the movement. She kept it open for the stall and for whoever came down the steps.

She had three eel-bones in her left hand and she flicked them against her thumb while she looked the morning over, one, and another, and the third, deciding something, and when the deciding was done she put one away in her pocket and went on with the other two. He had watched her do that in the birth room and at the edge of the Cross and had never once seen her put the bone away before the deciding was finished, so that the movement was a tell, and he had nobody to tell it to. She worked with a short knife that had been sharpened on a stone and not cleaned, and the eels came up out of a box of wet weed and went down under her hand in two passes, and the bones went into a bowl at her right and came out again when she wanted to think. Everything about her was quick and had been quick for fifteen years. Nothing about her was careless.

The knife went down the length of an eel and came up clean where the hand had been, and the bones came away in the bowl with a small sound like rain on a board, and the boy, who had once stood at a table and separated the parts of a body with an instrument he had chosen himself, recognised the economy of the movement: nothing wasted, nothing handled twice. Her hands were broad and worked and the nails cut short to the quick, and she never once cut herself, and she never once looked into the basket at her elbow. That last was the most difficult movement of all and she made it look like nothing. She talked to the eels as she worked and to the woman at the perch tub and to nobody in the basket, and the boy lay in the basket and understood perfectly that he was being kept by a person who would not acknowledge him in front of a slate. He had no word for the understanding and no use for it, and he kept it.

Berr Clay came down the steps with the red wool cord of his office folded away somewhere under his coat and his hands large and clean. He wiped his thumb on his apron before he touched the slate he carried, so that the slate would stay clean, and the ring of tasted glaze showed green at one corner of his mouth. He had the smell of hot clay on him and, under it, the smell of nothing at all, which the boy had learned to recognise as the most expensive smell in Grath. The green at the mouth was the colour of the glaze the potters tasted to judge it, and the boy knew a man in the old life who had taken something sweet into him for thirty years and had a pale line at his gum to show for it, and he set the two men side by side and joined neither of them.

“The levy is called for the feast,” Berr Clay said to the eels. “It is not called by me.”

“Two marks the pair or two marks the pair and two short?” Mira said. “Don’t trouble yourself. It’s two marks the pair and two short, and I’ve counted them twice, and I’ll count them again after I’ve eaten.”

He did not answer, because she had left nothing in the sentence that wanted an answer. He wiped his thumb once more on his apron, which was already clean, and set two marks on the corner of his slate, and she put four eels into his basket where six should have gone, and both of them went on with the morning as if nothing at all had passed between them. Six years of it, and neither had ever said a word about the two eels or the two marks. The boy had once watched a nurse and a billing clerk settle a question about a consumable with the same careful avoidance of the subject, two people standing close and speaking around a thing in front of a man who could not ask, and he took the shape of it in now without a word and without letting anyone see he had taken it. Berr went back up the steps with the slate held flat and the green at his mouth, reasonable to the last, and the gulls went over him.

Sella had not set the basket down. She stood a half-pace from the stall board where Berr’s slate had lain open, and when the slate was shifted an inch toward her she shifted an inch away, and none of it was visible as a movement anybody could have called a choice. She said to the wool, “Keep it off his ears,” and to the river wind, “You’ll pay for that come thaw,” and never once said a word to the Thane about the marks or the eels or the boy. The little-door burn had closed at the first edge of cold and had not opened since. The boy had been carried through two markets under this roof’s rule and knew its shape now: a kindness that would not stand where an account lay open, and a silence with a wall built into it. He did not expect it to be different here. He was wrong about the difference, though he would not know it until the hand went to the cord.

A woman with her arms in a tub of salted perch tipped her head toward the basket at Sella’s knee. “And that,” she said. “Whose is that, then?”

Mira did not look down. “Warm,” she said. “That’s all I’ll say on a slate, and I’m saying it off the slate. You want a number? There’s no number in it. You want a house? Ask the fish.”

The woman laughed, said something about Fishstairs women and their secrets, and went back to the perch. That was the end of it. Mira shifted her weight so that her back was to the corner of Berr’s slate, which had been left open on the stall board, and went on filleting with the knife, and the boy lay with the river wind coming into his ears and took the not-looking in beside the others he had learned to read. One man in a market had priced him without once looking into the basket, and one woman now kept him without once looking into it, and the two not-lookings had the same shape and were opposite things, and he had no word in Grathic for either of them. He had eight days in this country and could not have asked a question in it if his life had depended on the asking, which it had, twice, without anybody knowing it.

The cold got into him through the fold. A thread of drool froze at the corner of his mouth in the river wind and stayed there, and his head went too far toward the light and made the small wet sound that was not a word, and nobody at the stall happened to be looking, so nobody put it back. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the bowl of eel-bones at Mira’s right and came down. Two paces, then three. He had reduced a shoulder in a room the size of this stall with three people holding the light and nobody telling him what to do, and here he could not lift a bone from a bowl. The joke was there, as exact and as cheap as ever, and it cost him something and he did not laugh.

Berr’s voice came back from the top of the steps, given out to the stall and not to anybody in it. “Three days to the Five,” he said, and the voice had counted what it had and did not care to say more. He passed his finger over the figure on his slate the way a man might pass a finger over a thing he had set down carefully and would not now disturb. The boy had heard the number go around the district for days as a closed fist, and here it was let out into the market air in a man’s ordinary voice and was still nothing he could spend. He knew the shape of the withholding. He had sat at the head of a table where a number was given out with the exactness of somebody who has decided that the room need not know what it counts.

He felt rather than saw Mira’s hand go to the cord at her waist. Her thumb found a knot in it. It was a small movement, the hand going in under the apron and the thumb coming to rest and staying, and she went on talking the whole time, and the talking was about eels and the price of them and the weather on the river. The boy saw the hand go and saw the thumb settle, and he did not see the knots, and he did not know that there were knots to see. He knew only that a number had been said and a hand had gone to a place on her person, and that the place was not a slate and could not be taken from her by a man with a clean thumb. She did not say why she touched it. She did not say what it held. There was nothing in the movement that a collector would have recognised as an account, which was plainly the whole point of it.

The house he had come out of that morning kept its accounts in a roll nobody showed and a mark nobody touched except before an opening, and that keeping was a wall: a man could stand against it and be warm and never know what was written on the other side. This woman kept hers on her person, in a material no Court could confiscate, and made a small visible performance of not telling, which was a different order of thing. A wall could not be watched. A performance could. He did not trust her. He could not place her. He watched the way he had learned to watch, which was the only thing he had ever been able to do and the only thing that had never once given him a hand to act with.

He had known a hospital that kept two records: the one on the screen, which could be called up and corrected and signed by a man who had not read the note under it, and the small one a senior nurse carried in her pocket in pencil, with the things that had actually happened written small enough to hide beneath a thumb. He had never once asked to see the pocket record. He had been glad it existed and had been afraid of what it said about him, which was the same feeling from two ends. This woman’s cord was that kind of record, and the boy lay under the fold with eight days of life and forty years of knowing what a second record was for, and could not have told either of them a thing. He noticed all of it. He could not have entered a single line.

He filed the day’s new objects where he filed the others, side by side and joined to nothing: the eel-bones flicked and put away, the hand going under the apron, the two marks that were short and the four eels that were short, the not-looking that kept where the other not-looking had priced. He had the two ridges of Ash and the charred slate and the barrow of grey. He had the death-house bench with four and four and a knot the width of a thumb, and the interval where the pot on the bench had not given its the flat note. He knew the full note from its flat shadow and had no way in eight days earned to make anybody produce one. The cord’s movement lay beside the rest of it without a name and without a use, and he kept it carefully, the way he kept the interval of a sound that had never happened.

He had noticed the shape of the stall without deciding to notice it. A bench along the front of it, and a draught coming under the bench off the wet stones, and a space at one end of the bench where a slate should have lain and did not, because the Thane had taken his up and gone. A pot of eelweed stood where a pot stood in three other rooms he had been carried into. The arrangement followed him from house to house and then out of doors and down to the water. A bench by the door, a draught under it, a pot on the bench, and a gap in the count, and here the gap was a space on a board where an account had been set down and lifted away, leaving the shape of it in the cold. He set the shape beside the others and made nothing of the setting, because making anything of it would have been a deciding and he had no standing to decide. It was the third room in as many days that had arranged itself this way, and each time he had been carried in and set down where he could see the arrangement and not be part of it.

Mira’s thumb came away from the cord and she turned back to the eels, brisk about it, her face to the stall and the fish, and the movement of the hand was the only thing in the whole market that had been done for a reason he could almost see and could not name. Sella lifted him against her hip and said something to the wool about the wind, and did not say that the woman at the stall was her sister’s sister, or that the marks in the basket were short, or that the boy under the fold had been carried three doors past this place on his first morning and would be carried past it again. The boy lay at the edge of the steps with his hand outside the wool going up an inch toward the cord and coming down. Two paces, then three. The gulls went on over the ice, crying, and the river went on under its skin, and the north bridge kept its shadow on the steps.

By the time the market had opened in earnest the rumours had acquired prices. They had been in the air since before light, going stall to stall ahead of the fish, and now they were cried and weighed and knocked down like perch, each with a figure fastened to it that a man could repeat and a woman could refuse. Which boats on the the Ilm carried something other than fish; which collectors took their ale before a collection and which after; which households in Low Kiln were warm and which were cooling. The boy lay at the edge of the steps with a thread of drool freezing at the corner of his mouth and heard the figures go past him in a language he had absorbed through the ear and had never been given the key to. He could count what he heard. He could not spend any of it. It was an account read aloud in a tongue he did not have, and he was carried through it at the level of a hip, his mouth working under the wool, and there was nobody in the whole market who would have thought to hand him a slate.

He had sat at the head of tables where figures were read out by men who had already decided what the figures meant, and had watched other men lean in as though leaning could alter the number. The leaning was the same here. A woman with a board of perch leaned toward a man with a slate and said a figure; the man said another; the woman laughed and slapped the board with her hand, and the two went on to the next customer, and the figure lay where they had left it, unclaimed and unchanged. The boy, who had once been paid to know the difference between a variable and a constant, had eight days of life and no words in Grathic with which to ask which of the two was the patient.

The man with the slate at the perch board said something about a collector who took his ale before and a collector who took it after, and the woman named one and then the other, and neither lowered his voice, because there was nothing in the sentence an officer would have called a secret. The boy heard the order of it and set it down the way he had once set down the order of a script: who went first, who followed, where the break came, and what the break was for. A man who drinks before is steadying a hand. A man who drinks after is washing a taste out of his mouth. Both arrive at the bed with a clean slate and a reason, and the reason is the part that gets entered, not the ale. He had known a dozen men who could tell you exactly what they had done and exactly why it had been necessary, and had learned to listen for the one small thing they left out, which was never the necessary part.

Mira Silt knew which of these rumours were true because she had made some of them herself, and the boy knew that she knew from the order in which she repeated them. A boat going out with something other than fish in its hold was a boat whose owner had paid the levy in something other than coin, or so the stall said, and the stall said it in the same breath as it said the price of eels, so that a listener could take whichever part he could afford. He had learned years ago that a person who prices several things in one sentence is usually hiding which of them he is selling. He watched her mouth instead of her hands and learned nothing she had not decided he should learn.

She sold eels. She sold them the way she did everything, quick and without waste, the knife going down the length of a fish and coming up clean, the bones into the bowl at her right and out again when she wanted to think. A woman at the perch tub bought six and was given five and did not count them, which was a thing he had learned to see in the first year he worked and had never learned to unsee. Mira named a price and then named it lower for the same woman, and the woman thanked her for nothing in particular, and both went on with the morning as if no account had passed between them. She answered a question about the weather with a question about the ice, and answered that one herself, and the man who had asked went away satisfied that he had been told something, which was a trick he had known nurses to perform on families in a corridor outside an operating room.

She said a figure under her breath, low enough that it belonged to the eels and not to the stall, and one of the eel-bones went from her left hand to her right. Another figure; another bone moved. The boy watched the counting and then watched the hand go in under the apron to the cord at her waist, and tried to make the two agree, and could not. The counting belonged to the stall. The cord belonged to something the stall did not hold, and the thumb came away and the hand went back to the knife, and there was no seam between the two movements that a man with a slate could have entered.

On the walk in he had seen the hand go to the cord once, when a number was given out from the top of the steps. He had seen it again now, when the number was said under the breath. Twice in one morning, and no more visible the second time than the first. A thing done often enough becomes invisible to the people who do it, and she had done this for years; he understood that much, having spent a life watching men do small things with their hands until the small things became the whole man. He did not know what the cord held. He did not know that there were knots on it to count. He knew only that a figure had been spoken and a hand had gone to a place on her person where no collector’s thumb would think to look, and that she had gone on talking about eels the whole time.

Attention was the whole of what he had. It was the one possession he had ever been able to keep, and it had never once, in forty years or in eight days, given him a hand to act with. He could read the shape of a woman’s not-looking and the order of a man’s reaching and the gap in a flight of steps. He could not lift a bone out of a bowl two paces from his basket. His head went where it wanted toward the light on the water and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and nobody at the stall was looking, so nobody put it back. The cold came in at ear and temple and wrist. He knew its order. He had known the order of a great many things and had never been given a use for any of them.

The collector crossed the the north bridge a little before noon. The boy did not see his face, only the cuff, turned back twice at the wrist and softened by washing, coming along the shadow of the bridge with a flat satchel held against a dry coat. The hand went to the satchel’s clasp before the eyes went to the stalls, or so it seemed from a basket at the foot of the steps; the same order he had watched in a room two doors down the yard, where a man had settled his slate before he looked at the person lying on the board. It was an ordinary hand. It had done this ten thousand times and had never once been thanked for it, and the boy had no way to address it, no standing to speak, and no wish that was not a wound.

The light in the cafeteria is wrong and it is four in the morning and it hums. He has eaten the crumpled paper wrapper to its last crumb because waste offends him, and his hand is going into a pocket where a phone lies lit with a name he will not look at.

The river took it back. The gulls went over the ice, crying, and the boy lay under the fold at the Fishstairs with his cheek against cold wool and the cuff gone into the crowd at the top of the steps. He had been at home for two sentences in a room he could not have named and would never see again, and the homesickness of it was not for the room. It was for the appetite: the small clean satisfaction of having finished a thing that was put in front of him, crumb by crumb, because leaving it would have been an insult to something he could not explain. He had taken gruel from a dish that morning and bread the day before and would take eels tonight by somebody else’s fire, and the hunger of the infant body and the thrift of the grown man turned out to be the same appetite wearing two faces. He had always known that about himself and had never known where to put it.

Nobody on the steps turned to look at the man with the cuff. He was an ordinary man going about an ordinary morning, and when his work was done he would go home to a room with a fire in it and sit down, while the boy who had watched the order of his hand lay in a basket at the foot of the stairs with a thread of ice at his mouth. There was no cruelty in it. That was the part neither life had ever got used to: that men who did this work went home to be warm, and were kind to their children, and would have been offended by the suggestion that they had taken anything.

He counted the steps down to the water going out and he counted them again coming back. Four to the first landing, four to the second, and then a landing where a step should have been with no step in it, only wet stone and the shadow of the bridge thrown across the place a foot would have gone. Four, four and a gap. He put the gap beside the kiln’s missing fifth breath and the bell’s missing stroke under the Vail roof, and beside the fifth mark on the floorboard that was a knot and not a nail, and made nothing of the setting. To make anything of it would have been a deciding, and he had no standing to decide, and the count went on happening without anybody asking him to keep it.

In the Breaking the river would come up over these steps and take them, and put them back when the water went down with the landing a hand’s breadth out of line. The district read what the water did as weather and went on using the steps. The boy kept the place where the step was missing and let the river have the rest.

His hand went outside the wool to the place where a pocket should have been. There had been a coat once with a small cold rectangle of light in it, and he had reached for the place twice in the death-house two doors down the yard and once more in a room where the light hummed, and the name under the light had not been looked at then and could not be looked at now. The hand found wool. It closed on wool. He had promised a boat nine times to a woman who had organised her life around not waiting, and had never bought the boat, and the promise came back to him now the way the phone came back: as an absence in a pocket that did not exist, carried everywhere, heavier for being weightless. He had been a man who could reduce a shoulder in the dark. He could not lift an eel-bone out of a bowl, and the bowl sat at the woman’s right hand within reach of her thumb, and she moved a bone without looking and went on with her counting.

Sella Vail bought the eels. She did not ask about the boats or the collectors or which households were cooling, and she did not haggle; she laid down her marks and took up the basket the girl at the stall pushed toward her, and the little-door burn shut on the handle and held. “Two pair,” she said to the board, and the girl put four eels in the bucket, and Sella counted them with her eyes without touching them, which was a way of buying that trusted the count and refused the intimacy of checking it. To the wool she said what she said to the pot when the draught came under the door: “Keep it off his ears. It’s the river wind.” She did not say that a cuff had crossed the bridge, or that the woman at the stall had put her hand to a cord when a number was spoken, or where they had been that morning or where they were going. The boy had been carried through three markets under this roof’s rule and knew the shape of it: a kindness with a wall built into it, and a silence nobody would enter. He had noticed the cuff. He had watched the cord. He would carry both out of the market and enter neither anywhere, and she would not ask him what he had seen, which was the other half of the same arrangement.

She lifted him against her hip, took the eels by the handle of a bucket, and went up the steps with the bucket swinging at her knee. River silt went up with them, and salt, and the cold off the water, and over these, as the the Clay Yard came into view between the roofs, the smell of woodsmoke from the Vail chimney, which had been on the wool since the first morning and was entered in no account, not even the private one the house kept and did not show. He lay with his cheek against the fold and counted the way up the way he had counted the way down: four, four and a place where a step should have been. The bucket knocked her knee in time. The gulls went over the ice behind them, crying. He had been given the whole morning. He had taken in the figures and the bones and the cuff and the cord and the landing that was not a step, and he could carry none of it out where anybody could read it, and that was the end of the morning, a bucket at a woman’s knee and a smell of silt going up the yard.

At the the Vail kiln-house she put the fold right at his ear without once looking at his face, which was the whole of her attention and the whole of her refusal in one movement, and went in. The door did not quite shut behind them, as it had not shut on any night since he came under the roof, and the cold came in at a finger’s width and went along the floor to the corner where the Ash lay. The boy lay under the fold with the river on the wool and his hand outside it closing on nothing. Four, four and a gap. The coals held under their ash.

— Day 9 —

Stone 15, Y.H. 812 (Fifthday)

There was a skin of frost on the the Clay Yard on the morning of Stone 15, and it broke under the basket the way a crust breaks under a thumb, fine and without giving. Sella Vail carried him down with the wool over his ears and the little-door burn shut on the handle, and said what she said to the wind and not to him. “Keep it off his ears. It’s the river wind.” She had said it every morning of his nine days and had never once said it to his face, and he had never once been able to tell her that the wind off the the Ilm was not the only cold in the district. Nobody under the roof was free to mind him, so the house carried him, which was the reason stated nowhere and acted on every morning; a reason spoken aloud becomes a thing that wants an answer, and she had arranged her whole life around not wanting one. The gulls were already over the north bridge and the Fishstairs, crying as they had cried the day before, and the smell of salt and wet rope came up the steps to meet them before the fish did.

She set the basket down at the edge of the fish-trade where the shade of the bridge fell across the boards and stopped, in a patch of ground no slate had ever reached. It was the same care she had used at the Drawing: a place where he could see and not be seen into. The boy understood the shape of it without having a word for the shape, which was Grathic and which he had absorbed through the ear and had never been given the key to. He was nine days old and carried everywhere and entered nowhere. The shade smelled of river silt and cold rope, and the sun had not yet done anything about either.

It was another room of the same architecture he had been finding since the first day: a bench or a board, a draught that came under it from somewhere, a place where a thing was missing and nobody mentioned the missing. The kiln-house had a ridge along the skirting board; the death-house had the same ridge thicker; the Vail bench had a pot that gave nothing when it was set down; the Fishstairs had a landing where a step should have been. Here there was a girl with green at her mouth and a woman with a count in her thumb, and a patch of shade where a slate did not lie open. He set the room beside the others and did not join it, and the gulls went over and the shadow of the bridge moved half a hand across the boards.

Dessa Silt stood at the end of her nineteen steps with her shoulder against nothing.

She was twenty-four and had been worked older by a life spent in other people’s cold: thin, with slip-grey nails and wet clay dried the colour of old bone at her hem, and a pale scar the width of a sieve-wire running up the inside of her left forearm. The wind came off the steps and went into her, and she coughed from the slip pits in a low short way, as if the cough belonged to the weather and not to her; when it had passed she put her face back into the smile she kept for saying nothing further. The boy had seen that smile once before and had no name for it. It was a door shut quietly. The door had been shut for three years.

He read what he could read without guessing and without a diagnosis, which was not much and was all he had ever been allowed. The vessels under the skin were going thin; the cough came up from somewhere deeper than the chest and brought nothing with it; the thumb went to the pale scar whenever a truth came near enough to be almost said; the shoulder leaned on a wall that was not there. He set the observations down in the order they arrived, the way he had once set down a trauma note, with nothing added and nothing joined. The habit had been the whole of his trade. Here it was the whole of his nine days, and it bought him nothing he could spend.

Nobody in the market said anything else about the cough. A woman with a board of perch called something to a man with a slate and the man called something back, and the cough went into the general noise of the market and came out of it again, unentered. He had known rooms like this in the old life: a thing everybody could hear and nobody would name, because naming it would have made it somebody’s responsibility. The surgeon in him knew what the cough was worth and the infant in him wanted the breast, and the two went on arguing in the cold without either of them getting a hearing.

“Nineteen,” she said to the drying rack, or to the cold, or to somebody who had asked her nothing. “Nineteen out and nineteen back. I counted them when the pits were water and I counted them after they froze. Four loaves on the seventh of Stone in eight hundred and twelve, three to the levy and one eaten with my feet up, and a bit and a bit and a bit to the woman who comes with a basket.” She smoothed the scar with her thumb, the way another woman might smooth a cuff. “Weather,” she said, because the cough had come up again and wanted a name put on it before somebody put one there.

The date was a handle, and he knew a handle when he saw one: specific enough to hold, ordinary enough to be taken for nothing. She had put a year and a day and a number of loaves around the thing too large to say, and the thing stayed where it was, and the door stayed shut. The count itself had gone out of her feet into her thumb six years ago and had run there ever since without any legs to carry it. The boy lay at the edge of it and did not know he was inside it. He was put among the bits, and did not know he had been put there, and could not have corrected the entry if he had; he had been entered in two accounts already without being asked, and this was a third, and it was the quietest of the three because nobody thought it was an account at all.

He had counted the kiln’s breaths and the floorboards and the bell and the death-house bench, four, four and a gap in each, and had put them side by side and joined none of them. Nineteen was a new number and did not fit the shape, and he did not try to make it. To make a number fit was a kind of deciding, and he had no standing to decide, and the thumb went on counting because counting kept a larger thing out of the room.

The cold came in at cheek and temple and wrist, in that order, and he knew the order as he knew the back of his own hand, and could no more argue with it than he could have argued with the fact of having no teeth. The thread of drool at the corner of his mouth had frozen on the walk down and nobody wiped it, because a basket at the edge of a fish board is a pot set down and left; it is not a person who needs wiping. He had once been the man who decided whether a patient was worth warming. He was now a thing the shade was kept off, and the keeping was exact and unentered and he could not refuse it.

Sella said something short to the board about eels, and Dessa answered about the weather, and the two women did not look at each other in the way women look at each other when there is nothing between them. Dessa’s eyes went over the basket and past it and came back to the drying rack. She did not look into it. He had learned to read that kind of not-looking in nine days: the man on the block had priced him, this woman kept him, and neither had thought to ask whether he would consent to either. There was no word in the district for the difference and he had stopped expecting one.

Somewhere back toward the Cross the market bell went, and he counted it the way he counted everything: four, four, and a place where a stroke should have been. the bell was a Court instrument doing a temple’s work and nobody crossed himself at it, and the gap was the part he kept. The nineteen went on running in the thumb without legs, and the door stayed shut, and the morning went on being an ordinary morning in a district that had counted what it had and did not care to say it.

Across the market a girl stood with green at the corner of her mouth.

She was a silhouette against the pots and the light, with the pale staining of lead glaze at the corner of her mouth where she had been tasting her work and had not wiped it away. The boy took in the green and the stillness of her and the fact that her attention was on a row of pots three stalls away and not on the basket at his side. She said nothing. She did not look across at him once. He did not know her name and would not know it for years, and the green meant nothing to him except that it was green and belonged to her face the way the slime belonged to the woman at the eel board; he filed it without knowing he filed it, and went on looking at the pots because she was looking at the pots.

Mira Silt was at the eel board with slime dried pale on the sleeves of her coat and the top of her left ear gone. She held her head a little to the right against the cold where the ear had been, and she flicked an eel-bone against her thumb while she decided whether to lie, and put it away when she had decided. The boy had watched her do it the day before and had learned nothing from the watching except that the bones moved when the answer was being chosen, which was a piece of information he could not use and could not forget.

A man asked her the price of eels. “You want a price or you want the pair?” she said. “The pair’s two marks and two short, and I’ve counted them twice, and I’ll count them again after I’ve eaten.” The man went away with a price he had not asked for and thanked her for it, and she went on with the knife as if no account had passed between them. He had heard the trick all his life in corridors outside operating rooms, where a nurse told a family everything and nothing by answering the question the family had not put into words. He watched her mouth instead of her hands and learned nothing she had not decided he should learn.

Somebody said a figure — two marks, or two pair, or the days to the Five given out by a voice that had counted what it had — and Mira’s left hand went in under the apron to a cord at her waist, and her thumb found a knot, and the hand came away and went back to the knife. The boy saw the movement and not the knots. He had seen the hand go there three times in two mornings and had tried to make the counting and the cord agree, and had never managed it, because the counting belonged to the stall and the cord belonged to something the stall did not hold. A thing done often enough becomes invisible to the people who do it, and she had done this for years; he understood that much, having spent a life watching men do small things with their hands until the small things became the whole man.

The cord was grey wool the colour of a wet morning, and it lay at her waist where an apron string might have lain and could have passed for one. Under it, twisted twice around the belt so that it lay close and did not swing, was a second cord. That one had never gone up the river to Nine Pots. In all the years she had walked that road she had come back with it still dry and still in its place, and she had never once lifted it at a door. The boy could see the two of them where they lay, and could see that the thumb went always to the first and never to the second. He had no word for what the keeping was and no word for the difference between them, and the wool went on holding whatever it held while the eels went over the board.

Her not-looking was not the same as the other woman’s. Dessa’s was a door shut with somebody outside it; Mira’s was a door held on the latch, which could be opened to let a thing out and shut again before anyone saw the movement. He could not have said what either kept, but he could hear the difference in the way the sentences were built: one woman said the number of loaves, the other asked a question and answered it herself so that nothing in the sentence wanted a reply. One kept by counting; one kept by leaving no seam for anybody to enter. She did not look into the basket either, and he put that not-looking beside the other and kept the two apart, as he kept all his objects apart.

He watched a woman keep accounts in a material no Court could confiscate. He had spent forty years among institutions that could take a man’s name off a wall and put it in a book and send him to a room where nobody leaned in to ask whether the name was true, and he had learned early that the only record worth keeping was the one the institution did not know existed. Here it was: grey wool, at a fish board, in the hands of a woman who answered a question with another question so that nothing in the sentence would want an answer. He had no words in Grathic for the knowing. He had nine days of life and a hand that went up an inch and came down, and the knowing went in and stayed, and there was no one in the market he could have told it to and no reason he could have given if he had tried.

Attention was the only thing he had ever been able to keep, and it had never once, in forty years or in nine days, given him a hand to act with. He counted because counting was what he did when there was nothing to do: four, four and a gap in the bell, nineteen and nineteen in the thumb, four to the landing and a landing where a step should have been on the stairs. Every number was useless and none of them could be taxed. He kept them in a file no collector would think to look for and that he could not open himself, and the file grew, and the market went on with its ordinary arithmetic.

Mira gave him the crust before anybody could make a speech about it.

It came across the edge of the board in two brisk movements, the same movements she used for moving a bone or shoving a loose board out of a barrow man’s path, and it went into the fold at his side, not into his hand and not toward his mouth, because she knew as well as he did that there was no hand on the end of his arm that could close on anything. “It came from a warm house, that’s all,” she said; and then, because the sentence was turning into a speech, she shut it before it could: “There’s barley in it and a little smoke and it’s a crust, not a sermon.” She did not look into the basket. She had not looked into it all morning and she went on not looking now, and went back to the eels.

He wanted the breast. He wanted the crust. He could have wanted both at once and did, and the two wants argued in him while the women went on with their arithmetic, and neither want got a hand to act with. The adult in him knew that the crust had been given by a woman who had paid for it with something she would not name; the infant in him knew only that it smelled of smoke and barley and lay at his side out of reach. Both were true and the second was louder, which was the whole humiliation of nine days.

It was brown at the edge where the smoke had taken it. The barley smell came through the wool in small waves as the basket shifted, and under the barley was the smoke of a house he knew, the Vail smoke that had been in the wool since the first morning and was entered in no account, not even the private one the house kept and did not show. The warmth lay against his side through the fold: a small flat warmth with no weight in it that he could neither lift nor push away. He smelled it. He could not eat it. His mouth worked under the wool and made the small wet sound, and the thread of drool at the corner froze where Sella had left it, and the crust lay where the woman had put it, whole and warm and entered nowhere.

The barley had come from a house with a fire in it, and the fire had been fed by somebody who got up before light to feed it, and the smoke had gone into the crust in that house before the crust came across a market board in a woman’s hand. There was a payer for every warmth in this district and a recipient who could not refuse it, and the payer and the recipient lay at the edge of the same shade without either of them saying whose fire it had been.

He had once reduced a shoulder in the dark and packed a cavity with both hands and known, in the ninety seconds before anybody else knew, which of twenty people would live and which would die whatever he did. He could not lift a crust an inch into his own mouth. The hand outside the wool went up toward it and stopped at the height of a button and came down again, and the warmth went on lying at his side. The fact was so plain and so funny and so awful that there was no room in nine days of life to be angry at it. He had been given a warmth by a woman he could not thank, in a market that would not have believed he understood the gift, and the gift was entered in nothing, not even in the file he kept for things nobody else would enter.

Sella had not seen the crust go in, or had seen it and made nothing of it, which was the same thing where she was concerned. She stood a half-pace off from the board and half a pace from nothing, with the little-door burn shut on nothing, and she said to the wool what she said when the draught came under the door at home: “Keep it off his ears. It’s the river wind.” She did not thank the woman at the eels and did not refuse her, because a refusal would have been an answer, and a thank-you would have been an entry, and both would have turned the crust into a thing with a name on it. The house took what was given and kept the giving out of the account, and the boy lay in the fold with the warmth of another house against him and no word in Grathic for either house.

He had been carried to the edge of the market and set down to watch a woman count, and found himself counted by her without her knowing it, and the two things were the same thing seen from different sides. She had put him among the bits because a woman with no house counts what she can count and leaves the rest in the dark; he had watched her count because counting was the only thing in the district he had ever been given leave to do. Neither of them could have said what the count was for. The surgeon in him wanted to name the thing and the infant in him wanted the breast, and between them the morning went on, and the crust went on being warm.

He set the new objects beside the old ones in the file he could not open: the grey cord and the second cord kept apart, the eel-bones moving hand to hand, nineteen in a thumb, the brown edge of a crust, the green at a girl’s mouth, the bell’s gap back toward the Cross. Beside them lay the two the Ash ridges, the charred slate in the kiln ash, the barrow of grey going out toward the north gate, the death-house bench’s four and four and a knot, the interval where a note had not come. He joined none of them. Joining would have been a deciding, and he had no standing to decide, and the file went on growing without anybody’s permission and without a single entry another person could read.

Dessa had not watched the giving. She stood at the end of her nineteen steps with the door shut on her face and counted the nineteenth into her thumb again, the way a woman counts a thing already counted because the counting is the point and the thing counted is not. The cough came up once more and she called it weather and went on with the count. She did not look into the basket. She had not said the number in three years and had not said the name in three years, and the number lay in her thumb and the name lay under the pale scar, and neither went on any slate in Sill.

The boy lay at the edge of the shade with the crust warm in the fold at his side. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward it and came down. Across the market Mira’s thumb came away from the grey cord and went back to the knife; the girl with the green at her mouth went on looking at the pots; and Dessa’s smile shut the door on nothing at all. The bell went four, four and a gap somewhere back toward the Cross. Nobody had said the number and nobody had said the name and nobody had looked into the basket. The morning went on being an ordinary morning, and the warmth lay in the fold, entered nowhere.

By dusk the wind had gone round and the gulls had stopped crying over the north bridge and gone to roost in the stone of it. Sella Vail lifted the basket off the shaded boards with the hand that carried the little-door burn and settled it against her hip the way she settled a filled pot, and started up the Clay Yard with the eel bucket knocking at her knee. The boy lay under the fold with a crust warm against his side that she had not put there and would not take out. The smell of the river went up the yard with them: salt and silt and the iron cold off the Ilm, and under it the woodsmoke that had been in the wool since the first morning of his nine days. She said nothing about the market and nothing about the woman at the eels and nothing about the crust, because a thing said aloud becomes a thing that wants an answer, and she had arranged the whole of her life around not wanting one.

The warmth lay against his ribs through the wool, a small flat warmth with no weight in it that he could neither lift nor push away, and he had learned in nine days that a kindness which cannot be answered cannot be refused. He had once been the man who decided who would be warm by morning and who would not; he was now a basket at a woman’s hip with another woman’s barley against his side and no hand on the end of either arm that could close on anything. Both women had paid for the warmth and neither had entered it, and he was the place where the two payments met and went quiet. The gulls were gone. The bucket knocked. The yard rose under the basket in the ordered steps he had counted on the way down and could not count now, because a carried boy counts nothing that is done to him.

Back under the Vail kiln-house roof she put the fold right at his ear without once looking at his face, and set the basket on the bed under the roof that was not the loft above the shed. The arithmetic of the two beds had not changed in three days and would not change tonight: the foundling under the roof, the debt above the wheel. Nobody called it what it was. The door did not quite shut, as it had not shut on any night since he came under it, and the wind came in at a finger’s width and went along the floor to the corner where the Ash lay.

The first thing she did was to the fire, not to him. She took the back of a broken pot from the ash heap and used it as a shovel, drawing the coals together and laying the ash over them in a smooth blind layer, pressing the edges with the back of the pot so no air got in at the sides. “That’ll hold,” she said to the fire. Her hand did not go to the widow’s mark at her throat. That was for the hour before the kiln opened and for no other hour; the rule held as exactly in the dark as it had in the morning, and the little-door burn closed on nothing and opened and went to the bench.

Then she counted. It was not counting for anybody who could hear it. She moved her lips along the shelf where the loaves stood and the dish where the bits lay, and came back to the same place twice, and the boy watched the returning from under the fold. There was a number of days to the Five that the district had gone around and would not give out, and she would not give it either; she had arranged the loaves so that the number was not in them but the shape of it was, which was enough for a woman who had kept one house through one Long Cold and meant to keep this one through another. He had once counted twenty injured people in ninety seconds and decided which of them would be breathing by morning. He could not lift his hand to the dish, and nobody had asked him to.

Tarn came in behind her with the broom going, bent low over it the way he bent over everything, grey Ash worked into the creases of his knuckles and the pale ring at his left wrist showing where the iron clip had sat six years ago. He swept the threshold and swept it again and set the broom against the wall. Then he took the small bench knife out of his pocket — the boy had not seen him take it, only seen the place on the sill where it had not been — and put it back where it could be checked.

“I took the small knife. I put it back. You can check,” he said to the yard and the bench and the fire, and to nobody who had asked him. Six years of a seven he had counted and would go on counting; the seventh he held back, the one entry in the house the house could not check. He said nothing about the note that had not come three days ago, when a pot was set down softly on a bench and gave nothing. He had said nothing about it for three days and would say nothing tonight, and under this roof there were now two people keeping the same silence and neither of them able to call it by name. A loan that can hear is a loan that can be called; the sentence had never been said in the house and lay in it all the same, as the Ash lay in the corner.

“Yes, house,” he said to Sella’s back. “I’ll sweep it again.”

Jorra Vail came in from the shed with bone-coloured slip dried to old bone up both forearms and the little finger of his left hand bent the way it had been since he was eleven. There was no slate dust on him tonight. The boy looked at the clean creases above the bend of the finger and could not ask which the absence meant, any more than he could ask about the presence of the day before.

“You’ll do,” Jorra said to a pot on the bench, in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either. He went to the damper and moved it before Sella had finished telling him the fire was low, and the fire took the instruction and went on. He did not say the back room was cold. He had said it yesterday and nobody had taken it further, and tonight he did not say it at all, and the silence about the room stood where he had left it, which was the only answer he would give and the only one he would take.

The coat kept its salt-stiff shape on its peg behind the door and the hollow seat in the shed kept the lean of twenty years and the ridge along the skirting board ran to the corner and turned it without a break. Nobody swept it. Nobody had swept it since the Long Cold and nobody would sweep it before the Last Day. Garl Vail’s proverb went to the pot as if the pot were a person who could hear: leave the crack showing; people trust a thing they can see has been handled. It was said to the clay and not to the boy, and the boy took it into the file with the rest of the things said to pots. Three doors down the yard the threshold of House Silt stood in the angle of the wall, a dark line where a woman had stood that morning with her shoulder against nothing and counted nineteen into her thumb.

He set the day’s new objects beside the old ones in the file he could not open and had no hand to open, and did not join any of them.

There was the grey cord at a woman’s waist, the colour of a wet morning, with a second cord twisted twice under it that had never gone up the river to Nine Pots and had never been lifted at a door. There were the eel-bones moving hand to hand across a board in Sill, each movement a figure said under the breath and then put away. There were nineteen steps from a pit to a drying rack and nineteen back, now run in a thumb without any legs to carry them, and the pale scar under the thumb that the thumb went to whenever a truth came near enough to be almost said. There was the death-house bench with four nails and four and a knot the width of a thumb, and the interval where a pot on a bench under this roof had been set down softly and given nothing.

Beside these lay the older things: the ridge along the Vail skirting board near two thumbs wide, the ridge in the death-house thicker by a finger’s width and turning the corner without a break, the charred edge of a slate in the kiln ash with the writing gone off the end, the barrow of grey going out through the north gate toward the Bone Orchard in the ordinary work of a market-day. He set them side by side exactly, in the order they had arrived, with nothing added and nothing taken away, the way he had once laid out a trauma note before anybody came in to make it mean something. The habit had been the whole of his trade. Here it was the whole of his nine days, and it bought him nothing he could spend.

None of the objects had a name in Grathic. He had no word for the cord and no word for the difference between the two cords, no word for what a woman kept in a thumb, no word for the sound that had not come from the pot and therefore no word for the interval where it should have been. To name a thing in this district was to hand it to somebody who could enter it; he had watched a man with a soft cuff lay a hand on a slate before he looked at a dying man and say the verbs with the people taken out, and he had learned what a name does when it is written in a neat hand. So the file stayed wordless, and the boy lay silent, and the knowing grew in him without anybody’s permission and without a single entry another person could read.

Joining the objects would have been a deciding, and he had no standing to decide. He had learned that in the first days, in a room where a woman reached for a slate before she reached for a child: a thing written down is a thing handed over. The surgeon who had once sorted twenty people in ninety seconds and sent some to a warm bed and some to a cold one had no appetite left for deciding which of these objects was the same object wearing a different face. Two ridges, a charred slate, a barrow of grey, a bench with a knot, a gap in a count, a cord at a waist, a crust in a fold: they lay side by side and he let them lie. The suspicion that they were one thing seen seven ways was a thing he kept where he kept the gap, which was the only part of any count he had ever been allowed to have.

The count stayed four, four and a gap. Four breaths of the kiln and the breath it missed. Four nails on the near board, four on the next, and a knot the width of a thumb. Four strokes of the bell back toward the Cross and the stroke that was not there. Four nails on the death-house bench and four and the knot. Four steps down to the water and a landing that was not a step. Nineteen and nineteen in a woman’s thumb, which did not fit the shape and would not be made to fit. And always the space between her thumb and the pale scar, which was the same gap as the others and the only one that had a person in it. The gap was the part nobody had thought to enter and the only place in any room he had been in where nothing had been written down. He could not write there either. He had no hand, no slate, no word in the language of the house, and no standing; but the gap was his in a way the warmth in the fold was not, because nobody had paid for it and nobody could take it and nobody had even thought to look at it.

Forward, not rest. The household counted, without saying it, what it had against the days before Kindling. Sella had counted the loaves twice and come back to the same place; Tarn held the seventh year back as he had held it for six years running; Jorra moved the damper as if the fire owed him an answer it would not give. Low Kiln still said three days to the Five when it said anything at all and would not say what it counted. Account-day and the slate at the Cross were ahead of them, and with them another reading of the neat hand whose pause he had seen three times and had not joined to the pause at the flare or the pause on the birth-slate, and would not make a fourth until the room told him to. Alda’s notes lay turned down where no mother and no officer would read them. The Drawing was behind him and nothing had been explained.

The boy lay on his bed under the roof with the crust warm in the fold at his side, the coals holding under their ash, the grey ridge running to the corner and turning it without a break, and the door standing a finger’s width open to the wind. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth and came down. Four, four and a gap. Somewhere out in the district a voice that had counted what it had gave three days to the Five and would not say what it counted, and the fire went on holding, and the fold went on not staying under his chin.

On Stone 13 the kiln opened under a hand that touched the widow’s mark and let it go. Sella Vail fed the boy from the house’s dish, barley thinned with milk; she said the house, never I, and nothing was entered for the warmth. A pot waited on the bench. The boy turned his head before Jorra Vail set it down, expecting the flat knock; the pot gave nothing, and he kept the interval where the note should have been. Tarn saw it and stopped his broom half a stroke at the empty set-down. Two ears under one roof, and no word between them.

Jorra spoke to the clay, slip bone-pale on his forearms and no slate dust; Sella went around the number of days to Kindling; Tarn counted six years and held the seventh back, and stopped for two breaths when she said he had done well. The boy laid the death-house bench beside the two ridges of Ash, the charred slate and the barrow of grey --- four nails and four and a knot --- and joined none. His drool froze; his hand rose an inch and fell. At dusk she banked the coals and did not touch the mark.

On Stone 14 she carried him to the Fishstairs. Mira Silt worked among eels, pale slime on her sleeves, the top of her left ear gone --- a hook by her account, a knife by the scar. She gave Berr Clay four eels where six should go; he had paid her two marks short for six years. Asked whose was the basket, she said the child was warm, off the slate and not on one; when a number was spoken her hand went under her apron to a cord. The boy saw the hand, not the knots. A collector crossed the bridge, and for two sentences a cafeteria at four in the morning hummed and a wrapper was eaten to its last crumb. Sella bought eels and carried him home.
