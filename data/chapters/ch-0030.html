The Tally Under the Shelf

Days 129–131 · Reed 10–12, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, the Weir and Sill

The figure read at the Cross is a figure in the air. The mark under the shelf is a mark on the wood. One is heard by two hundred; neither is the account.

— A saying of the loaf-shelves of Low Kiln

— Day 129 —

Reed 10, Tenthday, Y.H. 813

The clay road came down into Sill with the river wind on it, and Sella Vail carried the boy down it at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, going around a board set out to dry as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. She gave the instruction to the wool and to the river wind and never to the basket: keep it off his ears, it had come all the way down with them and would go all the way back up, and a basket set down was a pot set down and left. The wool rode back from the small bare joint and the cold found the place it had come for and went under the fold at his ear in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody under any roof in the district had been given an instruction about the thread, so it stayed where it had been put. He was one hundred and twenty-nine days old, four months and nine days, and nobody on the road said so. Low Kiln thinned behind them, with its wet wool and its tallow smoke and the faint sour tang that had worked into the corners and would not come out with scrubbing, and ahead the square opened where the four roads met and the first Account-day of Reed was already leaning over its block.

The square had the look of a pot set on to boil with the lid not quite on. Two hundred people leaned toward the reading block, because a man who speaks low makes a crowd come close and being close is its own kind of having been told; the market went on behind them and around them, closing on the wet boards the way mud closes over a boot-print, the figs and the turnips and the pots finding their own level without ever running together. Eight pigs had gone over the wet boards since first grey and the assessor’s hand had been on the slate before his eyes went to the rail, entering them before anybody could ask which eight he meant. The reading block stood wet under the slate, the boards dark along their lower edges and the straw trampled into the seams behind, and the mark of the pigs went off it in the thin wind the way a wet footprint goes off a flagstone, leaving the shape first and then less than the shape. There were four ways out: the road down to the south bridge and Slate Row, the steep street up to High Kiln, the north bridge, and the road toward the Bone Orchard, and on an Account-day a man stood in every one of them with a figure he would not give. Sella set the basket in the moot-post’s finger’s width of shade where no slate lay open on the boards, and stood a half-pace off. The little-door burn across the back of her hand closed on the wicker’s edge and then on nothing. She gave the instruction again to the wool and to the wind and never to the basket, and did not look down into it.

The crowd had an appetite for a number the way a wet room has an appetite for fire: not for what it was but for the having of it. Figures came up out of fists and went into mouths and came out again on the shoulders of men going up the road, and nobody asked the figures to agree. A woman set a heap of turnips on a flat stone and left two figures standing side by side on it without making them equal; a man selling eels named one price back at another and let both stand. Tallow smoke from a pot stall lay low over the turnips and the trays, and hot clay came and went on the wind off the north road, so that the square smelled of a firing nobody had paid for and a breakfast nobody had sat down to eat. The boy lay at the edge of the shade with cold iron at his cheek where the post’s iron bracket had been bolted and left, and cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, the wool riding back from the wrist so the cold found the bare place and went under the fold. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far. The surgeon in him read the morning as a chart laid open over a note that had not been turned: the lines were neat, the hand was steady, and nothing under the neat lines had ever been entered. The child in him was cold at the ear and wanted the crust that was not in this square. Both were true. The second was louder, and neither could be handed to anybody.

Berr Clay came up to the block short, a little before the top of the morning, with the red wool cord of his office folded under his coat instead of worn across it and the green of tasted glaze at one corner of his mouth. Hot clay came off him with the river wind and under it the smell of nothing at all, which is the most expensive smell in Grath. His hands were large and clean. He wiped his thumb twice on his apron before he touched nothing, and held a narrow slate unopened against his forearm, the writing toward himself, and passed a finger over the figure as a man checks a seam before he trusts it, and let his eyes rest on the writing a breath longer than the writing required. He did not look toward the basket. He never said I; he said the notice, the figure, the levy and the house, and he used the phrase as the notice says the way another man might cross himself, a small sign made with the mouth instead of the hand. His voice was low enough that the two hundred came in closer to hear it, which they did, leaning, the way cloth leans when a weight is laid along one edge.

“One in twelve, as the notice says,” Berr said, and the words went into the top of the crowd and stayed there a moment before going on over the heads and the pigs and the posts and out at the four ways. He set the slate flat on the reading block with the figure uppermost, the way a man sets a dish down where it will not be slopped, and passed the finger over it once more. “The levy is called. It is not called by me.”

A man come up from the worked strips with dried mud in the creases of his wrists brought a number up out of a fist and held it toward the block, opening the fist enough to show he had one and shutting it again before anyone could see what it held. “Then what’s the cost of the hands in the strip?” the man said. “Now they’re out of it.”

“A number goes into a slate, not into a strip,” Berr said. “If your house wants it again, there’ll be a slate at the watch.” Your house. Not I. He took himself out of his own sentence as neatly as a surgeon takes a hand out of a field before the dressing goes on, and the man put the fist back into his sleeve and went off with the number still in it, going up toward High Kiln with a shoulder that carried the question and arrived nowhere with it. The figure Berr had read was not the figure paid. The true subtraction sat in a locked box in his house up the clay road, and a figure read aloud into a market is a figure that belongs to everybody and to nobody, exact, complete, and worth nothing anyone could take away and spend.

The bell went while they were still leaning. It came through the noise from the Cross and went out along the four roads longer than a church bell has any business going, four strokes, four, and then a gap where a fifth should have been, and nobody crossed himself at it. A woman with a creel shifted her load and went on weighing eels; a man with a pig by the ear did not stop. The boy kept the heard bell on a place of its own beside the told bell of the Long Night and did not lay one across the other, because a bell that has been heard and a bell that has been spoken of are two objects and not one account, and three likenesses in a row will stand up and call themselves a cause if a man lets them.

Below the bridge the steps came down four, four and a landing a hand’s breadth out of line, and a whole market had walked over the out-of-line landing since first grey without anybody making a fall of it. At the landing the soft-cuffed clerk was still set for a measure nobody had called, his cuff grey from washing and grey again by midday, his slate held to his forearm and his hand going to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls, every time. He had not been taken off since the month turned, or if he had been taken off somebody had put him back in the dark. The market sold its reeds and its eels over the top of him the way water goes over a stone that lies where a stone lies, and did not lower its voice for him; nobody stepped around him and nobody asked what he was set there for. A girl with a creel went over the landing sideways with one hand out to the rail and went on selling without looking at her feet, and nobody made an accident of it. The boy set the washed cuff on one place and the slate held where nobody else could see the figure on another, because a man who washes his own cuff has a hunger or a habit, and a man who holds a slate where nobody can read it has a use, and a use wants a payer. A measure set down before anybody calls it is a measure set down ahead of something; he kept that as a shape and did not make a rule of it.

A man come up from the strips and stood at the edge of the reading with the day’s mud dried pale in the creases of his wrists. His eyes went around a place in the returning line, the way a woman’s eyes go around Ash left in a corner or a broom goes around the space beneath the lowest rack, and his feet went around it when he moved off a moment later, though there was nothing on the boards at that place for his feet to go around. The hand that had gone in on Seed 15 had not come out on Seed 27, and the yard had gone around the space every morning since, with a look and a wide step and no figure, no name, no cause, no accident, no body, no payment. The boy read the going-around before the man’s feet moved, as he had once read the afterdrop before the pulse went, and set it on a place of its own, exact and unentered. Eight pigs had gone over the block and a levy had been called over it and the empty place had been gone around on the way to it, and none of the three had been made to stand for either of the others.

He was one hundred and twenty-nine days old and nobody in the square said so. The figure that had gone into the air belonged to everybody and had been given by nobody who could say what it counted; the only one who could have named the day was the one who could not form a word of it, and he lay in the moot-post’s shade with the thread frozen at his mouth and the cold at his cheek and the hand outside the wool going up an inch toward the noise and coming down. He had kept the day exact through a hundred and twenty-eight others and would keep this one exact too, not because exactness bought anything but because the alternative was to let it go the way the figures went, from a fist to a mouth to a shoulder and nowhere. He had learned in two lives that a number spoken over a crowd and a number written in a private place are two objects even when they are the same figure, and he kept them on two places with no line drawn between them. Six hearings of the full note stood where they had stood, beside and never among, with no seventh; the told bell lay beside the heard bell; the clerk’s slate lay beside Berr’s twice-wiped thumb and the man’s opened-and-shut fist, each on its own place and none of them joined. He set the figure read aloud among these as one more object on a tray, a chart closed over a note nobody had opened, worth nothing to a man who could not read the hand that had written it.

By mid-morning the crowd had stopped looking at the block. The figure had been written in market mud and the mark of it was already drying where the boots and the pigs had stood, the wet going off it in the thin wind until nothing but the shape of a stroke remained and then less than that. The figures the crowd had carried away went with them out at the four ways and left nothing on the boards but the drying mark and a little trampled straw. The basket sat in the moot-post’s shade at the half-pace, with the wool riding back and the thread frozen, and nobody in the square looked into it. Sella stood a half-pace off with the little-door burn shut on nothing, and gave the instruction to the wind and never to the basket, and the market went on over the top of both and left them where they lay.

The clay road came up from the square with the market noise following and losing hold, the way water dragged off a stone goes thin at the edges and then less than thin. Sella Vail carried the boy at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, going around a board set out to dry as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, going wide also around the space beneath the lowest rack where the broken birth-slates lay and nobody went. The instruction went with them the way the wool went, given to the wool and to the river wind and never to the basket: keep it off his ears, it had come all the way down with them and would go all the way back up, and a basket set down was a pot set down and left. The little-door burn on the back of her hand closed on the wicker’s edge and then on nothing. Low Kiln came back around them, wet wool and tallow smoke and the faint sour tang that had worked into the corners and would not come out with scrubbing. Cold arrived in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, the wool riding back from the small bare joint so the cold found the bare place and went under the fold at his ear. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours, and nobody under any roof in the district had been given an instruction about the thread, so it stayed where it had been put. He was one hundred and twenty-nine days old, four months and nine days, and nobody on the road said so.

At the Vail kiln-house she did not set him where the basket usually stood. She set it low and a little back, near the bench leg, at the angle at which a man lying on the floor sees the underside of a board that a man standing sees only the top of. It was not a place she chose for him; it was a place the light chose, coming in low through the finger’s-width door and running along the grain under the shelf. She told the fire it was the thaw. She gave the instruction to the pot and to the broom and never to the basket — feed it, turn it, go around what the water left, it cost enough to come in and would cost more to take out — and she did not look down into the basket. The boy lay at the low angle with cold iron at his cheek where the bench bracket had been bolted and left, and his head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far. The child in him was cold at the ear and wanted the crust two inches off in the fold; the man in him read the angle of the light and the grain of the board before he read either. Both were true. The second was quieter and the first was louder, and neither could be handed to anybody.

At the hearth she set the pot down where the light fell on its rim and turned the handle toward the side of the bench where a reaching hand would find it, and the pot gave its low note as it settled, full beneath the small flat sound that lived in it from the wheel. Six hearings of the full note stood where they had stood, five from four sources and the sixth from a person, beside and never among, and he did not make a seventh of the pot under this roof. The wheel’s own note came through the wall from the shed and went the same way, heard and not entered; the bell of that morning had been a told bell and a heard bell in two places, and three likenesses in a row will stand up and call themselves a cause if a man lets them. He let none. The surgeon’s ear, which had once sorted a cracked vessel from a sound one across a crowded room, sorted the notes and filed them and could spend none of them; the child’s ear was cold and wanted the crust.

Above him the loaf shelf held its row the way a row is held in a house that keeps its days badly: loaves pushed along it, a glance at the clay jar on the wall, and a space at the row’s end that nobody called a number. The row kept two gaps. One was the old space at the end, the one that had come in with the planting and that a neighbour could name in one breath without ever seeing it as a thing that was missing. The other was a palm’s breadth of bare board that had come in on the night of Seed 6 with no scrape, no crumb, no print and no word, as if a loaf had lifted itself off in the dark and gone. Sella moved her lips along the row and gave nobody a figure. Her glance went once to the clay jar on the wall and came back; the glaze was cold in the warm room, and she did not touch it. The fold at his ear kept one stiff brown-edged crust and an empty place beside it that had no name. That was a record out of the other room, the room under the roof where he slept, and the shelf did not touch it. Two rooms that did not touch. A saying of the loaf-shelves of Low Kiln had it that two gaps stay two gaps until a finger lays them together and an account then wants a name, and no finger in the district had laid these together.

From the low angle the underside of the shelf was a roof, and he lay under it the way a man lies under a bridge and reads the joints nobody thinks to look at from above. A shelf seen from below becomes a roof, and a roof is a thing a man keeps over him without reading the boards. He had spent a hundred and twenty-nine days under roofs and had never once been given a reason to turn one over.

There were marks on it.

Short strokes in charcoal, grouped in little clusters along the grain, some dark and pressed hard enough to leave a smooth track where the stick had dug, some the colour of old smoke and gone nearly into the wood. The clusters sat a finger’s breadth apart, and the ones at the end of the row were fainter than the ones near the middle, as if the hand that made them had gone away from the task or had grown tired of pressing. They did not make Grathic words and no slate had been set under them to catch what they meant. They were a keeping, put where nobody thought to look, above a row that was kept badly on top. The surgeon’s eye went to them the way it went to a note turned down behind a chart — not because the note was important, but because somebody had taken the trouble to set it where the chart would not show it, and the care told him more than the writing would. He saw the pressure of each cluster the way he had once seen the edge of a wound, dark where the hand had leaned and faint where it had lifted, and he knew before he had finished the first group that he would not be allowed to read them.

He did not read them. He did not count the strokes in a cluster, or hold the shapes in the dark where he could keep them, or turn his head to show them to a man from Slate Row, because there was no man from Slate Row under this roof and no hand of his that could have pointed. He did not lay the marks under the shelf beside the two gaps above it, or beside the one crust in the fold in the other room, or beside Alda Reed’s little tin with its six pages turned toward a floorboard, or Mira Silt’s cords with the second twist twisted twice beneath the grey, or Orra Weir’s knot-string with the word that would have named its last double turn still unspoken, or the round pewter tin above the abandoned board-bed that nobody entered, or the short grey cord the reed-cutter had put back inside his coat at the Weir. Neighbours had laid the fold’s empty place and the shelf’s old gap under one cause in a single breath more than once and gone off with their pots without seeing they had made two bare boards one absence; he had set each saying on a place of its own and had not made any of them his own. The marks under the board would only give those sayings company and let them stand up and call themselves a cause. He set the marks on a place of their own, advanced and unpaid, held for the far-off day when a hand not his laid the district’s private records together and an account at last wanted its name.

It was the commonest kind of keeping in the district, and that was why he put it furthest from the others. A slate, a tin, a cord, a jar on a wall, a fist shut on a number — those were things a person could be caught carrying, and a person caught carrying one looked for somewhere to put it. Marks on the underside of a shelf were set down by somebody who expected no one to look up, and a record nobody expects to be read is the hardest one to lay beside another without making the two agree. He did not know what the clusters counted, and not knowing was a reason to leave them where they lay rather than a reason to bring them near. In the other life he would have called it a note turned down and gone looking for who had turned it; here there was nobody to ask and no hand of his that could have asked, and the looking had nowhere to go.

A neighbour came in with a wet pot, her sleeves dark to the elbow and water standing in the creases of her wrists, and a smell of river water coming off her that was colder than the room, and she stood in the doorway holding the pot against her hip the way a woman holds a thing she has already set down in her mind. “What will the row want before the tend is out?” she said, and her eyes went to the shelf and not to the basket, because the shelf was where a woman looked for an answer in this house and the basket was a pot set down and left. Sella’s hand went toward the clay jar on the wall and stopped, and came away without touching the cold glaze, and the end of the instruction went to the pot instead. “Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there,” she said. “The house has said it.” The neighbour carried the question off as level as she had carried the pot in, with nothing slopped and nothing given, and the water went on dripping from her sleeves into the trodden ash behind her and was gone into it. The boy set the asking and the answering on a place of their own: a cost asked in the form of a direction answered with a direction, arriving nowhere as a figure, the two lines meeting nowhere and leaving nobody entered.

The widow’s mark hung where it hung, a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and Sella did not touch it. She touched it before an opening and at no other hour, and a shelf was not an opening and a day in the middle of a tend was not an opening, so the mark stayed on its cord against her throat while the dry smell of the loaf shelf went up past it and the sharper smell of charcoal went under. The Ash lay in the corner where the ridge had turned, the clean dark line and the new finer hairline beside it, unswept and called the thaw; she had told the fire it was the thaw and did not look toward the corner. She turned the coals with the back of the broken pot and gave the end of the instruction to the broom, and the house went on around its two absences without entering either.

Under the shelf the marks waited where they had been put. The boy lay at the low angle with the row above him standing with its two gaps and Sella between him and the board with her back to the basket, moving her lips along the row and counting nothing where nothing was to be counted. The low light went off the underside and the marks went dark in the corner where the grain turned, and the three things stayed each on its own place: the row above, the marks below, and the woman’s back between. None of them became an account. He was one hundred and twenty-nine days old and nobody under this roof said so, and the marks under the shelf stayed unread and joined to nothing while the house went on with its day.

The going of the light came up the clay road before the boy did, travelling ahead of him in long thin strips that lay across the ruts and went out one after another as the sun dropped behind the kilns. Sella Vail carried the boy at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, and the half-pace was not slowness but a measurement: a slate lying open on a bench was a thing to be gone around with the basket held level, and she had gone around so many of them that her walk had become the shape of a question she never asked. The instruction went with them the way the wool went, given to the wool and to the river wind and never to the basket: keep it off his ears, it had come all the way down with them and would go all the way back up, and a basket set down was a pot set down and left. The wind had come round to the east and carried tallow smoke and the faint sour tang that had worked into the corners of Low Kiln and would not come out with scrubbing. Cold arrived in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist. The wool rode back from the small bare joint and the cold found the bare place and went under the fold at his ear. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody under any roof in the district had been given an instruction about the thread, so it stayed where it had been put, stiff as a stitch set and never drawn out.

He was one hundred and twenty-nine days old, four months and nine days, and nobody on the road said so.

Under the lowest rack the broken birth-slates lay where the yard had gone around them since the year turned, entered by nobody, and she went around the space as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. A look, a wide step, no number. The boy read the going-around the way he had learned to read a chart laid face down on a desk: not the thing underneath, but the care taken with the covering, which told him more than the writing would have. He set the space beside the place in the strip where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out on Seed 27, and left the two lying side by side without drawing a line between them. Both were absences the yard kept by walking around them. Neither had been given a figure, and he gave neither one.

Inside the Vail kiln-house she set him on his bed under the roof and not in the Debt Loft. The bed had been his since he came under the roof and the loft had never been offered, and the distinction was kept the way the house kept most of its distinctions, by never mentioning it. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without once looking at his face, and left the crust where it lay. Her hand rested a half-beat longer than the setting needed before it went away, the way a hand rests on a thing it has carried a long way and cannot quite bring itself to let go of. The little-door burn closed on nothing. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and nobody under the roof had been given an instruction about that sound either.

The surgeon in him noted the half-beat and filed it where he filed the other small excesses of the house: a hand that stayed where a hand had finished, a thumb wiped twice where once would serve, a finger that rested on a slate after the figure had been read. The child in him was cold at the ear and wanted the brown-edged crust two inches off in the fold, stiff and past smelling of anything. Both were true. The second was louder; the first was the one he could not hand to anybody, because there was nobody under this roof who would have known what a half-beat was worth.

At the hearth she told the fire it was the thaw. She said it to the coals the way she said everything that had to be heard by something that could not answer, and the fire took the words and gave back nothing a slate could carry. She turned the coals with the back of a broken pot, feeding the low places and leaving the blind ash over the rest, and said, “The house will keep it,” where another woman would have said I. The I was not withheld out of modesty. A thing said aloud became a thing the Court could enter, and the house was a safer word to stand in front of a warmth nobody had paid for in public.

She stood a moment with the back of the broken pot in her hand, counting something under her breath toward no opening. Her lips moved on a row only she could see, the household’s arithmetic in loaves and glances and the space at the end nobody called a number; she came back to the same place twice and went on from there. The count was a shape kept for the pot, not a figure given to a man with a slate. She pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn, and the grey streak at her temple showed pale off the coals, the colour of the new fine line in the corner that she had not swept and did not intend to sweep. She set the pot where it could be found in the dark, rim out and handle toward the reaching hand, and did not look toward the bed.

“Go around what the water left,” she told the broom, giving the instruction to the broom before she gave it to anybody. “It cost enough to come in. It’ll cost more to take out.” The pot gave its low note as it settled, full beneath the small flat sound that lived in it from the wheel, and she left it where a hand groping in the dark would find the handle first.

The widow’s mark hung on its cord against her throat, a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it. Her hand went toward it and stopped, and came away to the pot. A mark was touched before an opening and at no other hour; a dusk was not an opening, a fire banked for the night was not an opening, a bed with a boy on it was not an opening. She had lost a husband to the danger and had never described it that way even to herself. The clean dark line where the Ash ridge had turned lay unswept at the corner with the new finer hairline beside it, and both were called the thaw and would go when they went. She did not look at either. The private roll she kept in her head was not shown to a man from Slate Row, and a dusk was not an hour for showing it to herself either. She went to her own bed.

Under the roof the dark came on in the order it always came on, first the light going out of the oiled paper at the window, then the fire’s low red, then the sound of water. Through the finger’s-width of the door came the river’s note of water over water, beyond the reed beds and the Weir and the whole length of Low Kiln, a fifth low thing he had kept on a place of its own since he was six days old, never a hearing and never counted among the six. Through the floor under blind ash the carried fire took its coal and gave back four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for a hundred and twenty-nine days. Above him the boards kept four nails, four, and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb.

Three counts in three places, and none of them became one.

He had learned in two lives what three likenesses in a row will do if a man leaves them lying together: they stand up and call themselves a cause. The bell at the Cross was a told bell and stayed beside the heard bell; the note of water was a fifth low thing and stayed beside the four pulls; the knot in the boards was a knot and had never been a nail. He kept them lying flat where they lay, as a man keeps instruments separated on a towel so that no edge is taken for another edge. The discipline cost him nothing and bought him nothing, and he had done it so long that it had become the way he lay awake.

He turned out his file as a man lays instruments on a towel before anyone calls what the operation is for.

Six hearings of the full note stood where they had stood: five from four sources, a cup across a birth room, a pot in the wheel-shed through a wall, the district’s slate twice at the Cross, a man giving at the Drawing, and the sixth, the small flat knock out of a woman as the men lifted her, filed beside the five and never among them. No seventh had come in the day that had gone, and he had not made one to fill the place where a seventh would stand. Three near-full answers stood on their own tray, advanced and not paid: a cup across a birth room, a pot under the wheel-shed wall, a rack pot lifted a hand’s breadth too high and set down again. On the interval tray lay the intervals from Stone 13, Stone 17 and Stone 25, with the half-beat in a man’s broom behind a standing finger, the stroke that went on over the flagstones without the check, his own held breath from the market, and the two stoppings of the near-saying: the sentence about an ear under this roof being worth more than the debt that held it, brought twice to the threshold of the word that would call the loan and stopped twice there with no figure and no name. They lay as objects among objects and not as a rule. The loan that could hear was uncalled and the seventh year had not given.

Orra Weir’s cord kept the shape of the forty-first double turn with the word that would have named it still unspoken, and the word forty had not been said under this roof in all his days. The cold foot below the sill was still the cold to one woman and weather to the yard, and no silt-leach, cause or cure had been written across it. The three greens stood each on its own place, a colour and not a name. The clerk at the landing still held his slate for a measure nobody had called, his hand going to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls. The reed-cutter’s cord had gone back inside his coat with its knots unread; Mira’s grey cord and the second twist twisted twice beneath it had not been lifted at a door, her board lying beside Orra’s knot-string without touching it; Berr’s narrow slate held a figure that did not know the hand that had not come back; Alda Reed’s six pages lay turned toward a floorboard in House Silt with both tins shut, and a woman more mornings dead than any living person could count lay uncollected with her name unsaid.

Orra Weir stood at the lower boards with her board on its side and a cord at her waist, turning the board with the wrist that had turned it ten thousand times and pulling one knot down with her thumb to set it among the others. Her hands were like old rope; the pale eel scars lay uncovered across both forearms, and the grey streak showed first through the black hair when she moved her head. The nasal half-song of the Fen came into her voice where she was tired, as it had at the blessing and every morning since, and she did not seem to know it was there.

A weir woman asked what the levy would take now the hands were out of the reeds.

“The ninth of Ilmen,” Orra said. “The collector’s boat came round the bend on the flood and had six oars. One man stood at the stern and never touched them.”

“That isn’t the levy.”

“It’s a date. The river takes what the Court doesn’t. You can shout at either one. I’ve tried.” She pulled another knot down.

A date given where a figure had been asked was an answer with the question taken out of it: a chart closed over a note nobody had opened, with the neat margin left standing where the finding should have gone. The man at the stern, keeping his hands off the oars while other men pulled, went on a place of his own, apart from the midwife’s reaching hand and the thane’s twice-wiped thumb, and the boy wrote no cause across the three. Her thumb went to the cord again as the sentence ended. He could not tell whether that was a lie or only the habit of counting, and did not try to tell; a thing done twice in front of him was a thing seen, and the not-knowing he kept whole.

The shape of the last knot on the cord was legible without its name: a double turn, tail tucked, tied left over right, with the small extra weight of a thumb pressed in. The word stayed where it had stayed since the year of the flower. She watched what there was to watch at the edge of the shade and got no answer from the basket, and nobody asked her and she did not ask.

At the outer boards Mira Silt worked the eels with slime dried in long grey strokes on her sleeves and river silt dried pale in the hollow of her neck, her head held a little to the right where the top of her left ear had been, her small smile kept on her face the way another woman keeps a door on the latch. Asked whether the run would be thin now the hands were in the reeds, she answered the question and then her own. “Thin? Who’s to say thin? It comes when it comes. Ask the river.” She flicked a string of eel-bones against her thumb while deciding whether to lie, moved two from hand to hand, put one away, and said something low about a boat that had come up on the flood and nothing about the hand that had not come out of the strips.

A buyer in a coat with the river still in its hem named a figure for two pair. Mira’s thumb went under her apron to the grey wool cord and stayed there longer than the figure required; shifting a creel showed the second cord twisted twice beneath it, never carried upriver to Nine Pots, never lifted at a door, the knots on neither counted by any thumb but her own and the knot for the thin child upriver untouched. “Two marks the pair and two short,” she said. “You’ve said it twice and I’ve counted it twice. There’s nothing in the sentence that wants an answer.” The buyer took the eels and went with his figure unchallenged, and she did not look into the basket.

Her not-looking was a keeping set on a place of its own, the opposite of a pricing face turned the same way across a market, and he had no word in Grathic for either. Mira’s board lay beside Orra’s knot-string and did not touch it. The reed-cutter’s cord had gone back inside his coat with its knots unread. Three private records stood on the boards in wool and teeth and thumb, and the Court’s neat slate waited a league downriver for a measure nobody had called. He could see every one of them and enter none of them, and the two boards lying side by side without touching held the whole lesson of the morning as plainly as a thing could be held and still be worth nothing to anyone who might have looked.

At midday Sella carried him three doors down to House Silt, at the half-pace, giving the instruction to the wool and the river wind and never to the basket, and set the basket just inside the finger’s-width-open door at the edge of the light and away from every place a slate might lie. She stood a half-pace off with her burn shut on nothing, opened no slate and turned no errand into a walk to Slate Row.

The room kept the shape it had kept since the first morning. The trodden ash floor had the spring of old bread under a boot; a grey ridge of Ash ran along the skirting from the bench under the door into the next room, left where it lay; a chipped cup stood on the shelf. On the abandoned board-bed the fold of grey wool held only the hollow where the small pale river stone had once lain, and nobody said it was gone. Above the bed the round pewter tin kept its pale fold at the seam and its thumb-bright rub on the lid, shut. A low song came up through the floor from a woman working below, flattened out of sense by work, and in the back room a neighbour scrubbed at a board with lye water and did not look up at the writing, the tin or the fold.

Alda Reed came short with soap-and-milk gone cold under river silt and lye sour under the soap, her nails scrubbed to the quick with clay still under them, and laid her birth-slates writing-down on the bench before she turned to the fold. The hand went to the record before it went to the child, as her hand always went, and the boy watched the order of it from the low angle at which a shelf becomes a roof. She pressed the wool flat at his shoulder a breath longer than a fold requires, crossed two fingers at the red edge of the birth-flower before she knew she had done it, and covered the sign with the date she had given Dessa: “Stone seven. The account went up on the eighth, before the paper went out.” The date stood over a woman more mornings dead than any living person keeps count of and uncollected, and there was no living person who could take it off.

“Warm and breathing,” she said. “That’s all I’ve set down.” It was exact and it was not true, and had not been true for three years.

Then she went on with the cooper’s seventh in Mud End, a potter’s third child in Stonemouth, the last high water, giving dates after the question had stopped, because dates are a handle on a thing too large to carry. A date laid over a sign is a cloth laid over a field before the instrument comes down and a hand lifted out of a field before the dressing goes on: the covering done with care, the field never opened, the account left standing in its neat margin. He knew the shape of it as he knew the shape of a clamp, and had no word in Grathic with which to hand it to anybody. He set it on its own place and called it neither kindness nor its opposite.

Her hand went once into her bodice to the little tin and came away without opening it. Her sixth narrow slate stayed face-down toward a floorboard in a house the boy could not name: six pages in a second hand no Court physician had ever seen, turned away from the light, read by nobody, with no seventh written. The mercy-lie curdled one day further against a death nobody could correct; the number stayed in a thumb gone into the water, and the name under the sieve-wire scar, and neither was spoken to him. Two private records in two materials stood apart in one room and were carried to no door.

Sella, within hearing of it all, asked nothing about the writing, the tin, the sign or the date; the half-pace and the turned slate stood in for the arrangement again, and neither woman looked toward the other. They were two careful hands entering the same child in two different accounts, and neither account had ever been shown to him, and the widow’s mark hung where it hung, because a midday is not an opening.

He was one hundred and thirty-one days old and nobody in the room had said so, or would have known what the number would count if somebody had. He set the morning’s objects down where they belonged and with nothing laid across them: the reed-cutter’s cord, Orra’s knot-string, Mira’s two cords with the thin child’s knot untouched, the date over the sign, the six turned pages, the little tin in the bodice. Each sat on its own place. No figure had been read, no knot counted, no tin opened, no seventh written, no name taken from a dead face, no joining made. Orra’s knot-string lay beside Mira’s board without touching it; the reed-cutter’s cord was gone back inside his coat with its knots unread; and in House Silt the pewter tin above the empty bed kept its thumb-bright rub shut.

The afternoon came back up the clay road with them the way a pot carried uphill comes back into the light: a little slopped, a little cooler, held level all the same. Sella Vail carried the boy at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, going wide around the space beneath the lowest rack where the broken birth-slates lay as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and gave the instruction to the wool and to the river wind and never to the basket: keep it off his ears, it had come down with them and would go back up with them, and a basket set down was a pot set down and left. The little-door burn on the back of her hand closed on the wicker’s edge and then on nothing. Low Kiln went around them in woodsmoke and wet wool and the faint sour tang of Ash worked into the corners; cold arrived in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, the wool riding back from the small bare joint so the cold found the bare place and went under the fold at his ear. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody under any roof in the district had been given an instruction about the thread, so it stayed where it had been put, stiff and doing nothing. He was one hundred and thirty-one days old, four months and eleven days, and nobody on the road said so.

At the Vail kiln-house she set the basket low and a little back near the bench leg, at the angle at which a man lying on the floor sees the underside of a board that a man standing sees only the top of. It was not a place she chose for him; it was a place the light chose, coming in low through the finger’s-width door and running along the grain under the loaf shelf. She told the fire it was the thaw. She gave the instruction to the pot and to the broom and never to the basket — feed it, turn it, go around what the water left, it cost enough to come in and would cost more to take out — and did not look down into the basket. He lay at the low angle with cold iron at his cheek where the bench bracket had been bolted and left, and his head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far. The child in him was cold at the ear and wanted the crust two inches off in the fold; the man in him read the angle of the light and the grain of the board before he read either. Both were true. The second was quieter and the first was louder, and neither could be handed to anybody.

Above him the shelf held its row the way a row is held in a house that keeps its days badly: loaves pushed along it, a glance at the clay jar on the wall, and a space at the row’s end nobody called a number. The row kept its two gaps. One was the old space at the end, the one that had come in with the planting and that a neighbour could name in one breath without ever seeing it as a thing that was missing; the other was a palm’s breadth of bare board that had come in on the night of Seed 6 with no scrape, no crumb, no print and no word, as if a loaf had lifted itself off in the dark and gone. Sella stood with her back to the basket and moved her lips along the row and gave nobody a figure. Her glance went once to the clay jar and came back; the glaze was cold in the warm room and she did not touch it. “The house has said it,” she said to the row, and the row went on standing with its two gaps.

Then she reached a finger beneath the board.

It was not a searching hand. It went under the front edge where the shelf’s underside dropped into shadow, the little-door burn closing on the wood’s lip and opening again, and stayed there long enough for the boy to see the knuckle set and the thumb come up against the grain; then it came away. She did not look at what her finger had gone to find. She did not look anywhere but at the row above the board, and her lips went on moving along the loaves as though the finger had never left the pot or the fire. No word went with the motion. The dry warm smell of the shelf went up past her and the sharper smell of charcoal went under, and for one breath the two smells crossed the way two hands cross in a dark room without touching.

From the low angle the boy saw the hand go under and come back and saw nothing of what the marks said. He had been set under this board that afternoon and the marks had waited there since Seed 6 or before, and he had not once been given a reason to turn them into a figure. He did not count the strokes. He did not hold the shapes in the dark where he could keep them, or press the pad of a thumb against the grain where her thumb had been, or lay what was under the board beside the two gaps above it, or beside the one stiff crust in the fold in the other room, or beside any of the other private records the district kept where no Court thumb could spell them. He did not make a cause of the hand’s habit. A hand that goes under a board without looking either knows the board by heart or has been told not to look; he had learned to leave the reason where it stood. The marks were a keeping of the same order as the other private records in the district: kept in a material no Court thumb could spell, visible, enterable by nobody. He set them back on the place where they had already been set, advanced and unpaid, held for the far-off day when a hand not his laid the district’s records together and an account at last wanted its name. The finger went under and came away, and the count went on being kept where nobody could spell it.

She had put the house into the sentence where another woman would have put herself, and gave no explanation of the count she kept. It was not a count she could have explained. A figure spoken aloud is a figure somebody can carry off, and a figure carried off wants a payer; she had learned to set a record where her own hand could reach it without her eyes going there. The boy did not follow her into that. He had no word in Grathic for the inside of a woman’s silence and would not have entered it if he had. The widow’s mark hung where it hung, a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and she did not touch it; she touched it before an opening and at no other hour, and a shelf in the middle of a tend was not an opening and an afternoon was not an opening. The clean dark line where the Ash ridge had turned and the new finer hairline beside it lay unswept in the corner and were called the thaw, and she did not look toward the corner. A shelf and a dusk are not openings. The row kept its two gaps and the mark kept its cord and the marks under the board kept their grain, and none of the three was laid across either of the others.

The light went off the underside of the shelf and the marks went dark in the corner where the grain turned, and Sella lifted the basket without ever looking into it. Dusk came down the clay road ahead of them the way a pot’s shadow crosses a table before the pot is set down, so that they walked into the house’s evening rather than bringing it in with them. She carried him at the half-pace from every open slate, giving the instruction to the wool and the river wind and never to the basket, going around the space beneath the lowest rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and she did not go back to the loaf shelf that night and did not take her finger under any board again. The house kept its day the way a cup keeps the shape of what was poured into it and then emptied out.

At the hearth she set the pot down where the light fell on its rim and turned the handle toward the side of the bench where a reaching hand would find it. The pot gave its low note as it settled, full beneath the small flat sound that lived in it from the wheel. Six hearings of the full note stood where they had stood — five from four sources and the sixth from a person — beside and never among, and he did not make a seventh of the pot under this roof. No seventh came with the dusk. The bell had been a told bell and a heard bell in two places; the wheel’s note came through the wall from the shed and went where it sounded; the marks under the shelf stayed where they were put. Three likenesses in a row will stand up and call themselves a cause if there is nobody to hold them apart, and he had spent a hundred and thirty-one days being the person who held them apart, an invisible labour that bought nothing anyone could have entered.

She told the fire it was the thaw, turned the coals with the back of a broken pot, and said “The house will keep it” where another woman would have said I. She stood a moment with the back of the broken pot in her hand, counting something under her breath toward no opening, as she had counted at a shelf with her lips and at a rack with her eyes, and pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn, the grey streak pale off the coals. She set the pot where it could be found in the dark, rim out and handle toward the reaching hand, and did not look toward the bed.

He was carried up under the roof and set on his bed and not in the Debt Loft. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without once looking at his face, and left the thread at his mouth frozen and unwiped, because nobody under any roof in the district had been given an instruction about the thread and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. Her hand rested a half-beat longer than the setting needed before it went away, the way a hand rests on a thing it has carried a long way and cannot bring itself to let go of; then it went, and the house straightened around its going as cloth straightens when a weight is lifted. One stiff brown-edged crust lay in the fold with its empty place beside it, and no third came into either place in the going of the light.

The door stood a finger’s width open. Through the gap came the river’s note of water over water, beyond the reed beds and the Weir, a fifth low thing he had kept on a place of its own since he was six days old, never a hearing and never counted among the six. Through the floor under blind ash the carried fire took its coal and gave back four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for a hundred and thirty-one days. Overhead the boards kept four nails, four, and a fifth that was a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts in three places, and none of them became one: the note through the door that was never a hearing, the fire’s four with the fifth missing under the ash, the nails and the knot in the boards above his head. Three counts in three places will stand up and call themselves a single account if a man leaves them alone together for a night; he had spent a hundred and thirty-one nights keeping them apart.

Cold arrived in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist. The wool did less than the wind wanted, riding back from the small bare joint and going under the fold where nobody had been told to put a hand. The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down. It could not reach the crust, the bucket, the pot with its handle turned out, the door’s finger’s width of light, or any one of the three counts it had spent the day keeping separate. That was the whole of it: a man with a surgeon’s eye and a child’s reach, lying under four nails and a knot while the fire missed its fifth breath and the river went on being water over water beyond a town that had never once asked him what he heard.

He turned out his file as a man lays instruments on a towel, each thing in its place and nothing laid across anything else. It was a habit of the first life that had survived into this one without his ever choosing it: field, exposure, instrument, complication, cost. Here there was no field and no cost he could spend, only the objects. Six hearings of the full note stood beside and never among, no seventh; the told bell stood beside the heard bell; the two tellings of the shut counting room stood side by side, each with its date, and no third telling came. Three near-full answers stood on their own tray, advanced and not paid: a cup across a birth room, a pot under the wheel-shed wall, a rack pot lifted a hand’s breadth too high and set down again. The intervals from Stone 13, Stone 17 and Stone 25 lay with Tarn’s half-beat behind the standing finger, the stroke that had gone on over the flagstones without the check, the breath drawn at the market and not spent, and now the three stoppings of the near-saying — the sentence about an ear under this roof being worth more than the debt that holds it, brought three times to the word that would call the loan and stopped three times with no figure and no name. Three intervals were three objects on a tray and not a rule. He had refused to let two stand up and call themselves a doctrine; he refused it now with three. A rule is a thing a man can hand to a clerk. These he could not have handed to anyone, and that was why he went on laying them out on a towel in the dark where nobody could see him do it.

The loan stayed uncalled. Tarn’s seventh year had not given, the small knife was back on its sill where it could be checked with six counted in a worn thumb-mark and the seventh held at the very edge, and the unforged collector sentence lay beside it with no figure in it. He did not join that to the mark under the shelf, though both were counts kept where a Court thumb could not read them and both had a payer nobody had named. Neither man had asked the other anything, and the two silences had stayed unjoined under one roof. Jorra Vail’s standing finger in the cold iron of the damper, the dust ground small as meal past the bent little finger, the men who came before first grey and were given no name: these lay on their own places with no third telling added. The coat on its peg kept its broader shape and the seat its twenty-year lean; the uncalled debt against the tiles went around the space where a name might have stood, neither paid nor settled nor done with.

The word forty stayed unspoken with the shape of the forty-first double turn still legible on Orra Weir’s cord and no name laid on it. The cold foot below the sill was still the cold to one woman and weather to the yard, and the surgeon’s reading of the extremity going before the core worked and bought nothing; no silt-leach, cause, cure or recognition had been written across it. The four greens kept their three places with no fourth and no cause; Berr Clay’s office cord stayed folded under his coat and his twice-wiped thumb stayed on its tray beside the finger that had passed over a figure, the true subtraction gone up the clay road in a locked box. Dessa Silt’s pewter tin above the abandoned bed kept its pale fold and thumb-bright lid shut, and the number stayed in a thumb gone into the water and the name under the sieve-wire scar, neither spoken to him. Mira Silt’s second cord stayed beneath her apron, never carried upriver to Nine Pots or lifted at a door; Alda Reed’s six pages stayed turned face-down toward a floorboard, read by nobody; the soft-cuffed clerk stayed set at the landing for a measure nobody had called. Each sat where it sat. He could see every one and enter none of them.

One crust in the fold and two shelf gaps in a room that does not touch. The marks under the shelf sat on their own place, joined to nothing, and the finger that had gone beneath the board had come away without a word. Nothing was joined, paid, named, opened, called or ended. He was one hundred and thirty-one days old, four months and eleven days, and nobody under the roof had said so or would have known what the number would have counted if somebody had. The river’s note came through the finger’s-width door; through the floor under blind ash the fire missed its fifth; overhead the boards kept four nails, four, and a knot the width of a thumb. He lay with the fold at his ear and his hand outside the wool, and the hand went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down with the day exact and unspent and spoken by nobody. In the next room the row of loaves kept its two gaps, and under the shelf the marks waited where they had been put, for a hand not his and a day that had not come.
