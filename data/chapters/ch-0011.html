11

The Coal the Gull Took

Days 45–47 · Ilmen 16–18, Y.H. 813 · The Fishstairs and Low Kiln, Sill

The gull takes a coal and the fire says nothing. The fold loses a crust and the house says nothing. The well writes frost where a slate should stand, and by noon calls it weather.

— A saying of Low Kiln, Sill

— Day 45 —

Ilmen 16, Y.H. 813 (Sixthday)

He came down the river road in the first thin light, carried the way a pot is carried when it is full and must not be slopped, with Sella Vail’s knee turning him until the fold lay right under his chin and the wool took the wind on its own broad back instead of on his ear. Under the north bridge the shadow was a room without walls, floored in wet stone and smelling of the cold that came up off the Ilm; she set him where a pot would be set, out of the draught that ran along the boards, and did not set him where the trade would slop him. Below them the Fishstairs were awake already, the boards slick with slime and salt, the eel-baskets breathing their cold fish smell into the shade. Corin, forty-five days old, could not have kept the fold right for himself and could not have said where he was. He lay at the low angle at which a basket sees the world, with the bridge’s underside going out above him on its dark beams, and the cold came in its known order, cheek, then temple, then the small wrist outside the wool, and the wool did less than the wind wanted. It was the sixteenth of Ilmen. The blessing was yesterday, and the fish were being sold this morning, and work had come back into the place that had put on its one clean day and taken it off again before noon.

A brine-gull went off upriver with a coal in its beak that was not yet cold. It had taken the coal from somebody’s banked fire, from a heap where a house had raked its coals and left them under ash, and it carried the thing low and quick across the water, a dark bird with a red heart in its mouth, and the warmth went up the river with it. The boy watched it go because watching was what was left to him. He knew a taking when he saw one and he knew a payer when he saw one, and here was a warmth leaving the carried fire’s account with no slate entered and no hand put out to stop it. Somebody had paid for that coal at Kindling and somebody had banked it and somebody had gone home warm the night before, and now a bird had it and the bird would drop it on a bank or into the water and the Cold would take it, and no account in Slate Row would show one mark less for the loss. He wrote nothing over it. He had learned early in the other life that a doctrine laid on top of a fact is a way of not looking at the fact, and here there was no fact but a bird and a coal and a warmth going off upriver, which was plain enough without a name. The gull went round the bend and was gone. The morning went on.

At the head of the steps stood the clerk who had been set at the Fishstairs for a measure nobody had called. He had a soft cuff gone grey from washing and a slate held against his forearm, and when a woman came down with a basket of eels he put his hand to the slate before he put his eyes to the stalls. The order of it was the whole of the thing: the hand first, the person after, the writing before the face. The boy had seen that order in a midwife bending over a birth, in a neighbour at a well, in a man who priced him across a market, in Berr Clay passing a finger over a figure, in the soft-cuffed man at the Court’s table at the Cross, and in Alda Reed going to her writing before she went to the child. He filed this clerk on a tray of his own. He was not the woman in the grey coat who had written at the edge of the flare light and paid with her fingers; he was not the collector with the grey fingertips at the Drawing whose hand had no pause in it; he was not even the soft-cuffed man at the Court’s table, though he had the same cuff and the same way of holding a slate. He was an ordinary hand set down at the steps for an uncalled measure, and the boy kept him apart from the other hands the way he kept the told bell apart from the heard bell, because a likeness was a likeness and nothing more had been given. No sixth sighting of the neat dark hand came that morning, and the five pauses lay where they had been laid and did not join.

Orra Weir came down from the Weir with eels, and the boy knew she had come before he saw her, the way everybody at the Cross knew it: silt and cold fish and tallow smoke moving together, and under that the wet wool smell of a woman who had stood too long on a weir. The grey streak showed first through her black hair. The pale arcs of eel scars lay across both forearms and she had covered neither, and her hands were like old rope that had been wet and dried and wet again for thirty years. She set her basket down where a basket would not be slopped and worked among the eels with the turn of a wrist that had done it ten thousand times, and the knot-string at her waist, grey wool gone the colour of river silt where her thumb had worried it, moved with her breathing.

Yesterday, tied low into the cord, there had been a knot she could not account for. The boy could not read Grathic in speech or on a slate, and he had no word of his own for the thing she had put into the wool, but this morning, lying in the shade with nothing between him and the cord but the length of a basket, he could read the shape of it. That had changed since yesterday. A double turn with the tail tucked under, tied left over right, the small extra weight of a thumb setting it down where it had not been before: he had held the shape in his head through the night and it had come out in the morning light still whole, like an instrument he had once been able to name and now could only describe to himself. It was the double turn that told him the hand had been a right-handed woman looking at something else while she worked, and it was the thumb that told him the knot had been given a place it did not have before. He had neither the name of the knot nor any word to spend it on, and he kept the shape on its own tray with the saying beside it, not on top of it, because a word and the thing it names are two objects and he had learned to keep such objects apart. forty was still a saying and not a vessel’s sound; it lay where it had lain since yesterday, low and watched and unanswered, and he did not say it again and nobody said it near him.

Orra worked the eels and watched him watching her. That was the whole of it. She did not say the word a second time and she did not ask what he had made of it, and when he turned his head a degree too far and the small wet sound came out of him, she let her eyes rest on the cord at her waist and then go back to her hands. Once she tied a knot and pulled it down with her thumb and set it among the others, and the boy noted the weight of the thumb and did not count the knots, because counting was an old habit and not a gift, and there was nothing in the count he could have entered anywhere if he had counted. He knew a vigil when he saw one. He had kept a bedside watch through worse nights than this in a country whose words he had not brought with him, and he recognised the particular attention of a person who has set a thing down in front of somebody and is waiting to see whether it will be picked up. He could not pick it up. He had no hand that would reach a league upriver to the hut where the cord hung, and no word to say across the space between a baby’s basket and a woman’s rope-hand, and the watching went on all the same, patient and half-sung, the way the Fen people did everything, with the story told sideways and the answer put where a date should stand. The cord went back into the hut at the end of the morning where no clerk could spell it and no boy could read it, and nothing was said of it between them.

Mira Silt worked the eels at the far end of the racks with pale slime dried on her sleeves and silt in the hollow of her neck, her head held a little to the right where the top of her left ear had been. A weir woman asked her whether the run would come with the blessing gone and done, and Mira answered the question and then her own answer. “Does it come now the stone’s blessed? It comes when it comes. Ask the river.” She flicked an eel-bone against her thumb and did not yet put it away, because the deciding was not done. When a buyer said a figure for the morning’s catch her thumb went under her apron to the grey cord at her waist and stayed there, and shifting the weight of her basket showed the second cord twisted twice beneath it, the one that had never been carried upriver to Nine Pots, never lifted at a door, with the knots on neither of them counted. She put the eel-bone away when the deciding was done and brought out another, and her small smile stayed on her face the way another woman keeps a door on the latch, able to be shut without anybody noticing the movement. The boy set her two grey cords on a board and Orra’s knot-string on a board beside it, and would not let the boards touch. Two women kept the district’s private account in a material no officer would think to read, and neither had said what she knew, and the boy had no standing to ask either of them. He watched the hand go to the first cord and never to the second, and kept the watching as carefully as the other keepings, writing no cause over it.

The ear held the full note in six hearings and the six stayed six. Five came from four sources: a cup across the birth room, a pot in the Vail wheel-shed heard through the wall, the district’s slate twice at the Cross, and a man giving at the Drawing. The sixth had come six mornings back out of a woman lifted wet and heavy from the clay-heavy brook, a small flat knock out of the body as the yard’s hands went under her, and it lay beside the five without being among them, from a person, not a seventh and never to be a seventh. No new hearing came this morning under the bridge; the eels slapped the boards and the gulls went over and the clerk’s slate lay open at the steps and gave nothing. The nearly full answer of Ilmen 6 lay on its own tray, advanced and not paid, a third sound that was not a hearing and not the thing it answered, and the boy did not let it go near the six. The told bell was still beside the heard bell and never on it; the five pauses did not join; the absence with the outline of a collection, the shape of a thumb on wicker, the word and the knot and the weight of the thumb that set it all stayed each in its own place. He had no Grathic word for the flat note and no Grathic word for nearly, and he had stopped a long time ago expecting the district to give him one. Keeping the trays apart was the whole of his work in the world, and it had never once changed a thing.

In the fold, both crusts still lay where they had lain through a death and a blessing and seven days of thaw that everyone called weather. The spent one, brown-edged and stiff since Stone 15, was gone past smelling of anything. The smoky one that Mira had put there on Stone 28 still gave a thin breath of barley smoke when the wind turned off the water, and the breath came and went with the turning of the wind, and the body wanted it with the whole appetite of a day carried from before light and fed on nothing. Neither had been eaten, neither had been lifted, neither had been named, and no third had come into the fold. A kindness that cannot be answered cannot be refused, which he had learned in bread, and the learning had cost him nothing and given him nothing he could spend. He could smell the barley smoke and he could not put out a hand to it. There was the warmth, and there was the hand, and the two paces between them were as fixed as the space between the basket and the cord upriver, and he had long ago stopped being surprised by the distance.

The body humiliated him exactly, as it had done every morning of his forty-five days. The thread of drool at his mouth froze where the draught found it and nobody wiped it away, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound it made when it went too far, and Sella did not put her hand to it because she was talking to the eel-woman about the weather. Cold came at cheek and then temple in the order it always took, and he could not alter one step of it. The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth that was not within two paces of him and came down. It had gone up an inch every morning since the first morning and it had never once reached anything. He was a surgeon who could not lift a hand to his own chin and the only adult in every room he was carried into, and the fact was not funny and not tragic, only exact; the joke of it, if there was one, cost somebody else and not him, and he had never been able to find who.

Sella turned the basket so the sun was off his face, and did it with her knee while she was looking at the eel-stalls, so that the moving of him was not a thing that had been done to him but a thing the light had done. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. “It’s the river wind.” She said the house where another woman would have said I, and gave the instruction to the wool and never to the basket, and stood a half-pace off a slate lying open on a stall and did not set him down where the writing might lie across him. Her little-door burn closed on the basket’s wicker and opened again, and she went on bargaining for eels as if she had not moved him at all, which was the care she used and the care she would never name. The boy watched her standing there with her hands never still, counting something on her fingers that she would not have called a count, and he kept her number beside him the way he kept all the household’s numbers, badly kept and exactly held, with no figure spoken between them.

The gull was gone upriver with the coal and the warmth out of the carried fire’s account, and the boy lay in the bridge’s shade with the fold at his chin. On its own tray the shape of the double turn lay beside a word that had not been said again, and the weight of a thumb lay apart from both, and the two cords of the two women stood on separate boards and did not touch. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth that was not within two paces of him and came down. The eels went on changing hands at the stalls. The clerk’s cuff went grey in the morning light and his hand kept going to the slate first. Nothing had been joined, paid or explained. The count went on.

❦

The afternoon came up off the river and went into the yards ahead of them, so that by the time Sella Vail carried Corin in from the Fishstairs the light had gone thin and the cold had taken on the edge it gets when the sun has lain on water all day and is done with it. She carried him up the Clay Yard the way she had carried him down, like a pot filled that must not be slopped, her knee turning him at the turn of the path and the fold of grey wool gathered in under his chin so that the wind met the wool and not the ear. She did not look into the basket. A pot carried is not looked into; a pot set down is a pot set down, and the thing to be looked at first when it is set down is the fire. Under the roof she set him on his bed, and not in the Debt Loft, and put the fold right at his ear with a hand that never once went near his face. The burn shaped like a little door closed on the wool’s edge and opened again on nothing. “Keep it off him,” she said to the wool. “It’s the river wind.” She gave the instruction to the fire and the pot and the wool and never to the basket, and she said the house where another woman would have said I, and the boy, who understood every word, understood that he was not the one being spoken to.

He lay where she had put him and watched the room come back to itself around him the way a room comes back after an operation, by degrees and in the order of its instruments. The bed was under the roof and the loft was above the shed, and the boy had the bed and Tarn had the loft, and that was the household’s arithmetic before any of its figures were gone into. Nothing about it had ever been said in his hearing. He had once stood in a supply room at four in the morning and read what was left on a shelf by the shape of the gaps, and he read this room now with the same part of him: the count first, the meaning afterward, if it ever came.

Sella went around the month’s days the way she went around every number she would not speak. She moved a loaf along the shelf, moved it back, set another with its brown edge turned up, and glanced at the clay jar on the wall shelf that she had brought three doors down from House Silt and had never once explained. Her lips went along the shelf without making a figure. She came back to the same place twice, the way she always came back, and set the loaf where it had stood before, so that the days that remained to Kindling stood as a shape no collector could take up and carry away. Nobody said the stores were thinner than they had been. The house kept its number in loaves and glances and the space where a loaf should have stood, and the boy kept the count exactly beside her bad keeping, four and four and the shape of the days, without ever once letting the two counts touch or speaking a figure between them. He had learned long ago that a number kept badly was still a number kept, and that a number kept exactly and never entered was worth no more than one counted on the fingers of a woman who would not say what she was counting.

In the fold both crusts still lay where they had lain through a death and a blessing and seven days of thaw that everybody called weather. The spent one, brown-edged and stiff since Stone 15, was gone past smelling of anything. The smoky one that Mira Silt had put there on Stone 28 gave its thin breath of barley smoke when the air moved off the yard, and the breath came and went, and the body wanted it with the whole appetite of a day carried from before light and fed on nothing since. Neither had been eaten. Neither had been lifted. Neither had been named. No third had come into the fold. He could smell the barley and he could not put out a hand to it, and the two paces between the warmth and the hand were as fixed as they had been that morning under the bridge, and as they would be tomorrow. A kindness that cannot be answered cannot be refused. He had learned that in bread, and the learning had given him nothing he could spend.

Before she opened the kiln she put her hand to the widow’s mark at her throat, the thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and held it for one breath, and let go. It was a rule that held as exactly as the rule about the loaves. She touched it before an opening and at no other hour, not for a pot, not for a date, not for a nearly full sound, and at the dusk bank she would not touch it at all, because a bank is not an opening. The boy watched the hand go up and the hand go down and kept the two gestures in two places, as he kept all the house’s gestures, because a rule and the reason for a rule were two objects and he had learned to keep such objects apart.

Behind him the carried fire lay under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, giving its warmth to the boards by degrees. It took its breath in fours and missed one. Four, four and a gap, and the gap was the part nobody had thought to enter. The coal that had gone up the river that morning with the brine-gull was entered nowhere and missed in no account. The fire did not miss it. The house did not miss it. One warmth less had gone out of the world since dawn, and no slate in Slate Row stood one mark the poorer for it, and the boy lying on his bed under the roof was the only thing in the district that kept the subtraction.

❦

Tarn had the small bench knife back on its sill before the light was fully gone and told the empty yard where it was, unasked, in the tone another man would use to state the weather. “It’s on the sill,” he said. “You can check.” He looked at the kindling stack without touching it, four bundles along the near row and four behind and a space at the end where a fifth should have been, and took his eyes off the space as a man takes his eyes off a thing he has no leave to look at. Six years of the seven were behind him and he had counted every one. The seventh he held back, the one entry in this house that the house could not check. Grey Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles the colour of the ridge that had turned the corner and been burned and called dealt with, and no scrubbing lifted it; the pale clip-ring at his left wrist caught what light there was and held it for a moment and gave it back. He called everyone house, including Sella, and said I owe where another man would say I am, and he agreed with everything so thoroughly that an argument had nowhere left to put its foot.

His broom went over the road that runs down toward the Fishstairs, over and over, without once turning to look along it. That is the road a man goes down when a house wants a collector. No collector went down it that afternoon, and nobody in the house sent one, and Tarn did not go. He swept where he had swept, and swept again, and when Sella told him he had done well he stopped altogether for two breaths before he said “Yes, house” and went on, because a kindness he could not enter in any account was a kindness he could not pay back, and he had stopped trying to reconcile the two. He wanted the thaw late. He agreed with the people who wanted it early and with the people who wanted it late and kept wanting the late one all the same, because a late thaw puts off the paying. A loan that can hear is a loan that can be called. The sentence had never been said under this roof, and lay under it all the same, and the boy on his bed kept Tarn’s silence beside his own and did not let the two touch, because two silences under one roof are nearer an equality than anything the house had a word for. A loan that can hear is a loan that can be called, and there was a man under this roof worth more than the debt that held him, and nobody had found the figure to enter it with.

Jorra Vail came in from the Vail wheel-shed with slip dried to the colour of old bone up both forearms and no grey worked into the creases past the bend of his little finger. The bent finger went in ahead of his hand at the damper. Sella had begun to tell him the fire was low; he moved the damper before she had finished and left the end of her sentence where it was, and she shut her mouth on it and gave the rest to the pot. He spoke to the pot and not to the person on the bed. “You’ll do,” he said, in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either. He told the wheel what he had told it a hundred times, that it did not care whose hands were on it, only if they were steady, and the saying had worn smooth as the seat worn hollow on the left by twenty years of another man’s leaning. Of the room off the shed he said nothing, and the door of it kept its finger’s width of open, and he did not look toward the bed. The boy knew something about Jorra that Jorra would not have said before a kiln-master: that the full note was in a Vail pot before it left the wheel, and that to say so in front of a man who would call it early was to have something made of it. He kept the knowing where questions can last for years, as he kept the bent finger and the clean forearms and the absence of dust on them, writing no cause over any of it.

Behind the door Garl Vail’s coat kept the shape of a broader man on its peg, salt-stiff and never taken down, and the wheel-seat in the shed kept the leftward lean of twenty years that nobody was allowed to fill. Where the grey ridge had turned its corner for a year and two days there was now a clean dark line, and it was not swept, because a line left where it is is still a line kept. His proverbs went to the fire and the pot and never to the basket: feed the fire before you feed yourself, and leave the crack showing; people trust a thing they can see has been handled. The second had gone on being said to the pot after the crack was gone and the ridge was a clean dark line, and nobody in the house had found anything strange in that. The charred edge of a slate lay in the kiln ash, black on one side and eaten into the shape of a strip of bark, swept around for a year and never picked up. The house kept a roll of its own in a careful hand and did not show it, and kept a dead man’s place in the shape of a coat and a hollow and a line, and kept a foundling under its roof without a name written against him, and entered none of the three where a man from Slate Row could read it.

The light went off the yard and the evening came on in the order it always came, at the edges of the room first and the middle last, and the fire behind him took its breath in fours. The boy lay on his bed under the Vail roof, forty-five days old, with both crusts still in the fold and no third; the smoky one gave its thin breath and the spent one lay stiff past smelling, and neither was lifted or eaten or named. Behind him the carried fire kept under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees and missing one breath in every four, four and four and a gap. The coal that had gone up with the gull that morning was entered in no account and missed in none. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth that was not within two paces of him and came down. Nothing had been joined, paid or explained. The count went on.

— Day 46 —

Ilmen 17, Y.H. 813 (Seventhday)

The morning came on in the order it always came, cold at the edges of the Vail kiln-house and warmer in the middle where the fire kept its own small day, and Sella Vail had woken that fire before she woke anybody else. She set a dish on the bench to take its warmth and fed Corin from the house’s own dish with a spoon she wiped on her sleeve, barley gruel thinned with milk that came from a house that was not his and would not be named. Two fingers behind his ear held his head where it wanted to go; when it went too far the small wet sound came out of him and the fingers brought it back, and she never once looked at his face. She gave the instruction to the fire and the wool, as she gave every instruction, and never to the basket. “Keep it off him,” she said to the wool. “It’s the river wind.” She said the house where another woman would have said I, and the boy, who understood every word, understood that he was not the one being spoken to, and that the warmth going into him was written in no account that any officer would ever open.

He knew the arithmetic of a body taking food before he knew the words for it. The gruel went in warm and the milk left a film on his lower lip and the film cooled while the spoon was still in the dish. He wanted more of it and could not ask for more, and the wanting was a small animal thing with no grammar in it. The surgeon in him noted the heat of the spoon against his lip and the rate at which the film went cold, and the infant in him wanted the breast and wanted the crust and had neither, and the two of them went on arguing in the same small body as they had argued every morning of his forty-six days. He was forty-six days old, could not hold up his own head, and had once reduced a shoulder in the dark. Both things were true. The second was the one that was louder.

Before she lifted the kiln lid she put her hand to the widow’s mark at her throat, the thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and held it for one breath and let go. It was a rule that held as exactly as any rule in the house, and the boy kept the gesture on its own tray, as he kept all the house’s gestures, because a rule and the reason for a rule were two objects and he had long ago learned to keep such objects apart. She did not touch it again. She would not touch it at the dusk bank, because a bank is not an opening. The carried fire lay behind him under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees and taking its breath in fours, and a coal had gone up the river with a bird yesterday and been entered nowhere and missed in no account. One warmth less was abroad in the district than had been abroad on the day before, and the boy on his bed kept the subtraction, which was the whole of what he could do with it.

Alda Reed came under the roof at mid-morning with the smell of soap and warm milk on her and the birth-slates under her arm. She had come from a birth or from the washing of one; the lye sat under the soap and the milk had gone cold under river silt, and she carried the two smells the way she carried the slates, without comment and without setting either down where a body could mistake it for comfort. She laid the birth-slates writing-down on the bench before she turned to the fold. That was the order she reached in, the record before the person, and the boy lying on his bed watched the order of the reaching and kept it on the tray where he kept the other hands that went to the slate first — the midwife, the neighbour at the well, the clerk behind the Court table, the man who had priced him, and a woman writing in a neat dark hand at the edge of a flare light, none of them joined to any of the others.

She pressed the fold flat at his shoulder longer than a fold required. Her hand was steady, scrubbed to the quick, with clay still under the nails, and it stayed a breath too long at the place where the wool met the edge of his ear, as if she were smoothing a thing that would not stay smooth. Under the shawl her other hand went once inside her bodice and touched the little tin and came away without opening it. She did not open it. She had never opened it in company and she did not open it now. Where the wool had ridden back at his wrist the red edge of the birth-flower showed, and she crossed two fingers at the corner of it before she knew she had done it, and covered the sign with a date nobody had asked for. “Stone seven,” she said. “The account went up on the eighth, before the paper went out.”

She went on giving dates after there was no question left to stop her. The cooper’s seventh in Mud End. A potter’s third child in Stonemouth, now a carter. The last high water, which was the date she gave where other women gave an opinion. To Sella’s “he lies quiet” she said “Warm and breathing — that’s all I’ve set down,” which was exact and was not true, and she said it in the tone of a woman stating the weather. She did not say the woman’s name. She did not say the third day. The lie she had told for mercy had curdled all the way through to a death nobody had entered, and no living person could now correct it, and she went on laying dates over it the way she had laid them over a cradle, going on after the question had stopped because dates were a handle on a thing too large to carry. She wrote no new note this morning. She turned to go with the birth-slates still writing-down under her arm, and the boy, who had watched the order of the reaching, could not have handed any of it back.

Jorra Vail came in from the Vail wheel-shed with slip dried to the colour of old bone up both forearms and no grey worked into the creases past the bend of his little finger. The bent finger went in ahead of his hand at the damper. Sella had begun to tell him the fire was low; he moved the damper before she had finished and left the end of her sentence where it was, and she shut her mouth on it and gave the rest to the pot. He spoke to the pot and not to the person on the bed. “You’ll do,” he said, in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either. He told the wheel what he had told it a hundred times, that it did not care whose hands were on it, only if they were steady, and the saying had worn smooth as the seat worn hollow on the left by twenty years of another man’s leaning. Of the room off the shed he said nothing, and the door of it kept its finger’s width of open. He did not say the back room was cold. He did not look toward the bed or the basket. Garl Vail’s salt-stiff coat kept the shape of a broader man on its peg behind the door and the hollow seat kept the leftward lean of twenty years that nobody was allowed to fill, and the clean dark line where the ridge had turned its corner held nothing and was not swept, because a line left where it is is still a line kept. His proverbs went to the fire and the pot and never to the basket. Of the boy under the roof the house said nothing, and the sentence Jorra had spoken on the day the boy arrived was sealed as tightly as it had been on the hour it was spoken, and nobody had undone it.

❦

The wind turned off the water in the early afternoon and the smell did not come.

It had come for weeks when the wind turned that way. The spent crust from Stone 15 lay stiff where it had lain and was gone past smelling of anything, but the other one, the smoky one that Mira Silt had put into the fold on Stone 28, had given a thin breath of barley smoke whenever the air moved off the Clay Yard, and the breath had come and gone and the boy had known the shape of it as a man knows the shape of a wound he has dressed every day. He had wanted that crust with the whole appetite of a morning carried from before light and fed on a dish that was not his. He had smelled it and could not reach it, and the two paces between the warmth and his hand had been as fixed as the day he was born.

Now the wind turned and there was no breath of barley. He lay still and let the absence come to him the way he let a symptom come, without flinching from it and without dressing it in a cause. One crust lay in the fold where two had lain. The smoky one was gone. It had not been eaten beside him and it had not been lifted in his sight and nobody had said it was gone. Nobody had said it had been given to the fire or to the eels or to a hungry woman, and nobody had said who had taken it. The spent one remained stiff where it lay, uneaten and unlifted and unnamed, and no third had come into the fold. Two had become one, and the one that was left was the one that had been there longest and was worth the least.

The surgeon in him registered the empty place with the exactness he had once brought to a wound, and he could do nothing with the knowledge. He had kept the crust on its tray as a kindness that could not be answered, and a kindness that cannot be answered cannot be refused. Now the kindness had gone and the absence could not be answered either, and the two things were the same shape and neither of them had a hand he could put out to either. He kept the empty place beside the coal that had gone up with the bird and beside the fifth breath the fire did not take and beside the sound that had not come when the yard lifted a woman out of the brook, and he wrote no cause across any of them. There was no word in Grathic for a thing taken and not spoken of, and no word of his own for the taking. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the place where the warmth had been and came down.

He did not think who had taken it. That was not the same as not knowing there were hands that might have. Sella’s hand put the fold right at his ear every morning and did not look at his face. Alda’s hand had pressed the fold flat at his shoulder too long this morning and had gone away with the tin still shut. Mira’s hand had put the crust there on Stone 28 and had done necessary things before anybody could make a speech about them. He set the three hands on three places on a tray and did not let the tray become a cause, because he had learned in a better-lit room what three likenesses laid in a row will do to a man who wants an answer. Whoever had taken it had taken it quietly, which was a kind of keeping, and the yard kept its keepings where no slate would think to look, and the boy kept the absence where he kept all the things that had happened to him without happening to anybody else. Nobody said the crust was missing. The house went on.

At the dusk bank Sella turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and did not touch the widow’s mark at her throat, because a bank is not an opening. She gave the instruction to the fire and let the pot have the end of it, and she said the house where another woman would have said I, and she did not count the warmth going off up the river yesterday or the warmth gone out of the fold today, and she did not say either one was gone. The carried fire kept under blind ash and the back of the broken pot, warming the boards by degrees and missing one breath in every four. Four, four and a gap. The gap was the part nobody had thought to enter. The clean dark line where the Ash ridge had turned its corner held nothing and was not swept, and Garl’s coat held a broader man’s shape on its peg and his hollow seat kept its twenty-year lean, and the two proverbs went to the pot and not to the basket.

The light went off the yard and the evening came on in the order it always came, at the edges of the room first and the middle last. The boy lay on his bed under the Vail roof, forty-six days old, with one crust in the fold where two had lain and the empty place at his ear where the warmth he had most wanted had been. He had no hand to lift to it and no word in the district’s tongue for the taking and no standing to ask who had done it. The spent crust lay stiff where it had always lain and the smoky one lay nowhere and neither was named. Behind him the carried fire took its breath in fours and missed one, and the coal that had gone up with the gull was entered in no account and missed in none. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the emptiness and came down. Nothing had been joined, paid or explained. The count went on.

— Day 47 —

Ilmen 18, Y.H. 813 (Eighthday)

The frost came on the stone lip of the yard well in the hour before the smoke cleared of the morning fires, and it was not the ice that lay on the trough or the rime that grew on the north side of the wall. It stood up in feathers. It grew off the stone in small white fronds, each one finer than the one beside it, the way a mould stands up off a loaf left too long in a damp corner — only white, and clean, and drinking the first of the light before the sun got over the roofs. Corin, forty-seven days old, came up from the Clay Yard in the basket under the fold and was set in the well’s shade where a pot would be set, out of the draught that ran off the water. The frost was at the level of a man’s knee above him. He could see the fronds from the low angle at which a basket sees the world, and he could no more touch them than he could touch the sun coming up behind the wash-tubs. He had known frost since the first week of his new life. He had never seen it write on a well-lip in Ilmen before.

Where the Warm had lately gone out of the stone, the cold wrote its own small hand. That was the whole of the thing. He set the writing on a tray by itself, as he set all the things nobody had entered, and put no cause across it. He knew the difference between a frost that came because the night had been cold and a frost that came because something warm had gone out of a thing and left the shape of its going. Water came up under the sill from somewhere below the town and carried a little heat with it even in the deep of Stone; a hand could feel it through the side of a drawn bucket in the small hours if the hand got there before the bucket stood and cooled. That heat had gone out of the lip in the night and the frost had come in where it had been, tracing the stone in white the way an officer traces a margin on a slate, fine and patient and without a word. By noon the sun would take it off and leave the stone bare, and no slate in Slate Row would show a mark less for the going, and nobody in the yard would call the writing anything but weather.

A woman with her sleeves rolled came and let the bucket down, and the rope went over the lip and knocked the heads off three fronds and sent them into the water. “It’ll go soft before its time,” she said, and looked at the white as if the stone had grown a tooth she had not been told about. A woman at the tubs said no, it would hold; the nights were cold enough yet; there was frost on the turnip grounds and frost would keep. The first woman said the water had been tasting of the Warm all week, had nobody noticed, and the second said a body could taste anything in a thaw and it was only the weather come early, and at that the subject was finished. They went back to their buckets. It had not been an argument anyone meant to win, because there was nothing in it anyone meant to own; it was a way of saying the morning out loud and setting it down again where it had been found. The boy kept the frost on its own tray and did not lay the words on top of it, because a thing said and a thing seen were two objects and he had learned, in a room with better light than this, what happens to a man who lays three likenesses in a row and asks them to stand for a cause.

Cold came at him in its known order — cheek, then temple, then the small wrist outside the wool — and the wool did less than the wind wanted. A thread of drool at the corner of his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had wiped it, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went past the point, and the women at the tubs did not look up. He was the only adult in the yard and the least use in it. The woman who had said the ice would go soft worked the bucket up with her arm and set it on the frosty lip for a breath before she carried it away, and the heat that had been in the water a moment ago went off with the bucket and into a house, and was entered nowhere.

Before the smoke was off the yard a figure came up from the road below in a man’s fist. It went the way such figures go in Low Kiln: out of the fist into the mouth of a man at the racks, out of that mouth onto the shoulder of a woman shifting slip, off the shoulder into the next fist, and there it stopped, because no one in the chain had been handed the figure to keep. At the end of it somebody asked a question and the shoulder lifted and the man with the fist said nothing at all. The boy watched a number pass the height of the basket, go over the fold, spend itself in the air and arrive nowhere, and he kept the shape of its going, which was the whole of what was ever on offer. He had seen the same shape at the Cross in a crowd of two hundred and at a market’s edge where eels changed hands six for five, and neither time had anybody given him the number to hold.

Berr Clay came up the yard behind it with the red wool cord of his office folded under his coat and green of tasted glaze at one corner of his mouth. There was hot clay on him under the smell of nothing at all, which is the dearest smell in Grath, and the women at the tubs made room for him the way people make room for a slate that has been left out in the rain. He wiped his thumb twice on his apron before he touched anything, and what he touched was nothing — the thumb went through the air twice and came to rest against his own narrow slate, which he held unopened against his forearm. The boy knew the small ceremony. It was a man making sure the record would be clean before he let a figure come near it. Berr passed a finger over what was written and let his eyes stay on it a breath longer than the writing required, as if to check that the figure had not moved while he was looking away.

“A number goes into a slate, not into a well,” he said, in the low voice that made the women lean in. “The house wants it again, there’ll be a slate at the watch.”

He did not say I. He said the number and the slate and the house, and took himself out of his own sentence as neatly as a surgeon takes a hand out of a field before the dressing goes on; and the boy, who had once held a retractor for a man who worked that way, kept the twice-wiped thumb on the tray of record-first hands and did not call it the same hand as the midwife’s, or a neighbour’s at this same well on an earlier morning, or the clerk’s behind the Court table, or the man who had priced him across a market, or Alda Reed laying her birth-slates writing-down before she turned to a child. Five hands and a sixth on six separate places on one tray, and no tray touching another. Berr looked nowhere near the basket. He went off up toward High Kiln with the box in his own house still locked and the true deduction still inside it, and the figure he had carried in his fist went with him into a slate and stayed there, and the yard went back to its buckets.

❦

Word came up the yard with the neighbours that the clerk was still set at the Fishstairs with his slate and his soft cuff for the measure nobody had called. He had been there since the morning after the blessing and there was no sign of his being taken off. He put his hand to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls; he had done it on the first morning and he was doing it still, and the eels went on being sold over the top of him. The boy filed the order on a tray of its own. It was not the woman in the grey coat writing at the edge of the flare light with a pause in her hand that had cost her fingers, and it was not the collector at the Drawing whose hand went to the vessel before it went to the face and had no pause in it at all, and it was not the soft-cuffed man at the Court’s table at the Cross, though the cuff at the steps was the same grey from the same washing. An ordinary hand, set down to write an uncalled measure, was still an ordinary hand on the seventh morning. No sixth sighting of the neat dark hand came with the word, and the five pauses lay where they had been laid and did not join.

He filed something else beside them, and he filed it as an absence. No collector had been called for the woman the brook took nine mornings ago. There had been no draw at her bed, no soft sentence over a slate, no iron tube under her arm, no hand stopping short of a pulse and then going away from it without saying what it had found. The yard had lifted her out and the yard had gone back to its work, and warmth left where it fell and the morning came. He kept the missing collection the way he kept the interval of a pot turned toward and giving nothing: as a thing with the outline of a sound that had not come, filed beside the intervals and never among them. It was not a hearing of the full note; it had no vessel and no note and no hour. It was the shape a collection would have had if anybody had thought to call one, and the six hearings stayed six, five from four sources and the sixth from a person, beside them and never among them, and no seventh came into the yard that morning.

❦

A weir woman came up from the river road with salt white at her wrists and at the cuffs of her sleeves, the salt of a morning spent over the eel racks, and she brought a piece of news the way such news always comes in Low Kiln, as something said to the boards and not to a person. “One board in the walk won’t carry a man,” she said. “Not when the river comes up. They’ll step on it and they’ll find out.”

The walk she meant was the plank walk at the Weir, the raised boards that follow the north bank out to the traps and stand over the water on posts, the only dry way to the eels when the Ilm comes up. She had not said which board. That was the manner of the place: a weakness was spoken of in the third person, and the board itself was left where it was with nobody’s weight on it, and the man who stepped over it in the days to come would step over it again without ever knowing what he had stepped over. The boy had seen the boards from the height at which a basket sees the world and had kept one of them as a gap in a sixth room, because the eye had gone to it and then away and he could not make his hand go where his eye had been. He set the news beside the missing step at the Fishstairs — four steps down and a landing that was not a step — and beside the hairline in the river ice that ran a while and stopped short of the edge, and he kept the three in three places and did not let them touch. A board that will not bear a man and a breath a fire does not take are the same shape and not the same thing, and he had learned the cost of making a cause out of a shape in a room where the light was better and the stakes were a life. Here there was no light and no leave and no hand, only the shape, which he kept.

The river was beginning to stir. That was what the men come up from the lower road said, though they said it sideways and finished with the weather. Under its skin of ice the Ilm ran a little higher than it had run a tend ago, and the dark veins of the current showed through the thinning skin in a pattern the eye wanted to read as a map and could not. The boy had put the rise in its own place three mornings back and had not moved it since. He added nothing to it today. A thing on its way to breaking is not yet broken, and a man who calls the break before it comes has only made a doctrine out of a board, which is the kind of thing that gets a listener believed and a vessel left unhandled. He kept the board heard-of and not trodden. He did not make it into an accident. Nobody had asked him to, and nobody could have, because he was forty-seven days old and lay in the shade with the fold at his chin and the frost writing above him on the lip of the well.

❦

Behind the drying racks the Slip Brook ran clay-heavy a little higher than it should, the colour of a pot washed into a bucket and left to settle. It came down off the pits with the morning’s slip in it and passed along the hedge where the woman had gone into the water, and the yard, which had looked at the place every day since, had stopped looking at it now. The water made a small sound against the stones that a man would not have heard over the buckets, but the boy, who lay at the height where such small sounds carry, heard it under everything: under the rope over the lip, under the women at the tubs, under the talk of the ice and the number and the board. Under all of it, fainter than any of it, came the low note of the Singing Sill, the fall below the bridges giving its early note through the boards and the water before its time. The note went in under the skin of the morning and stayed there. Nobody at the well said what it was. A woman shifted a basket and said the brook was up with the clay and it would go down again, and that was an end of that subject too.

He kept four shapes and he kept them apart. The honeyed ice of the first days of Ilmen lay in one place, the word for it still going around the yard by mouth and never written. The hairline in the ice that ran and stopped short of the edge lay in another. The higher clay-heavy brook lay in a third. This frost-bloom on the well-lip, white and feathered and already losing its heads to the rope, lay in a fourth. He did not let the four places touch. They were four likenesses of a vessel handing its work to the next thing until the skin held more than it was made to hold, and they were four likenesses and not one cause, because a cause is a thing a man can put his hand to and he had no hand and no word in Grathic for the failure and no standing to ask for either. The district’s tongue had a word for honeyed ice and a word for a thaw and a word for the weather, and the yard used all three over the top of them and went on warm. He wrote no cause across any of them. The count went on in the only place it could, which was inside him, and it bought him nothing he could spend.

Sella Vail came back across the yard with the basket at her hip and moved him through the groups the way she moved a pot through a room where things are being carried — at a half-pace from every open slate, never between a hand and the writing it was going to, never where a figure lay with its face up. There was a slate propped open against a crate at the rack end, and she went around it wide enough that the basket’s shadow did not touch the corner of it. When a neighbour began to ask where the figure had gone that had passed from fist to mouth to shoulder, Sella’s little-door burn shut on the empty air where a cup would have been and she said the house had sent it up the road, and the neighbour said well, and nothing more came of it. She gave the instruction to the wool and the bucket and never to the basket. “Keep it off him,” she said to the wool, with the wind coming off the water. To the bucket, when she set it down: “That’ll do.” She said nothing about where the number had gone or who had paid for the warmth that came up in the water and went off in a bucket into a house that was not anybody’s to enter.

The morning went on. The sun got over the roofs and took the frost off the lip, frond by frond, until the stone was bare and looked as if nothing had ever been written on it. The women went back to the tubs, the rack-women went back to the slip, the man who had carried the figure in his fist came back down the road with his hands empty and said nothing about what he had held. Tarn, down at the shed end with a broom, went once over the road that a man takes when a house wants a collector, and went over it again without once turning his head to look along it, and no collector came down it, and he did not say that the thing which had not happened had a name. Six years of the seven were behind him and he would not count the seventh. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles that no scrubbing lifted, the pale clip-ring at his left wrist caught what light there was and gave it back. He agreed with the ones who wanted the thaw early and with the ones who wanted it late and went on wanting the late one, because a late thaw puts off the paying. He said nothing about the board, and the boy kept his silence beside his own and did not let the two touch.

By midday the shade had moved a hand’s breadth off the well and Sella turned the basket so the sun was off his face, and the boy lay there with the fold at his chin and the stone lip above him, bare now, with the writing gone off it and nobody owning the day. The number had gone past him from fist to mouth to shoulder and had been given to nobody. The clerk was still at the Fishstairs with a slate for a measure nobody had called. The weak board was still at the Weir, heard of and not trodden. The woman in the brook was still uncollected and the omission still had no word. The four shapes lay in four places and did not touch, and the sixth hearing stayed where it was, and the fire under the roof behind him kept four, four and a gap, missing one breath in every four. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the bucket on the lip and came down. Nothing had been joined, paid or explained. The count went on.

The afternoon gave up its light edge by edge the way a fire gives up its heat, first the far side of the yard and then the near, until the last of it lay a finger’s width along the tops of the racks and the stone lip of the yard well was only a stone again with nothing written on it. Sella Vail came across when the shade had gone off it and lifted the basket as she lifted a pot filled that must not be slopped, the little-door burn closing first on the handle and then on nothing. She carried him up the Clay Yard with the basket held level, at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, never between a hand and the writing it was going to, never where a figure lay with its face up. A slate stood propped against the rack end that had been open since morning. She went around it as wide as if it had been a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it over, and there was no writing on the side of it that Corin could see, and that was not the reason she went around. He was forty-seven days old and went up the yard looking at the underside of her chin and at the way the smoke lay low behind her, and he kept the shape of her going, which was the shape she went around every keeping in this house.

Under the Vail kiln-house roof she set him on his bed and not in the Debt Loft, and put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without once looking at his face. “Keep it off him,” she said to the wool. “It’s the river wind.” To the fire, when she had banked it and put the pot where it would take the last of the heat: “That’ll do.” She said the house where another woman would have said I, and gave every instruction to the fire and the pot and the wool and never to the basket. The door did not quite shut. The room went on being the room he had known for forty-seven days, with the bench by the door and the pot on the bench and the draught going along the floor to the corner, and the gap in it that was the same gap as in the birth room and the death-house two doors down and the Court’s room heard through a doorway and the room with the tin above the bed and the Eel House a league upriver. Six rooms with a bench and a pot and a draught and a gap, and he kept them in six places and had not written a cause across any of them, and he did not write one now.

One crust lay in the fold where two had lain. The spent one from Stone 15 was stiff past the smell of anything and lay where it had always lain, uneaten and unlifted and unnamed, and the other was nowhere. The empty place beside it was the warmth he had most wanted in this life and the one he could least reach. It was not cold that he felt there. He knew the difference between the loss of heat and the loss of a thing, because a man who has stood over a body and known the moment it gave has no need to be told the difference; the one is a number on a slate and the other is a place in the hand. He set the empty place on a tray by itself and did not lay a cause across it. Beside it, in their own places, lay the fifth bundle the kindling did not have, the told bell kept beside and never on the heard bell, the collection that had not come for the woman the brook took with the outline of a sound that did not come, the clean dark line where the Ash had turned its corner and been burned and called dealt with, and the shape of a thumb pressed once into wicker. None of them touched. He had gone on keeping them as he had kept the presences, gently and without use, and the surgeon in him registered the empty place with the same exactness he had once brought to a wound and could do no more with it than he could with a wound in a body two paces off.

He did not think who had taken the crust, which was not the same thing as not knowing that there were hands that might have taken it. Sella Vail’s hand put the fold right every morning and did not look at his face. Alda Reed’s hand had pressed the fold flat at his shoulder too long and gone away with her tin still shut. Mira Silt’s hand had put the crust there on Stone 28 and had done necessary things before anybody could make a speech about them. He set the three hands on three places on a tray and would not let the tray become a cause, because he had learned in a room with better light what three likenesses laid in a row will do to a man who wants an answer. There was no word in Grathic for a thing taken and not spoken of, and no word of his own for the taking, and the district would have called the empty place a thing or called it nothing and gone on warm either way. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen again in the afternoon and nobody wiped it, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left. His head went where it wanted with the small wet sound, and his hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the place where the warmth had been and came down. He was the only adult in the room and the least use in it, which was a joke he had learned to find funny without laughing, a way of laughing that cost less and kept the same blade in it.

Behind him the carried fire kept under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees and taking its breath in fours. Four, four and a gap. It had kept that count since the year’s fire came up the yard in a clay dish from hands nobody named, and it had kept it through the burning of the ridge and the opening of the slates and the death of a woman in the water, and it would keep it after this house was cold and another house had it. The coal that had gone up the river on the back of a brine-gull two mornings ago was entered in no slate and missed in no account. The fire had not missed it. The house had not missed it. One warmth less was abroad under this roof than had been abroad on the morning of the blessing, and the only person who carried the subtraction was lying on a bed with a fold at his ear and a crust he could not eat. He had once read a chart with the nurse’s note turned down and signed the page anyway. He knew what it costs when nobody looks at the note. Here nobody had written a note at all, and the warmth had gone off with a bird and the morning had come anyway, and the house had asked for nothing but that the fire hold.

Tarn came in from the yard with the small bench knife back on its sill and told nobody where it was, because the yard had already been told that morning and a man does not say the same thing twice to a house that does not answer. Six years of the seven were behind him and he held the seventh where he always held it, and Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles that no scrubbing lifted. His broom went over the road down toward the Fishstairs once and then again without his once turning his head along it. No collector had been called for the woman the water took and no collector came down that road now, and he did not say that the thing which had not happened had a name. Jorra Vail came in from the Vail wheel-shed with slip dried to the colour of old bone up both forearms and no grey worked past the bend of his little finger, and the bent finger went in ahead of his hand at the damper. Sella had opened her mouth to tell him the fire was low. He moved the damper before the sentence was out and left the end of it where it was, and she gave the rest to the pot. “You’ll do,” he told the pot, in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either, and he told the wheel it did not care whose hands were on it, only if they were steady. He did not say the back room was cold. He did not look toward the bed. Garl Vail’s salt-stiff coat kept a broader man’s shape on its peg behind the door and the hollow seat kept its twenty-year lean and nobody filled it, and the clean dark line where the ridge had turned held nothing and was not swept, because a line left where it is is still a line kept. One of his proverbs went to the pot and another to the fire, and neither went to the basket.

Sella went around the house’s number badly, as she had gone around it every day of his life. She moved a loaf along the shelf and set another back where it had been and looked at the space between them, and her lips went around the days and the loaves and the jar on the wall shelf without letting out a figure. There was a space at the end where a loaf should have stood and there was no loaf in it, and she looked at the space twice and then at the fire, and the count in the house went on being a shape and not a number. The boy lay on his bed and kept it exactly. Four on the near row of the kindling and four behind and a space at the end. Four strokes of the bell and four and a gap. Four, four and a gap in the fire, in the boards, in the bench, in the told story, in the space between a woman’s thumb and her scar. He had never been given a figure in this house and had never asked for one and had no word in the district’s tongue with which to ask. At the bank she turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and did not touch the widow’s mark at her throat, because a bank is not an opening. She had touched it once before the kiln was opened in the morning and not at any other hour, and the rule held as exactly as the count held, and neither of them was entered anywhere.

Outside, the yard went on with its evening in the words it had used all day. A woman at the tubs said the ice would not hold and another said it would, and both went home with the argument unsettled and nobody owning it. the Ilm ran a little higher under its skin and gave its low note through the boards, and the house called it weather and banked its fire. The boy kept the morning’s frost-bloom on the well-lip in its own place with the honeyed ice and the hairline and the higher brook, and let the four places stay four. He had written no cause across them at the well and did not write one now in the warm. A cause is a thing a man can put his hand to, and he had no hand and no word in Grathic for the failure and no standing to ask for either.

No collector had been called for the woman the brook took nine mornings ago, and nobody in this house had found a word for the not calling of one. Nobody had made a doctrine of it. Nobody had stood up in the yard and said that warmth could be left where it fell and the morning would come and that this was a thing a man might do; the yard had simply left it where it fell and the morning had come and gone back to its buckets. The clerk who sat at the Fishstairs with his slate and soft cuff for a measure nobody had called was still sitting there for all the boy knew, and a woman lay unentered somewhere east of here where the clay had not wiped out of the creases beside her mouth. Those were two facts and they stayed two facts. He did not lay the slate across the body or the body across the slate, because a man who lays three likenesses in a row and asks them to stand for a cause ends by believing the cause and forgetting the likenesses, and he had once been that man in a room with better light and a life on the table. The absence stayed as an interval with the outline of a collection, filed beside the intervals and never among them, and the six hearings of the full note stayed six, five from four sources and the sixth from a person, beside them and never among them, and no seventh came into the room.

The nearly full note of Ilmen 6 lay on its own tray, advanced and not paid, neither a hearing nor the third thing. Five unjoined pauses lay in five places and did not join. Two cords under a woman’s apron and a knot-string in a hut a league upriver stood on separate boards and did not touch, and the knots on none of them had been counted by anybody who could enter them. The word said low into a basket at the Weir lay on its own tray as a saying, with the shape of a double turn and a tucked tail beside it and the weight of a thumb on wicker apart from both, and the woman who had said it was watching still and would get no answer from this bed. He had no hand to lift toward the cord and no word for the weight of a thumb and no standing to ask what any of it counted. So he kept it. That was the whole of the work available to him, and he had done it every day of his forty-seven days, and it had never once bought him a hand.

The light went off the yard and the evening came on the way it always came on, at the edges of the room first and the middle last, so that the fire was the last thing to go dark and then not dark at all but a few red eyes under the ash. The boy lay on his bed under the Vail roof, forty-seven days old, with one crust in the fold where two had lain and an empty place beside it that he had wanted more than he had wanted the truth and could not reach any more than he could reach the truth. Behind him the carried fire took its breath in fours and missed one, and the coal that had gone up with the gull was entered in no account and missed in none, and the district had entered the year and had not entered him. Nothing had been joined, paid or explained. Nothing had been solved. The count went on. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth and came down.

The Chronicle of Chapter 11

On the morning after the blessing Sella Vail set the boy below the north bridge as a pot is set, out of the draught, the fold at his chin. A brine-gull went off upriver with a coal from a banked fire not yet cold, and the warmth went into no account; the boy, forty-five days old, kept the subtraction nobody else would. Orra Weir came down with eels, and the knot at her waist she could not account for had a shape he could now read --- a double turn, tail tucked, tied left over right, set with the weight of a thumb --- though he had no name for it and the word she had said into his basket was not said again. She watched; he could do nothing.

Under the Vail roof that evening Sella went around the month in loaves and glances, giving every instruction to the fire, the pot and the wool and saying the house where another woman would say I. She touched the widow’s mark once before the kiln opened and not at the bank. Tarn’s knife lay on its sill with six years counted and the seventh held back; his broom went over the collector’s road without turning and no collector came, because a late thaw puts off the paying. Jorra’s bent finger went to the damper before she had finished, and he told a pot it would do.

On Ilmen 17 Alda Reed came short with soap-and-milk and birth-slates, touched the tin inside her bodice unopened, pressed the fold too long and laid exact dates where a wonder should stand; warm and breathing, she said, which was exact and not true. In the afternoon the wind turned off the water and the smell of barley did not come. One crust lay where two had lain --- the smoky one Mira had put there weeks before, the warmth he had most wanted and could never reach --- gone, and nobody said who had taken it or that it was gone. He kept the empty place as he kept a wound, without dressing it in a cause.

On Ilmen 18 frost wrote in white fronds on the well-lip where the Warm had lately gone out, and by noon the yard called it weather. A number went from fist to mouth to shoulder and arrived nowhere; Berr Clay wiped his thumb twice before touching nothing, said a number goes into a slate, not into a well, and went off with his box still locked. Word came up that the clerk sat still at the Fishstairs for a measure nobody had called. A weir woman said a board in the plank walk would not carry a man when the river rose. At dusk Sella set him on his bed, forty-seven days old, one crust in the fold and an empty place beside it, the fire keeping four, four and a gap. Nothing had been joined, paid or explained. The count went on.
