One in Twelve

A man wipes his thumb on his apron before he touches the slate. The slate is clean. It is the hand that wants cleaning.

— A kiln-master’s saying, High Kiln

— Day 4 —

Stone 10, Y.H. 812 (Tenthday)

The breath of the kiln went out behind him and did not come back the same, the way a room keeps the shape of a man for a moment after he has left it. Sella Vail carried him out of the Vail kiln-house in a basket with a fold of wool over him, and the door did its old trick of not quite shutting, and the heat that had held him for a whole day went out into the yard as if it had never been meant to stay. Four days old, Corin thought, and already carried out of the only warm room he had known, with the place where the stone had been empty at his belly and the wool smelling of woodsmoke and of the woman who had laid it there. He could not lift his head to watch the roof go away. He listened to it instead. The kiln took one more breath behind the wall and missed the one after it, and the gap was the last thing of the house that came with him.

Being carried was its own instruction. He had been carried on stretchers enough times to read the hands that did it: the height they held the weight at, the way they took the corners, whether they watched the face or the wheels. Sella carried him neither like a sack nor like a child. She carried him like a pot that has been filled and must not be slopped, her forearm under the basket and her hand at the small of it, the wool keeping his cheek from the rail of her hip. He had once been a man who decided where the stretchers went. Now he was the weight, and the weight was not asked.

She had not touched the thing at her throat. He knew the habit well enough now: she put her hand to the widow’s mark before she opened the kiln and at no other time, and this morning the coals were only being banked and the door not opened, so the hand stayed where it was, on the handle of the basket. A lesser woman would have crossed herself and asked the fire to keep him. Sella Vail carried him down the step and did not look back at the house she had made warm for him.

the Clay Yard was the colour of the day, which was the colour of ashes shaken out of a grate. The sleet had gone off in the night and left a dry cold behind it, the kind that gets into the nose before it gets into the bones, and every breath he made under the wool came back at him as a small smoke. Oiled paper lifted and let go at the windows of the low houses, over and over, the way a man’s thumb goes over the edge of a table while he waits for a number. The slip pits lay to his left with ice at their edges the colour of old bone. Beyond the last roofs the Ilm went under its skin of ice, and the whole district said, without saying it, that the ice was holding, which meant somebody had gone to look and come back with a number they did not give.

The cold got into him through the places the wool did not cover: his cheek where it had ridden back, the soft spot at his temple, the wrist lying outside the fold. He knew what cold did to a body before it killed it, the long surrender of the small vessels, the confusion that comes on like sleep, the afterdrop that takes a man down at the moment everybody thinks him warm. He had pulled men out of rivers in the old life and watched them get worse in a blanket. Here he was in the blanket and the getting worse was happening to him, and the only thing he could do about it was keep a count. The infant under the wool wanted his mother and wanted the breast and did not care about the small vessels at all.

The market came to him in layers, the way a room comes into focus for a man who has been sitting in the dark: first the smell, then the noise, then the things themselves. The smell of pigs came over the smell of clay, wet straw and warm dung and the sweet-sour of swill, and under that the smoke of barley bread from the bakehouses and, on the north wind, the heavy sweet smoke of tallow. Then the voices: the moot shouting at the Cross, a roar with edges on it, and under the roar the small separate sounds of a market setting itself up, a board dropped, a coin rung on a stone, a pig driven over wet boards with a weight behind it that he felt travel up through Sella’s arm and into the basket. Then a bell, struck somewhere out of sight. It was not a church bell, though it went on longer than a church bell would and nobody in the yard crossed himself at it. He let it be a bell. There were things in this town that went under one name and did another work, and in four days he had learned to let them stand.

The bell went on at its own time, not with the voices and not against them, and nobody in the market seemed to need it to agree with anything. It was the first thing he heard that morning that had no work attached to it that he could see, and he listened to it longer than he needed to, the way a man listens to a sound in a ward because it is the only thing in the room that does not need him to answer it.

He counted because there was nothing else to count that belonged to him. Daniel Marchetti had counted the exits in every room he walked into, two in the bay, one behind the curtain, a fire door that stuck; it was a habit from a place where knowing the door mattered more than knowing the man. In the old life he had counted the beats of a pulse before he spoke to a resident, and the count had outlived the man and come to rest in a body that could not reach a cup. He counted the nails of a floor, the breaths of a fire through a wall, the steps of a woman carrying him, because these were his and nobody had asked for them and nobody could tax them. Sella’s stride was long and even, the stride of a woman who had carried sacks of slip and knew what a weight would bear. He counted her steps out of the yard, up the short rise and onto the road that went down toward the water, and he came to a hundred and nine before the pace changed, and a hundred and nine was not four and four and a gap. It was not the kiln’s breathing or the floor’s nails or Dessa’s nineteen out and nineteen back. It was a line, the same line her steps up the yard had been on the third morning, and it ended where the road began to go down. He put the hundred and nine in the private place beside the gap and left it there, unentered.

❦

Then the market gave him its own numbers to keep, and he kept them in the same place. Three pigs came past before they were weighed, then two more, then a man with a pole drove a lean one over the boards and it went clumsy on the wet wood and was hauled up by the ear. Five. Then six. A woman counting a heap of turnips said a figure out loud and another woman said a different figure and they went on with their hands in the turnips. He did not know what the figures bought or sold, and it did not matter; the numbers were everywhere and meant nothing he could spend, while the one number that was truly his was the one nobody had written down. He kept them side by side the way a man keeps the small change of a pocket and never counts it against the rent.

The woman at the stall had a slate propped against a crate with a row of figures on it in a hand he could not read. He tried, because he had once read a chart upside down across a bed and known what it said; the marks would not come into words for him, and the effort of it brought the small animal sound up in his throat. He gave the row its shape and let the shape go. There was writing all over this market and none of it had been written for him to read, and that, he was beginning to understand, was the ordinary condition of a person in a district.

The head went where it wanted. He had given up trying to hold it up on the second day and had learned only to time the small animal sound that came when it turned past the point where the neck would permit it, a little wet noise in the throat that was not a word and did not ask for anything, though the women always treated it as if it had. He had once been able to tell, from the sound a man made in the dark, where the pain sat and how long it had been there. Here the sound was his own and meant only that he was cold and awake and uncomfortable, and it got the wool adjusted and nothing else. In the basket the fold had ridden down off his face and the cold had got into his mouth, and a thread of drool went from his lip and would not be wiped. He tried to push the fold back with the hand that had once put a shoulder into its socket in the dark, and the hand was a small soft thing with milky nails that closed on air two inches from his face and came away. He had put a thousand shoulders back and he could not keep a blanket on his own chin. The joke of it had gone flat around the third day, but the exactness stayed, and the exactness was worse than the joke had ever been.

The fold came back over his face before he could be ashamed of it. Sella did it without looking, the way she had put her hand near the slate in House Silt without touching it, a movement that had already happened by the time he knew she had moved. She did not say his name and did not say any finished feeling over him. “Keep that off his face,” she said, to the wind, or to the wool, or to the boy; it was not clear which. “The wind’s in his mouth. He’ll take the cold in his throat.” The little-door burn closed on the fold and let it go, and the hand went back to the handle of the basket.

A woman at a tub looked up as they passed and went back to her work without appearing to have seen them. Another, straightening from a stall with a slate in her hand, said something about the boy that was a question. Sella did not answer the question. She answered the wool.

“The house brings him,” she said. “Keep his chin covered. That’s the cold’s way in.”

“Whose house, then?” the woman said.

“The house,” said Sella, and shifted the basket to her other arm.

“And the mother?”

“Account-day,” the woman said, as if that were an answer to something.

“Keep the fold over his ears,” Sella said. “He’ll have the ache in them by noon.”

“Don’t set him in the draught,” Sella added, and that was the end of it. The question lay where it had fallen, not answered and not asked again.

He had been put back under cloth in a public place by a hand that would not meet his eye and would not let him thank it. A kindness that cannot be answered is a kindness that cannot be refused; he had learned that under another roof, and here it was given again in the shape of a fold of wool, and there was no version of the world in which he could hand it back. The surgeon in him noted the pressure of her arm through the cloth, measured and unhurried, knowing what the thing would bear. The infant in him wanted the breast and was cold and wanted his mother, and the two wanted different things and both were louder than the man who was trying to keep the count.

She did not set the basket down where a slate lay open on a block beside the stall. She set it on her hip instead and stood a half-pace off from the slate, the way she had stood a half-pace off from the birth-slate in House Silt, and the boy felt the small careful distance and filed it with the other distances he had been made to keep: the nineteen steps, the end of the yard, the wall between a bed and a loft. A slate with writing open on it was a thing to stand off from. Nobody had told him why, and nobody had to.

❦

At the yard well the stone lip had a rim of ice on it, white in the morning and already chipped where somebody had broken water in the dark. A bucket stood on its side with a skin of ice in the bottom. The woman at the tub had her hands in water that steamed and then did not, and she rubbed the same patch of the same sheet over and over, which is what a woman does when she wants a morning to go by without being made to think about it. Barley smoke and tallow smoke lay in the low air together. A little further on someone had set a pot of glaze to warm and the lead in it gave off its sweet sickly smell, and the smell of the pigs came over that, and the clay came under everything. The oiled paper at the windows of House Silt lifted three doors up the yard, and the tile roof of the smithy held a crust of frost that had not yet been touched by the sun.

The market did not look at him. That was the worst of it, worse than the cold and worse than the noise: the square was filling with people and not one of them had yet found a reason to look into the basket. He had seen a room full of people not look at a man before, in a bay where the man on the table was past saving, and he had understood it then as a kind of professional mercy, the silence of people who had already done what they could. Here it was ordinary, and it was done to him. He had moved from a room where he was kept into a room where he would be counted, and in neither room could he say a word.

The shouting at the Cross went on resolving into something with a shape to it. The roar broke into separate voices and the separate voices into words he could not quite hold, and under them the bell went on being a bell and the pigs went on being driven over the boards. He was carried into the middle of it and put down at the edge of it, and the market closed around the basket the way a room closes around a man who has come in and cannot find the door he came by. He had spent his life reading rooms: where the senior stood, who would not meet an eye, which nurse had been crying in the supply room and tried to hide it with a clean apron. Rooms told you what they were before anybody spoke. This room told him nothing he could use. It was a room made for counting and for being counted, and he was the thing being carried through it, and the mistake of his old life, which he had made a hundred times and never noticed making, was to suppose that being the man who read the room meant he was not also one of the things in it. He had counted a hundred and nine steps to get here and five or six pigs and a gap that belonged to nobody, and not one of those numbers could be said out loud by a four-day-old in a basket under a fold of wool. The Hearth Drawing was two days off, and Low Kiln said the Five were three days off, and the room he was now in had already started counting him without asking his name.

The shouting went on. He was inside it. There was no part of it he could address and nobody in it who had asked him anything at all, and the new number stayed in his head where nobody had asked for it, a hundred and nine, his and untaxable, while the market made its ordinary morning noise around the basket and the fold of wool and the small hand that could not lift itself to push the wool back.

❦

The shouting did not stop. It gathered and leaned. Two hundred people at the Cross were making one sound, and the sound wanted a figure the way a dry throat wants water and shapes itself around the word before the water comes. Under the one sound ran the small separate noises of the market setting itself up: a board dropped flat, a coin rung on a stone, a pig driven over wet boards with a weight behind it that he felt travel up through the woman’s arm and into the basket. Then a man stepped up to the block where the slate lay, and the sound dropped, not because he had asked for quiet but because every person in the square had to lean in at once to hear him, and leaning is a quiet business. Even the pigs seemed to hold still for it, the lean one with its ear in a man’s hand, the steam coming off their backs in the dry cold.

He could see very little of it from under the wool: the underside of a chin, the block’s near edge, a strip of boot, the sky stripped of colour. A man’s height in a crowd is a country to a child in a basket; he knew the size of the thing he was listening to by the way the sound came down to him, from above and a little to his left, and by the way the crowd leaned toward it as a field of barley leans one way in a wind. He had been carried into the middle of the market and set down at its edge, and the market had gone on arranging itself over him all morning without once finding a reason to stop.

Berr Clay took the slate by its edge. Before he touched it he wiped his thumb on his apron, one short dry stroke across the clay-coloured cloth, and then again on the other side, and only then set two fingers to the stone and turned it a little toward the light, the way a man turns a loaf to see which side took the fire. The slate was clean. He had wiped his thumb anyway. He wore the red wool cord of his office across his chest, folded square where another man might have worn a sash, and it was the only bright thing in the square besides the frost on the smithy roof. He was heavy in the way a wagon is heavy, with a back that had carried clay and grey stubble he had trimmed with a knife before market-day, and a ring of lead-glaze green at one corner of his mouth the colour of old copper gone bad. His hands were large and clean. He smelled of hot clay and, under it, nothing at all, which is the expensive smell of Court business. A man who tastes his own glaze in front of a moot is either thorough or afraid. Corin had no way of deciding which and had long since given up supposing he could tell from a face.

“The levy is called,” Berr said. “It is not called by me.”

He did not raise his voice. That was the frightening thing, if a thing in a basket could be frightened: the moot roared and he did not, and the roar had to come down to him.

“One in twelve, as the notice says.”

He said it the way another man might cross himself, and said it low, and the crowd took it the way wet ground takes a dropped board, flat and without echo. Behind him a square of paper lifted on a nail at the moot-post, gone soft at one corner, with a row of marks on it that the wind turned over and back. The boy had known the figure before he had ever seen the paper: three of four loaves in a room three doors up the yard, the slip in the pit to be paid for in the thaw, a bed and a warmth for a foundling nobody had agreed to pay for. The figure had had no name in that room either. A midwife had told a woman three days ago that a child had gone in the levy among the children taken for the account, and had given the number no spoken shape at all. Here the number was given a shape and said out loud in a low voice, and nobody withheld it, and it went out into the square and sat down among the pigs.

He could not read the row of marks on the paper, but he knew the shape of a figure held up where everyone could see it and the shape of a hand that would not let it be argued with. A number on a notice is a number that has been settled somewhere before anybody in the square has opened his mouth, and there is no arguing with a thing that has already been written down. The slate on the block was the same shape as the slate on the bench in House Silt, and the writing on it lay turned up to the light in the same way. He set the two together and then kept them apart, the way he kept all the things that had the same shape and did not yet make a fourth.

“If your household has heard otherwise,” Berr said, “your household has heard wrong. The account stands as read. There’ll be a slate at the watch for any household that wants the figure again, and the household can bring its own light.”

He apologised the way a man apologises to a pot he has cracked, by telling it what to do next. He never said I. He said the district and the account and the notice, and each of them stood in for a man who was standing right there on the block, so that the man himself was nowhere in the sentence and the figure was everywhere.

Daniel Marchetti tried to turn his head to see the face of the man pricing him. The neck would not permit it. Past a point the head went where it wanted and a small wet sound came up in his throat, and the wool rode back off his cheek, and the cold got into the corner of his mouth. He had once watched a resident keep his eyes on his shoes at the review of a chart he had signed, in a room with a long table and a window that would not open, and had read the not-looking then as clearly as a pulse: a man who will not meet your eye has already decided what the room will call the thing that happened. The chart had been his to sign, and the note he had not read had been lying under his own hand. He had read the resident correctly and had still said nothing, which was the part he had never managed to correct. Here the not-looking was done to him, and it was not even a refusal. The man on the block did not look into the basket and had no reason to; the figure went on being read over the top of it, and the boy lay under it like a fact under a stone.

❦

The figure did its work in the square. A woman near the basket put her hand on her daughter’s head and tightened her fingers in the child’s hair while the number was spoken, and let go when it had passed, the way a hand lets go of a rope once the load is set down. The girl was old enough to stand still for a figure and young enough not to know what it cost; she shifted her weight from one foot to the other and looked at the pigs. The boy had seen that hand in House Silt three days ago, on a woman who had counted nineteen steps she did not take. Nobody in the market said the word the figure was for. The word was not needed. The figure went into the account and the account held it, and two hundred people went on standing in the dry cold with the thing they had come to hear lying where they could all see it, and the market breathing over it as a kiln breathes over a pot it has decided not to draw.

The moot had wanted its figure the way a thirsty room wants the door opened; now it had the figure and did not know what to do with the wanting, so it put the wanting back where it had been, into the pigs and the turnips and the haggling over short weight. A man lifted a child onto his shoulders so the child could see the slate and then could not get him down again for a while, and the child cried, and nobody looked up at the crying because the figure was lying on the block and wanted looking at. Two women at a stall went on with their hands in a heap of turnips and said two different figures over them, and neither figure was the one that had just been read, and nobody thought the three had anything to do with one another.

Beside the block the Sill assessor stood with a ledger held shut against his coat, and gave the moot the only other voice it got that morning.

“The figure stands as read.”

He said it to the slate and not to the crowd, and settled the ledger more firmly under his arm, and that was the whole of what the Court had to add. His collar was narrow and clean and his hands were not clay-coloured at all; the boy noted the hands, because hands tell you a man’s work before a collar does, and these had never touched a pot or a pig or a slip pit, and had no callus between thumb and first finger where a carrying cord would lie. He could have read the man’s grip without being told what the man was. Here there was nothing to read but a figure that stood as read, which is a sentence that closes a matter before the matter has properly opened, and the man who said it had said it so often that it had stopped having a shape at all.

Berr’s tongue went once to the green corner of his mouth and came back, a man tasting his own work without appearing to taste it. He waited while the silence ran out, and when it had, his thumb went once more across his apron and he set the slate a fraction straighter on the block. He had the careful hands of a man who has handled a great many things that must not be dropped and has learned that a figure is the heaviest of them.

Eight pigs, two hundred people, one assessor and one slate. The market had its own arithmetic and none of it included the weight in the basket. The cold had got into the boy’s mouth again under the wool and the drool went down his chin onto the fold, and there was nobody in that square who would wipe it and nobody he could ask to. The head went where it wanted and the small wet sound came again, and the hand that had once reduced a shoulder in the dark closed on nothing at the edge of the basket and came away. He counted the pigs because the pigs were there and counting was what he had: eight, with a lean one that went clumsy on the wet boards and was hauled up by the ear. Eight was not four and four and a gap, and it went into the private place with a hundred and nine and with the strokes of a bell he had not yet counted, and none of the numbers could be spent. The infant in him wanted the breast and was cold and did not care a figure for any of it.

He counted the ways out as well, because a room has exits and a district has roads and he had never learned to stop telling them apart. There was the road down to the south bridge and Slate Row a quarter league east, the steep street up to High Kiln, the north bridge and the road that went out toward the Bone Orchard. Four ways out of the square and not one of them a door he could open, and the only roof that would keep him lay up the Clay Yard behind him with its coals banked under ash. He put the four beside the others and left them there, untaxable because nobody had asked for them.

Sella Vail had not set the basket where the slate lay open. She stood a half-pace off from the block with the basket on her hip, the same distance she had kept from the birth-slate in House Silt, and when the figure had been swallowed by the market she did not look at the slate and did not bring the basket nearer. She put her hand to the wool at his chin instead, without looking, the little-door burn closing on the fold and letting it go. The hand smelled of woodsmoke and of the sour edge that came up off her apron when she had been near the kiln, and it was the only hand in that square that had any business near his face, and it did not stay there.

“Keep his ears covered,” she said to the wind, or to the wool, or to the basket. “There’s cold off the river.”

She answered nothing else all morning, and the woman who had asked about the mother the morning before was not there to ask again.

❦

When the figure had been read and the moot had nothing more to lean for, Berr Clay set the slate down on its block. It gave a small flat sound, a dry knock with no body behind it, the flat shadow of the full note answering and then stopping at its own surface. It was the third flat note in four days. The first had come from a cup across a birth room, a small dead sound that stopped at the clay and would not go through; the second from a pot set down on a bench in a house with a hollow seat and a salt-stiff coat on a peg; and this one came from the district’s own account laid under a man’s hand. The boy set the three side by side the way he set his numbers, without joining them, and there was no word in Grathic that he had for any of them and no need of one. Nobody in the square tapped the slate for him. The knuckle that would have tapped it was a small soft thing with milky nails lying outside the fold of wool, and it could not have reached the block if the basket had been set against it.

He knew what the hearing was worth, which was nothing. A man who hears a vessel answer flat and cannot reach it has heard a fact and gained no purchase on it; he had known that in a birth room and known it in a kiln-house and knew it here with the whole district’s account under the same small dead sound. The surgeon in him wanted the slate on a bench with a knuckle to it and the answer taken clean, the way he had once taken the measure of a cup two paces from a bed and done nothing with the measure. The infant in him wanted the breast and was cold and had the wool in his mouth. Both wanted things two paces off and a lifetime away, and the square went on with its morning and did not wait for either.

He had closed a matter like that a hundred times. Signed the chart, let the institution call a thing what it wanted to call it, gone home to a refrigerator with four beers in it and a photograph on the wall he looked at every night without seeing. He knew the shape of Berr Clay’s relief standing there on the block, because it was his own relief he was looking at: not triumph and not even cruelty, only the clean settling of a thing that had been given a number and could now be called dealt with. He had been good at that. He had been so good at it that a man with a clean slate and a good reason could stand in a market square and price a child and go home thinking he had done the district a kindness, and Dan would have understood him perfectly, which was the worst of it, and the thing he had never once been able to say out loud to a person who would have understood the words.

The crowd leaned, heard, and went back to the pigs. The roar came up again in its layers: the driver shouting, a pig going over the boards with a weight behind it, a coin rung twice on a stone, a woman calling a price for turnips, the not-a-church bell going on being a bell over all of it. The slate lay where Berr had set it with the figure on it, and the figure stayed where it was, and the district went on breathing around it the way a kiln goes on breathing with the door left a finger’s width open. Sella lifted the basket a little higher on her hip and did not look down into it; the fold had ridden back and she did not put it right, which was a thing he noticed and could not ask her about.

He lay with a third flat note in his ear that nobody had asked him to hear and that he could do nothing with, while the pigs were driven over the boards and the market made its ordinary noise. The slate lay open on its block with the figure on it that would carry him whether he could read it or not. The Hearth Drawing was two days off, and Low Kiln said the Five were three days off, and he kept a hundred and nine and four and four and a gap and the third note, and none of them was a thing he could say out loud in a square that had already counted him.

❦

The market did not stop for the reading. That was the thing he kept coming back to, the way a man puts his tongue to a wound to find out if it is still there: the figure had been given a shape and spoken aloud, and the square had swallowed it, and now the square was about the business of being a market again as if nothing had been said over the top of it. Sella Vail set the basket down on the ground beside a barrel at the edge of the crowd, and the movement was neither a putting-down nor a forsaking, only the shifting of a weight from one tired arm to the ground, the way a woman sets down a sack she means to take up again in a moment. The barrel was an ordinary thing, staves the colour of weak tea, two iron hoops gone rusty at the seam, a dry pale ring where a hundred mornings of cups had come and gone. Four days old, Corin lay against its curved side with his cheek to a stave; the wood was cold and smelled of the cooper’s smoke and of barley, and through the stave the market came and went in small tremors the way a kiln wall trembles when the wheel in the shed takes a heavy piece. She did not look into the basket. She stood over it a moment with her hand on the barrel’s rim, the little-door burn closing on the iron hoop and letting it go, and then turned a half-pace away toward the pigs, which was the distance she had kept all morning from anything with writing on it.

He counted the hoops. Two. A third had been mended once with a flat strip of iron that sat proud of the stave, and the iron was the colour of an old wound that has closed badly. He counted the staves up to the rim and lost the count where the light changed, and began again, because a count that is lost is not a count and he had learned to begin again rather than guess. It was the same arithmetic he had used on the floorboards of a house three doors down the yard, four and four and a knot the width of a thumb, and the same he had used on the breaths of a fire through a wall, four and four and a gap where the fifth should be. The levy had been read out on Account-day as a figure and the figure had sat down among the pigs, and nobody in the square had looked into the basket to see what the figure might be carried toward.

The cold had got into his mouth under the wool again. A thread of drool went from his lip to the fold and stayed there, and there was nobody in that square who would wipe it and nobody he could ask to. He tried to push the fold back with the hand that had once reduced a shoulder in the dark, and the hand was a small soft thing outside the basket with milky nails that closed on air an inch from his cheek and came away. He had put a thousand shoulders back. He could not keep a blanket on his own chin. The joke of it had gone flat three days ago and the exactness had not, and the exactness was worse than the joke had ever been. The head went where it wanted, and past a certain point it took a small wet sound with it, and the sound got the wool put right by a hand that did not look and was gone again before he could be ashamed of it.

He counted the room because it was a room and rooms had to be counted before they could be borne. Two ways out toward the bridges, a steep street climbing to High Kiln, the road going north toward the Bone Orchard: four ways out of the Cross and not one of them a door he could open. Hands: the driver’s hand at the lean pig’s ear, a woman’s hand on her daughter’s head still tight from the figure, the assessor’s hands that never touched anything all morning, Sella’s hand on the barrel rim, a baker’s hand breaking a crust at a stall. Six hands he could see and not one of them his. The slate lay open on its block three paces off with the figure turned up to the light, and he kept the block in the count the way a man keeps the one piece of furniture in a strange room he would have to climb over to reach the door.

The bell went, and the market made its small separate noises under it, a board dropped, a coin rung on a stone, a pig driven over the boards with a weight behind it that he felt travel up through the stave and into his cheek. Then a voice that had not been in the square a moment ago is in it, and it does not need to be loud: Marchetti. You’re back on in ten.

The full name is an instrument in that mouth, a small cold pressure applied at exactly the right place; she had said Daniel Marchetti for twelve years the way a file is drawn across a piece of metal, taking a little off each time, and the metal had never once learned to stop expecting it. The voice belongs to Lena Ostrowski, who is a small spare woman with soap and machine coffee about her and hands that carry the pale scars of a thousand clamps, and she is standing in a room that is not this square and has not been for years, and still the square goes quiet around the sound of her the way a bay goes quiet around a voice that knows where the bodies are.

A beat. Then the other sentence, the one that had made him and had also excused him, arriving in the same flat instrument-voice, without warmth and without apology, the way a file does not apologise for taking a little off: The first duty is to decide. The second is to carry it. You seem to have trouble with the second.

He had heard it first at twenty-eight with his hands in a man’s chest in a room that smelled of lemon disinfectant and old blood, and he had heard it a hundred times after, and every time the second sentence had landed on him as a thing he could not quite hold. Not because he did not carry. He carried. He had carried a young woman’s chart out of a review with a signature at the bottom of it and let the institution call a note he had not read a systems failure; he had carried a father’s crooked casket down the aisle of a church he had not been paying attention to; he had carried nine years of a boat he never bought. He carried the way a man carries. What he had trouble with, and what the woman in the file-voice had had trouble with before him, was the other thing: the thing that comes after the carrying, when a man has to say what he did in the room and remain in the room while he says it. She had asked him one careful question in a corridor and he had told her the note was not there, and she had looked at him for a long moment and let it go. She who demanded an account of every resident who came under her had withheld one from the person she loved like a son, and had never said why, and the sentence she had just spoken in the market carried that omission inside it the way a clamp carries a crack.

❦

The taste comes first: machine coffee burnt in a pot that has stood on a warmer since midnight, bitter enough to be a solid thing, and under it the lemon-sour edge of disinfectant that no mop has ever taken out of a bay. The ceiling light is a white rectangle in a low ceiling and makes no shadow, which is a thing the winter sky over the Clay Yard has never managed. The body on the table is a girl of twenty-two with a dressed scalp wound and a blood pressure that has not yet begun to fall, and the nurse holding pressure in the cavity with both hands is watching the clock and not his face, and he is already past the airway and into the question of the spleen. Daniel Marchetti stands where he has stood a thousand times, with his hands in a cavity and a resident waiting for the sentence that will tell him whether to open the chest. The bell goes on being a bell over the pigs and over the table. The clay yard is under the table and the pigs are under the ceiling light and the barley smoke goes up through a hospital that has no chimney, and none of it is a surprise and all of it is wrong. His hands are steady. His hands are four days old and shaking, small soft things with milky nails lying outside a fold of wool against a barrel stave. The resident waits. The ceiling light holds. The pig goes over the boards.

Then the square was only the square again. The light went out the way a lamp goes out when a door opens onto a yard, and the low winter sky came back over the roofs, and there was barley smoke and pig smell and the bell going on being a bell, and the boy in the basket with his cheek to a stave and the wool in his mouth. He did not tag the going. He had learned by now that the shift came and went without asking him, and that naming it would neither make it stay nor make it leave, and that the sway of it was its own kind of cold.

He reached for the pocket. It was the oldest gesture in him, older than the counting: a man going into a room where a decision waits, and the hand going to the coat to find the phone, and the phone lit in the pocket with a name on it, and the name not looked at. There had been a boat. Nine times he had promised her a day out on Lake Michigan, nine summers and nine promises, and the boat was never bought and the call was never made, and the weight of the unsent sentence was the thing that had made him portable. In Chicago the phone had been a small cold rectangle in his coat at Cook County Hospital, with the name lit on it, and he had never looked at it, not in the car park, not in the corridor, not going back into the van through the fire. Here there was no coat. There was no pocket. The hand went to the place where a pocket should have been and found the fold of wool and the curve of a stave and nothing else, and the fingers closed on nothing and came away. He had not looked at the name then and he could not look at it now, and the not-looking had not changed shape in two lives, only its size. In the bay the name had been a small lit thing in a pocket he could have reached. Here the whole world was the pocket and he was the thing in it, carried from room to room by hands that had no reason to open him and read what had been written on him.

He knew what a room does to a man who has to decide in it and then stay. It is a small knowledge and a hard one, and it is the whole of what the sentence in the file-voice had been trying to hand him for twelve years: decide, and then remain in the room afterwards, with the thing you decided still in front of you and no clean way to leave. He had decided in rooms and he had not stayed in them. He had let an institution call a chart what it wanted to call a chart and gone home to a refrigerator with four beers in it and a photograph he looked at every night without seeing. He had been good at deciding and bad at staying, and the woman with the file in her voice had known it and had said the second sentence to mend him, and had spent the rest of her life not quite mending herself with it.

Here the grammar had gone outward and the room had become a market, and the boy in the basket was neither the man who decided nor the man who carried. He was the thing carried. The first duty was performed on him by a woman who had decided without asking him and would not say what she had decided; the second was performed on him by a district that had read a figure over the top of him and set it on a slate and gone back to the pigs. Ostrowski’s sentence had come into the square and found nothing in the basket it could address, because the basket held a thing that could not decide and could not carry and could not lift a hand to the place where a pocket had been. The grammar of the bay and the grammar of the block were one grammar: decide, then carry, leave the deciding unnamed and call the carrying kindness. He set the two side by side the way he set his numbers and his flat notes, and did not make a fourth, and there was no word in Grathic for any of it and no need of one.

Sella was a half-pace off with her hand on the barrel rim and her face turned toward the pigs. She did not look down. When the wool rode back off his cheek she put it right without looking, the little-door burn closing on the fold and letting it go, and said something to the wind about the cold coming off the river, which was an instruction to a thing that could not answer and not a word to the boy at all. He knew the shape of it: a kindness given in a form that cannot be answered is a kindness that cannot be refused, and here it was given again, and there was no version of the world in which he could hand it back. The surgeon in him measured the pressure of her fingers through the wool, exact and unhurried, knowing what the thing would bear. The infant in him wanted the breast. Both were louder than the man who was trying to keep the count, and the count went on anyway, because counting was the only thing in the square that belonged to nobody and could not be entered on any slate.

❦

The market closed over the break the way water closes over a stone dropped in it, without a gap and without a ripple that anyone but the stone would call a ripple. A crust was broken at a stall and the soft inside of it showed steam in the dry cold. A woman called a price for turnips and another woman called a different price and neither cared that the two prices had nothing to do with the figure on the block. The slate lay where it had been set with the figure on it that would carry him whether he could read it or not, and The Hearth Drawing was two days off, and Low Kiln said the Five were three days off, and he kept a hundred and nine and four and four and a gap and the third flat note and a sentence about two duties, and none of it was a thing he could say out loud in a square that had already counted him.

A pig went over the boards with a weight behind it and was hauled up by the ear. A coin was rung twice on a stone. The boy’s small hand closed on nothing where a pocket should have been, and the market went on without a gap.

❦

The market at the Slate Row end went thin in layers, the way a kiln goes cold first at the door. The people who had business in the narrow street drifted toward it and left their smells behind them — barley, wet straw, the sour woodsmoke of a barrow standing with its shaft up — and what they left was not emptiness but the shape of what had just gone, which is a thing a man learns to read if he has ever watched a ward empty out after a shift and counted the beds that would not be filled again that night. Sella Vail set the basket down beside a post with a notice nail in it, not where the slate lay open, and the post gave a finger’s width of shade at the foot, the only shade at that end of the Cross. Corin lay with his cheek to the cold iron of the post and felt the market come through it in small tremors, the same tremors a kiln wall gives when the wheel takes a heavy piece, and counted the tremors because there was nothing else at that end of the square to count.

A table stood at the fringe where the Court’s business was done in the open air before it was carried into Slate Row, which the market spoke of as a direction and not a place that anybody at this end meant to go that day. A clerk sat behind it. He was a narrow man with a cuff gone soft at the wrist from much washing and ink in the creases of two fingers, and the skin of those fingers was the colour of other men’s fingers, which Dan noticed because he noticed hands before he noticed faces and was always relieved, in a country full of officers, when a hand looked ordinary. He had a pile of slates to one side and a rag for the chalk, and he worked with the unhurried care of a man who has entered a great many names and has never once been paid to hurry.

When a woman came to the table with a question in her face, the clerk put his hand on the top slate before he looked at her. It was not rudeness. It was the order in which the table had taught him to do things, the same order Alda Reed had kept in a room three doors up the yard, where her hand went to the birth-slate on the bench before it went to the child. Dan knew the order of reaching before he knew what was being reached for; he had learned it in four days the way other men learn a trade, by watching hands that did not know they were being watched. The woman’s question waited while the slate was set straight. Then the clerk looked at her and said, in a soft voice without any edge in it, “It’s entered as it stands. There’s nothing in it the household need correct.”

He did not say I. He did not say the district, the way the man on the block had said it; he said it and the household, and the it stood for a line of writing that had been put down somewhere before either of them had opened a mouth. He was an officer and he had a reason, and the reason was in the way he laid the rag down: a man who has to get a morning’s names into slates before the market wears out, and has found the quickest way is to touch the slate first and explain afterwards. Dan had made the same bargain once in a hospital in Chicago, in a room with a long table where the chart went under the hand before the patient’s face did, and had never thought of it as a bargain until he was on the other side of one. He set the thought with the others and did not make anything of it. The thoughts that came in threes were the ones to keep; the one that tried to be a fourth was usually his own.

The upper line on the slate was in a strange neat hand.

That was all he could have said about it in Grathic, and he could not have said it in Grathic, so he said nothing and read the shape instead. The marks were small and upright and very even, with the corners squared off the way a potter squares a lip before the fire, and there was a space between the first cluster and the second where the writing had stopped and started again. A stop, not a fault. The hand that made it had lifted and set down again in the same place each time, as if the writer had been counting and had put the count down between the strokes. Dan had seen that stop once at the edge of a flare light in the country he had come from, and once on a slate lying flat on a bench under a woman’s hand in a room where a child had just been born. Now he saw it a third time, in a market, on a line that had been set down before anybody there had thought to look at the face it belonged to.

He kept the three side by side the way he kept his numbers, and did not make a fourth. He could not read a word of the line. He could read a hand, which is a different thing and, in his trade, the older one: the pressure, the lift, the small deciding pause before a stroke that a man does not know he makes. The pause was the whole of what he had, and it was no more use to him here than a pulse in a throat two paces off would have been. Nobody at the table looked into the basket. Nobody in the square remarked on the line, or on the fact that it sat above the others as if it had been there before the day began, which was the way the district had of putting a thing where everyone could see it and nobody would think to ask about it. The cold got into his mouth under the wool; a thread of drool went from his lip to the fold and stayed. He tried to push the fold back with the hand that had once reduced a shoulder in the dark, and the hand was a small soft thing outside the basket with milky nails that closed on air an inch from his cheek and came away. He had put a thousand shoulders back. He could not keep a fold of wool on his own chin. The head went where it wanted, and past a certain point it took a small wet sound with it, and the sound got the wool put right by a hand that did not look and was gone again before he could be ashamed of it.

❦

Berr Clay came down the fringe from the direction of the moot, wiping his thumb on his apron before he reached the table, though he was not going to touch the slate at the table; he had touched his own slate that morning and had set it straight twice already, and the wipe was for the hand and not for the stone, which Dan understood better than most men in the square. The red wool cord of his office lay folded across his chest, and he took it off only when he went home, folding it into a drawer, so that a man could see the office coming before he saw the man. He smelled of hot clay and lead, and when he leaned down toward the basket to speak over Sella’s shoulder the lead came on his breath, sweet and a little sick, the taste of a man who had been tasting his own glaze since morning and had forgotten he was doing it. The care he spent on a clean slate was the care of a man who keeps a thing he could produce if he ever chose to; Dan had known men who kept such things, and knew the certainty in the hand before he knew what the hand held.

“Sella,” he said. “Your widow’s mark is confirmed by the factor at Greybank. That is a kindness, and kindness is entered.”

He said it the way he had said the figure at the block: low, and to the woman’s shoulder rather than to her face, with the grammar of the district inside it so that no part of the sentence held a man who could be answered. The factor at Greybank had confirmed it; the confirmation was a kindness; the kindness was entered. There was nobody in the sentence who had decided to be kind, and so nobody who could be thanked, and so no thanks that a woman could refuse. Dan had heard the shape of it before, in a room with good light and a window that would not open, from a man with a file under his arm who had said a chart was a chart and that nothing had happened in the room that the chart did not already know about. That’s what a chart is for. The sentence had come warm and reasonable and had closed a matter that had never been open, and Dan had carried it out of that room and into the rest of his life without once saying what it had done. Here it had gone outward and was standing in a market in a man’s mouth with lead on his breath. It was the first time he had heard the grammar outside his own head. He had kept it inside for two days. Now it had a voice and a green ring at the corner of a mouth, and it was no less reasonable for being in the open, which was the frightening part and the part he would have recognised anywhere, in either country.

Sella did not answer him about the mark. She stood with her hand on the post, and the little-door burn on the back of her left hand — two straight sides and a lintel and a threshold — closed on the iron and opened again, and she put her hand to the fold of wool at his chin instead, pulling it up over his ear without looking down at him.

“Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool, or to the post, or to the cold coming off the river. “It’s the river wind.”

That was the whole of her answer, and it was an instruction to a thing that could not answer back, given in the form she had used since she took him in: the house would keep him, the house did not explain, and the boy’s ears were to be covered because the wind was off the river. Berr stood a moment with his mouth open on the next part of what he had come to say, and Dan watched the not-saying happen the way he had once watched a resident keep his eyes on his shoes at a review of a chart. There was a space in the man’s sentence, and the space had a shape. It was the shape of a debt left where it lay, against a firing of tiles that had gone into the floor of this market years ago and had never been entered on the slate Berr kept so clean. His care not to call it was another kindness entered nowhere, for a debt uncalled can never be paid and never be settled and never be done with; it sits where it is as solid as the hollow seat in the house up the yard that Sella will not let anybody fill. Dan knew the shape of an uncalled thing. He had lived beside one for three days in a house where the ash in the corner was the thickest in Low Kiln, and he had left one of his own across an ocean in a coat with no phone in it, and set side by side they had the same shape, a shape with a gap where the fifth thing should have been.

Berr did not call the debt. He set his thumb to his apron again, one short dry stroke, and his eyes went to the cord at Sella’s throat where the widow’s mark lay under her collar — a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it, which she touched before she opened the kiln and at no other time — and he did not touch it and did not ask to see it. Then he straightened. “The figure stands as read,” he said. “There’ll be a slate at the watch if the house wants it again.”

He had come to confirm a kindness and he had confirmed it, and he went back toward the moot with the lead going off him and the red cord folded square, leaving the space where the debt was as carefully unentered as he had found it. Dan lay with his ears uncovered where Sella’s hand had put the wool and watched the man go, and knew the shape of what had passed between them, and had no word in Grathic for any part of it and nobody to say it to if he had.

❦

Alda Reed came to the edge of the market with a bundle of birth-slates under one arm and her grey wool shawl pulled tight across her shoulders, which was the way she wore it in every season. The little tin on its cord was inside her bodice, where she touched it when a question was asked and never opened it in company. She had been keeping the birth-slates since the market opened, setting them in a row on a bench at the fringe and turning the writing down when a mother came near, the way she turns a slate flat on the bench in a birth room before she reaches for the child. The slates were flat grey things with chalk in their creases, and she handled them with the steady hands of a woman who has lifted four hundred children and never dropped one or a slate.

She stopped beside the basket to set the bottom slate straight, and her hand went once to the tin inside her bodice, a small pressed movement that a man would not notice unless he had been watching for it, which Dan had been watching for it since the day he was born. He had seen her touch that tin in a birth room and again over a bundle going up the yard, and here was a third time, and it was not yet a number that meant anything, only a thing that happened when a question came near. There was no question now. The wool had ridden back off his wrist where Sella’s hand had pulled it at his ear, and the edge of the birth-flower showed there, the red branching end of the mark that ran down his forearm and stopped short of the joint — a flower’s edge the colour of a line drawn under a word.

Alda looked at it. Dan saw her look, and saw what her hand did before she knew she had done it: two fingers crossed at the corner of the mark, the sign her mother had made and she had made over four hundred children, and did not know she was making it in front of a market full of people. It was done and gone in the time it takes to settle a shawl, and then she covered it the way she covered everything, with a date.

“Stone seven,” she said, to nobody, to the slate, to the row of flat grey things under her arm. “The account went up on the eighth, before the paper went out. A child born on the seventh is entered on the eighth, and that’s the whole of it.”

The date arrived too fast, the way her dates always did, and it arrived with no question in the air to answer. Dan lay under it and did not move. Nobody at the bench commented on the sign or the date or the red edge at his wrist. A woman bargaining over short weight called a figure across the market and another woman called a different figure, and the two went on standing side by side without either troubling to make them agree. The Hearth Drawing was two days off, and Low Kiln said the Five were three days off, and the market went on arranging itself around the things it did not read, which was most of what was in it.

Sella put her hand back to the fold at his wrist. The little-door burn closed on the wool — lintel, threshold, two straight sides — and the fold went up over the red edge, and the hand was gone again as if it had never been there, which was the way she did everything, leaving no trace of the feeling and only the practical fact of a covered wrist. A pace away, at the Court’s table, the clerk set his hand on the slate and drew the rag across it and turned it straight for the next name. Her hand and his hand were both careful hands, both closing on the same child in two different accounts: the house’s private roll entered in a careful hand that would not be shown to a man come down from Slate Row, and the Court’s slate with its neat upper line set down before anybody had seen the face it belonged to. Neither account had been shown to him. Neither had asked whether he wanted to be entered in it. Dan lay with his cheek to the cold iron of the post and his own small hand outside the fold with milky nails, and tried to lift it toward the wool; the hand went up an inch and came down, and the two careful hands went on entering him on either side of a space he could not fill.

❦

The afternoon took the Cross apart in the order it had been put together. First the pigs went, and the smell of them went with them, thinning until the eel smell came up from the Fishstairs and lay over the barley smoke the way a wet cloth lies over a table. Stalls came down board by board; a woman folded a length of oiled paper around nothing and tied it with a string that had been tied and untied so often the paper kept the crease of it, the way a field keeps the furrow after the plough has gone. Corin lay where Sella Vail had left him, his cheek to the cold iron of the post, and heard the square lose its people the way a slip pit loses water when the feed is shut off, not all at once but by the lowering of a level, until the heavy things sit on the bottom and the light things go away with the water. He counted what went. Two women. A man with a pole. A boy carrying a crate on his head. A woman with a shawl over her hair. The counts went into the place where the other counts went, and not one of them was a door.

The last pig went over the boards with a weight behind it that he felt travel up through the iron of the post and into his cheek, and after it there was only a man washing the boards with a handful of straw and sluicing grey water from a bucket. At a stall two women stood over a heap of turnips with a bargain between them that had been going since morning and would not be settled before dark. One said a figure; the other said the figure was short by two; the first said two was nothing and the second said two was two, and they went on turning the turnips with their hands while the light changed and the dry cold got into the spaces under the awnings. Nobody called either woman wrong. The figures stood side by side on the heap as the figures had stood all morning, and the bargain went on being a bargain because neither of the two had ever intended it to be settled.

Mira Silt worked at the edge of the eel stall with her back to the slate on its crate and her face to the open square. Eel slime had dried on her sleeves in long pale streaks that had caught the dust of the morning and turned the colour of old bone; the top half of her left ear was gone, and the cold had got into the place where it had been so that she kept her head a little to the right, as if listening to something on that side. She had eel-bones loose in one hand and flicked one against her thumb as she worked, a small dry tick that came over the other noises of the market and went under them again. She did not look at the basket. She had not looked at it once all day, and the boy, who had spent four days learning the difference between kinds of not-looking, put this one where it belonged without knowing what it was for.

“What’s the eel price, Thane,” she said, “two marks the pair or two marks the pair and two short? Don’t trouble yourself. It’s two marks the pair and two short, and I’ve counted them twice, and I’ll count them again after I’ve eaten.”

Berr Clay stood with the red cord folded across his chest and the green at the corner of his mouth, and did not answer, because she had left him nothing in the sentence that wanted an answer. He had come to confirm a kindness that morning in a voice with nobody in it, and here was a woman asking a question that had already swallowed its own reply. She moved an eel-bone from her left hand to her right and said a figure under her breath, and moved another. The boy counted the bones moving and not what they were for. It was not a slate. That was the whole of what he could have said about it in Grathic, and he kept it beside the other things that had the same shape and did not join them.

When she turned to go she put the side of her hand against a loose board lying across the path where the barrow men would take it and shoved it clear, brisk, with her face still toward the eels, and went on talking as if she had moved nothing. The jolt of it came up through the post into the basket. It was the way she did everything that had to be done before anybody could make a speech about it. A thread of drool went from his lip to the fold and stayed through the whole of the afternoon, and there was nobody in the square who would have wiped it and nobody he could ask to.

There were two ways of not looking at a basket, and he had cause to know both. The man on the block that morning had not looked because the figure he was reading had nothing to do with what lay under the wool, and a man who will not look at a thing has already decided what it is worth. This woman had not looked because she knew exactly what lay under the wool and had arranged her whole face, and the better part of her body, so that no part of the market would be given the chance to look at it either. One not-looking priced him. The other kept him. He could read the pressure of both and had no word in the language of the square for either, and no person in the square to say it to.

❦

There was a second reading for the households that had come late, or for those that wanted the figure said again where they could hear it with their own ears. The crowd was thinner now and did not lean. It had leaned once and taken what it came for, and a number that has been heard is a lighter thing to carry than a number that is still wanted, so the square went on with its own business while Berr wiped his thumb on his apron, one short dry stroke on each side, and set two fingers to the stone. He wiped it for the hand and not for the slate. The slate was clean. There was nobody in the thinning market who would have cared if it had not been, and the boy, who had watched a man in a room with good light wipe the rim of a glass before he put it to his mouth, knew that the wipe was not for anyone’s benefit but for the man’s own sense of how a thing ought to be handled.

“One in twelve, as the notice says,” Berr said.

He said it low, as he had said it in the morning, and the people at the far edge of the square who had not heard it the first time heard it now and went on with what they were doing. A woman with a basket of turnips put the figure into the same place she had put it three hours ago and did not look up. A man who had been waiting to have a pig weighed went on waiting. Nobody said the figure was wrong, and nobody said it was right; the levy went on being spoken around all morning the way a poor room speaks around a hole in the wall, and the second hearing of it lay down in the square as flat as the first.

Then Berr set the slate down on its block. It gave its small flat answer, a dry knock with no body behind it, the shadow of the full note stopping at the surface of the stone and going no further. Daniel Marchetti had heard that sound from a cup across a birth room and from a pot on a bench under a roof that did not ask where his memory came from, and once that morning from this same slate, and now a fourth time from the same stone under the same careful hand. He kept the four hearings side by side the way he kept his numbers and did not make them agree. There was no word in Grathic for the sound and no need of one, and nobody in the square tapped a slate for him. The knuckle that would have done it lay outside the fold of wool with milky nails on it, and went up an inch and came down, and there was no surface within two paces of it that it could have reached.

❦

A barrow went out through the north side of the square with its shaft down and two men at it. What was in it was the Ash, grey and ordinary, heaped a little above the sides in the middles where the load had shifted on the way across the market. One of the men stopped to pull a board out of the path and the other went on, and the barrow went on after him, and nobody in the square looked at it twice. It was the afternoon’s work going out toward the north gate and the Bone Orchard, the same work that went out every market-day, and there was no more to see in it than in a pot carried to the sink.

He looked at it, which was the difference between him and everybody else in the square. The grey in the barrow was the grey of the ridge along the skirting board under the Vail house, near two thumbs wide, turning the corner without a break, which Sella had not swept in two years and would not let anybody sweep. The same colour. The same plain fact of a thing left where it lay or carried away. He put the barrow with the ridge and the ridge with the charred edge of the slate in the kiln ash, and the three lay together in the place where he kept facts that nobody had explained and nobody had asked him to understand. He had no word for what the grey was, and it did not occur to him to want one. He had learned by now that the things nobody named in this district were the things the district had the most practice at carrying out of sight.

The bell went while the barrow was passing. It went on longer than a church bell would have gone and nobody crossed himself at it, and it was the only sound in the square that wanted nothing from him. He counted the strokes because there was nothing else to count that belonged to him, and the count came out four, four, and a gap where the fifth should have been. Four, four, and a gap. It was the shape of the kiln’s breathing through the wall of a house three doors up the yard, and the shape of the nails in the floor where he had lain before he had ever been carried anywhere, four on the near board and four on the next and a knot the width of a thumb where the fifth should have been. He put the bell beside the kiln and the kiln beside the floor and let them lie. He did not make them agree and he did not ask what the gap was for. The gap was the part he kept. It was the only part of any count that nobody had thought to enter.

When the last of the stalls had gone, the number of days to the Five was given out as three. It was given out in a voice that had counted what it had and did not want to say it, the way Low Kiln had been saying three since the ice came, and the square took it the way it had taken the figure at the block: flat, without echo, and went on with the business of going home. Three was a number like the others, a hundred and nine and eight and the two Dessa had counted out and back, a number that could not be spent and could not be argued with and was not his. The Hearth Drawing was two days off. He had the date by heart the way he had the shape of the post — Stone 12, and two days between it and the morning’s date — and nobody in the thinning square had said what the date was for, or what would be drawn, or from whom. Two days, and three days, and the whole district going home to its fires with the numbers lying where they had fallen.

Sella lifted the basket off the post. The move went through his belly before it went through his ears, and his head went where it wanted as she rose, and for half a breath he saw the length of the square emptied of people, the block with the slate lying small on it behind him, the boards washed and stacked, the pale ring on the barrel where a hundred mornings of cups had come and gone. Then the wool took his cheek again and the sight was gone, and there was no way to turn his head far enough to get it back without the small wet sound coming up in his throat. The fold had ridden back at his ear and she put it right without looking, the little-door burn closing on the wool and letting it go, and said something to the wind about the river, which was an instruction to a thing that could not answer and not a word to him.

She turned back up the road toward the Clay Yard with no word about where they had been or what had been read over the top of him. The market emptied behind them. The slate lay on its block with its second flat answer going into the noise of people taking down their stalls, and the barrow went on toward the north gate with its grey load and no one reading it, and the bell went on being a bell over both. Four, four, and a gap. He carried the new number up the yard with the hand outside the fold unable to lift to any of it, and Low Kiln said three days to the Five without saying what it had counted to make three, and the wool smelled of woodsmoke as the house with the banked coals came back into view, and its door did not quite shut.

The Chronicle of Chapter 3

On the tenth stone of the year Sella Vail carried the boy out of the only warm room he knew, a basket on her hip, wool over his face. Behind them the kiln took one breath and missed the next, and the gap went with him. She put the fold back without looking and said the house had brought him, and the house, and nothing finer. He counted a hundred and nine steps to the road and kept the number where no tax could reach it. The market came up in layers --- pigs, clay dust, a roar, a bell that was not a church bell.

At the Cross two hundred people wanted a figure. Berr Clay wiped his thumb on his apron before touching the clean slate and said one in twelve, as the notice says, low enough that the square leaned in. He did not say I; the assessor added only that the figure stood as read. He heard his own kind priced above him and could not turn his head to see who priced them. Set down, the slate gave a flat knock --- the third in four days, after a cup and a Vail pot. He joined none of the three. Nobody tapped a slate for him.

Beside a barrel, a voice arrived that had not been in the square a moment before: Marchetti, you’re back on in ten; the first duty is to decide, the second to carry it. For one breath the pigs and the trauma bay shared an air. He reached for a pocket with no phone in it, and the market closed over without a gap. Here he was not the man who decided or carried but the thing carried; the grammar of the bay and the block were one grammar.

At the Slate Row end a clerk touched a slate before he looked at the woman waiting; its upper line was in a strange neat hand with a small stop, seen for the third time. Berr told Sella her widow’s mark was confirmed at Greybank, a kindness, and kindness was entered; the debt against the tiles he left uncalled. Sella answered the wool. Alda Reed touched the tin at her breast, crossed two fingers at the flower’s red edge on his wrist without knowing it, and covered the sign with a date. Two careful hands entered one child in two accounts; neither asked him.

In the afternoon the market came apart. Mira Silt asked the eel price and answered it herself, never looking at the basket. A second reading gave a fourth flat answer; Ash went out in a barrow toward the north gate, and the bell counted four, four and a gap. Three days were given to the Five; the Hearth Drawing was two. Sella lifted the basket and turned up the yard without a word, wool smelling of woodsmoke, the door not quite shutting. He carried back numbers he could not spend and no hand to lift toward them.
