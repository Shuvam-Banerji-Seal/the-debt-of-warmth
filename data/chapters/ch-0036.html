Nine at the Teeth

Days 147–149 · Reed 28–30, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, the Weir and Sill

Nine times a word may come to the teeth and go back, and nine is a keeping still and not a payment. The last market sets every withholding in one square and reads a figure that does not know the hand that did not come back; what the square cannot count it calls weather, and what it cannot read it carries home in a coat.

— A proverb of Low Kiln, said at the last market of Reed

— Day 147 —

Reed 28, Y.H. 813 (Eighthday)

The reed month had three days left and nobody in Low Kiln was keeping them. It would go out as it had come in, without a thumb laid on a slate to say here and no further, and the last market stood two days off beyond the bend of the road and the third Account-day three. Sella Vail took the boy down in the early cold at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, woodsmoke going before her up the clay road and the river wind coming up to meet it, the two crossing between the houses the way two people pass on a stair in the dark, each with a hand on the wall and neither speaking. Keep it off his ears, she told the wool; it had come all the way down with them and would go all the way back up before the light was gone; and she gave nothing more to the wind, having said it once, because a wind takes an instruction and carries it away and there is an end of it. The instruction went to the wool and to the river wind and never to the basket. One hundred and forty-seven days old. Four months and twenty-seven days. Neither number was spoken under any roof that morning, and nobody in the yard had a word that would hold either one still long enough for a woman passing with a pot to set it down.

The night’s cold lay in the ruts under a skin thin as the fat skimmed from a pot, and the wool at his ear had taken the river wind all the way down and was damp against his cheek. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had wiped it; nobody under any roof had been given an instruction about the thread, and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. From the low angle he saw the hems of coats and the backs of hands and the mud worked grey into the creases of wrists, and not one face. The half-pace was measured from slates the way a surgeon measures a field from the one thing in it that must not be touched; it was not a slow pace and not a hurried one, but a distance kept. He knew the order in which cold arrives — cheek, temple, wrist, the wool riding back from the small bare joint — as certainly as he had once known the order in which a man’s breathing goes over when the core is going, and the knowing was of the old life and the want was of this body, and the want was louder. He wanted the wool up over his ear. He could not lift it. The two facts stood side by side, and neither cancelled the other, and neither could be entered anywhere.

The cutters had been downriver since before first grey and were coming back up with the last of the cutting across their shoulders, armfuls laid against the cold the way a woman lays a child against her coat. They came around the pans set out to dry and the trough by the well with its skin of ice, and the ice was called weather and left where it lay. At the edge of the worked strips Sella set the basket down level, as a pot is set down where it must not be slopped, and stood a half-pace from every open slate. The grey reeds stood in the ruts in long ranks, green at the broken ends and pale where the frost had bitten the tips, and nobody counted them. Behind the drying racks the river went over its stones with the note of water over water that was a fifth low thing and never one of the hearings; he left it where it sounded and did not make it a hearing to fill a place. There was tallow in the air from a pot scoured near the fire, and wet wool, and the Ash-dry mud that clung to nothing so well as to a working cuff.

A boot had set a bundle down in the mud and the mud kept the print and gave it back slowly, the way bone that has been set gives back the order of the breaking to a man who knows where to look. What it told was what had been done and in what order, and never what the doing cost. From the basket there were hems and hands and no faces, which was the whole of the world at that height.

A cutter come up from the reed beds with a bundle on his shoulder wiped his palms down his thighs before he set it down. A girl with a creel stood measuring it with her eyes, finding the place her hands would go, the way a woman finds the place on a pot before she lifts it.

“Lift it,” the cutter said. “Feel where the weight settles. Your arm’ll tell you what went into it, and that’s the whole of what it knows.”

She got it up with the green ends shivering and the pale tips brushing her coat, and the creel bumped her hip as she turned. “And what’ll it know about the hands that cut it?”

He was a dozen paces down with another armful before the answer came over his shoulder. “Nothing. Ask it how heavy it is. That’s a question it can answer.”

A man come up from the pans with a strip of reed in his hand put the older question to a cutter going past: what the hands would be paid now that the month was near its end. The cutter did not break his step.

“The weather and the river,” he said. “Ask them. They’ll tell you when they’ve had enough.”

The man shut his mouth on the next part and went back to his pans. No figure went down the yard with him and no figure came back. The boy kept the exchange on a place of its own, because a cost asked in the form of a direction and answered with a direction leaves no entry for any thumb to read.

It was the last work of the reed month done in the ordinary way, with no more ceremony than a pot set out to dry. Women worked the near rows with their skirts kilted and their forearms grey to the wrist, turning wet pots out of the shade and setting them where the light fell; the light went along the rims and came off them, and no woman called it a fine morning. Two men went past with a barrow that had lost its easy rolling in the flood, and neither put a number to what the barrow held. A girl at the slip pits lifted her hands out of a pan to show the woman beside her that they were clean, and the woman looked and went back to her own pan. The work was honest and complete and went on being worth nothing to the one person in the yard who could have read it best.

The yard went around its three places and its one empty place as it had gone around them every morning of the month. At the Vail corner Ash had turned and left a clean dark line behind it, and beside the line lay the new finer hairline of grey the house called the thaw, thinner and paler than the old mark, gathering where nobody had put a broom. Sella carried past with her shoulder tilted and both lines where they lay, gave the broom no instruction for them and swept neither. Farther on she went wide around the space beneath the lowest rack where the broken birth-slates lay, as wide as a woman goes around a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and did not look into the space and did not enter it. And past that, in the returning line where the hands came back at dusk, was a place the yard went around with a look and a wide step. A hand had gone in there on Seed 15 and had not come out with the rest on Seed 27. No name had come up, no body, no cause, no figure, and no hand had come up to be counted; a slate would be found and a figure made if one came up in a month or a year, and nothing had come up.

He had seen the going-around in the planting, in the flood and through the cutting, and each time it was the same piece of work performed carefully and without comment: an eye laid on the place, a shoulder turned, a step taken wide enough that no heel came near it, and then the ordinary work resumed as though nothing had been avoided. He had learned to read an omission by the care spent on the surface around it, the way a man reads a wound by the cleanliness of the field about it; here the care was spent in a step, and the step was the whole of the record, and the record was a direction and not a figure. He did not lay that empty place under the empty space at the rack or under the gaps on the loaf shelf; an absence in a line of hands is an absence in a line of hands until somebody puts a name to it, and nobody in the yard had put one in a year.

A reed-cutter came along the boards past the basket with a bundle and the short length of grey cord carried inside his coat, the wool or reed-fibre gone the colour of a stitch-hole a thread has been drawn through a dozen times, which is the grey a working thumb leaves and the colour of use and nothing more. He set the bundle down where no slate lay, straightened the cord on his thumb and turned it once so the knots stood in the small light; he tied one low with his teeth and a pull of the thumb, bit the end off and put the cord back inside his coat, drawing the coat closed over it with the flat of his hand. Nobody asked him what it held and he counted nothing where anybody could hear him count. The boy let the cord lie where it lay. A cord on a board is a cord on a board; a mark under the loaf shelf is a mark under a shelf; an empty place in a line of hands is neither of these. He did not lay the cord beside the charcoal under the shelf, or beside the empty place the yard had just gone around, or beside Mira’s two cords, one of which had never been carried upriver to Nine Pots or lifted at a door, or beside Orra Weir’s knot-string on its own board below the sill. He left them where they had been put, unread and not joined, as he had left them on the days before and would leave them on the days after.

The carrying arm was the record he could read best and the one that told the least. It knows what it has carried and never what the carrying cost: how often it lifted, how the weight sat, which shoulder took the longer armful when the wind came upriver, and never what the lifting took out of the woman who lifted. In the life he had left, a chart told what had been done to a body and never what the body had paid to go on standing; here a forearm told the same and kept the same silence, and there was no word in the district for the difference between them. Exact, complete, worth nothing to a thumb looking for a figure. The yard paid its last week’s cut in armfuls and weather and questions answered with directions, and there was no entry for a Court thumb to read because there was no entry. The last bundles going out of the reeds were an ending the whole yard could see and nobody would date; his one hundred and forty-seven was an ending nobody could see and a number nobody would speak, and it went on exact and unspent in a body that could not lift a hand to lay it down.

He noted the order of it again without deciding to. The hand went to the record before the hand went to the person: in a woman turning her shoulder half a beat ahead of her gaze, in a cutter feeling the weight settle before he looked at the girl who had asked, in a man shutting his mouth on a figure before he looked at the face of the man who had asked for one. He had first seen that order in a room whose walls he did not carry with him now, where a hand went to the chart before any eye went to the face on the table; here it wore the clothes of a yard, and wore them so completely that no woman in it would have known she was doing it. The surgeon’s register noted the order and had no standing to enter it anywhere, which was the whole of what the register had been able to do since he came into this body.

The morning went on into midday without anybody marking it, as the month had gone out without anybody marking it. Sella shifted the basket a hand’s breadth out of the draught and gave the instruction to the wool and to the running water again, and never to the basket. She went around the pan set out to dry and went around the rack space again on the way back, and did not touch the widow’s mark, because a yard is not an opening and a basket is not a door.

The competence was the whole of the helplessness: he could read a carrying arm the way another man reads a chart, and he could not lift a hand to the bundle the arm had carried. There was no dishonesty in the reading and no use in it, and he held both facts in one breath without either cancelling the other. He wanted the bundle two paces off, the pot under the roof, the place in the returning line that had been given a look and a wide step; he could not reach any of them. The cutter went back up the boards with the bundle counted only in the arm that carried it, and his coat closed over the uncounted cord as he went. The boy’s small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water that came from behind the racks, and came down against the fold. The last bundles stood grey in the ruts with nobody counting them, and one hundred and forty-seven went on exact and unspent with nobody’s thumb upon it, the last market two days off and the month going out without a boundary drawn.

Under the Vail kiln-house the midday came down through the oiled paper in a brown sheet that stopped at the floor and went no farther, so that the far corner kept its darker air to itself and the bench kept the shadow it had always kept. Sella Vail came in from the yard with the basket held level against her hip, the way a woman carries a pot filled to the brim and will not have it slopped, and the boy lay with his cheek to the fold and the small wet sound coming and going as his head wandered and was not set right. Woodsmoke went in before her and caught in the beams; under it lay the dry warm smell of the loaf shelf, crust and ash and old baking that was almost a warmth in itself, and under that again, for one breath and then folded away, the clean bitter smell of charcoal from beneath the board. A basket set down is a pot set down and left.

She set him low near the bench leg, at the angle at which the underside of a board becomes a roof to a thing lying on the floor while a woman standing sees only the top of it. The cold iron of the bench bracket was at his ear. Keep it off his ears, she told the wool; it had come all the way down with them from the reeds and would go all the way back up before the light was gone. She told the pot not to stand near the door, and the broom to go around, and the fire it was the thaw, which it was and would go when it would go. The instruction went to the wool and the pot and the broom and the fire and never to the basket. One hundred and forty-seven days old, four months and twenty-seven days; neither number was spoken under the roof, and there was no vessel in the house that would have held either one still long enough for a woman to set it down.

Above him the shelf kept its two unnamed gaps, in the room the fold did not touch. There was the old space at the row’s end, which had stood through every morning of his life and which nobody had decided what to call; it was said of the loaf-shelves of Low Kiln that two gaps stay two gaps until a finger lays them together and an account then wants a name, and no finger in the district had laid these together. And there was the palm’s breadth that had come in on the night of Seed 6 with no scrape, no crumb, no print and no word — a loaf’s worth gone off a board between dusk and light and no woman saying by whom. Two gaps. A third would have been somebody’s doing, and nobody in the house had done it, and the saying went around the houses the way a pot goes from hand to hand and was never carried into this room and set down.

In the other room, on the bed he was not lying on, one stiff brown-edged crust lay two inches from the place his ear would have been, with its empty place beside it still unnamed. He had wanted it since morning. He could not reach the fold. The wanting and the not reaching sat together in him without either cancelling the other, as the adult’s knowing sat beside the infant’s appetite without either being asked to give way; the knowing was older and the appetite was louder, and they lay in the same small body like two instruments in one case, one of which he could not lift. The two rooms of the house did not touch. The shelf’s two absences and the fold’s one were kept apart by the whole width of a wall and by a woman who had never once carried a saying from one to the other.

A neighbour came up from the strips with a pot in her hand that she set down wrong, near the door where Sella had told the pot not to stand. It was the same woman who came up most days with the wet sleeves and the question ready; her wrists were grey to the creases with the slip water of the near rows, and she had the look of a woman who had already decided what answer she would carry away and had merely come to collect it.

“What’ll the row want before the last market?” she said to the shelf, or to the board, or to Sella’s back — it was hard from the floor to tell which she had addressed, and the answer came from the same place whichever it was.

Sella’s eyes went once to the clay jar standing cold under its glaze at the shelf’s far end. A jar cold under its glaze is a jar cold under its glaze, and an answer to nobody. “Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there,” she said. “The house has said it.”

The woman stood a moment with her mouth still arranged around the next part, then took her pot up level, the way a woman carries a thing filled to the brim, and went out with the question unanswered and nothing slopped. The cost had been asked in the form of a direction and answered with a direction, and the answer left no entry for any thumb to read. The boy kept the exchange on a place of its own beside the yard’s weather and river and did not make a row of them and call it a rule. A question carried away level is still a question carried away, and a house that has said it has said nothing that a slate could take up.

Sella’s lips went on moving along the row. She stood with her back to the basket and counted the loaves the way another woman might count beads, without touching any of them and without arriving anywhere; the row kept its shape and gave back no figure. Then, still with her back to him and still moving her lips, she reached a finger beneath the front edge of the board where the charcoal tally lay. The little-door burn on the back of her hand closed on the lip of the wood and opened again as the finger went under. She held it a breath out and a breath back — the way a woman feels along the underside of a shelf for a thing she has put there and knows is there — and brought it away. She did not look at what the finger had touched. She did not wipe the finger. She did not go back to the shelf.

From the floor the boy saw the hand go under and come back and saw nothing of what the marks said. He knew the tally was there: short grouped strokes of different darkness pressed into the underside of the board, some hard enough to have left a smooth track where the stick dug, some the colour of old smoke and gone nearly into the grain at the row’s end. It was a keeping put where nobody thought to look for one, and a keeping hidden that carefully tells more by the care of the hiding than the writing would, which was a thing he had learned and could not spend. He did not count the strokes. He did not press a thumb where her thumb had been. Such a hand, going under a board without looking, either knows the board by heart or has been told not to look, and the reason stood between the two and would not come down on either side; from the basket the knuckle going under and the hand coming away would have looked the same for a woman who knew and a woman who had been warned. He had once been able to tell the difference in a room where a hand went into a field before anybody said what the field was, and the telling had bought him nothing then either. A hand’s knowing is not a thing a slate will take, and a warning is not a cause, and the space between the two was a place he could hold open and nothing more.

He did not lay the charcoal under the board across the two gaps above it. He did not lay it across the one stiff crust two inches from his ear in the other room. He did not lay it across the place in the returning line the yard had gone around that morning with a look and a wide step, or across the cord in the cutter’s coat, or across any of the other keepings carried about the district in sleeves and under aprons and on boards turned inward. The two rooms of the house did not touch, and no third gap came. the widow’s mark hung where it hung at her throat, a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and she did not touch it; she touched it before she opened the kiln and at no other hour, and an afternoon under a shelf is not an opening and neither is a basket or a pot told the weather.

The order of it was the thing he could read without being able to use. Her hand had gone to the record before her eyes went to the row and before anybody’s eyes went to the basket — the same order he had watched in a yard shoulder turning half a beat ahead of a yard gaze, in a cutter feeling the weight settle before he looked at the girl who had asked, in a man shutting his mouth on a figure before he looked at the face that had asked for one. He had learned it in a room where a hand went to the chart before anybody looked at the face on the table, and here it was again under a shelf, performed by a woman who would have said the house had done it. He had no standing in the order and could not enter any part of it. An inventory is not a key, and a man who cannot lift a board cannot use what he has catalogued.

At the hearth she told the fire it was the thaw again, which it was, and turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and fed them. “The house will keep it,” she said where another woman would have said I. The fire answered with its four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, and she did not count the pulls aloud and did not ask anybody to hear the silence, and its payer had gone unnamed for a hundred and forty-seven days. The warmth came through the floor under blind ash and went into the boards and into the boy’s wrist by degrees; he knew the order in which a body takes heat back — the small joint first, the sting and the itch of it, the core last — and knew too that a warmth arriving without its being named is a warmth nobody can be thanked for, and that the almost-smile of a warming extremity is not peace but the afterdrop waiting past it. He kept the payer off the slate because there was no slate in the district that would have taken the entry and no voice under the roof that would have spoken it.

She stood a moment with the back of the broken pot in her hand, counting something under her breath toward no opening. Then she pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn, the grey streak at her temple showing pale off the coals, and set the pot where it could be found in the dark — rim out, the handle standing two inches beyond the reaching hand that would have closed on it and did not. She did not look toward the bed. She did not say whose fire it was, or whose child lay in the basket, or whose count went on under the shelf. The household straightened around her silences the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted, and nobody under the roof asked what her finger had found, and nobody under the roof had given a name to the room off the shed or said it was cold. Two silences stood under one roof without a word passing between them, as they had stood for a hundred and forty-seven days.

The afternoon went off the boards without anybody marking it, as the morning had gone into midday without anybody marking it. The brown sheet drew in toward the hearth and left the far corner to its own darker air; the oiled paper held its last and let go; somewhere along the yard a pan was set down and a voice went out with the reeds and did not come back. Under the roof the woman’s shadow went up the wall and stood there while her hands went on with the small work of the house, and the boy watched the shadow and not the face, which was the whole of the world at that height. Nothing within two paces could be reached: not the pot with its handle turned out, not the broom, not the shelf above him, not the clay jar cold under its glaze, not the marks on the underside of the board where her finger had been. The small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water that came through the finger’s-width door and came down against the fold. He did not count the strokes under the shelf. He did not lay the two gaps together. He did not make a cause of a woman’s habit, or a doctrine of a keeping, or a payer of a silence.

The pot stood rim out with its handle two inches beyond the small hand that would have closed on it and did not. The charcoal waited under the shelf, unread, for a hand that was not his and a day that had not come. One hundred and forty-seven lay where the day had left it, exact and complete and entered nowhere, and the last market stood two days off across a district that would read a figure into the air over the basket and go home believing it had paid something.

The afternoon gave out under the Vail roof without anybody marking it, the way the reed month was giving out without a boundary drawn at its going; the brown sheet of light drew in toward the hearth and left the far corner to its own darker air, and Sella Vail took the basket up from its place by the bench leg. The boy lay with his cheek to the fold and the small wet sound coming and going as his head wandered and was not set right. Keep it off his ears, she told the wool; it had come all the way down with them in the morning and would go all the way back up before the night was out, and then she gave nothing more to the wind, having said it once, because a wind takes an instruction and carries it away and there is an end of it. The instruction went to the wool and never to the basket. Woodsmoke went before her up the clay road and the river wind came up to meet it, the two passing in the dark between the houses the way two people pass on a stair without speaking, and neither gave the other a figure for the passing.

Halfway up she went around a pan set out to dry, tilting her shoulder and the basket with it so the pan stayed where the pan stood and nothing knocked; and at the well she went around the trough with its skin of ice thin as a skim of fat set aside in a dish, and did not break the skin to get at the water underneath. Weather, the neighbours had called it all through Reed, and it would go when it would go. She gave the instruction to the bucket, which stood where a bucket stands and wanted nothing from her. He had learned before he could hold up his own head that a thing called weather is a thing nobody has agreed to count, and that a man who wants a figure from weather is a man who will be given a direction instead. Two days off, the last market stood, and the reed month had three days left and would not say so; the house kept its days in loaves pushed along a board and glances at a clay jar, and no vessel under the roof would have held a hundred and forty-seven still long enough for a woman to set it down.

At the yard she went wide around the space beneath the lowest rack, where the broken birth-slates lay, as wide as a woman goes around a pail of hot water set down where a child might knock it — a step taken for the thing’s sake and not for her own, and not explained to anybody, least of all to the child in the basket, who could not have knocked over a cup if he had been set beside one. The Ash in the corner lay where it had lain: the clean dark line where the ridge had turned and turned again, and beside it the new finer hairline of grey gathered since, both called the thaw all through Reed and neither swept, and the name had not made either go. Beyond the ruts the last bundles stood grey in the dark where the afternoon had left them, green at the broken ends and pale where the frost had bitten the tips, and nobody had counted them since the light went. The mud kept the print of the boot that had set them down and gave it back slowly, and from the low angle he read the print the way a man reads a bone that has been set and is healing: what was done, and in what order, and never what it cost.

Under the Vail roof she lifted him out of the basket without once looking into it, the way a woman lifts a pot by the handle and does not look inside because looking will not change what is in it. She set him on the bed and not in the Debt Loft, setting him down as one sets a pot down where it will not be slopped — the base first, the weight settled, the hand kept where it is until the thing is sure of its place. Then she put the fold right at his ear and under his chin, and did not once look at his face. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the cold and she left it frozen and unwiped, because nobody under any roof in Low Kiln had been given an instruction about the thread, and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. Her hand rested a half-beat longer than the setting needed before it went away. She had learned that half-beat in the winter she had sat up three nights with a child of her own who cooled while everybody was kind, and had never told anybody what she had learned, and the house had straightened around the silence every time since. Then the hand went away and she did not go back to it.

She told the fire it was the thaw, which it was, and would go when it would go. She turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and fed them and said “The house will keep it” where another woman would have said I. The fire gave its four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, and she did not count the pulls aloud and did not ask anybody to hear the silence, and its payer had been unnamed now for a hundred and forty-seven days. She stood a moment with the back of the broken pot in her hand, counting something under her breath toward no opening. She pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn, the grey streak at her temple showing pale off the coals, and set the pot where it could be found in the dark, rim out and the handle toward the reaching hand — the handle standing two inches beyond the hand that would have reached for it and would not close on it — and did not look toward the bed.

The widow’s mark hung where it hung at her throat, a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and she did not touch it. She touched it before she opened the kiln and at no other hour, and a dusk is not an opening. She went to her own bed without explaining the count and without looking toward the bed where the boy lay, and the two rooms of the house did not touch.

In the small hours he turned out his file the way a man lays instruments on a towel before a case begins, each thing going into its own hollow and nothing resting across anything else. He had kept it a hundred and forty-seven days in a body that could not have lifted a spoon from the towel, and the keeping of the things apart was the only work in the room that no slate would enter.

Six hearings of the full note lay beside one another and never among them: a cup across a birth room, a pot under the wheel-shed wall, the district’s slate twice at the Cross, a man giving at the Drawing, and the small flat knock out of a woman as the men lifted, that last from a person and filed beside the five and never counted with them. No seventh had come. The bell was a told bell and a heard bell, kept in two places; the wheel and the pots under the roof were notes he had left where they sounded; the clerk’s slate was a figure he could not read. He had not invented one to fill the empty place. An empty place kept honestly is a record of a kind, and a man who fills one to make a row come out even is a man with a reason, and he had learned two lives ago to be afraid of the shape of a reason in a man with clean hands.

Three near-full answers stood on their own tray, advanced and not paid: a cup across a birth room, a pot under the wheel-shed wall, a rack pot lifted a hand’s breadth too high and set down again, and Jorra Vail’s knowing of a pot’s note withheld from the kiln-master who said it sang before its time. Each had wanted a following stroke that nobody had made, and he had not made it for any of them; a knowing held back is not a hearing given, and the knowing went to the tray and not among the six.

The older intervals lay where they had always lain: the half-beat behind a standing finger, the stroke that had gone on over the flagstones without the check, his own held breath from the market, the unchecking stroke come up beside the half-beat with no cause written across them.

And on the same tray, in the place he had made for the stoppings and had widened by one since the night before, lay eight of them — Reed 5, Reed 8, Reed 11, Reed 14, Reed 17, Reed 20, Reed 23 and now Reed 26 — eight objects and not a rule, eight intervals and not eight payments. The eighth had come two nights before at this hearth, brought all the way to the word that would call the loan by a man telling the kindling that six were counted and the next he would not count; the sentence about an ear under this roof being worth more than the debt that holds it had gone to the threshold of the word and stopped there, with no figure and no name, and the breath that should have gone in at the top of the reach had stayed where it was. He had filed it on the tray and not among the hearings, because a stopping is not a hearing and a held word is not a paid one. He held all eight apart by hand, because he had no other hand to hold them with. Sella Vail took the basket up from the market’s edge when the shade had come down onto the shade, woodsmoke going before her along the clay road and the river wind coming up to meet it, the two crossing between the low houses the way two people pass on a stair in the dark, each with a hand on the wall and neither speaking. Keep it off his ears, she told the wool; it had come all the way down with them and would go all the way back up before the night was out, and she gave nothing more to the wind, having said it once, because a wind takes an instruction and carries it away and there is an end of it. The instruction went to the wool and to the river wind and never to the basket.

One hundred and forty-nine days old. Four months and twenty-nine days. Neither number was spoken under any roof in Low Kiln that evening, and there was no vessel in the district that would have held either one still long enough for a woman passing with a pot to set it down. The house kept its days in loaves pushed along a board and glances at a clay jar, and the count he carried had no handle and no rim and nobody who would have known what a number was to say it over.

Halfway up she went around a pan set out to dry, tilting shoulder and basket together so the pan stayed where the pan stood and nothing knocked; and at the well she went around the trough with its skin of ice thin as a skim of fat set aside in a dish, and did not break the skin to get at the water underneath. Weather, the neighbours had called it all through Reed, and it would go when it would go. She gave the instruction to the bucket, which stood where a bucket stands and wanted nothing from her. He had learned before he could hold up his own head that a thing called weather is a thing nobody has agreed to count, and that a man who wants a figure from weather is a man who will be given a direction instead. At the yard she went wide around the space beneath the lowest rack, where the broken birth-slates lay, as wide as a woman goes around a pail of hot water set down where a child might knock it — a step taken for the thing’s sake and not for her own, and not explained to anybody, least of all to the child in the basket, who could not have knocked over a cup if he had been set beside one. The Ash in the corner lay where it had lain: the clean dark line where the ridge had turned and turned again, and beside it the new finer hairline of grey gathered since, both called the thaw all through the going-out of the month and neither swept, and the name had not made either go. The last bundles stood grey in the ruts where the afternoon had left them, green at the broken ends and pale where the frost had bitten the tips, and nobody had counted them since the light went, because the hands that had cut them were out of the beds and a cost asked in the form of a direction is answered with a direction, which goes off along the water and leaves no entry anywhere.

Under the Vail roof she lifted him out of the basket without once looking into it, the way a woman lifts a pot by the handle and does not look inside because looking will not change what is in it. She set him on the bed and not in the Debt Loft, setting him down as one sets a pot down where it will not be slopped — the base first, the weight settled, the hand kept where it is until the thing is sure of its place. Then she put the fold right at his ear and under his chin, and did not once look at his face. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the cold and she left it frozen and unwiped, because nobody under any roof in Low Kiln had been given an instruction about the thread, and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. Her hand rested a half-beat longer than the setting needed before it went away. She had learned that half-beat in the winter she had sat up three nights with a child of her own who cooled while everybody was kind, and had never told anybody what she had learned, and the house had straightened around the silence every time since. Then the hand went away and she did not go back to it.

At the hearth she told the fire it was the thaw, which it was, and would go when it would go. She turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and fed them and said “The house will keep it” where another woman would have said I. The fire gave its four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, and she did not count the pulls aloud and did not ask anybody to hear the silence, and its payer had been unnamed now for a hundred and forty-nine days. She stood a moment with the back of the broken pot in her hand, counting something under her breath toward no opening. She pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn, the grey streak at her temple showing pale off the coals, and set the pot where it could be found in the dark, rim out and the handle toward the reaching hand — the handle standing two inches beyond the hand that would have reached for it and would not close on it — and did not look toward the bed.

The widow’s mark hung where it hung at her throat, a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and she did not touch it. She touched it before she opened the kiln and at no other hour, and a dusk is not an opening. She went to her own bed without explaining the count and without looking toward the bed where the boy lay, and the two rooms of the house did not touch.

In the small hours the boy turned out his file the way a man lays instruments on a towel before a case begins, each thing going into its own hollow and nothing resting across anything else. He had kept it a hundred and forty-nine days in a body that could not have lifted a spoon from the towel, and the keeping of the things apart was the only work in the room that no slate would enter.

Six hearings of the full note lay beside one another and never among them: a cup across a birth room, a pot under the wheel-shed wall, the district’s slate twice at the Cross, a man giving at the Drawing, and the small flat knock out of a woman as the men lifted, that last from a person and filed beside the five and never counted with them. No seventh had come. The bell was a told bell and a heard bell, kept in two places; the wheel and the pots under the roof were notes he had left where they sounded; the clerk’s slate was a figure he could not read. He had not invented one to fill the empty place. An empty place kept honestly is a record of a kind, and a man who fills one to make a row come out even is a man with a reason, and he had learned two lives ago to be afraid of the shape of a reason in a man with clean hands.

Three near-full answers stood on their own tray, advanced and not paid: a cup across a birth room, a pot under the wheel-shed wall, a rack pot lifted a hand’s breadth too high and set down again, and Jorra Vail’s knowing of a pot’s note withheld from the kiln-master who said it sang before its time. Each had wanted a following stroke that nobody had made, and he had not made it for any of them; a knowing held back is not a hearing given, and the knowing went to the tray and not among the six.

The older intervals lay where they had always lain: the half-beat behind a standing finger, the stroke that had gone on over the flagstones without the check, his own held breath from the market, the unchecking stroke come up beside the half-beat with no cause written across them.

And on the widened tray, in the place he had made for the stoppings and had widened once more, lay nine of them — Reed 5, Reed 8, Reed 11, Reed 14, Reed 17, Reed 20, Reed 23, Reed 26 and Reed 29 — nine objects and not a rule, nine intervals and not nine payments. The ninth had come the night before at this hearth, brought all the way to the word that would call the loan by a man telling the kindling that six were counted and the next he would not count; the sentence about an ear under this roof being worth more than the debt that holds it had gone to the threshold of the word and stopped there, with no figure and no name, and the breath that should have gone in at the top of the reach had stayed where it was. The market had stood with every one of these in one square that morning and had read none of them, and they had come home under one roof and gone each into its own hollow. He held all nine apart by hand, because he had no other hand to hold them with. Nine likenesses standing in a row will stand up and call themselves a doctrine if nobody keeps a finger between them, and a doctrine is an account that has found a payer; no payer had been named to any of the nine. He had learned in a better-lit room what a row of likenesses will do when a man with a clean slate comes to count them — how they stand up out of their places and arrange themselves into a rule, and how the rule acquires a foot at the bottom of it with a name under the foot, and how quickly the name becomes a person. He was not going to let nine stoppings stand up in the dark and acquire a payer because he had been too tired to keep his hand between them. The cost of the keeping was that he was the only adult under this roof and the least use in it: a man who had once reduced a shoulder in the dark could not lift the fold off his own chin, and the doctrine he would not let stand up was a doctrine nobody in this house wanted to stand up either, and still it had to be held.

A held word is a debt and a debt that can hear is a debt that can be called, and he held the nine apart because a debt called at the foot of nine likenesses is a debt with a name and a number under the name and a collector coming down the road. The loan stood uncalled. The breath from Seed 16 stood unspent, with its small interval at the top of each reach to show where it should have gone in. The seventh year stood one tend from its end and had not given. The unforged sentence about a collector lay beside the knife at the very edge of its sill where it could be checked, and he could not have reached the knife to check it himself, and had never wanted to.

Beside these lay the pieces that had no tray of their own and would not be put on one: the damper as one iron with two bites, the bite that holds a vessel whole and the bite that can take the hand off a man, filed joined to no kiln; the two tellings of the counting room shut in the war, side by side with no third, and nobody under this roof having gone into a shut room to make one; the told bell of the Long Night beside the heard bell out of the yard, two sounds of the same shape and not the same sound. He laid them out and looked at them and did not let the look become a joining. An inventory is not a key and a list is not a hand. He had catalogued a hundred and forty-nine days of other people’s keeping and could not carry one object of it to any table.

In the other room the loaf shelf kept its two unnamed gaps and the charcoal under the board stayed on a place of its own, unread by him and unread by anybody who had gone past it that day; a hand had gone beneath the board and come back, and what the marks said had stayed under the board. One brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold two inches from his ear, with its empty place beside it still unnamed and no third coming into either room. The saying of the loaf-shelves of Low Kiln went around the houses the way a pot goes from hand to hand, and was never carried into either room and set down. The two rooms did not touch.

Everything the square had displayed that morning had come home and been put away without being spent. The figure read aloud at the reading block was not the figure that had gone into the stall-keeper’s palm, and the box had gone up the clay road with the true subtraction locked inside it; the cord no Court thumb could spell was back inside a coat under the flat of a hand; the soft-cuffed clerk was still set at the out-of-line landing below the north bridge for a measure nobody had called, slate to forearm with the writing inward, hand to slate before eyes to stalls, and nobody had stepped around him and nobody had taken him off. The sixth green at a silent woman’s mouth was a colour joined to no ear and kept on a place of its own; no woman in the market had ever been given that colour as a name, and he had not made it a face, and would not call it one in the dark. The foundling at the market’s edge was a shape with no sale — a half-turn of a head stopped in the middle of its arc, a green sliver going dull as the child turned back, a mouth opened and shut on the tail of a sentence — and nobody under any roof had said whose child it was, and he had been given no word of his own to put to it. Six greens standing up in a row will call themselves a face if no hand keeps them apart, and six half-turns standing up in a row will call themselves a sale, and a sale is a thing with a payer at its foot; he kept a finger between them as he kept a finger between the nine, and no payer stood under any of them.

The word forty stayed unspoken in the district while the forty-first double turn stood legible on Orra Weir’s knot-string beside a board that did not touch another board; it was a shape and not a name, and no name was going to be made of it in the dark. The reed-cutter’s cord lay back inside his coat at the Weir with its knots unread, straightened and turned where no slate lay and put away with the flat of a hand. Mira Silt’s second twist lay twice beneath an apron and had not been carried upriver to Nine Pots or lifted at a door. A child’s foot below the sill stayed the cold to the woman who had wound it in three turns of wool and weather to the yard, and the reading of it — the extremity going before the core, the almost-smile that is not peace, the afterdrop waiting past a warming nobody would perform — worked as perfectly as it had on the day he learned it and bought him nothing he could spend. He kept every one of these where it lay and laid none of them across another.

Three doors down, under another roof, Alda Reed had laid a date over a sign at a wrist and said warm and breathing where the truth had gone out of a woman more mornings ago than any living person kept count of — exact and not true, and standing over that woman still, uncollected, with no hand alive to lift it and set it right. The mercy had curdled into a fact and then into a piece of the house’s furniture, something a room goes around the way it goes around Ash, and a fact of that kind cannot be corrected by anybody who is still living. Her six pages lay turned toward a floorboard and the little tin stayed shut in her bodice, and the round pewter tin above the abandoned board-bed kept its pale fold and thumb-bright lid closed; nothing in either house had been opened for a figure, and the sign, the date and the hand that made them stayed on three places, because a covering is not the thing covered and a likeness is not a cause.

Through the finger’s-width door came the river’s note of water over water, a fifth low thing and never counted among the hearings; it was not a hearing and he did not make it one. Through the floor under blind ash the carried fire gave four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for a hundred and forty-nine days. Overhead the boards kept four nails, four, and a fifth that was a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts in three places, and none of them became one: a note that was not counted, a fire that missed a pull, a fifth that was a knot and not a nail. He had learned early that three likenesses in a row want to stand up as a rule, and that the only honest work was to keep a hand between them; and here were three counts, and nine stoppings, and a sixth colour, and a sixth shape, and a hundred and forty-nine days, and not one of them was an account anybody had asked him to enter.

Cold came in its known order: cheek, temple, wrist, the wool riding back from the small bare joint. One hundred and forty-nine days old and four months and twenty-nine days, told by nobody, and if somebody under that roof had said the number nobody would have known what it counted, and there was no word in Grathic that would have held it long enough for a woman passing on the clay to set it down. He wanted the crust two inches from his ear, and he wanted the number spoken by somebody who would have known what a number was, and he got neither and could not ask for either. The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water and came down against the fold, exact, complete, unspent, and spoken by nobody.

The reed month went out without a boundary drawn and the count crossed into Smoke unspent, with nine held words on the tray, a sixth colour and a sixth shape on places of their own, the seventh year one tend from its end and the loan uncalled. Nothing was joined, paid, named, opened, called, spent or ended. The note went on over water through the gap in the door, the fire missed its fifth under the floor, and the charcoal waited under the shelf for a hand that was not his and a day that had not come. One hundred and forty-nine stood exact and unspent in the dark as his hand came down against the fold.

The reed month had three days left and nobody was keeping them. Sella Vail carried the boy at the half-pace, instruction to the wool and never the basket. Bundles came out judged by the arm that carried them; a cutter told a girl an arm knows weight and not cost, and a man asking what the hands would be paid was answered with the weather and the river. One hundred and forty-seven stood exact and unspent.

The yard went around the clean dark line, the thaw, the broken birth-slates and the empty place where a hand went in and did not come out. A reed-cutter’s cord went past, tied low with teeth and hidden in his coat, its knots unread. Under the Vail roof Sella sent a woman asking the row’s price to the pot, then reached a finger beneath the board where the charcoal lay, without looking. Such a hand either knows the board by heart or has been told not to look.

On Reed 29 men went low into the back room, unnamed. Jorra Vail came through with slate dust on both forearms, spoke to the wheel first, withheld a pot’s full note from a High Kiln kiln-master and put his bent finger into the cold damper --- one iron with two bites, one holding a vessel whole, one taking a hand off a man.

That night Tarn told the kindling Six counted.
