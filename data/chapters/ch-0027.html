The Cord That Keeps a Count

Days 120–122 · Reed 1–3, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, Sill

A bundle is counted in the arm that carries it. The hands that cut it are counted in nothing at all.

— A saying of the reed-cutters, the Weir

— Day 120 —

Reed 1, Firstday, Y.H. 813

The wind off the Ilm came downriver on the first day of Reed with wet mud and the smell of cut grass in it, and Low Kiln turned its face after it the way a field turns when the light goes off one edge of it. No boundary had been marked. There was no word spoken over the change and no line drawn across the ground to say that one month had ended and another had come on; the planting was behind and the reeds were ahead, and the two things stood in the same yard under the same brown skin the flood had left over the ruts. The wind had a smell he knew before he had any word for it in this country: wet mud, cut grass, and the thin mineral edge the river leaves on everything it touches. It was not strange to him, which was the strange part of it, the sense of coming back to a room after it has been rearranged and finding that you still know where the door stands. The seed stood up in the worked strips east of the houses, and the hands were out of them, and the ground kept the small dark pits where fists had let seed go and the heel-marks along the drills and the pale dust of clods broken and left where they fell. Sella Vail carried the boy up from the rack end at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, the basket held steady the way a pot is held that must not be slopped, and gave the instruction to the wool and the wind and never to the basket. “Keep it off his ears. It’s the river wind.” The instruction went into the wool and into the wind and nowhere near the child in the basket, and her little-door burn closed on the handle and then on nothing as she walked. He was one hundred and twenty days old and four months, carried where he could not have crawled, and nobody in the yard or under the roof had said so, or would have known what it counted if they had. Cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, the wet wool riding back from the bare joint so the cold found the place it had come for. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and nobody in this yard had been given an instruction about that sound. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had wiped it. He was a man who could have told you by the back of a hand laid on a brow how far a body had gone into the cold, and he could not close two inches on the rim of the basket that held him. Both things were true. The second was the one that was loud.

The reeds were spoken of as a direction and a going-downriver, never as a figure. A man come up from the sluice with his sleeves wet to the elbow said the beds would be wanting hands by the morrow, opened his mouth on what the hands would be, shut it on a direction instead, and went off downriver with the sentence still in his teeth. “Downriver, then,” a woman at the racks said to nobody in particular, shifting a pot an inch along the board so the light fell where she wanted it. “That’s where they’ll want us.” The woman beside her said “Downriver” back, and neither put a distance to it or a number, because a distance would have wanted a measure and a measure would have wanted a slate, and nobody in this yard had a slate for the reeds. They stood a moment with their hands on their hips looking at work that was not there yet, and went back to their pots. Two women at the rack end spoke of the Weir as a place the work would go to, not a place anybody here would stand, and of bundles in the way people speak of armfuls when there are any to carry and of glances when there are none. A bundle was a thing you saw go past and judged by the arm that carried it. Nobody set a number on a bundle standing in a field, any more than anybody had set a number on a back bent in a strip. The boy had learned the district’s arithmetic early and had never once found a figure in it. It was a counting done in the open air with the hands and the eyes and the mouth, and it arrived as a direction or a pointing or a grunt shaped and swallowed, and the thing it counted was never written down. He had watched a midwife reach for a slate before she reached for a child, and a neighbour reach for a bare board before she looked at him, and a man take a number out of a fist and put it back into a sleeve; he had watched the same refusal performed a hundred times in this yard with nothing but a turned mouth and a wide step. The yard had a way of withholding a number that was older than any slate and more complete. It left the number lying where it was, like a tool left out in the rain, and went on with the work.

The drying racks stood in a row along the east edge of the yard with the lowest one set a little apart, and the space beneath it was gone around as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. The broken birth-slates lay under there where they had lain since the flood, entered by nobody and spoken of by nobody, and the yard took them in passing the way it takes in a stone it has decided not to lift. The boy’s basket went around the space with the rest of it. Sella’s step widened there by the width of a hand and came back again, and the even carry of the basket did not change. At the corner of the Vail house the clean dark line where Ash ridge had turned lay unswept, and beside it the new finer grey that had gathered in its place lay unswept too, and the two lines were called the thaw and left where they were. A broom stood in the corner beside them and had stood there through the planting month, and nobody had taken it up to either line. The boy had seen the broom given its instruction often enough to know what the instruction was: go around, it cost enough to come in and will cost more to take out. The instruction went to the broom and to the lines, and the lines kept their places and the space under the rack kept its emptiness and the district went on calling the going-around care. Nothing in the yard entered any of it. A place gone around with a look was still a place entered by nobody, and the yard had gone around these three places every day of the planting month and had not once called what it did by its right name.

Out in the worked strips the returning line held its gap. The hands had come out of the seed the day before last; one hand that had gone in on Seed 15 had not come out with the rest. The yard went around the place where it should have stood the way it went around the line and the space under the rack, with a look and a wide step and no number. No body had come up out of the brown ground. No cause had been said over it, no accident named, no hand identified and no figure set at the foot of what the month had cost. The place was held and not entered, carried forward into Reed the way it had been carried through the end of Seed, and the work going-downriver had already begun to arrange itself around it as the planting had. The boy read the going-around as a surgeon reads a chart laid face down on a table: a neat line drawn to close a note that nobody had opened. It was exact. It was complete. It was worth nothing. He had spent two lives learning the difference between a record that tells you what happened and a record that tells you only where to stop reading, and the field’s empty place was the second kind, closed as neatly as any slate in Slate Row and with as little inside it. He had signed such a chart himself once, in a room with better light than this, and had let a note lie turned down under the signature while he went on to the next body, and the shape of that had stayed with him across two lives and crossed with him into a country that had no word for it. Here the chart was a field with a hole in a line and no signature at all, and the not-reading was done in the open air before a whole yard that leaned toward it the way a crowd leans toward a man about to speak low. He set it on a place of its own where he kept such things and did not lay it down beside anything else. Not beside the two unnamed gaps on the loaf shelf in the room that did not touch this one, not beside the one stiff crust in the fold and the empty place waiting next to it at his ear, not beside the half-beat of a checked stroke or the note of water over water or the hand that had not come back. A likeness laid on a likeness grows a cause. Three places held apart were three places; four laid together would make a cause with a clean hand at the foot of it, and the clean hand was the one object in this world he had spent two lives declining to become. The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the worked strip and came down. Nothing within two paces could be reached.

A woman come up from the strips with her sleeves pushed above her elbows and the dry mud of the rows worked into the creases of her wrists. She stood a half-pace off, the way people stood off from Sella without knowing they had done it, and wiped one hand on the other and did not wipe it clean.

“What will the reeds cost,” she said, “now the hands are out of them?”

It was the same question the yard had put to the planting at the well and had put to the flood and would put to the next thing that wanted hands, and it went the same way. Sella did not answer it. She glanced once at the clay jar on its wall shelf and let her eyes rest there no longer than the glance required, and her hand went to the little-door burn and closed it on nothing and opened again.

“Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there,” she said. “The house has said it.”

The woman stood with her mouth still open on the next part of the question and found that there was nothing to put into it. She wiped her hand on her other hand again and went off down the yard with the answer she had been given, which was no answer and had the house behind it, and the boy watched her go. She carried the shape of a refusal away in front of her the way a woman carries a pot she has been told is not for her, holding it steady so nothing slops, and she would set it down somewhere out of this yard and it would stand there unanswered. Sella had given the instruction to the pot and not to the woman and not to the basket, and she had said the house where another woman would have said I, taking herself out of her own sentence as neatly as a surgeon takes a hand out of a field before the dressing goes on, leaving the field open and clean and nobody’s. The boy set the once-glanced-at jar and the sent-away question on places of their own in the file he kept where no slate could read it. He had heard this answer before in this same yard and in a better-lit room in another life where a man took himself out of a sentence to leave a figure standing clean in it. Two places, two rooms, the same grammar. He laid no finger across them.

The going of the light came down over the yard the way a cloth comes down over a table that has not been cleared, covering what was on it without moving any of it. The reeds went on being a direction downriver. The seed stood up in the strips with the gap in the returning line gone around by every hand that passed it, and the clean dark line and the finer grey lay unswept in the corner and the space under the lowest rack kept its broken slates and its wide step. Sella carried the basket at the half-pace from every open slate with the basket steady and the burn closing on nothing at the handle, and went around the space beneath the rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. The wet wool had ridden back from his wrist and the cold had found the bare place and gone under the fold at his ear, and the thread at his mouth had frozen where nobody had been instructed to wipe it. His head wandered and made the small wet sound and came back. One hundred and twenty stood where one hundred and nineteen had stood in the small hours, exact, unspent and spoken by nobody who would have known what a number of days counted when they said it. The yard turned downriver toward the reed beds while the empty place in the worked strip was still gone around with a look and no figure, and his small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water and came down, and nothing was joined, named, paid or ended.

Back under the Vail kiln-house roof the going of the light came down through the oiled paper in thin grey squares, and the house took the boy in the way a cup takes water poured against its inside: carefully, and with a little left over that nobody entered. Sella Vail set the basket down where no slate lay and, before she gave any instruction to anybody, gave it to the things. The bucket: stand and settle, you have come a long way up out of the dark and you will be finer in a moment. The broom: go around, it cost enough to come in and it will cost more to take out. The pot: don’t set that near the door, you will pay for it in the thaw. The fire: feed it before anybody feeds themselves, because Garl’s proverb had gone to a pot with no crack long ago and cost the more now that there was nobody to say it in his own voice. The instruction went into the bucket and the broom and the pot and the fire and nowhere near the basket, and the basket kept its level carry with the fold put right at his ear and never a word spent on the child inside it. She said the house where another woman would have said I, and the boy, who had spent two lives learning how a person takes himself out of his own sentence, heard the taking happen and did not ask for the answer that never came.

She went around the space beneath the lowest rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, the step widening by the width of a hand and coming back again, the basket steady through it all. Between the door and the bench she kept her half-pace from every open slate, the little-door burn closing on the handle and then on nothing. The river wind came under the eaves off the Ilm with the thin mineral edge the water leaves on everything it touches, and went under the fold at his ear and found the place it had come for. Cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist. The wool had ridden back from the bare joint in the going light and the cold found that place too, and went below the fold to the place where it always went. His head wandered and made the small wet sound and came back, and nobody under this roof had been given an instruction about the sound. The thread at his mouth had frozen where it was and nobody had wiped it, because nobody had been instructed about the thread, and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. He was a man who could have told the temperature of a body by the back of a hand laid on its brow, and he could not close two inches on the rim of the basket that held him. Both things were true. The second was the one that was loud.

the loaf shelf stood in the next room, and the house kept its days there badly: loaves pushed along a board by their edges, a glance at the clay jar on the wall, a space at the row’s end nobody called a figure, and a newer space the width of a palm nobody called anything at all. Sella stood before it and moved her lips along the row without a figure, the way a woman reads loaves by their edges and not by their number, and the two gaps kept their places in the row while her lips passed over them. The old gap at the row’s end had come in with the planting, and had been named again in the yard that morning as a thing the planting brought in, and the new palm’s breadth had come in the night of Seed 6 with no scrape, no crumb, no print and no word, and the two of them stood side by side and were not one absence. The room the shelf stood in did not touch the room where the boy lay. In that other room one stiff brown-edged crust lay in the fold two inches from his ear with its own empty place beside it unnamed, and no third crust had come into it in a hundred and twenty days, and no third gap stood on the shelf. He had learned early what three likenesses in a row will do: laid one on another they grow a cause, and a cause wants a clean hand at the foot of it, and the clean hand was the one object he had spent two lives declining to become. Here the two rooms did not touch, and he kept them not touching, and the keeping cost him the only exactness in the district and bought him nothing anybody would take.

Her glance went once to the clay jar on the wall shelf, cold under its glaze, and went back to the loaves, and rested there no longer than the glance required. Her hand did not go to the widow’s mark at her throat, because a shelf is not an opening and a loaf row is not a door and no opening had come under this roof in all his days. The clean dark line where Ash had turned under the corner lay where it lay, and the new finer grey hairline beside it lay where it lay, and neither was swept. The house called both the thaw, and the thaw would go when it went. The broom stood in the corner where it had stood through the planting month, and the instruction had gone to it twice, and the lines kept their places; going around was care, the yard said, and the yard had gone around these things every day of the month and had not once called what it did by its right name. No figure went with any of it. A neighbour come up from the strips that morning had asked what the reeds would cost now the hands were out of them, and Sella had sent her to the pot with Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there. The house has said it — the house, never I, taking itself out of the sentence and leaving the question standing clean in it, and the answer had gone off up the yard in the woman’s hands held level so nothing slopped. The boy set the sent-away question on a place of its own, and did not lay it beside the two gaps on the shelf or the gap in the field or the empty place at his ear, because a likeness laid on a likeness grows a cause, and there are more ways of making a cause than there are hands to make them.

At the going of the light she carried him to his bed under the roof and not to the Debt Loft, and set him down the way a woman sets down a pot she has carried a long way and cannot bring herself to let go of. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without once looking at his face, and left the thread of drool at his mouth frozen and unwiped, because nobody under this roof had been given an instruction about the thread and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. Her hands were never still; when they had nothing to do they counted, and here they counted the breaths at his ear and entered the count nowhere. The hand rested a half-beat longer than the setting needed before it went away — rested, and went, and did not look toward the face it had covered. That half-beat was the whole of what the house would allow itself, and it went out with the hand. He had read such half-beats before in chambers with better light, in the pause of a hand before a chart was turned, in the extra breath a nurse spent at a shoulder that had already been dressed, and had never once found a word that would carry what it meant into a slate. Here it arrived as a hand that would not let go quite soon enough, and he took it for what it was and set it nowhere, because a thing entered becomes a thing entered, and a thing entered can be called.

She went to the fire. “It was the thaw,” she told it, the way one tells a pot what it has been through and then leaves it to stand. She turned the coals with the back of a broken pot, lifting the blind ash and letting the air into the banked heat beneath and laying the ash back again, and the fire took the coal in its own time and gave back nothing a man could have taken up as a figure. “The house will keep it,” she said, where another woman would have said I, and the keeping went into the fire and into the ash and nowhere into the room where the boy lay. She stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand, counting something under her breath toward no opening, and the count went the way all her counts went, along the shelf and back to the same place twice, keeping the shape of the days as a shape no collector could take up as a number. The little-door burn on the back of her left hand closed on the pot’s rim and then on nothing as she pushed a strand of coal-black hair back from her temple with the same wrist; the grey streak there showed pale in the dying light off the coals, and she had said it came in the winter Garl died and would not discuss it further, and Garl’s name had not been spoken under this roof in all his days. She set the pot where it could be found in the dark, rim out, handle toward the hand that would reach for it, and did not look toward the bed. The carried fire answered four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, warming the boards by degrees under blind ash, its payer unnamed for a hundred and twenty days. Every warmth in this house had a payer somewhere, and no slate in the district carried the name.

The boy turned out the private file as a man lays instruments on a towel, and found every object where he had left it. Six hearings of the full note stood beside and never among, five from four sources and the sixth, the small flat knock out of a woman as the men lifted, from a person; no seventh had come today and he had not invented one to stand in the empty place where a seventh would have stood. Three near-full answers stood advanced and not paid on their own tray — a cup across a birth room, a pot under the wheel-shed wall, a rack pot lifted a hand’s breadth too high and set down again — and three intervals on another, with the half-beat of a checked stroke and the stroke that went on without it lying beside them as two objects and not a rule. The told bell lay beside the heard bell in two places that did not touch. The word forty lay unspoken with the readable double turn of a cord twisted twice beneath another beside it and the weight of a thumb on wicker apart from both, and he had not said the word and no thumb had counted the knot. Four low things kept four places with the river’s note of water over water a fifth that was not a hearing and had never been counted among the four. The two tellings of the shut counting room lay joined to no kiln, and the double bite of the damper lay filed as an object spent on nothing, the bite that holds a vessel whole and the bite that can take a hand off a man in one piece of iron with no word in Grathic for both mercies. The held breath in a man’s chest lay where it had lain, unspent, with the collector sentence nobody forged beside it and the loan that could hear still uncalled. The hand that had gone into the strip and had not come back out lay on a place of its own, entered in no slate, with no name, no cause, no body and no figure set at the foot of what the month had cost.

To these he added the day’s two objects. The reed work had been named as a direction and a going-downriver and never as a figure, the bundles spoken of as armfuls when there were any to see and as glances when there were none, and a woman had been sent to the pot with a question and had carried the unanswered thing away held level in her hands. He set the direction on a place of its own and the sent-away question on another, and laid no finger across either, and laid no finger across the space between them. Two likenesses can be held apart by a hand that knows what a third would do. Three make a cause, and a cause says what happened when nobody has said it, and the hand that says it is never the hand that paid. He had been the hand that said it once in a better-lit room and had let a note lie turned down under the signature while he went on to the next body, and the shape of that had crossed with him into a country that had no word for it, and had stayed. Here he kept his places separate and did not make the joining, because the only exact account in the district was the one nobody could read, and the more exact it grew the more it isolated him, and isolation was a price he had learned to pay without flinching and without liking.

The door stood a finger’s width open where it had stood through the planting month and through the flood and through the lean week, and the river’s note of water over water came through the gap from beyond the yard, low and continuous and belonging to no vessel that would ever be tapped. Through the floor under the blind ash the carried fire gave its four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, and the two sounds came from two separate places and were kept in two separate places, and the three counts did not become one: the river’s note, the fire’s four, and the gap where a fifth pull should have been. Above him the boards kept four nails, four, and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb. He had counted them every night of his hundred and twenty days and would have counted them tomorrow, and nobody under the roof knew a number had moved. One hundred and twenty stood where one hundred and nineteen had stood in the small hours, exact, unspent and spoken by nobody who would have known what a number of days counted when they said it. He had stopped expecting to be told, which was not the same as not wanting it, and the wanting went on quietly below the not-expecting the way the fire went on below the ash.

Cold came at cheek and temple and wrist, and the wool did less than the wind wanted, riding back from the joint so the cold found the bare place and went under the fold at his ear. The dry brown-edged crust lay two inches away with its empty place beside it, and the thread at his mouth had frozen again in the going light, and his head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound and came back. His small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down. The river went over water beyond the finger’s-width gap and the fire missed its fifth breath under the floor, and the two counts did not meet, and nothing was joined, named, paid or ended.

— Day 121 —

Reed 2, Secondday, Y.H. 813

Before first grey the Clay Yard was a shape felt sooner than seen, the racks a row of darker uprights against a sky the colour of wet ash, and the men came up it with their collars high and their heads bent toward one another as though they carried between them something that would break if the wind got under it. They went low-voiced into the room off the wheel-shed, which stood a finger’s width open and did not shut. Sella Vail set the boy at the edge of the light in the Vail wheel-shed, the basket level on the boards and his cheek turned to the cold of them, and stood a half-pace from every open slate. She gave the instruction to the wool before she gave it to anybody. Keep it off his ears; it’s the river wind. The instruction went into the wool and into the wind and nowhere near the child in the basket. Cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist; the wool had ridden back from the bare joint in the grey and the cold found that place and went under the fold at his ear, and the thread at his mouth had frozen where nobody under this roof had been given an instruction about the thread. His head wandered and made the small wet sound and came back. He was one hundred and twenty-one days old, and nobody in the shed or under the roof had said so, or would have known what it counted if they had.

Jorra Vail gave the house no name for the men. He did not say the room was cold. He came through the finger’s-width door with fine grey slate dust ground small as meal into both forearms past the bend of the little finger, and it was not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel; the two states of one pair of forearms went down on one tray in the boy’s keeping, dusty and clean, and he asked neither which slate had been broken nor whether it had been a birth-slate. The broken birth-slates lay where they had lain since the flood beneath the lowest rack, and the yard went around the space above them as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and entered them into nothing. Nothing in Low Kiln had entered them into anything. A place gone around with a look was still a place entered by nobody, and the going-around had a name in the yard, and the name was care.

He spoke to the wheel before he spoke to anyone. “It doesn’t care whose hands. It cares if they’re steady.” It was the same sentence the boy had heard from the same mouth through the whole length of the planting month, given to the wheel as a man gives a beast its handful and not to the people standing by, and nothing in it asked who had been in the room or what had been ground into the creases past the little finger. A pot came up on the wheel and he looked at its wall a moment longer than the wall required and said, “You’ll do,” in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either. He knew the the full note that was in that pot before it ever left the wheel; he had known such notes in pots of this house since he was eleven, and he would not say so in front of a kiln-master come up from High Kiln, who would call it early and make something of it. Garl Vail’s proverb had gone to a pot with no crack long ago and cost the more now that there was nobody to say it in his own voice, and the boy, who had spent two lives learning the exact weight of a note before a thing breaks, kept the knowing where questions could last for years.

A kiln-master come up from High Kiln went along the row of pots with his hands behind his back, looking at the rims and not at the boy, and said nothing that any slate could carry. Jorra said nothing either. The note was in the Vail pot before it left the wheel and he had known it there and would go on knowing it, but a thing said in front of a kiln-master becomes a thing the kiln-master has heard, and a thing heard is a thing entered in some book the boy would never see; the house had taught him that much without ever saying it. The kiln-master went off up the yard with his hands still behind him and the glaze smell on his coat, and the wheel took up its low note again as though no man had stood over it. The boy set the unsaid knowing on a place of its own and did not lay it beside the dust on the forearms, though the two things had come in on the same morning.

“The fire’s low, Jorra. Feed it before—”

Sella had not finished. His bent finger went into the cold iron of the damper ahead of her sentence, the tip of it taking the cold out of the iron and giving a little back in the other direction, and he moved the tongue of the thing a hair’s breadth and left it standing there. The end of her instruction arrived at the pot instead of at him. The household straightened around his silence the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted, the bucket settling, the broom settling, her hands settling into the shape of the next thing they would hold, and no order went out and no word came back. He had not answered her and had not looked at her, which was how he obeyed her instantly.

The boy read the iron once more as he had read it on Seed 16 and on Seed 28 and on a dozen mornings between: the bite that holds a vessel whole and the bite that can take the hand off a man, two doings of one piece of iron with no word in Grathic that held both mercies in one sound. It was the same iron set at two depths, and a man who knew the feel of teeth could tell which bite he was closing before he closed it; here there was no choosing, because the iron stood where it stood and the warmth went by degrees and the payer of it was named by nobody. He set the reading on a place of its own with the other objects he kept where no slate could read it, exact, worth nothing, joined to no kiln. The two tellings of the shut counting room stayed on their own tray and did not come across to it. A likeness laid on a likeness grows a cause, and there are more ways of making a cause than there are hands to make them.

His own hand outside the wool could not have closed on the damper’s tongue if the whole house had told him to. It could go up an inch and come down, and that was the whole of its reach; the iron lay two paces off with the cold standing on it like water on a stone, and he could no more have set a finger on it than he could have told the men in the room their names. He had been a man who chose his instrument before a field was opened and knew by the weight in his palm what the clamp would do; now he lay at the edge of the light and watched a boy of sixteen put a bent finger into the cold ahead of a woman’s sentence, and the watching was the only instrument left to him, exact and worth nothing. A small sound came out of his mouth and went back into it, and nobody in the shed had been instructed about that sound either.

Tarn took the broom only after Sella had given it its instruction. Go around, it cost enough to come in, it will cost more to take out; the instruction went into the broom and the flagstones and never into the man, and he took up the tool as a man takes up a thing already named. “Yes, house,” he said, and went around the clean dark line and the new finer grey beside it called the thaw, and around the space beneath the lowest rack, and out toward the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector. His seventh year was pressed past the end of the planting and still held. The small bench knife lay at the very edge of its sill with six years standing in a mark his thumb had worn almost flat and the seventh nearly gone and never named, the one entry in this house the house could not check; the unforged collector sentence lay beside it where he had kept it since the frozen ford, and neither was entered. His chest had been tight since the dark, and the breath from Seed 16 was still unspent, a small interval at the top of each reach showing where it should have gone in, a breath stopped against iron and never let through. To the kindling, later, the account stayed as it had stayed through the planting month: “Six counted. The next I don’t count.” He said “Yes, house” to the ones who wanted the thaw early and to the ones who wanted it late, agreed so entirely that no argument could find a foot, and kept wanting the late one because a late thaw puts off the paying, though the paying had stood in the ruts since the water went.

He held the pale clip-ring at his left wrist up to the light while he said it, and the Ash in the creases of his knuckles showed the colour of the clean dark line no scrubbing lifted, and the ring gave back what little light there was. He had been sent for a collector once in the Long Cold and come back at the frozen ford and told the house it had been done, and that sentence lay where he kept the seventh year and was not entered; no collector had come down the road this morning and no one had gone down it for him. He put the small knife back where it could be checked, though nobody had asked, and went on sweeping toward the road with his chest held the way a man holds a door he means to keep shut.

His stroke went on over the flagstones without the half-check. Behind Jorra’s standing finger the bristles passed the place where the stroke had stopped on Seed 22 and did not stop there now, and the stones kept that place all the same, a shallow bright patch in the pattern of the paving that no foot would enter and no broom would call by its name. On Seed 25 the change had been held and not repeated; on Seed 28 it had been held again; on this morning of Reed it went on as though the half-beat had never been made, and the two things lay on one tray in the boy’s keeping as two objects and not a rule. He set the unchecking stroke beside the earlier half-beat and laid no cause across them. Neither man named the other’s silence. Jorra did not ask about the ear and Tarn did not ask about the room, and the loan that could hear was not called; a loan that can hear is a loan that can be called, and the sentence that would call it had lain under this roof for six years without being said. An ear worth more than a debt is an unsafe thing to own, and the boy, who had spent two lives learning what an uncalled account costs, kept the two silences apart on the tray where they had lain since the night he came, with no finger laid across the space between them.

Under the roof that private file kept its objects where he had laid them the night before: six hearings of the full note beside and never among with no seventh, three near-full answers advanced and not paid, three intervals, the told bell beside the heard bell in two places that did not touch, the word forty unspoken with its readable double turn beside it, the cold foot, the two tellings of the shut room joined to no kiln, the hand that had gone into the strip and had not come back held on a place of its own. To these he added the morning’s two objects — the dust ground small as meal past the bent finger and the stroke that went on without the check — and set each on its own place, and laid no finger across the space between them, because a likeness laid on a likeness grows a cause and a cause wants a clean hand at the foot of it, and the clean hand was the one object he had spent two lives declining to become.

Jorra did not look toward the bed and did not look into the basket. He’s not Vail stayed where it had been spoken on the hour the boy came under the roof, sealed as a lid nobody had tried to lift in three years, and the promise of the foundling’s name to the men who burn slates was neither given nor withdrawn. The wheel went on under his hands, the pot took its note and kept it, the dust stayed ground small as meal in the creases past his bent finger, and the men in the room off the shed kept their names to themselves because the house had been given none to carry. Sella told the fire it was the thaw and turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and said “The house will keep it” where another woman would have said I, and the household went on straightening around the silence without an order. Cold came at cheek and temple and wrist, and the wool did less than the wind wanted; the thread at his mouth stayed frozen and unwiped, and his small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the low note of water over water and came down. One hundred and twenty-one stood where one hundred and twenty had stood, exact, unspent and spoken by nobody who would have known what a number of days counted when they said it, and the count went on into the rest of the day with no hand joining the two silences in the room.

Midday came on over the stalls without anything marking where the morning ended and the other half of the day began. The reeds had come down from the beds in the time it took a man to turn a pot on the wheel: cut bundles stood in the ruts beyond the Cross where the barley had stood a month before, and hands that had carried seed in shut fists now carried reed in armfuls, and nobody among them put a figure to an armful or a direction. Sella Vail carried the boy down from Low Kiln at the half-pace she kept from every open slate and set the basket at the outer edge of the stalls’ shade, where the boards gave up their shadow and no slate lay, the way a woman sets a pot down where no passing hip will knock it. The instruction went to the wool and to the running water and never to the basket. Keep it off his ears, it is the river wind; keep it dry, it is running water. She said it the way she said everything to everything, twice, because the wool had not moved and the wind had not listened. Then she went off among the reeds to price a bundle, and the basket stood where she had set it, and no hand under the boards had been given an instruction about the child in it.

The market smelled of cut reed and river silt and the sweetish breath of glaze that came down on a bad wind from High Kiln, with hot clay under all of it and eel slime somewhere below the bridge. Pigs went over the weighing board and came off with their sides marked in a hand that would not keep the mark; a girl with a creel went over the out-of-line landing sideways, caught the rail, and went on selling; a man with a slate under his arm set the slate against the turnip heap before he looked at the turnips. The weigher put his hand to the slate before his eyes went to the pig, and the buyer put her thumb to her purse before her eyes went to the reeds, and a reed-seller took the bundle by its middle before she looked at the face of the woman who wanted it: three orders of reaching in three parts of one market, and the boy set them on three places and laid no finger across them. Voices came up out of the stalls and went down into them again, and he took them as he had taken everything since the stone at his belly went cold — the sound first, the sense of it after, the thing itself never. Cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist. The wool had ridden back from the bare joint and the wind under the boards went under the fold at his ear and found the place it had come for. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and nobody at the stalls had been given an instruction about that sound. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and had not been wiped since.

Below the north bridge at the Fishstairs the soft-cuffed clerk was still set at the out-of-line landing. His cuff was grey from washing and grey again by midday, the colour of a thing that has been made clean in one place and dirtied in another without anybody asking which. He held his slate to his forearm for a measure nobody had called, and his hand went to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls, every time, the way a man crosses himself before he sits down to meat. The market sold its reeds and its eels over the top of him as though a man with a slate were a thing a landing always had, and nobody stepped around him and nobody asked him what he was set there for. He did not speak. He had not been taken off since the month turned, or if he had been taken off somebody had put him back in the dark, which was the same thing to a market that went on over him.

The steps came back four, four and a landing a hand’s breadth out of line where a step should have been, and a whole market walked over it carefully without making a fall of it — a girl with a creel took it sideways and caught the rail, a man with a pot stepped over and did not look at what he had stepped over, and nobody called it an accident. The boy set the out-of-line landing beside the missing fifth stroke of the bell and laid no finger across them, two things in two places, one walked over and one told in the dark. The grey of the washed cuff went on one place and the slate held at the edge on another, because a man who washes his own cuff has a hunger or a habit and a man who holds a slate where nobody else can see the figure has a use, and a use is a thing that wants a payer. A measure set down before anybody has called it is a measure set down ahead of a death; he kept that as a shape and did not make a rule of it, because the day it wanted a name would come from another direction and another mouth, and there was no hand under the boards that could have written the name down if it came.

At the market’s edge, where the reeds thinned out into the ruts and the shade gave up, a foundling stood among the grown faces. He had been there a while and nobody had put him out and nobody had given him anything to do. His clothes were somebody else’s cast-offs worn as though they had been measured to him: a man’s shoulders on a child’s narrow frame, breeches cut off at the knee, a string at his throat with a sliver of green lead-glaze on it. Silt had dried pale in the creases of his wrists, the way it dries on a man who has been down at the water and has not washed, and there was a gap where a front tooth had been. The sliver caught the light when he turned and went dull when he turned back, and he watched the grown faces all morning with the patient attention of a child who has no place in a thing and means to stay in it all the same. He said nothing. Nobody spoke to him, and he did not speak to the pots or the reeds or the eels, and he went on being there the way a board goes on being under a market that walks over it.

A kiln-hand came along the row with a pot on a tray and set it down on the boards a little way from the basket. The boy heard the full note in it before the hand had finished setting it — whole and full under the wall of the thing, the note a vessel gives when nothing has happened to it yet — and his head went toward the sound as it had gone toward a hundred such sounds since he came into this body, before anyone tapped the pot and before anyone looked at it. A knuckle went against the wall and the note came back full, which was the confirmation any man in the yard would have waited for before he said a thing about the pot. Half a beat after the knuckle, half a beat after the note, the foundling’s head went toward the pot too.

It stopped in the middle of the turn. The sliver at his throat caught the light and went dull again as he turned back, and he rubbed it twice with his thumb, the way a glaze-taster rubs a brush clean on an apron while he decides what to say. A kiln-hand at the edge of the row had said the tail of a sentence and left it hanging, and the child’s mouth opened on the end of it the way a child’s mouth opens to finish a thing heard a hundred times, and nothing came out of it that any clerk with a slate could have taken down. There was no word in the market’s language for the thing that had made him turn, which was not the note and came after it, and he swallowed whatever had opened his mouth and went back to watching the grown faces.

The boy laid the delay on a place of its own. It was a thing that came after the knock and was not a hearing, not a seventh and not a vessel’s note; it came after the note as a shadow comes after a body and was not the body. He set beside it his own turning toward the pot and the watcher’s late turns from the days before, and joined none of them, because a thing that comes after a knock is a different object from one that comes before it and there is no word in Grathic for the difference. Two faces turned the same way can mean opposite things: one turns to keep, one turns to price. He had seen a woman across this square turn a cup a quarter and set it back where it stood, and seen another reach past a bare board before her eyes found a child, and he had filed both without a name. Here was a third turning on a third place, and no hand of his went across to join it. He did not call it a sale. A sale is a thing that crosses from one hand to another with a figure in it, and nothing had crossed here — no loaf, no slate, no secret spoken, no sentence finished. The half-beat stood on its tray where the other delays stood, held for the day it would land and called by a name nobody at the stalls had yet put to it, exact and worth nothing and joined to no kiln.

No loaf passed anywhere within reach of the basket, and no slate was opened within two paces of it. The foundling went back to watching the grown faces and the clerk at the landing went on holding his slate to his forearm, and the two stood in two places that did not touch, one a child with a green sliver and one a man with a cuff grey from washing. The boy’s own small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water that came from below the bridge and came down without reaching it. He could not close two inches on the rim of the basket that held him; he had once held pressure in a cavity with both hands while a student counted the pulse out loud and been right about the spleen before the knife went in. Both things were true and the second was the one that was loud. One hundred and twenty-one stood where one hundred and twenty had stood in the morning, exact, unspent and spoken by nobody who would have known what a count of days counted when they said it, and the market went on selling its reeds and its eels over the top of the clerk as though the count and the slate and the hand’s breadth out of line were all of them weather.

By the going of the light the stalls had given up their shade and the reed bundles stood grey in the ruts with nobody counting them. Sella came back through the reeds with a bundle under her arm, lifted the basket without looking into it, and carried him up the clay road at the half-pace from every open slate, going around the space beneath the lowest rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. Under the Vail kiln-house roof she would set him on his bed and not in the Debt Loft, put the fold right without once looking at his face and leave the thread of drool frozen and unwiped, because nobody had been given an instruction about the thread; but the last thing in the file as the light went was not the road or the basket or the fold. It was the foundling’s head stopping in the middle of its turn half a beat after the pot was set down, the green sliver going dull as he turned back, while below the north bridge the soft-cuffed clerk held his slate to his forearm for a measure nobody had called, his hand going to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls. Two late things in two places that did not touch. The boy laid them where no Court thumb would read them, and went on keeping the count, and nothing was joined, named, paid or ended.

— Day 122 —

Reed 3, Thirdday, Y.H. 813

The first bundles came into Low Kiln before the light had settled properly on the yard. Men down from the reed beds carried cut reed across their shoulders, green at the broken ends and pale grey at the tips where the last frost had taken them, and laid the armfuls in the ruts where the barley had stood a month before. They laid them down and straightened and looked at what they had laid, the way a man looks at a thing he has carried and is willing to have judged by the carrying. A woman come up from the racks lifted two fingers out of a fist and shut them again without showing what they had held. A man with a barrow shaped a number with his mouth and thought better of it and went off with the sentence still in his teeth. Nobody among them put a figure to a bundle standing in the mud. A bundle was a thing you saw go past and judged by the arm that brought it; a day’s cutting was a direction downriver and a coming-back at dusk; the question of what it would cost was a question that had not yet been asked in a form anybody here could answer.

“A bundle’s counted in the arm that carries it,” a reed-cutter said to the woman at the racks, wiping his palms down his thighs.

“And the hands that cut it?” she said.

He looked at her a moment with his mouth still open, and then he took up another armful and went past her with it, and the question went down the yard unanswered. It was not refused. It was simply not a question the yard had ever learned to put into words that would stay put long enough to be entered anywhere. Sella Vail carried the boy up from the rack end at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, the basket held level the way a pot is held that must not be slopped, and gave the instruction to the wool and the river wind and never to the basket. “Keep it off his ears. It’s the river wind.” The instruction went into the wool and into the wind and nowhere near the child in it, and her little-door burn closed on the handle and then on nothing as she walked.

He was one hundred and twenty-two days old and four months and two days, carried where he could not have crawled, and nobody in the yard or under the roof had said so, or would have known what it counted if they had. Cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist. The wet wool had ridden back from the bare joint and the wind found the place it had come for and went under the fold at his ear. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far and came back. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had been given an instruction about the thread. He read the arm going past with its load as a record kept in the muscle of it — an entry made in the only material the yard allowed, and one that would be gone by evening when the muscle had cooled and the arm had gone home to its own fire. It was exact. It told a man what had been carried and told him nothing about what it had cost to carry. He set it on a place of its own and did not lay it down beside the empty place in the worked strip that the hands had gone around with a look and no number since the seed came up. A likeness laid on a likeness grows a cause, and a cause in this district always arrived with a clean hand at the foot of it. His small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water and came down.

The road downriver to the Weir ran on the bank above the Ilm, which had gone down inside its banks since the flood and now moved full and brown between the willows with the light coming up along it. Sella carried the basket at the half-pace, going around the clean dark line at the Vail corner and the new finer grey beside it without sweeping either, and around the space beneath the lowest rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. The grey hairline was called the thaw and left where it lay. The two lines had not been crossed by any broom in three days, and the broken birth-slates under the rack were entered by nobody. At the turn of the road she told the pot it would do and said the house where another woman would have said I, and when a man come up from the sluice asked what the reed work would want before the week was out she gave the answer to the river.

“Ask it,” she said. “It’ll tell you when it’s had enough. The house has said it.”

The man wiped his wrist on his sleeve and went on down toward the water with an answer he could not spend, and the boy went past him at the edge of the work where no slate lay. That was where Sella had set him all his life when there was work to be looked at: at the edge, where nothing could be written down within reach of him and nothing could be taken up from him without a woman first putting her hand under his back. Below the bank the traps stood in the gaps of the weir with their mouths upriver, and a plank walk went out over the water on posts, and salt had dried white on the posts where the flood had touched them and gone away. The smell of the place came before the sight of it: cold fish, wet rope, tallow smoke and the green mineral edge of the river. A man who walked the traps at dusk went along the boards with his collar up and did not look down at the bank. Nobody spoke his name. He went the way the dusk went on that stretch of water, and the boy did not know him from any other man who used the walk.

A reed-cutter come along the boards with a bundle on his shoulder and a short length of cord looped inside his coat. He set the bundle down on its side on the flat stone where the hearth-maids had stood to bless the traps in Ilmen, straightened the cord on his thumb, and turned it once so that the boy, low in the basket at the edge of the shade, saw the knots standing along it in the small light. He did not show it to anyone. He did not count it where anyone could hear him count. He tied one knot low down with his teeth and a pull of the thumb, put the length of cord back inside his coat, and went to lift another trap. A weir woman with salt dried white at her wrists came along the boards behind him.

“How many went up yesterday?” she said.

“You can ask the cord,” he said.

“I can’t read a cord.”

“No more can the Court,” he said, and went on with the trap.

The boy held the shape of it where he had seen it. It was a tally kept in a material no Court thumb would read: wool or reed-fibre gone grey with handling, knots set at the lengths a man’s arm could judge, a record that would burn if it were put to a flame and that no clerk could take up and enter in a slate because there was nothing on it to enter. He had seen such keeping before and had never once been shown it. There was the grey wool cord at Mira Silt’s waist, touched when a figure was spoken and carrying a second twist beneath it that had never been taken upriver to Nine Pots or lifted at a door. There was the knot-string lying beside her board, the last knot on it with a double turn and a tucked tail and the small weight of a thumb put there by a woman who watched and got no answer. There were six pages in a little tin turned writing-down toward a floorboard in a house the boy could not enter, written in a second hand no Court physician had ever seen; and there was the round pewter tin above a bed nobody used, with a pale fold at its seam and a thumb’s bright rub on the lid, shut and unopened for more mornings than anybody in the district kept count of. Another way of keeping a count in a material an assessor would pass over, gone past him on the plank walk in a man’s coat.

He did not count the knots. He did not read a figure into them, and no figure came with them to be read. He did not lay the cord beside the empty place in the strip where a hand had gone in and had not come out, because two absences laid together make an account and an account wants a payer. There was a word he had heard once said low into a basket, a word with a readable double turn standing beside it and no name laid on the turn; it stayed where it had been kept. He held the reed-cutter’s cord on a place of its own, beside the other second records and not touching them, carried and not counted, worth nothing and exact.

The trap-keeper stood at the lower boards with her board on its side and a cord at her waist, pulling one knot down with her thumb and setting it among the others. When the weir woman asked her a question about the levy she gave a date instead of an answer and then went back to the knots, and when the reed-cutter lifted his bundle she watched what there was to watch at the edge of the shade and got no answer from the basket. She did not ask, and nobody asked her. The shape of the last knot on her cord was legible to the boy without its name, and he did not spend it.

Mira worked the eels at the lower boards where the shade gave out, with pale slime dried in long grey strokes up both sleeves and river silt dried pale in the hollow of her neck. She held her head a little to the right where the top of her left ear had been, and her small smile stayed on her face the way a door is kept on the latch. The weir woman asked her whether the run would be thin now that the hands were out of the strips and the reeds were coming down.

“Thin?” Mira said. “Who’s to say thin? It comes when it comes. Ask the river.”

She moved eel-bones against her thumb while she decided whether to lie and put one away when the deciding was done. A buyer in a coat with the river still in its hem named a figure for two pair. Mira’s thumb went under her apron to the grey wool cord at her waist and stayed there longer than the figure required; shifting a creel showed the second cord twisted twice beneath it, the one that had never been carried upriver to Nine Pots and never lifted at a door. Neither knot was counted by any thumb but her own, and the knot for the thin child upriver was not touched. Her board lay beside the trap-keeper’s knot-string and did not touch it.

She did not look into the basket set at the edge of the shade. She had not looked into it on any of the mornings he could remember, and her not-looking was a keeping set on a place of its own, the opposite of the pricing face across a market and joined to no cord and no figure. No crust passed between the boards and the basket, and the old brown-edged crust under the roof stayed where it had lain since the month before, stiff and past smelling of anything, with the empty place beside it still unnamed. The boy laid Mira’s keeping beside the two women’s records and the reed-cutter’s cord and set no finger across any of them. Each keeping on a place of its own, and the place where the hand had not come back out of the strip stayed empty and entered by nobody, touching none of them. The market downriver sold its reeds and its eels over the top of everything it did not enter, and the day went on without a figure being given for any of it.

By the going of the light the bundles stood grey in the ruts with nobody counting them, and Sella came back up the clay road at the half-pace, going around the space under the lowest rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. She carried him under the Vail kiln-house roof and set him on his bed and not in the Debt Loft, and put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without once looking at his face, and left the thread of drool frozen and unwiped, because nobody under this roof had been given an instruction about the thread. Her hand rested a half-beat longer than the setting needed before it went away, the way a hand rests on a thing it has carried a long way and cannot bring itself to let go of.

She told the fire it was the thaw and turned the coals with the back of a broken pot. “The house will keep it,” she said, where another woman would have said I, and stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand counting something under her breath toward no opening. She pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn, the grey streak pale in the dying light off the coals, and set the pot where it could be found in the dark, and did not look toward the bed. The widow’s mark hung where it hung, untouched; no opening had come in three days and a dusk is not one.

Under the roof the boy turned out the file as a man lays instruments on a towel and found every object where he had left it. Six hearings of the full note stood beside and never among, with no seventh; three near-full answers stood advanced and not paid; three intervals lay on another tray; the told bell lay beside the heard bell in two places that did not touch; the word stayed unspoken with its readable double turn kept without a name; and the hand that had not come back held a place of its own. To these he added the new object: a length of cord gone past him on the plank walk and carried not counted, its knots unread, its tally kept in a material no Court thumb would read. It lay where he put such things, beside the other second records and touching none of them, held for a far-off day when a hand that did not yet exist might lay the private accounts together and call the joining an account.

One hundred and twenty-two stood where one hundred and twenty-one had stood in the morning, exact, unspent and spoken by nobody who would have known what a count of days counted when they said it. The door stood a finger’s width open and the note of water over water came through the gap from a place the reed work had gone down to and come back from. His small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward it and came down. Nothing was joined, named, counted, paid or ended, and the count went on.

The house settled into the small hours the way a vessel settles after it has been filled: not by going still, but by giving up its small noises one at a time. The pot had been set where it could be found in the dark. The coals lay under blind ash with the back of the broken pot resting against the hearth where Sella Vail had left it, and the little-door burn had closed on the handle and then on nothing, and she had gone to her own bed without once looking toward his. Above him the boards kept their old arithmetic: four nails on the near board, four on the next, and a fifth that was not a nail at all but a knot the width of a thumb. He had counted those boards before he had known how to count his own days, and the count had never once changed. Four, four, and a knot. It was the only fifth thing under this roof that anybody would have admitted was part of the floor, and it held nothing, drove nothing, kept nothing from coming apart. He had known men in the other life hang a coat on a nail that was not a nail and call it a nail because the wall needed one. Here nobody called the knot anything. It was simply there, and the boards held.

Cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist. The wool had ridden back from the bare joint and the wind had found the place it had come for, going under the fold at his ear. The crust lay stiff two inches from that ear, brown-edged, past smelling of anything, with the empty place beside it still without a name. No third crust had come into the fold and no third would come tonight. The thread at his mouth had frozen before the light went and nobody under this roof had been given an instruction about the thread, so it stayed where it had frozen, a small white line drawn tight against the corner of his mouth, exact and kept by nobody. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far and came back. He could not lift it. He could not turn it. He could not wipe the thread or put the wool back over the wrist where the cold was finding the bare place. He was a man who had once reduced a shoulder in the dark and he could not keep a fold of wool on his own arm. The joke had stopped being funny around day forty and had not become kinder since.

The door stood a finger’s width open. Through the gap came the note of water over water, the river going downriver beyond the reed beds and the Weir, a sound he had first heard under this roof when he was six days old and had kept on a place of its own ever since, never a hearing, never a vessel’s note, never counted among the six. Through the floor under the blind ash came the other sound, the carried fire taking its coal and giving back four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been. Its payer had been unnamed for a hundred and twenty-two days. The two sounds sat in two separate places and did not meet: the water over water beyond the finger’s-width gap, the fire missing its fifth breath under the floor. Three counts lay in the room and none of them was one count. The river’s note was one. The fire’s four, four and a gap was another. The boards’ four, four and a knot was a third. A man with a slate might have laid the three together and called the matching fours a rule, and the rule would have been worth exactly what the knot was worth. He kept them apart. A likeness laid on a likeness grows a cause, and a cause in this district always arrived with a clean hand at the foot of it.

Somewhere in the room Sella Vail breathed in the way of a woman who has spent the day carrying and has not yet put the carrying down, a breath held a little at the top, let go, held again. He heard it without turning toward it. She had moved her lips along the loaf row at the going of the light without a figure and had not touched the mark, because a shelf is not an opening and a dusk is not one. The mark hung where it hung, thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and she had not touched it for a bed or a basket or a count of days. If she counted now it was under her breath, in the dark, toward no opening that any man could enter. He had stopped trying to hear what she counted. It was not his to take up, and the taking-up of another person’s private number was the oldest trade in this district and the one he had spent his whole new life declining to learn.

The loaf shelf stood in the next room, through a wall and a door that was not this door. It kept two gaps and would keep them until morning: the old space at the row’s end that a neighbour had named as a thing the planting brought in, and the new palm’s breadth that had come in the night of Seed 6 with no scrape, crumb, print or word. Two neighbours had laid the old gap and the empty place at his ear under one appetite in a single breath and gone off without seeing they had joined two rooms that do not touch. He had set their sayings on places of their own and made neither saying his own. The shelf did not reach through the wall. The fold did not reach into the next room. One stiff crust lay two inches from his ear with its empty place beside it, and the shelf’s two gaps lay where they lay, and no finger of his had ever been long enough to lay a line between them, and if it had been he would not have laid it. A line drawn through three absences is a cause with a clean hand at the foot of it, and the clean hand was the thing he had spent two lives refusing to become.

He turned out the file as a man lays instruments on a towel, in the order he had laid them the night before and the night before that, and found every object where he had left it. There was no new object to add today beyond the one already added at the going of the light: the length of cord gone past him on the plank walk at the Weir, carried and not counted, its knots unread, its tally kept in a material no Court thumb would read. It lay on its own place beside the other second records and touched none of them. Beyond that the file was as it had been, and the fact that it was unchanged was the one thing about it he could not file.

Six hearings of the full note stood beside and never among. Five had come from four sources: a cup across a birth room, a pot in the Vail wheel-shed through a wall, the district’s slate twice at the Cross, and a man giving at the Hearth Drawing. The sixth was the small flat knock out of Dessa Silt as the men lifted, from a person, set beside the five and never counted with them. No seventh had come today. No whole vessel’s note had come past the door, the bell at the Cross was a told bell and not a hearing, the wheel and the pots under the roof were notes he had learned to leave where they sounded, and he had not invented one to fill the place where a seventh would have stood. Six stayed six. Beside the hearings lay the told bell in one place and the heard bell in another, two objects in two places that did not touch, and the word forty on a third place of its own, unspoken for a hundred and twenty-two days, its readable double turn kept without a name laid on the turn. He had never said the word in this world and had no intention of saying it to a room full of sleeping people who would have taken it for a figure and entered it.

Three near-full answers stood advanced and not paid: a cup across a birth room, a pot under the wheel-shed wall, a rack pot lifted a hand’s breadth too high and set down again. Three intervals lay on another tray, Stone 13, Stone 17, Stone 25, and beside them the half-beat of Tarn’s check and the stroke that had gone on over the flagstones without the check, two objects on one tray and not a rule. The held breath from Seed 16 sat at the top of each reach where the breath should have gone in and had not gone in, unspent and carried now into a new month, and the loan that could hear was still uncalled. The unforged sentence stood beside it, the one that would call the loan if any man under this roof said it, and nobody had said it. Six counted, the next not counted, the seventh year pressed past the end of the planting and still held at the very edge of the sill. Something must give at the end of that seventh year and nothing had given yet, and the waiting was itself an object on its own place, exact and worth nothing.

The cold foot below the sill stayed where it had been kept: the cold to one woman who would not go toward it, weather to a yard that had gone around it with a glance and a step and no word. He had laid the two tellings of the shut room on a tray of their own and joined neither to the damper’s double bite, though the bite was the truest thing in the house and the least spendable: the iron that held a vessel whole and the iron that could take the hand off a man, with no word in Grathic for both mercies. Jorra Vail had put his bent finger into that cold iron ahead of an unfinished sentence and the household had straightened around his silence the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted. The boy had read the iron once and filed it as an object joined to no kiln. Two men had carried the story of a counting room shut in a war into this yard and a third had only mentioned it, each with a date in it and none of the weight, and he had set the tellings beside the iron without laying one across the other. A cause made from a likeness is the cheapest thing a man can make, and here it would have cost somebody an arm.

The hand that had not come back out of the worked strip held a place of its own. No name, no cause, no body, no figure for what the month had cost. The yard had gone around the empty place with a look and a wide step since the seed came up, and a slate would be found and a figure made if a hand came up in a month or a year, and nothing had come up. He laid nothing across it. Not the cord. Not the shelf’s two gaps. Not the crust two inches from his ear. Two absences laid together make an account and an account wants a payer, and the payer under this roof was a woman breathing in the dark with a half-beat held at the top of each breath, who had carried a boy up from the rack end at the half-pace from every open slate and set him on his bed and not in the Debt Loft.

There were other shapes on their own places, and he passed over them in the dark the way a surgeon passes a hand over a row of instruments without lifting any of them. The grey wool cord at Mira Silt’s waist with the second twist beneath it, neither knot counted, the cord never carried upriver to Nine Pots or lifted at a door. Her board lying beside Orra Weir’s knot-string and not touching it. Six pages in a little tin turned writing-down toward a floorboard in a house he could not enter, written in a second hand no Court physician had ever seen. The round pewter tin above a bed nobody used, with a pale fold at its seam and a thumb’s bright rub on the lid, shut for more mornings than anybody in the district kept count of. The foundling’s head going half a beat late toward a pot set down at the market’s edge, the green sliver rubbed twice with a thumb, the mouth opening on the tail of a sentence and nothing coming out that any clerk could take down. The soft-cuffed clerk at the out-of-line landing with his slate to his forearm for a measure nobody called. Berr Clay’s thumb wiped twice and his finger passed over a figure that was not the figure paid, and the man taking himself out of his own sentence as neatly as a hand out of a field before the dressing goes on. Five record-first hands on five trays and a sixth shape kept off every tray. No sixth sighting of the neat dark hand had come today, and no knot had been counted, and the word stayed unspoken. He did not lay the cord across the half-beat or the half-beat across the thumb. Each shape kept its own place because he had put it there, and because the putting was the only work in this world that nobody could tax.

Nobody under this roof said he was a hundred and twenty-two days old. Nobody had said it at a hundred and twenty-one, or at a hundred and nineteen, or on the day he had come under the roof and a woman had carried him up the yard in a basket and told the fire what to do. There was no word in Grathic that would have made the sentence mean what it would have meant in his mouth, and if there had been, nobody here would have known to say it over a basket. He had stopped expecting to be told. That was not the same as not wanting it. The wanting had gone quiet, the way a wound goes quiet when the nerve in it dies, and he had learned to tell the difference between the two the way he had once learned to tell a quiet abdomen from a relieved one, and the knowledge had not helped him. He was the only adult in this room and him with the hands of a child, and the count went on because there was nobody else to keep it.

He lay with the crust two inches from his ear and the thread frozen at his mouth and the wool riding back from the wrist, and he held the number where he had held the others, exact and unspent and spoken by nobody. A hundred and twenty-two stood where a hundred and twenty-one had stood in the morning. It had cost what all the others had cost and had been paid by nobody who would have known what a count of days was when they said it. The household kept its account badly in loaves and glances and unnamed spaces, moving her lips along a shelf so the days stood as a shape no collector could take up, going around an empty place in a strip with a look and a wide step, letting the fire answer four low pulls and leave the fifth out. He kept his account the other way, one day laid edge to edge against the next in a file nobody could read and nobody had asked for. The more exact it grew the more it isolated him, and he had no hand with which to lay it down and no voice with which to offer it, and if he had had both he would not have offered it, because an account laid on a table in this district is an account that wants a payer.

The note of water over water came through the finger’s-width gap and went on coming. The fire missed its fifth breath under the floor and went on missing it. The boards overhead kept four, four, and a knot. The three counts sat in three places and did not become one, and the payer of the missing breath remained unnamed through a hundred and twenty-two days of naming everything else badly, and the river went downriver toward the reed work that had gone down and come back without a figure. Out beyond the gap the bundles stood grey in the ruts with nobody counting them, and downriver the cord lay inside a man’s coat with its knots unread, and in the next room the shelf kept its two gaps, and three doors down a tin stayed shut above a bed nobody used, and upriver a woman who watched got no answer from a basket. All of it held. None of it was joined.

His small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water and came down. It had gone up an inch toward the worked strip and come down, and toward the loaf shelf and come down, and toward a pot two paces off and come down, and now toward the river beyond the gap, and it came down. The hand was his and small and could close on nothing at two inches. In the other life it had been the hand of a man who could find a vessel in the dark by the note it gave under a knuckle, and it had closed on clamps and on a phone that held a name he would not look at, and had come back from the minivan with wool where a lit rectangle should have been. That hand was gone. This one went up an inch and came down, and the count went on with it.

One hundred and twenty-two stood where one hundred and twenty-one had stood, exact, unspent and spoken by nobody who would have known what it counted when they said it. Six hearings stayed six beside and never among, and no seventh came, and no sixth sighting of the neat dark hand came, and no knot was counted, and no third crust came into the fold, and the word stayed unspoken with its readable double turn kept without a name. The river went over water beyond the finger’s-width gap. The fire missed its fifth breath under the floor. The two sounds sat in two separate places and the two counts did not meet. Nothing was joined, named, counted, paid or ended, and the count went on.

The Chronicle of Chapter 27

Reed came on at Low Kiln without a boundary drawn: the yard turning. Work was a direction, never a figure, a bundle judged by the arm that carried it. A woman who asked what the reeds would cost was sent to the pot with Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there. The house has said it. Sella Vail instructed bucket, broom, pot and fire, never the basket, and said the house where another woman would have said I. The yard went around the unswept lines, the rack space and the place in the strip where a hand went in and did not come out: a look, a wide step and no number. The boy read it as a chart closed over an unopened note --- exact, complete, worth nothing.

Before first grey men came up the Clay Yard and went low-voiced into the back room; Jorra Vail gave them no name. He came through with grey slate dust ground into both forearms past the bent little finger, told a pot You’ll do and withheld its note. His bent finger went into the cold damper ahead of Sella’s unfinished sentence, and the household straightened around his silence. The boy read the iron as holding and taking. Tarn took the broom only after it had been instructed; his seventh year stood held, and his stroke went on over the flagstones without the half-check. Neither man named the other’s silence.

At the market the soft-cuffed clerk sat at the out-of-line landing with his slate to his forearm for a measure nobody called, and the trade went on over him. A foundling in cast-offs stood with silt pale in his wrists and a green sliver on a string at his throat. When a kiln-hand set a pot down his head went toward it half a beat after the boy’s, after the knuckle and note, then stopped; he rubbed the sliver twice and let nothing out that a clerk could take down. No loaf passed and no slate was seen. The boy filed the delay as a thing that comes after the knock and is not a hearing.

On the third day bundles came in armfuls, and at the Weir a reed-cutter carried his tally in a cord, telling a woman who could not read it that the Court could not either. Mira Silt worked the eels, answered a thin run with Ask the river, and let her thumb rest on the cord at her waist; her board lay beside Orra Weir’s knot-string. Each evening Sella set the boy on his bed and not in the Debt Loft, put the fold right, left the thread frozen and unwiped, told the fire it was the thaw and said The house will keep it. Three days stood in his private file --- one hundred and twenty, one hundred and twenty-one and one hundred and twenty-two --- exact and unspent, as the river ran beyond the gap and the fire missed its fifth breath.
