The Seventh of the Green

Days 219–221 · Grain 10–12, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln and the Cross, Sill

The stall goes up and the slate comes out, and the figure goes over the basket twice. Two greens are one colour and two turnings one shape only to a man who wants them to be. The bell gives four, four and a gap, and a body under a roof keeps what the market cannot enter. Ask the pot. Ask the weather. Nobody is told what the hand was holding.

— A saying of the Vail kiln-house, kept over the first market of Grain

— Day 219 —

Grain 10, Y.H. 813 (Tenthday)

The hand lay down against the fold and stayed down where it had been put, the way a chisel lies where a man has set it on a bench and turned away from it. In the small hours under the Vail kiln-house roof in Low Kiln, the boy did not go back into the dark to find a meaning in what had been given there the night before. A holding was a holding; it lay where it lay, and to pick it up and turn it over would only wear the edge. He had taken one rhythm through another and brought the two together on the river’s next low note, and now the hand was down and the two rhythms were apart again and neither had been asked to answer for the other. Nothing had grown. Nothing had reached. There was no second hand under the first to make the keeping into a sign, and the making of a sign would have wanted a payer standing behind it, and no payer stood. He was two hundred and nineteen days old and six months and thirty-nine days, and nobody under any roof in Sarn or Sill or Grath said so, and there was not a slate in the district with a place to enter either number. The wanting of the number went on under the not-expecting of it the way a coal goes on under ash; the two states occupied him without either buying the other a thing.

The day came to him first as a smell and then as a gap in a sound. Hot clay under tallow, and tallow under wet wool, and under the wool the sour mineral breath of the unswept corner the house called the thaw, as if a thing left long enough might earn a season’s name instead of a cause. The kiln drew in fours beyond the wall, taking the air in great mouthfuls and letting it go short; then the gap. Not a bell. A bell is rung and counted and answered, and this was neither; it was a fire keeping itself the way a man keeps his own breath without knowing that he counts it. Under the floor the carried fire gave four low pulls and withheld the fifth, not by a decision of its own but because nothing had been set beneath it to make a fifth possible, and the space where the fifth should have stood had gone on being a space for two hundred and nineteen days with no payer named to it. Through the finger’s-width door the river kept its note of water over water, a fifth low thing never counted among the hearings; overhead the boards kept four nails on the near one, four on the next, and a thumb-width knot where the fifth nail should have stood. Three counts in three places, and none rose up and became the others, because a man who wanted them to agree would put a payer under all three and no payer stood to any of them. The fire went on in the room that warmed him and the river in the room he could not enter, and neither stopped for the ear under the roof.

One stiff leathery brown-edged barley crust lay two inches from his ear in the fold of grey wool, and the empty place beside it was still unnamed and no third had come to stand there and make the row come out even. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the dry heat and was unwiped; nobody under any roof had ever been given an instruction about the thread, and a basket set down was a pot set down and left. Cold had come to cheek and temple and the small bare joint at the wrist where the wool rode back, and stopped there as if it had reached the end of a written order. It went no farther and it did not go away. The afterdrop waited on the far side of a warming nobody in this house would perform. He knew the afterdrop when he saw it, and he wanted the crust, and both were true and the child’s wanting was the louder of the two, and neither was a lesson written upon the other.

The room of the fire and the room of the bed did not touch, and no breathing number in either had ever been offered to the other. There was no count anywhere in the house at this hour, nothing to hold the hand against but the river’s own low interval drawing out and slackening behind the finger’s-width door. He had once reduced a shoulder in the dark by feel alone, following the bone through the swelling until the joint gave and the man’s arm came down quiet at his side; he could not now lift a crust two inches to his mouth. The two facts stood on separate trays and neither was a lesson about the other, and the surgeon’s register took the measure of both and found them exact, complete and worth nothing.

He turned out the file as a man lays instruments on a towel — no towel, no instruments and no case, but the order of the laying was the whole of the discipline. He found it exact, complete and worth nothing. Six hearings of the full note stood beside and never among: five from four sources, a cup across a birth room, a pot in the Vail wheel-shed through a finger’s width of door, the district’s slate twice at the Cross, and the sixth, the small flat knock out of Dessa Silt as the men lifted her, from a person and filed where a thing out of a person must go and never among the sources. No seventh had come in two hundred and nineteen days and none would be invented to fill the empty place, for an empty place kept empty is a finding and a finding filled to make a row come out even is a finding forged.

Three near-full answers stood on their own tray, advanced and unpaid: a pot under the shed wall that answered nearly full with air under the note and a want in it, a rack pot lifted a hand’s breadth too high and set down again, and the knowing the wheel-keeper kept back from a man come up on a wrong wind. Beside them stood three shapings of the pot saying, all sayings and no rule — a pot that sings before its time mostly sings itself out early, the pot that sang early last month not heard from since, and a pot gone quiet on every row come the thaw. Beside them, on a place that did not touch them, stood the fourth telling of the burned-slate word, come over the boards from Slate Row on the breath of barley price and weather, with a hand between it and Jorra Vail’s dust and the two tellings of the counting room shut in the war. Four tellings of a burning were not four witnesses to one fire, and a word about a slate was not a word about a pot. A direction with a date was not a slate opened, and no slate had burned under any roof and no new fire had been lit at the kiln mouth, for a fire would have cost something and the telling cost nothing, and he kept the two apart.

The told bell lay beside the heard, four, four and a gap with nobody crossing himself at either; the told figure lay beside the paid, one in twelve read three times over a basket and still not the figure paid, the third reading gone up the clay road in Berr Clay’s locked box with the true subtraction inside and Hessa Rale’s slate gone up unread with the writing to her sleeve, answering nothing for the man carried out on Day 188. Nine stoppings stood on Reed 5, 8, 11, 14, 17, 20, 23, 26 and 29, objects and not a rule, with no tenth before the seventh year at its very end and still ungiven; the Seed 16 breath was unspent and the loan uncalled. Three held changes stood on three places: the fraction from the morning of Day 161, when the hand waited at the iron for a sentence to finish; the beat that stayed under the board on Day 166, the reason standing between knowing the board by heart and being told not to look; the half-check made on the flagstones on Tallow 22. The small dry scrape from the loft stood on a fourth, the pale line at the glaze-room hand’s gum on a fifth, and the held inch on a sixth with all its answers, each filed on a place of its own and none standing up to call itself a longer reach, a key, a power or a rule. He laid nothing across them and named no payer to any of them. The pan’s gone round ring stayed on a tray of its own beside the loaf shelf’s two gaps and the fold’s one crust and the field’s empty place, and he did not lay a finger on any of them. No third gap came into the yard, no fourth likeness stood up to call itself a cause, and nothing was filled in to make a row come out even. Nothing in the file had been joined, named, opened, called, spent or ended.

Sella Vail came to the fire before she came to the basket. The instruction went out to the wool, the fire, the pot, the broom, the running water and the river wind, and never to the basket; she said the house where another woman would have said I, and told the wind to keep it off his ears because it was the river wind. Her eye went once to the clay jar cold under its glaze on the wall shelf and her hand started toward it and shut half over nothing without a touch, the little-door burn closing on air the way it closed on everything that came within reach of it. She stood with her back to the basket and moved her lips along the loaf row, and the row stood with two unnamed gaps and she gave nobody a figure. Her finger lay on top of the shelf’s front edge where any woman in the district could see it lying, and the charcoal tally under the board stayed unread, uncounted and uncopied, because a woman’s back turned was not the same thing as a finger beneath it and the reason stood between the two, unspoken.

She told the fire it was the thaw, turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and said “The house will keep it” where another woman would have said I. She stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand counting under her breath toward no opening — small intake, hold, letting go — and pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the burn, so that the grey streak at her temple showed pale off the coals. Then she set the pot rim out with its handle two inches beyond the reaching hand and let her hand rest a half-beat longer at the setting than the setting required, and did not look toward the bed. The half-beat stood where it stood and asked nothing and was entered nowhere. At a morning and a shelf she did not touch the widow’s mark; it hung on its cord at her throat where it had hung since the first firing, touched once before the kiln on Ember 1 and at no hour since, and there had been no opening in thirty-nine days, because a morning and a shelf and a basket are not openings. Her private roll stayed unshown and Dessa’s cost stayed where it had been put down and never entered.

Garl Vail was kept in objects and not in a story, which is the way a house keeps a man it cannot collect. The salt-stiff coat held a broader man’s shape on its peg with the crease at the left shoulder; the seat kept its twenty-year hollow and its lean to that side; the clean dark line where the Ash ridge turned and the new finer hairline of grey beside it called the thaw lay unswept, and a line left where it is is still a line kept. The proverb about leaving the crack showing had gone years ago to a pot with no crack in it, and the debt against a firing of tiles lay where it lay, circled without a name and worse for being uncalled. He had not been collected, and the keeping of him was maintenance and nothing more, and nobody under this roof had asked what the debt was for. His name had not been spoken here for two hundred and nineteen days, and no lesson was made of any of it and no collector was called.

Asked in the morning, the hand went up an inch toward the note and came down at once against the fold — a miss with no failure hidden under it, an answer refused set on a tray of its own, filed on the sixth place with no payer named and nothing standing up out of it as a reach, a hearing, a seventh, a stopping, a payment, a held change, a key, a power or a rule. He could not close the two inches to the pot handle she had set out, lift his chin the two inches to the crust or speak a number that anybody under any roof would take down. Exactness and humiliation were one motion in that small hand, and both were true, and neither was a sign.

Word went up the clay road before first grey: stalls going toward the Cross, a neighbour ahead with boards on a barrow, the reading block being made ready under the moot-post for Grain’s first Account-day. The market came first as boards on a road and only afterward as a reading. Over the boards went the price of barley and the weather, two sayings travelling together without either paying for the other, and the boy filed them as a direction with a date and not as a summons to himself. A neighbour at the threshold with a pot hitched against her hip asked what the stalls would want come the tenth and was told to ask the pot, the house had said it, and to ask the weather too, and went away level with nothing priced, refused or entered. The boy took the question and its answer as a direction and a direction only; no number crossed the yard and no slate was opened under any roof. Two hundred people would lean to a block and straighten and go back to their pots before the morning was out, and a figure read aloud over the top of a basket would still not be the figure paid. The printed one in twelve stood unchallenged; Berr’s box was locked up the road with the true subtraction inside; Hessa’s slate had gone up unread with the writing to her sleeve; the soft-cuffed clerk at the out-of-line landing was still set for a measure nobody called. All of it stayed offstage in the boy’s file and none of it reached back to the basket.

Sella carried him up at the half-pace, and she kept the half-pace from every open slate. Woodsmoke went before her in long low bars and the river wind came behind her with the taste of water over water; the carrying arm was a record in muscle that remembered weight and not cost, and had carried him up and down this yard for two hundred and nineteen days. In the Clay Yard she went wide around the pan’s gone round ring, the bare dark trough, the space beneath the lowest rack with its broken birth-slates, the field’s empty place and the place the carriers crossed, each with a look and a wide step and no number. The seventh green was owed as a colour at a silent woman’s mouth and the seventh sighting of the foundling as a shape at the market’s edge, and neither had come early, and no loaf, slate or sale had crossed the yard before the first market.

Her half-pace stopped short of the reading block. She set the basket in the moot-post’s finger’s width of shade where no slate lay and stood a half pace off, and gave no instruction to the basket. The cold iron of the post waited at his cheek; the stalls were going up across the square and the block stood ready with nobody yet called to it, and the boy had not been carried to the block. The market was arriving at a body that had not been carried to it. He lay with his hand down against the fold and one stiff brown-edged crust two inches from his ear, its empty place still unnamed and no third coming, and the thread of drool frozen and unwiped at his mouth. Two hundred and nineteen stood exact, complete, unspent and spoken by nobody, and the count went forward into the first market of Grain with nothing settled and nothing ended.

The basket lay in a finger’s width of shade cast by the moot-post, and Sella Vail stood a half pace off from it with the little-door burn on the back of her hand shut on nothing. The boy had been carried to the edge of the Cross and no nearer. The shade was a thin thing, no wider than a finger laid flat against the iron, and she had set him at the edge of it where no slate lay and where the reading could not be said to be addressed to a basket, because a basket is a pot set down and a pot set down hears what the market says over it and answers nothing. The cold iron waited at his cheek. Beyond it the square went on assembling itself out of boards and noise: the reading block stood wet with the morning under the post’s thin shade, two hundred people would lean to it before the bell had done, and eight pigs would go over the weighing board with a slap of wet hooves that the boy filed as a direction and not as a number addressed to him. She gave the instruction to the wool and to the river wind and to nothing that could hear her in the basket. Keep it off his ears, she said, the way she had said it for two hundred and nineteen days, and It’s the river wind, and stood a half pace off from every open slate with her burn closed and her hands not still.

He took the morning in as he took everything now, through the body that carried him and the ear that could not be carried away from it. The block was a plank set on two trestles, wet at one end where the boards had been washed, and the figures that went into the air above it belonged to the moot and to the households that came late and to nobody who lay in a basket at its edge. A figure read aloud was a figure spoken into weather; it did not travel down a finger’s width of shade and enter a child who could not speak a number. Two hundred people leaned and straightened and went back to their pots. The boy kept the printed one in twelve standing unchallenged on its own place, and kept beside it, on a place that did not touch, the knowledge that the figure read over a basket was still not the figure paid. He laid nothing across the two and named no payer to either. The man carried out on Day 188 had gone up the road breathing, and no figure, collector or slate had known what he cost, and the reading that morning over the wet block had not mended that or made it any worse. A reading is an account going into the air. It does not go down into the ground after a man and come back with an answer.

Berr Clay came up short off the clay road as he had come up short off it a hundred mornings, stopping before the reading block with the red wool cord of his office folded square under his coat instead of worn across it. He smelled of hot clay and, under that, of nothing at all, which is the most expensive smell in Grath. The green of tasted lead glaze stood at one corner of his mouth, a small sweetish stain the colour of a thing that had been put in and taken out again; he had tasted it before he came and would taste it again before he left, and the masters read the habit as thoroughness and Sella read it as nerves. His hands were large and clean. He wiped his thumb twice on his apron before he touched nothing, once for the slate and once for the figure that would be on it, and held a narrow slate unopened to his forearm with the writing turned inward where the crowd could not take a figure off it. Then he passed a finger over the figure and let his eyes rest on the writing a breath longer than the writing required, and only then raised them to the crowd. The order of reaching — thumb, slate, finger, eyes — went down into the boy’s private file as an old shape on an old place, and he did not file it twice and did not let it stand up beside the other hands and call itself a rule.

“One in twelve, as the notice says,” Berr Clay said low into the top of the crowd, and the crowd leaned in because a low voice in a moot makes people come close rather than fall quiet. The figure went twice over the basket, once for the moot and once for the households that had come late, and went nowhere that the boy could enter. “The levy is called,” he said. “It is not called by me.” To a man at the rail who had brought a number up out of a fist and wanted to know what the month’s firing would want now the kilns had been brought up, he said that a number goes into a slate and not into a bundle, and that if your house wanted it again there would be a slate at the watch — your house, never I — taking himself out of his own sentence as neatly as a surgeon takes his hand out of a field before the dressing goes on. The number went from the fist to his mouth to the shoulder of a man going up toward High Kiln, and arrived nowhere. The true subtraction went back up the road in the box in his house, locked, and the printed figure stood where it had always stood. He never looked into the basket, and went off toward High Kiln with the cord folded square and the narrow slate under his arm, believing he had done the district a kindness. The boy kept the twice-wiped thumb and the finger over the figure on their own tray and did not join them to the clerk’s hand at the landing or to any of the other record-first hands in the district, for eight likenesses standing in a row do not become one hand and a row of hands is not an account until somebody finds a payer to stand under it.

The bell went four, four and a gap. It struck longer than a church bell, and nobody crossed himself at it, not at the reading and not at the stroke that was missing, and the heard bell lay down beside the told bell of the Long Night on a place of its own where the two could be looked at separately and not mistaken for one ringing. Four was four in the fire under the floor, four in the nails overhead, four in the bell, and the gap stood where the fifth should have stood in each of the three places and rose up in none of them. The boy let the counts lie. A man who wanted them to agree would have to put a payer under all three, and no payer stood to any.

Hessa Rale went up the clay road before noon with Berr Clay’s narrow slate held edge-on to her forearm and the writing turned to her sleeve. Her hand arrived at the slate before her eyes arrived at the crowd; the small flat correction of the strap came first, then the turn of the wrist that brought the edge round, and only afterward did she take in the square. Her coat was shortened at the skirt for the road’s mud and the mud had dried on it in a pale scurf she did not brush off, because a runner’s coat is a tool and nobody scours a tool for its own sake. Two straps crossed her chest, the shorter one let out for the long road. When a woman at the rail asked what she carried, she said, “I don’t read it. I carry it. There’s a difference, and the road doesn’t care which.” To the woman who wanted more she said, “Cross to Greybank. Reading goes up before noon. I’ve got a road to walk,” and went on with the pace of a person who has made a bargain with distance and keeps it without thinking. She answered the question put and not the one behind it, and she was done with the conversation when the road was done with it, without rudeness and without apology. No figure came off the slate. The boy kept her order of reaching on its own place and did not file it twice and did not make her a reader because she carried a figure she would not look at; a runner who does not read is not the same object as a clerk who does, and a slate going up the road with its writing inward is a thing somebody else set down.

Below the north bridge, at the out-of-line landing where a fifth step should have stood, the soft-cuffed clerk sat still set for a measure nobody had called. His cuff was grey from washing and grey again by midday, and the slate lay to his forearm with the writing inward where no goer-by could take a figure off it. His hand went to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls, and a buyer’s number went into it before he looked up to find the man who had said it. Nobody stepped around him and nobody took him off. The market sold its pots and its eels over the top of him as water goes over a stone that lies where a stone lies, and the stone does not rise and does not ask what it is set there for. He was not Marra, not the foundling, not the man carried out on Day 188, and he was joined to none of them. The boy set the inward order of that hand apart from the runner’s inward order and kept the two apart from Berr’s twice-wiped thumb; two records going inward are two records going inward, and a row of them will call itself an account if a man leaves it standing long enough to want an answer from it.

Across the square at the pot board a woman stood looking at pots and said nothing all morning. There was clay under her nails in the small crescents, and a pale line across the back of one hand where something had once been worn tight and long enough to leave its mark, and the sweetish green of lead glaze in the crease beside her lip. She turned a cup a quarter without lifting it and set it back exactly where it had stood, and went off with the light. The boy looked at the green and called it green. It was the seventh such colour to come to the mouth of a silent woman at a pot board, and he filed it as a colour on a place of its own beside the six behind it, with a finger between it and the hearings and no name spoken over it. He gave the woman no name and the colour no ear, because a colour at a mouth is not a hearing of the full note and a seventh of a colour is not a seventh of the full note. The pale line on the back of her hand went on its own place too, and he did not lay it across the line read at the well on Day 187 or the line on the carried man on Day 188, for a pale line is a pale line and a row of them is not a cause. The pot board kept the shallow print where the cup had stood and gave nobody a figure.

At the market’s edge stood a shape in cast-off clothes worn the way a uniform is worn by a boy who was never measured for one: a man’s shoulders on a child’s frame, breeches cut off at the knee, silt dried pale in the creases of the wrists, a gap where a front tooth had been, and a green sliver of lead glaze on a string at the throat that caught the light when the shape turned and went dull when it turned back. Nobody spoke to it and it spoke to nobody. When a pot was set down near the eel shade its head went toward the sound a half-beat after the boy’s own head had gone, after the knuckle and after the note, and stopped in the middle of the turn; a thumb went to the sliver and rubbed it, and the mouth opened on the tail of a kiln-hand’s sentence and let nothing out that any clerk with a slate could take down. No loaf passed anywhere within reach of the basket. No slate came near it and no sale was made. The boy filed the half-turn as the seventh such shape on a place of its own beside the six behind it, after the knock and not before it, never joined to his own turning, and gave it no name. The sale to a clerk of the Waiting Room stayed years ahead of the basket and was not brought early into a market the shape had only walked through; the loaf a man from Slate Row had put into a pocket holding no slate went on gathering interest in an account the child could not read, and he left the interest to gather.

He took the two things apart as a surgeon takes apart a wound, separating what lay next to what without pretending the two had grown together. The seventh green was a colour. The seventh shape was a shape. A colour is not a hearing, and a shape that turns a half-beat late is not a seventh of the full note. Neither was a tenth stopping standing before the seventh year at its very end, and neither was a payment, a cause, a name or a romance. Six greens behind the seventh and six shapes behind the seventh made a row of two separate rows, and a fourth likeness does not stand up and call itself a rule, because a rule is an account that has found a payer, and no payer stood to either row. The hearings remained six, five from four sources and the sixth out of a person, and no seventh had come to stand beside them or among them, and none was invented to fill an empty place kept empty. The market offered all its withholdings at once — the figure read and the figure paid, the cord folded under the coat, the slate going up unread, the clerk set for an uncalled measure, the green at a mouth, the shape at the edge — and not one of them could be entered by a child who could not close two inches on the rim of the basket that held him. He set each on its own place and laid nothing across any of them. Nothing in the file had been joined, named, opened, called, spent or ended.

Six greens behind a seventh green made a row of greens; six shapes behind a seventh shape made a row of shapes; a row is not an account until somebody finds a payer to stand under it, and no payer stood under either row. The woman had no name in the narration and the shape had none, and the sale to a clerk of the Waiting Room stayed years ahead of the basket and was not brought early into a day that had no market in it.

And beside the dust and the two shut-room tellings, with a hand between the three, the burned-slate word stood where it had stood: a direction arrived nowhere, four times told, no cause, no hearing, no seventh, no stopping, no payment, nothing a clerk could chart, advanced and not paid. Four tellings of a burning are not four witnesses to one fire, and a word about a slate is not a word about a pot. No slate burned under any roof in these three days and no new fire was lit at the kiln mouth: a fire would have cost something and the telling cost nothing, and the boy kept the two apart. No third gap came in the returning line, no fourth likeness made a cause, and nothing was filled to make a row come out even.

Then he put the question to his hand. Sella was gone to her bed and there was no breathing number anywhere in the house — nothing to hold against but the rhythm that came through the finger’s-width door, the low note of water over water drawing out and slackening, and the next low note coming when it came. In the other room her breaths had gone small intake, hold, letting go at her fire, a count she keeps toward no opening and has never offered to any ear under this roof. The two rooms do not touch and neither do the two rhythms. The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note and stayed; he took it through one of those counted breaths and kept it through the little silence after her count had ended and brought it down on the river’s next low note. The two-rhythm coordination was remembered, not a change made again. It was a holding and a holding is a holding, set down like a tool: not a longer reach, not a miss, not a hearing, not a seventh, not a stopping, not a payment, not one of the three held changes, not a key, not a power and not a rule, filed on its sixth place with no payer named to it. He still could not close the two inches to the pot handle Sella had set out, lift his chin two inches to the crust, or speak a number that anybody under any roof had been asked to believe. Exactness and humiliation were one motion in that small hand and both were true, and neither stood up to call itself a sign.

Two hundred and twenty-one stood exact, complete, unspent and spoken by nobody in Sarn, in Sill or in Grath. The carried fire gave four low pulls and withheld its fifth, its payer unnamed for two hundred and twenty-one days; the river kept its fifth low note through the finger’s-width door; overhead the boards kept four nails, four and a thumb-width knot. Three counts in three places, and none rose up and became another, because a man who wanted them to agree would have to put a payer under all three and no payer stood to any of them. The seventh year stood at its very end and had still not given; the seventh green was a colour and the seventh shape a shape, gathered and unjoined; Grain 20, eight mornings off, waited for the second Account-day and market and was not brought early into the small hours. The widow’s mark hung where it hung with no opening in forty-one days; the shelf kept its two gaps and the fold its one crust with the empty place beside it still unnamed; the charcoal stayed unread and the stone cold in its loft pot. Nothing was joined, named, opened, called, spent or ended.

The fire went on in the room that warmed him and the river in the room he could not enter, and neither stopped for the ear under the roof. The hand came down on the river’s next low note after the house’s count and its little silence, the thread frozen and unwiped at his mouth, one stiff brown-edged crust two inches from his ear with its empty place still unnamed, and the count went forward exact and unspent into Day 222, Grain 13, with nothing settled and nothing ended.

The Chronicle of Chapter 56

Three days went over the Vail roof, and the tenth brought Grain’s first market. The boy was two hundred and nineteen days old on the first and two hundred and twenty-one on the last; nobody said either. Each day came as a smell --- hot clay, wet wool, the sour corner --- then as a gap, the kiln drawing in fours and stopping, not a bell. One crust lay two inches from his ear, its empty place unnamed; the thread froze unwiped; cold stopped at cheek and wrist.

Sella set the basket in the moot-post’s thin shade where no slate lay. Berr Clay came up short, wiped his thumb twice, passed a finger over the figure and read one in twelve twice over the basket, saying the levy was called and was not called by him, and your house, never I. He went off with the box locked and the subtraction inside, believing he had done a kindness. Hessa carried the slate up unread; the bell went four, four and a gap. The district’s arithmetic changed and could not be changed back; the figure read was not the figure paid.

The market’s one change came as two sightings. At the pot board a silent woman with clay in her nails, a pale line across one hand and green glaze beside her lip turned a cup a quarter without lifting it and set it back where it stood. At the market’s edge a shape in cast-offs with a man’s shoulders and a green sliver on a string that caught the light, then turned toward a set-down pot a half-beat late and stopped. He filed the green as a colour and the shape as a shape on places that do not touch: no eighth, no name, no cause.

The eleventh and twelfth went back to ordinary tending. Sella set the pot handle two inches beyond the hand, kept her finger above the charcoal and left the widow’s mark untouched --- no opening in forty-one days. Garl was kept in objects: salt coat, hollow seat, a proverb gone to a pot, an uncalled tile debt. Tarn kept six where the house could check and the seventh at the lip, stopped the ear-sentence short of the calling word; no collector came. A wrong-wind voice let one pot saying stand as a saying still.

Six times the hand went up an inch and came down: at once and short of a breath on the tenth, before the counted breath and then to the river’s note on the eleventh, at once under conditions that had answered and then, on the twelfth, through Sella’s counted breath and the silence after it, down on the river’s next note. It was remembered, not a power. The file on the last night was exact, complete and worth nothing; he still could not close two inches. Two hundred and twenty-one went forward, unspent and spoken by nobody.
