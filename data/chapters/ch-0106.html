The Tenth Figure and the Cold Iron

Days 369–371 · Stone 10–12, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, Sill

The bell gives four, four and a gap. The slate gives the same figure twice. Neither of them has ever told a mother what it cost.

— A saying of the Clay Yard, Low Kiln, said at the reading block

— Day 369 —

Stone 10, Tenthday, Y.H. 813

The hand outside the wool had just come down on answer three hundred and twelve and lay flat against the fold as a man lays down a tool where the next hand will find it without looking, and the room went on around it the way a room goes on around a thing put away. The boy lay on the bed at the low angle of the loaf shelf under the Vail kiln-house roof, in Low Kiln, Sill, Grath, in the small hour before light, and this morning had a date that no roof under that sky would speak aloud: Stone 10, the first Account-day and market of the deep-winter month. He kept the date exactly, as he kept all dates, and did not stand it up as a line for anybody’s foot to cross. The file lay open on the towel at the head of the bed with every gap left gap. Nothing in the room had been moved for him or taken away, and the small satisfaction of that cost him nothing, which was how he knew it was not peace.

Through the finger’s-width door the river kept its note of water over water, a low thing over a lower thing, muffled as a voice is muffled by a shut door, and over it from bank to bank lay a winter skin put on by the cold and taken off by the cold, found where it had been put and not made again, sitting on its own place beside the low note and not upon it. Under the floor the carried fire gave four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have stood, its payer unnamed three hundred and sixty-eight days; overhead the boards kept four nails on the near one, four on the next and a thumb-width knot where a fifth might have been driven and was not. Three counts in three places and none rose into another, for agreeing any two would have wanted a payer put under both and none stood; the bell’s gap stayed in its square and was not folded into them. Three hundred and sixty-eight stood exact, six months and one hundred and eighty-eight days told by nobody, a whole year finished and named by no roof, and nothing under any roof had altered by one mark on account of it. Fire’s fifth stood unpaid the same number; the name of the man whose coat kept his shape on the peg behind the door was unspoken the same number. Nothing from the seventh, eighth or ninth of the month had crossed under this roof as sign, lesson, mending, cause, payment or payer, and the mark at her throat had not been touched on those days; a market-day made no eighth opening due.

The surgeon’s register took the body in its old order before the light came, because the light would not wait and the body would. One stiff leathery brown-edged barley crust lay two inches from his ear, gone harder and browner in the dry cold, its corner standing a hair off the wool; the empty place beside it kept its name to itself and no third had come to make the row come out even. A thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the dry kiln heat and was unwiped, because nobody under any roof in Sill, in Grath, had ever been given an instruction about the thread, and a basket set down was a pot set down and left. Cold had stopped at cheek, temple and the small bare joint of the wrist where the wool rode back, as though it had come to the end of a line written out for it with neither an order to go farther nor an order to go away. The Afterdrop waited beyond all three, past a warming nobody in this house would perform.

He knew the composed face that was not composure, knew it the way he had once known it across a table in a white room: a cold patient cannot clot, will seem better before he is worse, must be warmed from the outside and slowly, never hurried, never rubbed, never set too near the fire; pink returning to a face is the surface still cold and not the core, and the core gives heat up in the reverse order to the one in which it takes it back, which comes sooner than anybody likes. He knew all of it exactly and it was worth nothing at two inches of air. The want underneath was louder and would not be argued down: lift the crust the two inches to his mouth, wipe the thread, set a hand that would close on the pot handle where the fingers could reach it. Both were true, the second louder, neither won, and the quarrel between them was the work. He had reduced a thousand shoulders in a room that smelled of disinfectant and could not lift a crust to his own mouth; here he could not keep a fold of wool over one ear. He could still find the joke funny without laughing, which was a way of laughing that cost less and kept the same blade in it.

In the first grey Sella Vail came to the hearth before she came to the basket, the way she had come to it at the going of the light and at every morning for three years. Fire before basket; the instruction to the wool, the pot and the river wind and never to the basket. “It’s the thaw,” she told the coals, and turned them with the back of a broken pot. The thaw lay unswept at the Vail corner with its finer grey beside it, thickened in the month’s dry cold, and she had not swept it in two winters and was not sweeping it now. “The house will keep it,” she said, where another woman would have said I. She stood a moment counting a small intake, a hold and a letting-go toward no opening, giving neither gap a figure; her finger lay on top of the loaf shelf so the charcoal tally underneath stayed unread, and her lips moved along a two-gapped row and figured neither gap. Her eye went once to the clay jar standing cold under lead glaze, and her hand shut half over nothing, the little-door burn on the back of her left hand closing on air. The widow’s mark hung where it hung on the cord at her throat, the cord oiled where a working thumb had touched it ten thousand times, and it was not touched on a market-day, for a hearth and a basket and a reading are not openings, and seven openings in seven months are seven and not a rule, with no eighth made due and the empty place where one might have stood not dug for or filled.

At the pot board she lifted the twice-mended pot with her thumb first where the wire crossed the crack, held the weight a half-beat before she let it come on the handle, and left the hairline showing between old-brass staple and tighter wire. “Leave the crack showing. People trust a thing they can see has been handled,” she told the pot. “If it’s worth mending it’s worth mending twice. The second time is the real one,” she told the fire. Both sayings went to the pot and the fire and never to a person, improved in the keeping and very likely not as good as the man had said them, and repair stayed apart from restoration, for a restored thing hides the hand that handled it and a mended thing leaves that hand showing. Garl Vail was kept in objects and not in a story: the salt-stiff coat with the left-shoulder crease on its peg, the hollow wheel-seat nobody was allowed to fill, the clean dark line at the corner, the unforged collector sentence lying at the kindling with no name on the man who had turned back at the frozen ford and no figure on the debt, and the tile debt circled without a name and worse for being uncalled.

She turned the dressed grain under wool with a touch lighter than a woman would use on a sleeping child, her fingers going in and coming out without closing, no figure lifted off it and no handful. Then she set the pot rim two inches beyond the reaching hand, with a half-beat at the setting and no look toward the bed. The half-beat was the distance between intention and arrest, bought at nothing and not a gift, for a gift wants a giver standing and none stood. She lifted the basket as a woman lifts a pot filled to the brim, weight taken before the handle, and carried him down out of the kiln-house into the Clay Yard at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, neither hurrying nor stopping, with the whole of her attention on the level of the wicker and none of it on the date the boy kept under the wool. Instruction went to the wool, the river wind and the fire she would bank against their coming back, and never to the basket. “Keep it off his ears,” she told the wool. “It’s the river wind.” The half-pace said a slate might be open anywhere and a slate was an account and an account had a hand waiting at it; the level of the basket said a pot filled must not be slopped. Nothing in the step said a tenth arithmetic was earned, and the boy had long ago stopped asking the step to say it, which was not the same as not listening for it.

At the Cross the market had come on like water coming over a stone, and the reading block under the moot-post stood wet with the morning. At the moot-post’s finger’s width of shade she set the basket down where no slate lay, the way a pot is set down out of the draught, level, with the whole of its weight on the flat of the ground. The shade was a thin thing, no wider than a finger, and it lay where the post’s own edge cut the light; she chose it as she chose the cool side of a hearthstone, without thinking about choosing. His cheek took the cold iron of the post through the basket’s wicker, and the iron was colder than the river wind, and the iron did not give. She put the fold right under his chin without looking into the basket, straightened the wool with the back of one knuckle, and went half a pace off and stood there through everything that followed. Half a pace was far enough that a man could read a figure over him and near enough that the house could answer for him if a hand were laid on the wicker. She said nothing to the basket at any hour of the reading, and if anybody had asked her why she would have told them to ask the pot.

Eight pigs went over the wet boards with the small clumsy scrambling sound pigs make on boards, and a man with a pole kept their heads down, and nobody counted them aloud. People brought their figures to a heap of turnips and left them standing side by side without agreeing, as they had done at every market since the boy could remember and as the district would do after he was gone. Over the Ilm, from bank to bank, the winter skin lay where the cold had put it and where the cold would take it off; it was not a fourth covering under any roof and had no hand that laid it and no payer standing to be asked, and it was not spent on this month’s first Account-day. He kept it on its own place beside the low note and did not make a sign of it.

By the time Berr Clay came up short off the clay road the two hundred had leaned in to the block and were leaning back again. He came up short the way he always came up short, stopping before he reached the block so that the crowd had to turn to him, with the red wool cord of his office folded square under his coat instead of worn across it, and the sweetish green of tasted lead glaze at one corner of his mouth. Under the hot clay of him was the smell of nothing at all, which is the most expensive smell in Grath: a man who smells of nothing has had the sweating and the dust and the ordinary evidence of a body taken off him by somebody whose accounts are neat. His hands were large and clean. He wiped his thumb twice on his apron before he touched nothing, once for the slate and once for the figure that would be on it, held a narrow slate unopened against his forearm with the writing inward, passed a finger over the figure through the cloth of his own sleeve, and let his eyes rest on the writing a breath longer than the writing required. He did not raise his voice. The crowd leaned in to hear him, which was why he never raised it.

“One in twelve, as the notice says,” he said low into the goers-by. He sent the figure over the basket twice, once for the moot and once for the late households, and his eyes passed across the top of the wicker and did not go into it. “The levy is called. It is not called by me.” To a man who had pushed a number up out of a fist and held it there, he said, “If your house wants it again, there’ll be a slate at the watch” — your house, never I, the house standing in for the man who had spoken and the man who had signed. He took himself out of his own sentence as a surgeon takes a hand out of a field before the dressing goes on: the sentence stood and the hand was gone and there was nobody in it to be asked. The crowd took the figure the way a crowd takes a figure, with a sound like a held breath let go in many places at once, and went back to the pigs.

He said something more to the block, low, in the tone of a man setting a cup where a cup belongs: kindness was entered, he said, or a kindness had been done and entered, the words too small to carry past the block and the post’s cold iron. The block answered nothing. It was a block and had no answer in it, and he had not expected one. He went off up the clay road with the cord still folded square under his coat, the narrow slate under his arm and the box locked, Stone’s first true subtraction riding inside beside the three of Frost, the first, second and third of Bone, and the first, second and third of Ash. Ten arithmetics of the year now stood behind one printed figure. He believed he had done a kindness. There was no anger in his face and no triumph in it and nothing a crowd could have fastened on if it had wanted to, and by the time the pigs had been weighed he was a direction up the road and not a man standing anywhere to be asked.

Ten readings were ten and not one account. The figure read was still not the figure paid and the space between had no signature. A doctrine is an account that has found a payer, and no payer stood under the ten; the finding sat on a place of its own with a finger between it and every other kept thing, and the boy did not make a lesson of it, a grievance, a sign or a power. Nothing in the reading answered the hand that had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out, or the man carried out on Day 188 with his cost entered nowhere, or the cold foot below the sill, or the carried fire’s missing fifth, or the burned slate at five, or the shut-room tellings at two, or the room shut against the cold behind the damper. There was no answer in the box and no answer in the crowd that had gone back to the pigs. He had lain under a slate while the district’s arithmetic changed where two hundred people stood and could not be changed back, and had not been asked and had not asked. The quiet exactness of the field was the whole of what he took away, and it was worth nothing at two inches of air, and that was its honesty.

Before noon Hessa Rale took the slate up the road toward Greybank, and took it at the pace of a person who has made a bargain with distance and keeps it without thinking. The small flat correction of the strap came first, the second strap let out for the long stretch, the turn of the wrist bringing the edge round, and only then did her eyes go to the crowd; her hand was at the slate before either. The dried pale scurf of road-mud stood unbrushed on the skirt she had shortened for the road, because a runner’s coat is a tool and nobody scours a tool for its own sake. The slate lay edge-on to her forearm with the writing turned to her sleeve, the thumb along the grain of the frame and nothing pressing where the writing was, not concealment but the manner of carrying a slate so the writing was not rubbed and no goer-by took a figure off it. To a woman by the rail who asked what she carried she answered the question put and not the one behind it: “I don’t read it. I carry it. There’s a difference, and the road doesn’t care which.” To the one who wanted more she said, “Cross to Greybank. Reading goes up before noon. I’ve got a road to walk,” and went on, leaving the rest of the conversation standing where it had been set down, without rudeness and without apology. No figure came off the slate. The boy took the order of her reaching apart as a surgeon takes a field apart — hand to the record before eyes to the person, eyes before mouth — and kept it on its own place as the eighth such hand with no tenth, joined to none and not made a reader by the arithmetic that had gone up the road behind her.

Below the north bridge, at the out-of-line landing where a fifth step should have stood, the soft-cuffed clerk stayed set for a measure nobody called, his cuff grey from washing and the grey worked into the seam at the wrist, his slate turned inward and his hand to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls. The market sold over him as water over a stone, and went away leaving him where he stood, and he did not take himself off and nobody took him off. Across the square at the pot board a silent woman with clay dried pale in the crescents of her nails and a pale line across the back of one hand turned a cup a quarter without lifting it and set it back exactly where it had stood; the sweetish green of lead glaze sat at one corner of her mouth. At the market’s edge a small shape in cast-offs caught the light on a green sliver and went dull. Each was a seventh with no eighth, a colour and a shape on places of their own with a finger between them, not joined to Berr’s green at his own mouth, not joined to one another, not to a loaf, a slate, a sale, a hearing, a cause or a romance, and no second look was given to either. They were never Fenna in the telling and never Ulda, for a colour is not a name and a shape is not a house. No ownerless voice came over the boards on this morning, and the Ash 5 joining and the Ash 17 and Ash 23 directions stood where they had been put and did not repeat and bought no lesson.

The bell went four, four and a gap with nobody crossing himself, its long note spending itself into the common air above the pigs and the turnips and the two hundred. The heard bell stayed in its square beside the told bell of the Long Night, and its gap was not folded into the three counts under the roof and was not made a seventh of the full note; six hearings of the full note stood beside and never among, the seventh place empty, the sixth the small flat knock out of as the men lifted her, filed where a thing out of a person must go. The boy’s own count went to three hundred and sixty-nine exact, six months and one hundred and eighty-nine days told by nobody. The tenth arithmetic rode up the clay road in Berr’s box behind the first nine, a tenth reading and not a tenth account, and the figure already stood twice over the basket with no signature on the space.

The hand outside the wool came up an inch toward the bell’s long note as it finished under the morning, the way a hand goes up in a field it has learned not to disturb. It did not reach, and the inch was not a reach; most of what a hand does in a field is wait, and he had once taken a hand into a cavity slowly, with the whole of the attention, and knew the difference between the moving and the waiting. He took a small intake from the morning whose ground does not touch him — the wicker had its own floor between him and the square — held the interval whole on the note’s own finishing, let the intake go as the note ended, and came straight down and laid itself back against the fold as a man puts away a tool where the next hand will find it without looking. The note had done before the hand came down, so the letting-go was neither early nor stayed, and the hand did not stay; it was the note’s own finishing that ended the interval and not any decision of the hand. The intake was not a breath kept for warmth, the interval not a stopping of the bell, the note not his, and the gap in the bell stayed in its square and was not made a hearing.

He filed it as answer three hundred and thirteen: exact, complete, worth nothing at two inches of air, a restraint and not a payment borrowing no house count and supplying no payer, laid across neither the ten arithmetics, the bell’s gap, the covered grain, the handful, the thumb’s resting, the held breath at the sill, the staying at the inch, the early letting-go, the seventh opening, the pot-crack resting, the river’s winter skin, the setting-down-wrong, the eighth hand at the rail, the Ash 5 joining, the Ash 17 direction, the Ash 23 direction, the turned writing, the strip in the sleeve nor his own kept gaps. Three hundred and thirteen honest answers stood on the sixth place, each an object and not a power, and not one stood up and called itself a doctrine, for a doctrine is an account that has found a payer and none stood.

Underneath the exactness the infant went on wanting the crust lifted two inches to his mouth, the thread wiped and a hand that would close on the pot handle set beyond his reach. Both were true, the second was louder, neither won, and the quarrel was the work. The basket stayed level in the moot-post’s finger’s width of shade, his cheek against the cold iron that did not give, and the figure stood twice over it with no signature on the space and nobody under any roof who would know what a signature was worth. The pigs went back to being pigs and the two hundred to being households. Stone kept its tenth day without a boundary drawn, and the month carried both its names the way a pot carries two handles, the older women naming it by the weather and the slatemen by the count of Account-days, and no hand set either name right or left. The tenth arithmetic stood behind the unsigned figure with the nine before it, and no eleventh was brought into a day that had not earned one. Count three hundred and sixty-nine exact. Forward, not closed.

The afternoon carry came down the nineteen steps with the basket level and the fold set right at his ear, the weight taken before the handle as a woman takes the weight of a pot filled to the brim before she lets it come on the grip. Sella Vail did not hurry and did not stop. She gave the instruction to the wool, the river wind and the fire she would bank against their coming back, and never to the basket. “Keep it off his ears,” she told the wool. “It’s the river wind.” The half-pace said a slate might be open anywhere and a slate was an account and an account had a hand waiting at it; the level of the wicker said a pot filled must not be slopped. Then the yard, and the going around. She went wide around seven places with a look and a wide step and no number: the clean dark line at the Vail corner with the finer grey hairline beside it called the thaw, unswept both and thickened in the month’s dry cold; the space beneath the lowest rack where the broken birth-slates lay and were entered by nobody; the round ring where the shallow clay pan had stood, with no scrape, no crumb, no print and no word; the well with its stiff rope, the skinned trough and the silt-clouded plate of ice out of the path of feet; the field’s empty place; and the returning-line place where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out. She was widest at the returning-line place, by nothing measurable and by no longer look, and she did not go around it twice to make a figure of going around it once. A line left where it is is still a line kept and not a lesson. A sign is a thing that points to a payer, and no payer stood under that ground.

Three coverings stayed where the weather had put them, with a finger between every pair and none entered anywhere: the gone white frost off the north lip of the yard well, the skin on the trough, the clouded plate on the flat stone by the well. Another with a cracked jar in a sling of rope asked what the month’s mending would want and was told to ask the pot; the pot, being a pot, said nothing. Tarn said, “Yes, house,” to each, and kept the late because a late thaw puts off the paying, agreeing so thoroughly that no argument found a foot anywhere. Jorra Vail came through the finger’s-width door off the Vail wheel-shed with the day’s meal-fine grey slate dust ground small as meal past the bent little finger and white in a fist-crease where a hand had opened, and it was not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel. Three or more men unnamed had gone low into the back room before first grey and got no name from him and no word that the room was cold. He kept his dusty forearms and his clean ones on one tray and parted them as a surgeon parts clot from vessel, asking neither which slate had been broken nor whether it was a birth-slate while the broken ones lay under the lowest rack and were entered by nobody. He spoke to the wheel before he spoke to any person. “It doesn’t care whose hands. It cares if they’re steady,” he said, and told a pot, “You’ll do,” in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either. He knew the full note that pot would give three turns before it left his hands, with air under it and a want in it, and kept the knowing where nobody could make a song of it, withheld from any master come up from High Kiln who would call it early and sing a pot before its time.

Sella had not finished telling him the fire was low when his bent finger went into the cold iron of the damper ahead of her sentence, took the cold out of the fingertip, moved the tongue a hair’s breadth and was left standing so that the end of her instruction arrived at the pot and the household straightened around the waiting as cloth straightens when a weight is lifted. One piece of iron had two bites, the holding and the taking, and no Grathic word covered both mercies. He took the finger out without leaving the extra holding of Ash 26 a second time; it had been gathered once, on a place of its own with no payer standing, and a second would be a habit and a third a way. “He’s not Vail,” stayed sealed as a lid nobody had tried to lift in three years, and the promise of the boy’s name to the men who burn slates was neither given nor withdrawn under the shed.

Jorra Vail came through the finger’s-width door off the Vail wheel-shed with the day’s meal-fine grey slate dust ground small as meal past the bent little finger and white in a fist-crease where a hand had opened, and it was not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel. On the tenth day of the deep-winter month the district’s arithmetic changed for the tenth time where two hundred people stood. Berr Clay came up short off the clay road with the red cord of his office folded under his coat and, under the hot clay of him, the smell of nothing at all. He wiped his thumb twice, passed a finger over the figure through his sleeve, and read one in twelve twice over the basket --- once for the moot, once for the late households --- denying the levy with your house, never I. The block answered nothing; he went off with the box locked and Stone’s first true subtraction inside beside the nine, believing he had done a kindness. Hessa Rale took the slate up toward Greybank before noon with the writing to her sleeve, answering that she carried it and did not read it. The bell went four, four and a gap, and nobody crossed himself.

The boy in the basket kept the count nobody under any roof kept: three hundred and sixty-nine days, then three hundred and seventy, then three hundred and seventy-one, six months and one hundred and ninety-one days told by nobody. Ten readings stood behind one unsigned figure and ten were not one account; the figure read was still not the figure paid, and no doctrine came out of ten. On the eleventh morning Ilva drew at the well and answered what Stone’s firings had taken with Ask the pot what it cost. I keep the count, not the account, her hand to her sleeve before her eyes to the asker and the strip of birch unread.

On the twelfth morning Sella Vail carried him two doors down to the Drawing house and set the basket at the edge of the light, where he could see and not join. The collector, cuff soft from washing and fingertips grey as skimmed milk gone off, set a cold iron tube against the neighbour’s forearm by touch without pressing. The neighbour gave under it with a small flat knock and was left with a pale round place, not a wound and no blood. On the other side a rekindled child went worse on the third day, grey at the fingertips and red at the hairline, while the room called it a graft settling and the mother held a cup of warm milk she never drank. The surgeon knew that pink returning to a face is the surface still cold and not the core; he could not speak, reach or warm. Under the Vail roof the house kept four pulls and the silence where the fifth should have stood. She carried him home and said nothing of where they had been, and the hand outside the wool came down on the three hundred and eighteenth answer, laid where the next hand would find it without looking.
