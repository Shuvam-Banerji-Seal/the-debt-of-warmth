The Morning Not Yet Earned

Days 306–308 · Bone 7–9, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, Sill

Bone goes out as it came in, without a boundary drawn. A pot mended twice is trusted because the crack shows; a count kept in wood is trusted because no slate has taken a figure off it. The last tend ends with the hand going up an inch and coming down, and the reading still one morning ahead.

— A saying of the Clay Yard, told at the pot board and over the boards in the last tend of Bone

— Day 306 —

Bone 7, Seventhday, Y.H. 813

The hand had been laid down against the fold at the low angle of the loaf shelf as a man lays a tool where the next hand will find it without looking, and it lay there still in the small hours under the Vail kiln-house roof, the small soft wrist going no further than the wool, the one hundred and eighty-sixth answer finished and put away. The boy came into his three hundred and sixth day with nobody under any roof to tell him so. Six months and one hundred and twenty-six days were told by nobody in Low Kiln, Sill and Grath; the count had gone from three hundred and five to three hundred and six the way a board laid beside a board gives a dry knock and asks nothing of the seam behind it, and he took the knock as he took all his counts, whole and unspent and worth nothing to a hand that could not close two inches of air. Under the floor the carried fire kept its fifth unpaid three hundred and six days with its payer unnamed, and the name of Garl Vail had not been spoken under the roof the same number of days; nobody lifted the floor and nobody supplied the pull. The file lay open on the towel at the head of the bed with every gap left gap, and he turned over no page of it, because a file turned over twice is a file being asked to say something.

Bone came on without a boundary drawn, the way the last of a thing comes on in a house that does not keep its months in numbers. The older women named the months by what the weather did and the slatemen named them by how many Account-days had gone into them, and the two habits lay side by side like two hands hauling on one rope, neither set right and neither left wrong in so many words. Seventhday had no market, no reading at the Cross and no bell; the district kept the face it turns to a morning when nothing is due — a pot set down, a broom drawn over stone, water going over water somewhere behind the racks. Three hundred and six stood exact, complete, unspent and spoken by nobody; six months and one hundred and twenty-six days were told by nobody; the carried fire’s fifth was unpaid the same number of days with its payer unnamed, and Garl’s name unspoken the same number. He had stopped expecting to be told what any of the numbers counted. That was not the same as not wanting it, and the wanting went on under the expectation the way a coal goes on under ash.

Nothing that had gone before this morning came under the roof as a sign, a lesson, a mending, a cause or a payment. The fifth telling of the burned slate stood where it had been set down on the fifth of Bone, a voice from Slate Row over the boards with no owner standing under it and no hand named at the flame; five were five and a sixth was not due on a morning that had not earned one, and the finger stayed between the fifth and the four. The pot on the pot board carried its twice-mended handle and its crack showing, but a repair that held was not a restoration and mended nothing anybody would name; the two sayings belonged to the pot and the fire and had not been said to a person. The hand at the well that went to a sleeve before it went to an asker, with the notched strip staying in the wood where no slate could take a figure off it, was nine and stayed nine; no tenth stood up out of it and no figure came off that wood into this room. He kept these on their own places with a finger between every pair and wrote no cause across any two, and the edges nearly touched and did not touch.

Under the fold the body made its inventory in the old order, as a file is turned out on a towel before the instruments are laid back into it. One stiff leathery brown-edged barley crust lay two inches from the ear, gone past the point where any child under any roof would eat it, its empty place beside it keeping its name to itself and no third coming in the night to make the row come out even. The thread of drool at the mouth had frozen in the dry heat and was unwiped, because nobody under this roof had ever been given an instruction about the thread, and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. Cold had stopped at cheek, temple and the small bare joint of the wrist where the wool had ridden back, as though it had come to the end of a line written out for it and had neither an order to go further nor an order to go away. The Afterdrop waited beyond all three, past a warming nobody in this house would perform.

He knew the composed face that is not composure, the ease cold puts on a face that is not ease, the grimace of severe cold that passes for a smile among people who do not know better. He knew a cold patient cannot clot and will seem better before he is worse, and that a man so taken must be warmed from the outside and slowly, never hurried, never rubbed, never set too near the fire. The knowing was exact and worth nothing at two inches of air. He wanted the crust lifted the two inches to his mouth and the thread wiped away, and he wanted both louder than he wanted the inventory taken in its order; both were true and the second was louder, and neither was entered, not even on the place where he kept the things he did not enter. He had once reduced a shoulder in the dark by feel alone and now could not close two inches on the rim of the basket that held him, and the pot handle set beyond his reach was two inches of the same air. The surgeon took the inventory. The infant wanted the crust. The two voices argued and neither won, and the checking was the work and not a virtue.

Under the floor the carried fire kept four low pulls with the silence where the fifth should have stood, its payer unnamed three hundred and six days; through the finger’s-width door the river kept its water over water, a low thing over a lower thing, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming; overhead the boards kept four nails on the near board, four on the next and a thumb-width knot where the fifth nail should have stood. Three counts in three places, and none rose into another, because agreeing any two would have wanted a payer put under both and no payer stood to any. No fire-sitter stood over the coals to be borrowed from, and no house count came to be lent. He laid them side by side with a finger between and did not make the third a key to the first two, for a key is an account that has found a payer and none stood.

Six hearings of the full note stood beside and never among, five from four sources and the sixth, the small flat knock out of a person as the men lifted her, filed where a thing out of a person must go and never with the things that came from vessels; the seventh place kept its emptiness and none was invented to fill it. The bell’s four, four and a gap, heard at the last market of Frost with not one hand in two hundred going to a face, stayed in the square beside the told bell of the Long Night; no bell had come on the Seventhday and none was lent an ear it had not earned before its morning. Berr Clay’s first, second and third arithmetics, with their three true subtractions, rode locked up the clay road in his box toward High Kiln, one in twelve printed and unchallenged; the figure read was still not the figure paid and the space between had no signature on it. Three readings were three and not one account, and the finding sat on a place of its own with no payer under it, not a lesson, not a grievance, not a mending, not a doctrine, and not laid across the house’s not-doings, the grain under wool or the boy’s own kept gaps with the edges nearly touching and the finger going between.

Nine stoppings stood with no tenth before the seventh year at its very end; the row of askings stood at five with one shaping and no witness, and no sixth asking had been supplied on a day that had not earned one; the saying at the well stood at three shapings beside Hessa Rale’s eighth hand with a finger between every pair, and a fourth shaping was not to be stood up as a witness. Nine record-first hands stood with Ilva’s ninth unread in a sleeve and no tenth, and nine were nine and must not call themselves a rule, for a rule is an account that has found a payer and none stood at the well. The burned slate stood at five with a finger between the fifth and the four; the two tellings of the counting room shut in the war stood at two with no third; the three pot sayings stood with the Smoke 14 note on a place of its own and no fourth likeness made a cause; the seventh green stood a colour at a woman’s mouth and the seventh shape a shape at a market’s edge, with no eighth of either and no name spoken over them. Every tray was parted by the width of a finger, and nothing on any of them stood up and called itself a rule.

The first light came into the room the way tallow light comes, not as a beam but as a softening of the dark on the underside of the shelf, and Sella Vail went to the hearth before she went to the basket because it was the grammar of the house and the grammar held. She told the coals it was the thaw when the sour mineral breath came off the unswept corner, turned them with the back of a broken pot, and said The house will keep it where another woman would have said I. She stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand counting something under her breath toward no opening — small intake, hold, letting-go — and gave neither gap a figure. Her finger stayed on top of the loaf shelf’s front edge so the charcoal tally beneath remained unread; her lips moved along a row standing with two gaps and neither gap was entered; her eye went once to the clay jar standing cold under lead glaze on the wall shelf, and her hand shut half over nothing without touching it. She pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the burn-wrist and the one grey streak at her temple showed pale in the low light, and the little-door burn on the back of her hand shut on nothing, as it shut on nothing whenever her hand was empty.

The widow’s mark hung where it hung on its cord at her throat, a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it under a working thumb, and she did not touch it, because the hand goes to the mark before the kiln and at no other hour, and a morning is not an opening. Five openings in five months were five and not a rule; the next stood in a later month’s first firing, far off, not hoped for and not counted toward. When she set the pot down on the pot board she set the rim two inches beyond the reaching hand with its handle turned toward it, and there was a half-beat between the setting and her going away, and she did not look toward the bed. The half-beat was not a gift and the handle turned toward the hand was not an instruction to take; a gift wants a taker, and he was not given the means to close two inches of air. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without a look at his face, and left the thread where it lay.

The twice-mended pot stood where she had set it, with the repair standing visible and no attempt made to hide it. Two staples held the handle: one the colour of old brass, gone soft with years of use, and the second a wire drawn tighter and left longer than it needed to be, with a hairline of the original crack showing dark between them. She put her thumb where the wire crossed the crack before letting the weight come on the handle, felt what the thumb had to feel, and only then lifted; the pot answered full under the handle and the crack stayed showing. Leave the crack showing. People trust a thing they can see has been handled, she said to the pot and to the fire, and If it’s worth mending it’s worth mending twice. The second time is the real one. She said both to the pot and the fire and never to a person; the sayings had belonged to a man who mended pots for a living and would not let anyone mend him, and they had improved in the keeping and were very likely not as good as the man had said them. Repair is not restoration; a mended vessel carries its break, and a repair that holds mends nothing anybody will name. The boy took the measure of the two staples with his eyes and made no figure of them, and did not lay the crack across his own kept gaps for want of a payer.

At the kindling Tarn had the small knife at the very edge of its sill where the house could check it and where it could slip, six years standing in the worn thumb-mark where the house could check and the seventh at the lip beyond the mark where it could not, and Garl’s unforged collector sentence lying beside it with no name on the man who had turned back at the frozen ford and no figure on the debt. He told the wood Six counted. The next I don’t count in the tone of a man stating the weather, and put the knife back where it could be checked, announcing it to nobody because the yard had already been told. The sentence about an ear under this roof being worth more than the debt that held it came up to the word that would call the loan and stood there. He did not say the word. It came as a remembered ninth stopping with no tenth before the seventh year at its very end and still ungiven, and the breath he drew at the top of the reach did not go in and was not spent; the Seed 16 breath stayed unspent with it. He said Yes, house to an early thaw and a late, agreeing with both so thoroughly that no argument could find a foot, and went on wanting the late because it puts off the paying, though the paying had stood in the ruts since the water went.

Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line and no scrubbing lifted it; the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back; his stroke went over the shallow bright patch on the flagstones without the half-check, the stones keeping the place where the stroke had stopped. Up in the Debt Loft, which reached the boy only as a sound with a direction, the warm stone Sella had given him in the winter Garl died lay cold in its unmarked pot, and he did not reconcile it with the debt and did not try, which is not forgiveness. The loan stayed uncalled and no collector came up the clay road or went down it; the two ears under the roof kept their equality and the two silences their one shape, and nothing on this Seventhday turned the seventh year into eight.

At the edge of the Vail wheel-shed Jorra Vail came through the finger’s-width door with the day’s meal-fine grey slate dust ground small as meal past the bent little finger and under the nails, white in the creases where a fist had opened, and not bone-coloured slip. He kept dusty forearms and clean ones on one tray and parted them as a surgeon parts clot from vessel, asking neither which slate had broken nor whether it had been a birth-slate. The men who had gone low into the back room before first grey had no name from him and the room was not called cold; to the boy it was a direction and a sound. The shed held the sweetish edge of lead glaze in its dust, a wrong wind’s direction that had once gone over the boards and out again on the wind that brought it, arrived nowhere and entered nowhere and not a second edge paid. Jorra spoke to the wheel before he spoke to any person. “It doesn’t care whose hands. It cares if they’re steady,” he said, and told a pot “You’ll do” in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either.

He knew the full note that pot would give three turns before it left his hands, with air under it and a want in it, and withheld the knowing on the near-full tray beside the hearings and never among them; he would not have said it before a kiln-master come up from High Kiln who would have called it early and made something of it. The burned slate stood at five tellings and he put a finger between the fifth and the four and supplied no sixth; when the saying went around the boards he grew quieter and the household straightened around the quiet without an order. The two tellings of the counting room shut in the war stood at two and had not gained a third; the three pot sayings stood with the Smoke 14 note on a place of its own and no fourth likeness made a cause; the row stood at five askings with one shaping and no witness. He’s not Vail stayed sealed as tightly as on the hour it was spoken, and the promise of the foundling’s name to the men who burn slates was neither given nor withdrawn. When Sella’s sentence came low and unfinished, his bent finger went into the cold iron of the damper ahead of the end of it, took the cold out of the fingertip, moved the tongue a hair’s breadth and left it standing so that the end of her instruction arrived at the pot. The boy read one piece of iron with two bites, the holding and the taking, with no Grathic word for both mercies, joined to no kiln and entered in no slate. He joined nothing on the wheel-keeper’s forearms to a cause, and Slate Row stayed a direction over the boards, and the fifth telling waited on the far-off day the promise would be called in, not a confession and not a payment.

Garl Vail was kept in objects and not in a story, and his name had not been spoken under the roof for three hundred and six days. The salt-stiff coat on its peg behind the door held his broader shape with the crease at the left shoulder; the wheel-seat in the shed kept its twenty-year lean and its hollow on that side and Sella would let no one fill it; the ridge that had been his ash-marker was now a clean dark line with a new finer hairline of grey beside it called the thaw, and neither had been swept. His proverb went to the pot and the fire and never to a person — Leave the crack showing. People trust a thing they can see has been handled — and it had improved in the keeping, as all his sayings did, and was very likely not as good a sentence as the man had said it. The debt against a firing of tiles lay where it lay, neither paid nor settled nor done with, and worse for being uncalled; a debt circled without a name is a debt that gathers weight from being gone around. Up in the loft the stone lay cold in its pot and the debt lay uncalled, and the two were kept apart by a man who had stopped trying to reconcile them, which is a kind of keeping and not a kind of peace. Nobody made a lesson of any of it and nobody said the name.

Three hundred and six stood exact, complete, unspent and spoken by nobody in Low Kiln, Sill and Grath; six months and one hundred and twenty-six days were told by nobody; the carried fire’s fifth was unpaid the same number of days with its payer unnamed, and the name of Garl Vail had not been spoken under the roof the same number. A hundred and eighty-six honest answers stood on the sixth place, each an object and not a power, and neither the number nor the small hand-movings that made them stood up and called themselves a doctrine, for a doctrine is an account that has found a payer and none stood. Nothing was opened, joined, named, called, paid or ended on the seventh of Bone: no sixth asking, no tenth hand, no tenth stopping, no seventh hearing, no third telling of the shut room, no sixth telling of the burned slate, no fourth shaping of the well-saying, no eighth green or shape, no fifth pull supplied and no payer beneath the pot’s mended handle. Bone 10 stood three mornings ahead and was not brought into a day that had not earned it. The story moved because the hand kept answering and the count kept going, not because anything under the roof had settled.

In the small hours the hand outside the wool came up an inch toward the river’s note, and the thing he took and laid away was a small intake from the room whose floor does not touch his. He laid it beside the carried fire’s fourth low pull on a place of its own, whole and unjoined, and did not carry it into the silence where the fifth should have stood, or into the bell’s gap left behind in the square, or into the river’s low over lower. It borrowed no house count and supplied no payer. The hundred and eighty-seventh answer was exact, complete and worth nothing at two inches of air; it did not stand up as a reach, a hearing, a seventh, a stopping, a payment, a held change, a key, a power, a freedom, a gift, a doctrine or a rule, and no payer was named to it. The surgeon in him took a clean satisfaction in one sound placed against its note, and the infant in him wanted the crust two inches off at his ear, and the two voices argued and neither won, and the checking was the work and not a virtue. The hand came down on the river’s note as it always did, and the silence where the fifth should stand was left a silence.

The day went on into the afternoon without a line drawn across it, the way Bone went on without a boundary drawn, and Sella Vail came for the basket when the sun had gone past its height and the wind had come up off the sill. She lifted with the hand that carries the burn, the little-door shape of it closing first on the wool and then on the weight, and gave the instruction to the fold as she gave it at both ends of every day: keep it off his ears, it’s the river wind. She said it to the wool and to the wind and never to the basket. The basket was a pot taken up and carried and set down, and pots are not told why. The boy went down at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, which is the pace of a woman carrying something that must not be slopped and that must not be seen into.

In the Clay Yard the dry cold held the way a coal holds under ash, and the river kept its note of water over water beyond the racks. He took the yard in the order it arrived, as he took a wound in the order it bled: first the smells, wet clay and cooled tallow and the sour mineral breath off a corner that had not been swept in two years, then the sounds, a pot set down flat somewhere, a broom drawn over stone, water going over water, then the places the feet were going to avoid. A child in a basket sees the ground first. There were seven of them, and she went around all seven with a look and a wide step and no number. At the Vail corner the clean dark line where Ash had turned lay beside a finer grey hairline the older women called the thaw, and both were unswept; she set her foot past them as she would set a cup past the edge of a table, and gave the broom its instruction before she gave it to anybody. Beneath the lowest rack the broken birth-slates lay where the yard had put them, and the space under the rack was gone around as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. On the stone lip of the well the shallow pan’s gone place kept its round ring, with no scrape, no crumb, no print, no chip and no word on it, a thing named from the shape it had left and not from anything that remained. The water in the trough wore its skin. Far out on the worked strips a field’s empty place stood where a field should have stood, and in the returning line a place stood where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out. She looked at each as a woman looks at a stone in a path she knows well, and stepped wide, and entered none of them into anything. A thing gone around with a broom is still a thing kept.

The stayed frost had gone off the north lip of the well on Bone 3 while the yard was looking elsewhere. There was no white on the lip now, only the grey stone showing where the white had been, and the rope had lost its stiffness and gave again under the bucket. The trough kept its skin from edge to edge, and the plate of ice lifted out of it stood on the flat stone by the well out of the path of feet, clouded with the silt that had risen into the water as it froze. Three coverings in three places, and the yard called all three weather and entered none of them. He took them apart as he had been taught to take a wound apart, by what was there and by what was not. The frost that had gone from the north lip had been a covering laid over a depth, thin and white, gone in an afternoon without anybody seeing it go. The skin on the trough was the same covering laid over another depth, grey where the first had been white, holding its shape while the water under it went on being water. The clouded plate was a piece of the depth itself lifted up and set where no foot went, with the silt that had been in the water held in it the way a clot holds what was moving in a vessel. He kept the three with a finger between every pair. The first was not the second and the second was not the third, and no fourth likeness was made a cause. To call them one thing would have wanted a hand under all three, and no such hand stood in the yard, and he named no payer to any of them.

At the rack end the dressed grain lay under wool away from the hearth, spread on the cool stone in the shade the racks threw across it. She set the basket down where no slate lay, lifted a corner of the wool with two fingers and turned the grain beneath it with a touch lighter than the touch she had given it in the morning, and laid the wool back. The grain had been lifted for damp and turned and spread thin and gathered by the handful, changed in state a dozen times and given no figure on any of them. Three readings had gone over it up the clay road in Berr Clay’s box with their true subtractions locked in, one in twelve printed and unchallenged; the figure read was still not the figure paid and the space between had no signature on it. He did not bring any of that in as a lesson. Three readings were three and not one account, and the finding sat on a place of its own with no payer under it, not laid across the covered grain and not laid across the house’s not-doings or across his own kept gaps. Some of those edges nearly touched. The finger went between them.

Bone was a month of hunts and repairs, and both stayed directions judged by the carrying arm and the weather and never figures. A man went past with a hare across his shoulders, the hind legs along one collarbone and the head tucked against the other, hill frost grey in the fur at the throat, and a woman with a pot asked what the month’s carrying cost. She was told the weather, and the man shifted the hare higher and went off toward the north gate road while the yard went on with its work. Nobody weighed the hare, nobody opened a slate, nobody turned twice. The boy watched the way the arm settled its weight higher on the shoulder without asking the other arm for help, and filed the carrying where an arm’s knowledge goes and not where a cost goes. The cost asked of the month had been answered with a direction that left no entry. No hunter was brought back for a second appearance, and the hare went into the weather once and stayed there.

At the well Ilva was drawing with the pail against her knee, the rope running true through her palm and the bucket going down without a splash. Clay had dried pale in the crescents of her nails and the finer grey dust of the racks was worked into the seam of one thumb, which she did not scrub out. Her hands knew the weight of a pail and the edge of a wet board and were not soft. A woman with a pot asked what the month’s firings had taken. Ilva’s hand went toward her sleeve and stayed there; the short strip of birch bark with its notches, pale where a thumb had rubbed them and dark in the uncut grain between, was not taken out, not read, not copied, not counted and not joined to any slate, charcoal mark, cord or private keeping, and no figure came off the wood. Her eyes went to the asker and her mouth opened after both, with a half-beat between the looking and the word. “Ask the pot what it cost. I keep the count, not the account.” She put the flat of her hand once more on the sleeve and went back to the pail, and nobody turned twice.

The boy lay at the edge of the work where no slate lay and took the order of her reaching apart as a surgeon takes a wound apart, by what was there and by what was not. Hand toward the record. Thumb passing over the notches. Eyes to the asker. Mouth answering the question put and not the one behind it. He did not read the notches and could not have read them, and the count stayed in wood where no slate could take a figure off it. He filed the order as the ninth record-first hand on a place of its own beside Hessa Rale’s eighth with a finger between, and filed no tenth. Nine were nine and must not stand up and call themselves a rule, for a rule is an account that has found a payer and none stood at this well. He did not make her a reader of the count she kept, which she is not, and did not join her strip a pace away to the ownerless voice that had come up from Slate Row over the boards on Bone 5, or to the charcoal beneath the shelf, the cord, the knots, the pages, the tins or the inward slates. She was not the woman whose voice came over the boards, not Marra, not Hessa, not the soft-cuffed clerk and not an officer, and she did not know she had been read.

The seed of the saying waited on the far-off calling-in of Jorra’s promise, not a confession and not a payment, and the asking stood at five with one shaping and no witness and expressly no sixth.

The small keepings stayed small and were not brought forward. The charcoal beneath the loaf shelf stayed unread under the finger laid on top of the shelf’s front edge; Ilva’s strip stayed in her sleeve offstage on this day and the day before, her ninth hand nine with no tenth and no figure coming off the wood; the three shapings of the Frost 14 well-saying stayed beside Hessa Rale’s eighth hand with a finger between every pair, and a fourth shaping was not to be stood up as a witness; the seventh green remained a colour at a woman’s mouth and the seventh shape a shape at a market’s edge with no eighth of either and no name spoken over them; the reed-cutter’s cord lay where no Court thumb had read it; the soft-cuffed clerk’s inward slate stayed at the out-of-line landing for a measure nobody called. None was a sign, a lesson, a mending, a cause or a payment. Nine hands were nine and must not call themselves a rule, for a rule is an account that has found a payer and none stood at the well. He kept them where they lay and did not carry one of them under the roof.

At the kindling Tarn had the small knife at the very edge of its sill where the house could check it and where it could slip, six years standing in the worn thumb-mark where the house could check and the seventh at the lip beyond the mark where it could not. Garl Vail’s unforged collector sentence lay beside it with no name on the man who had turned back at the frozen ford and no figure on the debt. Six counted. The next I don’t count, he told the wood in the tone of a man stating the weather, and put the knife back where it could be checked, announcing it to nobody because the yard had already been told. The sentence about an ear under this roof being worth more than the debt that held it came up to the word that would call the loan and stood there. He did not say the word. It came as a remembered ninth stopping with no tenth before the seventh year at its very end and still ungiven, and the breath he drew at the top of the reach did not go in and was not spent; the Seed 16 breath stayed unspent with it. He said Yes, house to an early thaw and a late, agreeing with both so thoroughly that no argument could find a foot, and went on wanting the late because it puts off the paying, though the paying had stood in the ruts since the water went.

Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line and no scrubbing lifted it; the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back. His stroke went out over the shallow bright patch on the flagstones and over it without the half-check; the movement of the day before stood where it had been left, remembered and not made again, and he set no finger of his across it to make four into one. Up in the Debt Loft, which reached the boy only as a sound with a direction, the warm stone Sella had given him in the winter Garl died lay cold in its unmarked pot, and he did not reconcile it with the debt and did not try, which is not forgiveness. The loan stayed uncalled and no collector came up the clay road or went down it; the two ears under the roof kept their equality and the two silences their one shape, and nothing on this Ninthday turned the seventh year into eight.

Garl stayed in objects and not in a story, and his name had not been spoken under the roof for three hundred and eight days. The salt-stiff coat with the crease at the left shoulder kept a broader man’s shape on its peg behind the door; the wheel-seat in the shed kept its twenty-year lean and its hollow on that side and Sella would let no one fill it; the ridge that had been his ash-marker was a clean dark line with a finer hairline of grey beside it called the thaw, and neither had been swept. His proverb went to the pot and the fire and never to a person — Leave the crack showing. People trust a thing they can see has been handled — and had improved in the keeping and was very likely not as good a sentence as the man had said it; the other saying about a thing worth mending twice went with it to the pot. The debt against a firing of tiles circled without a name, neither paid nor settled nor done with and worse for being uncalled. The keeping of an absence is maintenance and not a story, and nobody made a lesson of any of it.

At the edge of the Vail wheel-shed Jorra came through the finger’s-width door with the day’s meal-fine grey slip dust ground small as meal past the bent little finger and under the nails, white in the creases where a fist had opened, and not bone-coloured slip. He kept dusty forearms and clean ones on one tray and parted them as a surgeon parts clot from vessel, asking neither which slate had broken nor whether it had been a birth-slate. The men who had gone low into the back room before first grey had no name from him and the room was not called cold; to the boy it was a direction and a sound. Jorra spoke to the wheel before he spoke to any person. “It doesn’t care whose hands. It cares if they’re steady,” he said, and told a pot “You’ll do” in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either.

He knew the full note that pot would give three turns before it left his hands, with air under it and a want in it, and withheld the knowing on the near-full tray beside the hearings and never among them; he would not have said it before a kiln-master come up from High Kiln who would have called it early and made something of it. The burned slate stood at five tellings and he put a finger between the fifth and the four and supplied no sixth; the two tellings of the counting room shut in the war stood at two and had not gained a third; the three pot sayings stood with the Smoke 14 note on a place of its own and no fourth likeness made a cause; the row stood at five askings with one shaping and no witness. He’s not Vail stayed sealed as tightly as on the hour it was spoken, and the promise of the foundling’s name to the men who burn slates was neither given nor withdrawn. When Sella’s sentence came low and unfinished, his bent finger went into the cold iron of the damper ahead of the end of it, took the cold out of the fingertip, moved the tongue a hair’s breadth and left it standing so that the end of her instruction arrived at the pot. The boy read one piece of iron with two bites, the holding and the taking, with no Grathic word for both mercies, joined to no kiln and entered in no slate.

Sella’s last grammar of the day held as the first had held. She went to the fire before the basket, told the coals it was the thaw when the sour mineral breath came off the unswept corner, turned them with the back of a broken pot, and said The house will keep it where another woman would have said I. Her finger stayed on top of the loaf shelf’s front edge so the charcoal tally beneath remained unread; her lips moved along a row standing with two gaps and neither gap was entered; her eye went once to the clay jar standing cold under lead glaze on the wall shelf, and her hand shut half over nothing without touching it. She pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the burn-wrist and the one grey streak at her temple showed pale in the low light. The widow’s mark hung where it hung on its cord at her throat and she did not touch it, because the hand goes to the mark before the kiln and at no other hour and a going of the light is not an opening; five openings in five months were five and not a rule. When she set the pot down on the pot board she put her thumb first where the wire crossed the crack, felt what the thumb had to feel, and only then let the weight come on the handle. She set the rim two inches beyond the reaching hand with its handle turned toward it, and there was a half-beat between the setting and her going away, and she did not look toward the bed. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without a look at his face, and left the thread where it lay.

In the small hours he turned out the file as he had once turned instruments out on a towel at the end of a list, laying each thing on its own place and asking nothing of any of them. The held interval lay where it had been laid; the empty interval lay empty; the missing fifth under the floor was missing and nobody had supplied it; the two shelf gaps stood unfigured; the ninth hand stood nine with the strip in the sleeve and no tenth; the fifth telling stood at five with the finger between it and the four; the four held changes stood four on four places. Nothing had been spent to make a row come out even. Nothing had stood up out of its place as a reach, a hearing, a seventh, a stopping, a payment, a held change, a key, a power, a freedom, a gift, a doctrine or a rule. The file lay open on the towel at the head of the bed with every gap left gap, every tray parted by the width of a finger and nothing moved. He had once laid out a tray for a man who never came off the table and had known the exactness of an instrument count without ever asking the count to save anybody; the old satisfaction came and went without bitterness, which is its own small grief.

At the very end of the day the hand outside the wool came up an inch toward the river’s note, and the thing he took and let go was a small intake from the room whose floor does not touch his. He let it go whole on the same low note, a restraint and not a payment, borrowing no house count and supplying no payer, carrying it into neither the carried fire’s missing fifth, the bell’s gap left behind in the square nor the river’s low over lower. The hundred and ninety-second answer was exact, complete and worth nothing at two inches of air; it did not stand up as a reach, a hearing, a seventh, a stopping, a payment, a held change, a key, a power, a freedom, a gift, a doctrine or a rule, and no payer was named to it. The older man in him took a clean satisfaction in the keeping of the number; the infant wanted the crust lifted the two inches to his mouth and the thread wiped away; both were true and the second was louder, and neither won, and the argument was the work and not a virtue. The hand came down on the river’s note as it always did and laid itself back against the fold as a man puts away a tool where the next hand will find it without looking.

Three hundred and eight stood exact, complete, unspent and spoken by nobody in Low Kiln, Sill and Grath; six months and one hundred and twenty-eight days were told by nobody; the carried fire’s fifth was unpaid the same number of days with its payer unnamed, and Garl Vail’s name had not been spoken under the roof the same number. A hundred and ninety-two honest answers stood on the sixth place, each an object and not a power, and neither the number nor the small hand-movings that made them stood up and called themselves a doctrine, for a doctrine is an account that has found a payer and none stood. Nothing on the ninth of Bone was opened, joined, named, called, paid or ended: no sixth asking, no tenth hand, no tenth stopping, no seventh hearing, no third telling of the shut room, no sixth telling of the burned slate, no fourth shaping of the well-saying, no eighth green or shape, no fifth pull supplied and no payer beneath the pot’s mended handle. The month’s first Account-day stood one morning beyond the reach of this day and had not been brought into it. The story moved because the hand kept answering and the count kept going, not because anything under the roof had settled.

The stiff leathery crust lay two inches from the ear with its empty place unnamed and no third to make the row come out even. Cold had stopped at cheek, temple and the small bare joint of the wrist where the wool had ridden back, and the Afterdrop waited beyond all three, past a warming nobody in this house would perform. Under the finger’s-width door the river went water over water without a number put to it. The silt-clouded plate of ice stood on its flat stone out of the path of feet, and the file lay open on the towel with every gap left gap, and Bone went on without a boundary drawn.

Bone came on without a boundary drawn, and the boy beneath the Vail roof came into his three hundred and sixth, seventh and eighth mornings with nobody to tell him what they counted. The count went from three hundred and five to three hundred and eight; the fire’s fifth stayed unpaid with its payer unnamed and Garl Vail’s name unspoken. Each morning the body was inventoried in its old order --- crust, frozen thread, cold, the afterdrop waiting --- and the surgeon’s knowledge that a cold patient cannot clot bought nothing against the infant’s want of the crust.

Sella Vail went to the fire before the basket, told the coals it was the thaw, turned them with a broken pot, said The house will keep it, and kept the charcoal unread and the widow’s mark untouched, for no hour here was an opening. She lifted the twice-mended pot with her thumb first where the wire crossed the crack and said both proverbs to the pot and fire. At the rack end she turned the dressed grain beneath the wool with no figure; three readings had gone over it in Berr Clay’s locked box, the figure read still not the figure paid.

The yard went wide around its seven walked-around places with a look and a step and no number, called the gone frost and the skinned trough weather, and answered a man carrying a hare with the weather. At the well Ilva drew with the pail against her knee, kept her notched strip in her sleeve, and answered the question put rather than the one behind it: she kept the count, not the account. Tarn kept six where the house could check and a seventh at the lip, and stopped his ear-sentence at the word that would call the loan; Jorra parted dusty forearms from clean, withheld the note a pot would give three turns ahead, and set his bent finger in the cold damper ahead of Sella’s sentence.

At the going of the light on the eighth, Tarn’s broom checked half a beat behind that finger on the flagstones and went on toward the road without a word or a turn of the head --- the old half-check returned, changed, after a hundred and thirty-six days. Neither man named the other’s silence. The boy filed it beside three older held changes, four on four places, and forbade them to stand up as a rule, for a rule is an account that has found a payer and none stood; he did not make it again on the ninth. Six times the hand took a small intake from the next room and let it go whole on the river’s note as a restraint, not a payment; the answers from the hundred and eighty-seventh to the hundred and ninety-second were worth nothing at two inches of air. At the close the file lay open on the towel with every gap left gap, and Bone went on without a boundary drawn.
