The hand lay flat on the fold where it had been put down, and the small hours came on under the roof the way weather comes, without a door opened for them and without anybody saying what they had crossed. Three hundred and ninety-two days stood behind it exact and unspent, six months and two hundred and twelve of them told by nobody who would have known what a number counted. Under the floor the carried fire gave four low pulls and kept the fifth where it had always been kept, and the boy lay at the low angle of the loaf shelf in the Vail kiln-house, in Low Kiln, Sill, Grath, with the dry kiln heat coming to him sideways and the smell of woodsmoke and wet wool in the fold and, under that, the faint sour tang of Ash lifting out of the Vail corner where no broom had gone in two years. The room held its heat the way a cup holds water, with a crack in it nobody mentioned. Outside, the dark was going about its own work along the Clay Yard, and the fourth of the Five had a name and no tend-day and no market, and was not a door or a boundary or a thing paid.

The surgeon took the body in the order it arrived, because a field read out of order is a field read wrong. One stiff leathery brown-edged barley crust stood two inches from his ear with its corner a hair off the wool; beside it the empty place held the fold put there on the fifteenth, found where put again in the dark and not made again, with no second fold, no third crust and no name spoken over it. The thread of drool at his mouth had gone stiff in the dry heat and was unwiped, because nobody under any roof has ever been given an instruction about the thread. Cold had come to cheek, temple and the bare joint of the wrist where the wool rode back, and stopped there as though it had reached the end of a line written out for it; the Afterdrop waited past a warming nobody in this house would perform. A face composed by cold is not composure. Pink returning is the surface still lying over a cold core, and a cold patient cannot clot, and the rewarming he would choose is a basket set level out of the draught where the heat comes sideways, slow and from the outside, never hurried and never rubbed, and he could not ask for it. He had once stood at a bay door and sorted twenty people by what they would not survive, and his knowledge of cold was of the same order and as useless here: he knew the order in which a body gives up heat and the order in which it takes it back, with the extremities before the core and the surface warm while the middle is still cold, and there was no hand two paces long to put any of it to work. Under the exactness the infant wanted the crust lifted, the thread wiped and a hand on the pot handle. Both were true. The second was louder. Neither won, and no verdict was looked for; the quarrel was the work.

Sella Vail came to the hearth before she came to the basket, as she had come to it every morning of his life, and gave the coals their instruction first. “It’s the thaw,” she said, in the voice another woman would have used for a child who could not hear her, and the low red eye under the back of the broken pot answered her with a small breathing that was not an answer to anything anybody had asked. She turned the coals with the back of the pot and stood a moment with her hands not still, counting a small intake, a hold and a letting-go toward no opening, the count arriving nowhere as it had arrived nowhere every day of his life, with neither gap figured and neither made to stand for a number. Her finger lay along the top of the loaf shelf where any woman in the district could see it lying, and the charcoal tally underneath stayed unread; her lips moved along the two gaps in the loaf row and gave nobody a figure. Her eye went once to the clay jar cold under lead glaze beyond the pot board, with the sweetish edge of a wrong wind coming and going over it, and her hand started toward it and shut half over nothing, the little-door burn on the back of her left hand closing on air. She lifted the twice-mended pot thumb-first where the tighter wire crossed the crack, and the hairline of the original break showed between the old-brass staple and the new binding, and she said both proverbs to the pot and the fire and never to a person, keeping repair apart from restoration as a surgeon keeps a graft apart from a cure; a restored thing hides the hand that handled it and a mended thing leaves that hand showing. The pot gave back a small flat note as she set it down — not the full clear note of a sound vessel and not the dead note of one cracked through, but the note of a thing that carries its break and goes on holding — and she had heard it every morning of his life and had never once called it mended. A strand of coal-black hair came loose at her temple where the one grey streak was, and she pushed it back with the wrist that carried the burn and did not discuss either the hair or the winter it had come in. The dressed grain came out of the drying racks and went back under wool with a touch lighter than a woman would use on a sleeping child, changed in state and given no figure and lifted in no handful. The pot rim went down two inches beyond the reaching hand, with a half-beat at the setting and no look toward the bed. The half-beat stood where it stood and asked nothing and was not entered. The cord of her widow’s mark lay against her throat with the oil of ten thousand working thumbs in it, and her hand did not go to it; a holy day is not an opening, seven openings in seven months are seven and not a rule, and no eighth was made due on a morning that had not earned one.

The names began going up the yard before the sun cleared the eaves, and they went the way a coal goes in a dish from hearth to hearth, passed from door to door along the row of houses and set down at the last threshold with nobody saying where the dish had been filled. It was the Remembering, and the yard said it the way the yard says everything it has kept a long time: practically, low, with the words flattened by work. A woman said a name to a pail. A man said one to the latch of his door as he lifted it, and a girl further down said one to the dust at her feet, and each house took the sound and passed it on and kept its own; nobody put a date to any of them and nobody said where the count began. The boy lay under the fold and counted the spaces between the sounds without knowing which of the sounds were names and which were the small noises of a yard waking in the cold; he gave no interior to any of the voices, for a voice in a yard is a voice in a yard and not a room entered, and a pause is not a person. He had learned that from pots: a cracked vessel answers flat, and the flatness is a fact about the vessel and not a confession from whoever held it.

A door opened up the yard and a woman’s voice went out of it into the cold and came back to the person on the step as a thing handed over and taken; the person on the step said nothing, only set her hand on the pail and held it, and then the voice went on down the row. At the next house a man stood with his coat held closed at the throat and said a name into the gap between his hand and the lintel, and a child inside said it back wrong, and the man did not correct the child, because in the yard the saying is the keeping and the exactness of the sound is not what is kept. Nearer the well another man went around a place in the trodden ash with his pail held out from his body, took the long way past it without looking, and the boy had learned not to ask what the place was called or what had gone into it; a wide step is a wide step, and the yard had no number for it and gave him none. He followed the intervals rather than the words, as he followed the fire under the floor by its pulls and its silences: a sound, a space, a sound, a longer space where a door shut, a sound again from a house further up with the wind taking the top off it. The yard was doing the work it had always done with what it had, passing its dead from mouth to mouth the way it passed its fire in the dark, and nobody in any of the houses stood under a sound to be asked who had begun it or what it cost to keep going.

One name went among the others and did not come under this roof. It passed a door, went down a step and was taken up by another mouth, and the boy did not know which of the sounds it was, and no roof said it to him, and no number went with it. Among the names that travelled that morning was one that had belonged to Dessa Silt, dead since the opening of the year and uncollected, carried now in the mouths of neighbours who had known her as the yard knows all its dead, by sound and by the shape of a silence rather than by any slate; and the boy did not know it was hers, and her number stayed where it had been put, in a thumb gone into the water, and the name under the sieve-wire scar stayed where it had been put too. The mercy-lie stood as a fact nobody under any roof could correct, and no telling of it under the Vail roof corrected it now. The house kept its own quiet. No whole return was announced for Garl Vail; his name went unspoken its three hundred and ninety-third day, kept in the salt-stiff coat with its left-shoulder crease on the peg behind the door, in the hollow wheel-seat nobody filled, in the clean dark line at the Vail corner with the finer grey beside it called the thaw, thickened in the dry cold with the faint sour tang of Ash coming up off it where no broom had gone in two years, in the hairline between old brass and tighter wire and in the unforged collector sentence lying at the kindling with no name on the man who turned back at the frozen ford and no figure on the tile debt. He was kept there and nowhere spoken, and the keeping was maintenance and not a story.

The clean voice’s missing name came up in the ear as the yard’s voices went past, not as a voice now but as the space a voice had left, and the story’s empty name came up beside it out of the dark behind the finger’s-width door. The boy set them on the gap tray beside the told bell’s gap with a finger between every pair, and did not fill either. He did not make a witness of a space, for a witness wants a person standing somewhere who can be asked; did not make a reader of it, for a reader wants a hand on a slate; did not make a payment of it, for a payment wants somebody paid; and did not make a doctrine of it, for a doctrine is an account that has found a payer and none stood. The heard bell stayed in its square beside the told bell of the Long Night, four, four and a gap, and the gap stayed in its own square, not folded into the fire’s four and a gap under the floor, nor into the boards’ four nails and four and a thumb-width knot overhead, nor into the twelve arithmetics standing behind one unsigned figure, nor made a seventh hearing of the full note. Six hearings stood beside and never among with the seventh place empty and the Drawing house’s flat knock outside them; House Silt, this kiln-house, the room where the slate was read on market-day and the Drawing house kept the same gap in four places with a finger between every pair and no Court room brought under the roof as a fifth likeness. Three counts in three places stayed three, because agreeing any two would want a payer put under both and none stood to either. The Ash 5 joining and the Ash 17, Ash 23 and Stone 23 directions stood where they had been put, did not repeat and bought no lesson.

Sella carried him down the nineteen steps at the half-pace she keeps from every open slate, with the fold right at his ear and his chin and the instruction going to the wool and the river wind and the banked fire and never to the basket. She went wide around the seven walked-around places with a look and a wide step and no number, once and not twice, widest around the returning-line place where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out, and the look stayed the length of a step before it went off the place. The three coverings stayed weather entered nowhere with a finger between every pair, and the river kept its note of water over water under the winter skin beyond the door, a covering put on by the cold and taken off by the cold, found where put and not made again. She set him under the roof and never in the Debt Loft, righted the fold without a look at his face, added no second fold, and left the thread where it lay. Under the floor the carried fire answered four low pulls with the silence where the fifth should have stood, its payer unnamed and its fifth unpaid three hundred and ninety-three days; nobody lifted the floor and nobody supplied the pull, and the fire that warmed the house was a finding and not a cause.

It was not put away for good; another case would come and the towel would come out again, and the hand would find the place of every instrument without looking for it. The crust’s gap, the shelf’s two gaps, the gone pan’s round ring, the field’s empty place, the keeping at the fold found where put and still a finding, the told bell’s gap, the silence after the name from Weighing, the place this remembering had not filled, Dessa’s name going among the yard’s others and not under this roof, the stopped ear-sentence brought to the calling word at the very end of a term already ended, the promise under the Vail wheel-shed neither given nor withdrawn, and Garl’s unforged collector sentence beside the seventh notch — each lay on its own place with a finger between, and none was made to stand up and call itself a rule. The afternoon came on without being announced, the way light in Low Kiln goes from the colour of wet ash to the colour of a banked coal and then to nothing. Before she took the basket down Sella Vail came to the hearth first, as a woman goes to a door before she opens it, and told the coals it was the thaw in the voice another woman would have used for a child who could not hear her; she turned them with the back of a broken pot and gave the small intake, the hold and the letting-go toward no opening, with neither gap figured and neither made to stand for a number. Then she lifted the basket and carried the boy down the nineteen steps at the half-pace she keeps from every open slate, the instruction going to the wool and the river wind and the banked fire and never to the basket. Three doors down the Clay Yard she stopped at House Silt, whose plank door stood finger’s-width open as it had stood on the day he was born out of it and on the day she carried him away, and set the basket just inside the finger’s-width door at the edge of the light where no slate lay open and no writing could fall across the wicker. She gave it no instruction. A thing in a basket was a thing under a roof, and that was the whole of what she would say of it.

He was three hundred and ninety-three days old, was carried everywhere, and was told by nobody what a day was; the woman carrying him discussed the room with the pot and not with him. The adult in him had once walked into rooms like this one and read them in a single pass — the bench, the water, the draught at the floor, the place where a hand had been — and had been paid for the reading in a language nobody under this roof would have understood. The infant in him was held against a shoulder that smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and wanted the crust two inches from his ear. Both were true. The second was louder. Neither won, and no verdict was looked for; the quarrel was the work. He had learned that a room seen from a basket is a room seen and not entered, and that a woman who will not name a thing will still put it where she keeps it, which is a kind of telling if a man has the hands to read it, and he had not.

The surgeon took the room as he had taken it before, by its furniture and by the gap in its count. A bench by the door. A pot on the bench. A draught running along the floor to the corner where the grey lay thick against the skirting. It was one of six rooms in the district that held the same four things, with a finger between it and each of the others, and he had never once let one stand for another; a birth room is a birth room, a kiln-house a kiln-house, and the room where a slate is read on market-day keeps its own gap under its own name. The oiled paper at the window hung slack in its frame. The trodden-ash floor had a board worn smooth where a woman had stood a great many mornings. The low song came up through the floor from the back room, flattened by work until the words were only the shape of words, and under it came the small sour tang of Ash and the sharp clean smell of lye water put on with a cloth. On the bench a birth-slate lay turned toward its owner with no figure standing on the side the basket could see.

From the low angle of the basket the pewter tin hung above the empty bed, round and dull, with a pale fold at its seam where a hand had opened it and shut it and left it, and a bright rub on the lid where a thumb had gone often enough to wear the pewter shiny. It was no taller than a man’s palm and no wider than a cup’s bottom, and it was set high where a child lying on the board-bed could see it and not reach it, which is where women in this yard put the things they mean to keep and not use. Pewter goes grey in the smoke and a thumb brings it back to a small bright place; the bright place is not a repair and does not hide the seam, and the boy had known a hundred vessels that carried their break in full view and went on holding. The bed beneath the tin was a board-bed of grey wool with a hollow where something had lain, and the hollow had kept its shape after the weight went out of it the way a shore keeps the shape of water that has gone off it.

The surgeon in him noted the seam as a man notes a wound dressed by somebody else: pale, dry, drawn tight, with a lip of worn metal on either side and nothing coming out of it, and he had no instrument small enough to take it apart and no leave to stand over it. Grief in this world had no object he could name, and so it had learned to attach to the small hard things a house leaves in place — a lid worn bright, a fold gone pale, a hollow in wool that holds the shape of a weight that is gone. He let the register go over the tin and move on, because a field read out of order is a field read wrong, and there were seven places in the yard still to go around.

He did not want the tin open. He had wanted a great many things in this life and had been given none of them, and the wanting of the tin was not among them; it was a kept thing, and he had no hand two paces long to lift it and no leave to lift it if he had. The surgeon read it the way he read the wool in the empty place at his ear: a thing left where it lies is still kept, entered nowhere, and the keeping of it costs the hand that left it nothing and the hand that finds it nothing. That was all. He did not turn the errand into a visit to a bank, did not make a quest of a pewter lid, and did not write a cause across the pale fold. There was no name under the seam that he could say, no number in any thumb that he could give, and the lie told for mercy stood as a fact nobody under any roof could correct. A lie told to keep a child safe does not become true because the child grows; it curdles into furniture, and the furniture stays where it was put.

The neighbour at her cloth did not look up. She had a tub of lye water at her knee and a board across it, and her hands went down and came up and went down again with the rhythm of a woman who has done the same work since before the boy was born, and the low song went on under her breath with the words worn off it. She did not look at the writing on the slate turned toward its owner, and she did not look at the tin. If she knew what the fold at the seam was, she had put the knowing somewhere a Court thumb would not find it, as this yard puts everything it keeps. Sella stood half a pace off with her hands not still, took what the house had come for — a small wrapped thing that went into her apron without a figure spoken over it — and straightened a chipped cup on the bench with a finger that knew the place of it without looking. “Don’t set that wet on the bench,” she said to the cup, or to the room, or to the woman at the cloth; she had a way of giving an instruction to the furniture so that no person would have to own it. “You’ll pay for it in the thaw.” The neighbour’s hands went down and came up, and once the song broke the surface into a sentence about the cold and the price of barley and went back under with the words worn off it again, the way the yard says what it has to say and then says nothing more. Neither woman named the room or made a lesson of the visit; what the house had come for went under Sella’s apron, and the tin went on keeping what the tin kept, and the slate kept its writing turned to its owner. He did not look at the tin a second time as the basket went out. A thing seen once from a low angle is seen, and a second look would make a habit of the looking, and a habit wants a hand that placed twice where no hand had placed anything but the one that shut the lid.

At the threshold Sella went around the open slate at the half-pace, the way she went around every slate whether it was written on or not, and carried the boy back up the yard saying nothing of the room, the tin, the cup or the cloth. Out in the Clay Yard the grey lay in the places the yard had left it, and she went around the seven walked-around places with a look and a wide step and no number, once and not twice, widest around the returning-line place where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out. The look stayed the length of a step and then went off the place, as a man’s eye goes off a thing he has been told not to keep looking at and is not the same as never having looked. A woman going past with a pail took the long way around the same ground without appearing to know why, and the boy gave her wide step no name and no number: a wide step is a wide step and the yard had no word for what had gone into the place. The clean dark line at the Vail corner kept its finer grey beside it called the thaw, thickened in the dry cold with the faint sour tang coming up where no broom had gone in two years; the space beneath the lowest rack kept its broken birth-slates; the gone pan’s round ring kept its round place; the trough stood bare and dark; the field’s empty place kept its emptiness. The three coverings — the stayed frost, the skinned trough and the clouded plate on the flat stone by the well — were weather entered nowhere with a finger between every pair, and the river kept its note of water over water under the winter skin, a covering put on by the cold and taken off by the cold, found where put and not made again. Beside the crust in the fold the wool lay folded once in the empty place, found where put again at the going back, and it was a finding still and not a placing: not a second fold, not a third crust, not a habit (a habit wants a hand that placed twice), not a way (a way wants a third), not a sign, not a gift, not a payment and not a rule.

At the yard well the day’s costs came as directions and went away as weather. A man come down the clay road brought a number up out of a fist toward the pail, looked at it, and put it back into his sleeve so that the figure arrived nowhere; nobody asked him what it was, and the boy gave the fist no interior, for a fist is a fist and not a hand on a record. A woman asked what the day’s work had cost and was sent to the pot with the house has said it; the pot said nothing, and the question went off with the light as questions do in this yard. Another asked where the weather had been bought and was told to ask the weather, which had laid the cold on and would take it off, and she went back to her pail with the answer she had come for and no figure in it. Under the small sounds the boy filed the order of reaching: hand to the record before eye to the person, and mouth after both. He filed it as anatomy — the hand arriving first where the damage is, the eyes going to the face only when the record has been read, the mouth opening last of all — and gave no interior to any of the hands, for a hand moving first is a fact about a hand and not a confession from the person who owns it. He had seen it in a midwife laying slates before turning to a fold, in Berr Clay wiping his thumb twice before touching nothing, in Hessa Rale going up the road with the writing turned to her own sleeve, in Ilva passing a hand toward her sleeve before her eyes went to the asker, and in a collector with a soft cuff and a flat satchel setting his hand to the slate before his eyes went to a bed. The narrow slate up the road stayed unread, the notched strip stayed in its sleeve, and the twelve true subtractions stayed locked in the box up the clay road with the key gone up in a coat pocket that never came back down and no key in the boy’s hand. Nine record-first hands stood on nine trays with a finger between every pair and no tenth stood up in the empty place; the clean voice that had spoken a name on Weighing stayed taken out of its own sentences on a place of its own and was not made a tenth by being clean.
