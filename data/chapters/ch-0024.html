The Last Tend of Seed

Days 110–112 · Seed 21–23, Y.H. 813 · Low Kiln, Sill

A tend is counted by what is left on the board, not by what went into the ground. A hand reaches for the empty place and closes on the loaf beyond it and goes on; the shelf keeps its own account and gives nobody a figure.

— A saying of the clay-bound, Low Kiln

— Day 110 —

Seed 21, Y.H. 813

The last tend of the planting came up under a sky the colour of wet ash, and the seed stood in the worked strips east of Low Kiln with nobody giving a figure for the hands that had set it there. A tend is ten days and the month stood near its end, yet the yard kept its account the way it kept everything else: in a glance thrown at a strip, a fist opened and shut, a handful let fall and covered over with brown earth. On Seed 15 the hands had gone into the ground; on Seed 21 they were still going in, stooped along the turned rows, and the boy carried at the edge of the work was one hundred and ten days old, three months and twenty days, and nobody in the strips or under the roof had a word in which to say what that counted. He lay in the basket with the fold at his ear and the wool ridden back from one bare wrist, and his small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the direction of the seed and came down. He could no more reach the strips than he could reach the number of hands in them, and the two things stood on the same place in his mind: a direction with no figure at the foot of it.

The last tend had a shape the yard would not put into words. The seed was barley, the last of the store, and it went in with the dry, faintly sweet smell it had had on the threshing floor, under the mineral tang that came up off the brook. A woman went along the second strip with seed in her fist and let it out in a handful, and when a girl beside her asked how many hands it would take to finish before the month turned, the woman shut the fist and said the ground would tell her when it had enough. A man with a barrow counted the backs along the row with his mouth, shaped a number, and thought better of giving it, so the figure went from the strip to his teeth and back and arrived nowhere. The boy watched the two of them from the edge and set the direction he had carried for a month beside the number the yard would not give, and kept them on two places. A direction become worked ground is still a direction, and a number withheld in the very act of planting is withheld like any other number.

Sella Vail carried him at the half-pace from every open slate. She had brought him out through the Clay Yard with the basket held level and the little-door burn shut on nothing where her hand had nothing to hold, and she set him where he could see the work and not be part of it, as a woman sets a pot down where it may be seen and not slopped. The yard was drying without care after the flood. Wet wool hung on a line and took the wind as it came; woodsmoke lay flat under the roofs with no lift in it; and in the unswept corner by the lowest rack the sour mineral tang came up out of Ash as it came up every morning, whether the day was called the thaw or weather. Before she spoke to any person she gave the instruction to the bucket, and then to the broom, and then to the pot and the fire, in the order a woman gives a household its breath. “Over the road first,” she told the broom, “then the Clay Yard, and let the line lie where it lies.” To the basket she gave no instruction at all. The basket lay where it was set and took what came.

“The house wants it dry,” she said, where another woman would have said I, and went around the space beneath the lowest rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. The broken birth-slates lay under there and were entered by nobody. A slate somebody had set out to dry stood propped at the rack’s end, and she went around it with the same half-pace she kept from every open slate, giving it the width of a thing that might be knocked and could not be put back. The boy watched the width of the going-around and filed it as the care the house gave every open record, exact and worth nothing. Her lips moved along the row of loaves when she passed through the kiln-house, but she gave nobody a figure, and she did not look into the basket to see whether he had understood any of it.

By midmorning she had carried him back under the Vail kiln-house roof and set the basket at the edge of the light with his face toward the room and not toward the door. Across from him stood the loaf shelf, waist-high and worn smooth where a generation of hands had pushed loaves along it and taken them away. There were two gaps in the row. One stood at the end where the row had once reached farther and had come in, so the yard said, with the planting; the other was a palm’s breadth nearer the middle that had come in the night of Seed 6 with no scrape, no crumb, no print and no word. Both were bare board. The board had been worn to the colour of old rope, and the light that fell on it showed no more than it had ever shown.

A neighbour came in with a pot in her hand. She did not look at the child. Her hand went first to the shelf, the way a hand goes to a familiar place in the dark, and passed over the palm’s breadth of bare board without stopping; it opened for the half-beat it takes to find what is not there, shut on the hard heel of a loaf beyond the gap, and came away with the loaf and the pot together. She had reached past the empty place before her eyes had found the boy at all. It was the order he had watched all his days in this district: the hand to the record, the hand to the loaf, the eyes to the person last of all. Here the person could not have taken the loaf from her if he had wanted to, and she went on holding it. Her thumb had chaff in the creases and a sliver of husk under the nail, and the heel of the loaf was hard enough that her hand left no impression on it. She held the loaf against the pot with her forearm, already turning away.

“The old place at the row’s end came in with the planting,” the neighbour said, speaking to the board as a woman speaks to a pot she is scolding, “and this one’s the price of a wet thing set down wrong.”

She said it in one breath. The two sayings lay together in the air and she took them away with her pot, going out with her back to the boy and her mind already on the next thing. She had not seen that the old space and the new one were the same absence wearing two coats. She had not seen the empty place at his ear, where one brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold with nothing beside it. She had made a cause out of two bare boards and gone home satisfied with the thing she had made, and the boy, who could have told her exactly what her hand had done before her eyes did, could not lift his own hand to his own chin. The small hand outside the wool went up an inch and came down, and the fold at his ear kept its one stiff crust without a second beside it. A stranger passing the door would have seen a woman with a pot and a bare board and a basket by the wall, and would have gone on about her errand with nothing to tell.

Sella had come up beside the shelf with her burn shut on nothing. She moved her lips along the row, counting what the house was owed in loaves and in the spaces where loaves had stood, and gave nobody a figure. Her eye went once to the clay jar on the wall shelf, cold under its glaze, the way a woman checks a pot set down where it may be seen; she took nothing from it and said nothing about it. Her hand went over the bare board and came away with no loaf and no number. She did not touch the widow’s mark at her throat. A shelf is not an opening, and a strip, a rack, a yard and a basket are not openings either; the rule held on the last tend of Seed as it had held on the first day of Stone. Around the clean dark line in the corner and the new finer grey that had gathered beside it she went with a care that looked like not seeing them at all, and told nobody they were there.

She had left the widow’s mark where it hung. It was not for a shelf or a gap or a neighbour’s one-breath grammar; it was for the opening of a kiln and for nothing else under this roof, and there had been no opening since the coals were banked on the Last Day. Whatever she put away she put in the private roll nobody was shown, and the roll went on not being shown. The boy watched the cord at her throat and the hand that did not go to it, and set the not-going on the same tray as her not-figuring: two keepings that did not join.

The boy kept three places and did not let them touch. On the shelf in this room stood two gaps; in the fold at his ear, in a room the shelf did not touch, lay one stiff crust with its empty place unnamed; and below the sill his foot stayed dark inside three turns of wool, cold to one woman and weather to the yard, the two words never let into the same sentence. The dark foot was the extremity going before the core, and the almost-smile that is not peace would come onto a face before anybody thought to warm it, the afterdrop waiting on the far side of a warming nobody would perform. He read it as cleanly as he had ever read anything across a table and could not carry the reading two paces. Three absences, three places, no line drawn through them. He had learned what a line drawn through three absences will do: it gives a man who wants an answer a cause with a clean hand at the foot of it, and the clean hand was the thing he had spent two lives declining to become. He kept his finger off all three without thinking about it now, the way a man keeps his finger off a wound he has decided not to open. The neighbour’s joining was not his joining. He set her one-breath saying on a place of its own beside the other tellings of the shelf and the watcher’s late turn at the market, and laid no finger across any of them. It was a third shaping of the same held thing, advanced again and paid nothing, and it would wait for a far-off hand that was not his.

He read the reaching hand as he had once read a chart laid on a table: a clean line drawn neatly across a note nobody had opened, exact, complete, and worth nothing. The neighbour’s hand had closed on the loaf beyond the bare place and gone off with her pot, and nothing in the room had made an event of it. His own hand went up an inch toward the note of water over water that came from the Ilm beyond the yard and came down. He wanted the crust two inches from his ear, the breast, the bucket’s lip two paces off, and the number of his days said aloud by somebody who would know what it counted. He could reach none of them and ask for none of them. He was the only adult in this room and the least use in it; a man lays his instruments out in order because he means to put his hands into a body, and there was no field here to open and no person who would let him carry any of it to a door.

The ordinary work went on around the absence. A pot was set down and left. Someone at the yard well lifted a bucket and set it down again, and the water in it took the light and gave nothing back. Wet wool went on drying without care. The low mutter of voices came up from the strips about an early thaw and a late, and neither side won, because the house had said the seed would go in when it went in, and no figure had been spoken anywhere. Sella gave the instruction to the wool and the pot and the fire and never to the basket, and went about the keeping with her hands that never stilled, counting spoons, nails, the breaths of a sleeping child. Nobody said how many mornings of the month were left; the house kept its days in loaves pushed along a board, and the board kept two gaps nobody called a figure. The carried fire answered four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for one hundred and ten days. The boy lay at the edge of the light with one crust stiff in the fold and the shelf’s two gaps in the other room, and the two rooms did not touch. His small hand went up an inch toward the water and came down. One hundred and ten stood where one hundred and nine had stood, exact and unspent, and spoken by nobody.

The light went off the yard the way it went off everything in Low Kiln, at the edges first and in the middle last, and when it had come down to a finger’s width along the rack-tops Sella Vail came across to the basket at the edge of the light and lifted it as she lifted a pot filled to the lip. The little-door burn on the back of her hand closed first on the handle and then on nothing, the way it closed on nothing whenever her hand had nothing to hold, and she carried him up the Clay Yard at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, neither between a hand and the writing it was going to nor where a figure lay with its face up. A slate stood propped at the rack’s end with its writing turned away; she went around it as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and around the space beneath the lowest rack with the same width, where the broken birth-slates lay and were entered by nobody. the boy rode level under the fold, one hundred and ten days old and awake to every jar of the carrying, looking at the underside of her chin and at the smoke that lay low behind her with no lift in it. He kept the shape of her going, which was the shape she went around every keeping in this house.

Under the Vail kiln-house roof she set him on his bed and not in the Debt Loft, and put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without once looking at his face. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. “It’s the river wind.” She did not say the boy, and she did not look down. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and she left it where it was, because nobody in this house had been given an instruction about the thread, and a basket set down is a pot set down and left; a thing set down and left is a thing still kept, which is not the same as a thing tended. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and nobody put it right and nobody made an event of the sound. Cold came in its known order, cheek, then temple, then the wrist where the wool rode back from the bare place, and he could no more alter one step of it than he could keep the wool on the wrist. He was a surgeon who could reduce a shoulder in the dark and could not keep a blanket on his own body, and the joke of it had cost somebody else from the first morning and had never once been funny enough to say aloud.

Presently, with the room gone quiet around the fire, he turned out the private file the way a man lays instruments on a towel before he opens a field: each thing in its place, handle out, edge in, nothing that could roll. He had no hands for it and no towel, but the order was the order, and an order kept without a hand is still an order. Every object lay where he had left it. Six hearings of the full note stood at six — five from four sources, a cup across a birth room, a pot in the wheel-shed through the wall, the district’s slate twice at the Cross, and a man giving at the Drawing — and the sixth, the small flat knock out of a person as the men lifted her, stood beside the five and never among them. There was no seventh. He had listened for one all day in the ordinary work and in the going of the light, and the ear that held six had added nothing.

On another square of the towel lay three near-full answers, advanced and not paid, neither hearings nor the third thing; he had no hand to tap the pots and ask them to say it again. On a third lay the three intervals, Stone 13, Stone 17, Stone 25, each a place where a sound had stopped and not come back. The word forty lay on a place of its own, unspoken, with the readable double turn beside it — a cord twisted twice beneath a grey one, tail tucked, tied left over right — and the weight of a thumb on wicker set apart from both, a hand reaching into a pocket-place in another life and coming back with wool. He knew Grathic in neither speech nor writing and had no right to the word and no way to spend it; the shape was legible to him without its name, and a word and the thing it names are two objects, and he had kept them on two places since the Weir. Four low things kept their four places — the told bell out of the dark, the heard bell over the water, the early note out of the stone, the kiln’s missing breath — and the river’s note of water going over water was a fifth thing on a place of its own and not a hearing. The two tellings of the shut counting room lay joined to no kiln, whatever the shape of them; the double bite of the damper lay filed as an object, mercy and mutilation in one piece of iron with no Grathic word for both; and the loan that can hear lay where it had lain, uncalled.

Into this order he put the day without entering any of it. The neighbour’s one-breath naming at the shelf went on a place beside the two other tellings and the watcher’s late turn, a third shaping of the thing that waited for a far-off hand and was not his; the reaching hand that had gone to the board before the eyes went to the boy went on its own square, exact, complete and worth nothing, a chart closed with a neat line over a note nobody had opened. Berr had not yet come up the clay road and the figure he would read was not yet read, so the day’s arithmetic was still a direction with no figure at the foot of it; the seed stood in the strips with the hands in it counted in glances and handfuls, and the last tend went on being a tend and not a number. He added the day and spent none of it. The file lay complete and useless as a set of instruments laid out on a towel in a room with no patient and no light to work by; there was no field here to open and no person who would let him carry any of it to a door.

His body kept its own account and kept it badly. One brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold at his ear with its empty place beside it unnamed and no third, and the loaf shelf in the next room kept two gaps in a room this one did not touch. Below the sill his foot stayed dark inside three turns of wool, cold to one woman and weather to the yard, the two words never let into the same sentence; he had kept them apart for one hundred and ten days and would keep them apart now, because a cold foot and a cold day laid in one sentence make a cause with a clean hand at the foot of it, and the clean hand was the thing he had spent two lives declining to become. The dark foot was the extremity going before the core. The almost-smile that is not peace would come onto a face before anybody thought to warm it; the afterdrop waited on the far side of a warming nobody would perform. He read it as cleanly as he had ever read anything across a table and could not carry the reading two paces. Perfect and worth nothing, like every other object on the towel.

In the corner the clean dark line where the Ash ridge had turned lay unswept, and the new finer grey beside it lay unswept too, and the house called both of them the thaw and would go when it would go. Around the space beneath the lowest rack she had gone wide with her half-pace and had not entered it, and around the clean line and the finer hairline she had gone with a care that looked like not seeing them. No opening had come in all these days, so the widow’s mark hung where it hung, untouched by any hand at a shelf, a strip, a rack, a yard, a basket or a bed; it was for the opening of a kiln and for nothing else under this roof, and there had been no opening since the coals were banked on the Last Day. Whatever she put away she put in the private roll nobody was shown, and the private roll went on not being shown. He set the not-going beside the not-figuring, two keepings that did not join.

Somewhere off the bed, on its sill in the wheel-shed where he could neither reach it nor check it, the small bench knife lay at the very edge with its blade to the wall and its handle out. Six years stood in a mark a thumb had worn almost flat, and the seventh stood nearly gone and still held; it was the one entry in this house the house could not check, and it lay where a man could find it in the dark without looking. To the kindling a voice had said six counted and the next not counted, and the breath behind that sentence had not gone in, held from Seed 16 and still held, the small interval at the top of each reach showing where it should have gone. Beside it, kept on the same square and entered nowhere, lay the sentence no man under this roof had said — the sentence that would call the loan, if it were ever said, and the loan that can hear if ever it were called. Something must give at the end of the seventh year. The year stood one day nearer its end than it had stood the morning before, and it did not give here; the knife kept its six and the breath kept its place, and the boy on the bed could no more carry either to a door than he could lift his hand to his own chin.

The fire had been going down since the light went off the yard. Sella stood over it with the back of a broken pot in her hand and turned the coals, and the ash went over them blind and smooth as a face with its eyes shut. “It was the thaw,” she told the fire, and turned the coals again. “The house will keep it.” Where another woman would have said I she said the house, and she did not look toward the bed. The fire took the coal under the ash and gave back four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, as it had given back for a hundred and ten days; its payer had gone unnamed for a hundred and ten days, and nobody under this roof had asked who paid, and the boy had asked the question in his own head every morning and had never once had a mouth to put it in.

She stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand, counting something under her breath toward no opening, the way she had stood at the shelf moving her lips along a row of loaves with two gaps in it and given nobody a figure. The grey streak at her temple showed pale in the dying light off the coals, one strand among the coal-black, and she pushed the black back with the wrist that carries the burn, the little-door shutting on nothing as the wrist passed her brow. It was told of her in the yard that she had once sat three nights with a child of her own who cooled while everybody was kind, and the boy had heard it and had not laid it beside the dark foot below the sill; two keeps, two places, and he held his finger off both. She set the pot where it could be found in the dark and went on with the keeping, which needed no light and had never once been explained.

The door stood a finger’s width open, as it had stood for a hundred and ten days. Through the gap came the river’s low note out of the Ilm beyond the yard, water going over water, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming; through the floor came the kiln, four, four and a gap, under blind ash and the back of a broken pot. He kept the two sounds in two places as he had kept them on the first day and every day after, and the two counts did not meet. Above him the boards kept four nails, four, and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb. One count on the ceiling, one in the floor, one through the gap in the door, and none of the three would ever add up to the number of days he had been warm, which nobody under this roof knew was a number.

His small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down. One hundred and ten stood where one hundred and nine had stood, exact, unspent and spoken by nobody, while the river went over water beyond the gap and the fire missed its fifth breath under the floor. The crust kept its empty place in the fold, the shelf kept its two gaps in the room that did not touch this one, and the count went on.

Seed 22, Y.H. 813

Before first grey the men came up the Clay Yard in Low Kiln with their collars high, and went into the room off the wheel-shed that stood a finger’s width open. The low voices went in ahead of them and stayed where they had gone; the cold came out through the finger’s width and went along the ground, but the voices did not carry as far as the edge of the light where Sella Vail had set the basket down. She had set it there before she set anything else, at the place the yard’s work reached and its writing did not, and she gave the instruction to the wool and the wind and never to the basket. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. “It’s the river wind.” the boy lay with his ear under the fold and one bare wrist out of it, one hundred and eleven days old and three months and twenty-one days, and nobody in the yard or under the roof had a word in which to say what that counted. The men’s boots went past at the edge of the light and he counted them without meaning to, as he counted nails, and put the number on a place of its own and gave it nobody. A count kept in a head is still a count kept, and in this district the keeping of a thing is the whole of the ownership.

Cold came to him in its known order, cheek, then temple, then the bare wrist where the wool had ridden back from the joint, and he could no more alter one step of it than he could keep the wool where it had been put. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen again in the small hours and nobody had been given an instruction about the thread, so it stayed where it was, a small bright line at the corner of his lip that no hand would wipe. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and nobody under the roof had been told to put it right, so nobody did. He was a surgeon who could have taken a shoulder apart in the dark and could not keep a fold of wool on his own wrist, and the joke of it had been paid for by somebody else every morning of his life here and had never once been funny enough to say aloud. The men’s boots went on past the basket; the yard’s work went on around the place where they had gone; the boy kept the cold in its order and the thread in its place and said nothing to either.

Jorra Vail gave the house no name for them. He did not say the room was cold. He came through the finger’s-width-open door with fine grey slate dust worked into both forearms past the bend of his little finger, ground small as meal and rubbed in with the splitting of slate, not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel that dried pale on top and left the creases clean. The little finger had been bent since he was eleven and it led the rest of his hand into everything, the damper, the wheel, the bench. The boy kept dusty forearms and clean ones on one tray, two states of one man’s mornings, and asked neither which slate had been broken nor whether it was a birth-slate. A question asked aloud in this house would have gone to the pot before it went to the man, and the pot would not have answered; he had learned to keep his questions where questions can last for years.

To the surgeon’s eye the grey was a foreign thing ground small and driven into the creases of a working man’s skin, the way grit drives into a field nobody has cleaned; the bone-coloured slip sat on top and dried pale and came off with washing, but the slate dust worked in past the bend of the finger and stayed. He read the difference without deciding to, the way he read the colour of a man’s lips before the man spoke of pain. Two forearms were two records of one morning, and the men who had come before the light put the second record there and went back into the finger’s width of open door without a name. Which slate had been broken, and whether it had been a birth-slate, were the two questions standing behind the dust; he kept them behind it, as a man keeps an instrument out of a field until the field is ready, and asked neither aloud nor in the shape of a look toward the door.

Under the lowest rack the broken birth-slates lay where they had lain, gone around as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. Nobody stepped into the space above them. The yard went around it with a broom and a glance and no figure, as it went around the clean dark line at the Vail corner and the new finer grey beside it that the house called the thaw; a line left where it is is still a line kept. The boy carried the going-around at the edge of the basket and set it beside the dust on the same tray, two keepings that did not join and did not grow into a cause. Whole slate under a rack is a thing the yard enters into nothing, and the space where it should have stood is kept as carefully as the line nobody will sweep.

Jorra spoke to the wheel before he spoke to anyone. “It doesn’t care whose hands,” he said. “It cares if they’re steady.” The wheel turned for a steady man and for an unsteady one and kept no record of which, and the dust on his forearms was the only thing in the shed that had come from somewhere the house did not name. He set a pot on the bench and looked a moment longer at the shoulder than the shoulder required. “You’ll do,” he said, in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either. He knew the full note of a Vail pot before it left the wheel — what it would give under a knuckle when it was whole, what it would withhold when it was not — and he would not say so before a kiln-master come up from High Kiln, who would call the knowledge early and make something of it. The boy had watched him know it for three years and had never once heard him say it. Knowing a thing and saying it were two objects, and he set them on two places as he set the dusty forearms and the clean ones.

His quiet was a weather signal in this house; the yard had learned to read the lowering of his voice the way it read the wind off the Ilm, and when he went quieter the work went around him with a care that looked like not seeing him. From the finger’s width of open door the low voices went on and came back with nothing in them that could be carried to a slate; he did not take them up and he did not give the house a name for the men. A kiln-master come up from High Kiln would have called the knowing early and made something of it, would have set a figure at the foot of it and asked the pot to agree; Jorra kept the knowing where the boy kept his questions, unspoken and unentered, and the wheel went on turning under both of them.

Sella had not finished telling him the fire was low. “The fire’s low. Feed it—” The end of the sentence never got as far as him. His bent finger went into the cold iron of the damper ahead of her sentence, the little finger leading, and the iron took the cold out of his fingertip and gave it back a little warmer. The warmth came back into the fingertip before it came back into the hand, the extremity leading the core in reverse, and the boy noted the order as he had noted it on a hundred cold mornings in a person who could not tell him what was happening to his own body. Iron does this: it takes the heat out of a thing and gives a little of it back, and a man may take the small return for kindness. The bite that closes on a vessel to hold it whole is the bite that closes on a finger to take it off, and there is no word in the yard’s speech for the two mercies in one piece of metal; the house had the object and had no name for what it did, and went on using it as a woman uses a door she has never looked at closely. He moved the tongue of iron a hair’s breadth and left it standing there. The end of her instruction went past him and arrived at the pot, and the household straightened around his silence the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted: the fire took its coal, the pot took its place, the broom took its instruction, and nobody gave any instruction to the basket. The little-door burn on the back of her hand shut on nothing as her hand came away from the handle, and she did not look at him and he did not answer with a word. Obedience in this house arrives before the instruction that calls it, and the arriving is also a correction neither of them will ever name.

Tarn took the broom only after she had given it its instruction. “The rack first,” she said to the broom. “Then the road. Leave the lines where they lie.” She said the house where another woman would say I, and she gave the instruction to the broom before she gave it to the man, and Tarn came and took it up as a man takes up a tool that has already been named. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line, and no scrubbing lifted it; the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back. His chest had been tight since the dark, and there was a small interval at the top of each reach where the breath should have gone in, and the breath did not go in. Six years stood in a mark his thumb had worn almost flat and the seventh stood nearly gone and still held, the one entry in this house the house could not check; to the kindling he had said six counted and the next not counted, and the saying had cost him nothing in words and everything somewhere else.

The bristles went over the flagstones and came to the damper. Jorra’s bent finger was standing in the iron. Tarn’s stroke checked. Half a beat, no more — the bristles stood on the stone with a whole reach held between one sweep and the next — and then they went on, and he went on toward the road without turning his head and without a word. The flagstones kept the place where the stroke had stopped as a thing nobody would enter. Neither man named the other’s silence; the two silences stood in one room with no hand joining them, and the loan that can hear was not called. The boy set the half-check beside the bent finger on one tray and laid no finger across the two, because a likeness laid on a likeness grows a cause, and a cause in a house like this would come with a clean hand at the foot of it. He had known two keepings under one roof for a hundred and eleven days and had not once made a third thing of them.

Tarn had turned his head a degree at a pot’s note in the yard often enough for the boy to know the turn before he made it, and he had never once named the sound or looked toward the basket to see whether it had been heard. Two ears kept the same silence under one roof, and an ear that can hear is worth more than a debt that holds a man, which is why the keeping of it is unsafe and why he keeps it where he keeps the seventh year. The half-beat at the damper went on the tray beside the dust as another interval, a place where a stroke should have gone on and did not, and he wrote no cause across the space from the iron to the broom. A household can straighten around a thing it will not say and go on straight for years, and the strain of it shows nowhere except in the small places where a stroke stops and starts again.

He read the damper once more while the dust worked into his attention. It was the bite that holds a vessel whole and the bite that can take the hand off a man, one piece of iron with two mercies in it, and there was no word in Grathic that held both. He filed the reading as an object joined to no kiln, exact and worth nothing, on the tray with the other exact things that had bought him nothing in two lives. The wheel gave its low note and the pot gave its note and the iron gave back no sound at all, only cold; no seventh hearing came before midday in the wheel-shed, and the three near-full answers stayed advanced and not paid on the tray where he had left them. Four low things kept their four places and the river’s note of water over water was a fifth on a place of its own, and the two tellings of the room that had been shut in the war stayed joined to no kiln, whatever the shape of them. He had a file laid out like instruments on a towel and no towel, no instruments and no field to open; the hour went on without adding to it, and the fact that nothing had been added was itself an entry he did not enter.

He’s not Vail stayed sealed as tightly as a lid nobody had tried to lift in three years, and the promise of the foundling’s name to the men who burn slates was neither given nor withdrawn. Nobody looked toward the bed. Nobody looked into the basket. No opening came before midday, so the widow’s mark hung where it hung, untouched at a shelf, a strip, a rack, a wheel, a damper and a door, and the private roll nobody was shown went on not being shown. One brown-edged crust kept its empty place in the fold in a room the wheel-shed did not touch, and the loaf shelf kept its two gaps in a room that did not touch that one, and he carried the three absences in three places that did not join. Something must give at the end of the seventh year; it did not give here, and the breath behind the man’s chest stayed where it had been put on Seed 16, unspent, the small interval at the top of each reach showing where it should have gone in.

Jorra’s bent finger stood in the cold iron of the damper, and Tarn’s broom had stopped half a stroke on the flagstones behind him before it went on about the yard, two silences in one room with no word passing between them. The boy was carried at the edge of the light with the fine grey dust and the half-check laid on one tray and not joined, his small hand outside the wool going up an inch toward the note of water over water beyond the yard and coming down. One hundred and eleven stood as one hundred and ten had stood, exact, unspent and spoken by nobody under the roof, while the wheel went on turning for whoever had steady hands and the fire went on missing its fifth breath under blind ash with its payer unnamed.

The afternoon came down on the yard in a thin light that made nothing new and took nothing away. At the yard well the women let the bucket down and brought it up, and the rope went over a stone lip wet with the week’s weather and mud ground into it to the last strand. A woman with wet sleeves stood aside to let another draw, and used the waiting to ask what the planting had cost now that hands had been in the strips. She asked it first of the ground, then of the bucket, and only then of Sella Vail, who had come to the kiln-house doorway with the basket at her feet and her hands already busy at something else.

She did not turn. She moved her lips along the loaf shelf in the next room, the way a woman reads a row she has read a thousand times and will not read aloud, and her eyes went for the space of a breath to the clay jar on the wall shelf, cold under its glaze, and came back to the row. The space at the end of it stood where it had stood. She gave it no figure.

“Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there,” she said. “The house has said it.”

She had given the bucket its instruction before she gave it to the women, and the broom its before she gave it to the man, and she gave none at all to the basket. the boy lay at the edge of the work where the yard reached and its writing did not, one hundred and eleven days old and three months and twenty-one days, and the cold iron of the well’s handle stood two inches beyond his hand. He could have taken a shoulder apart in the dark and he could not close two inches. The woman’s sleeves were wet to the elbow and she kept them away from her body with her hands half-lifted, the way a woman keeps a wet thing she has set down wrong; chaff had got under her fingernails from gripping the rope. She went off with her question still in her mouth, satisfied with an answer that had been addressed to a pot. The bucket went on being filled. The broom went on with its instruction. The basket was not looked into.

To the surgeon’s eye the answering of a question addressed to a pot was a chart closed with a neat line over a note that had not been read. The line was exact. The figure at the foot of it was exact. Neither told him what lay under the note, and a man who tried to make them tell would be a man asking a slate to agree with a strip. He kept the woman’s reaching hand on a place of its own and did not lay it across the hand that had reached past the bare board at the shelf the day before, though both reached in the same way and toward the same sort of empty place, and a third likeness would have made a cause with a clean hand at the foot of it. The surgeon read a chart that way once, and the note turned down was the part that mattered, and the hand that went past it to the signature was always a clean hand. He had spent two lives declining to become that hand, and here it was in every yard, doing a kindness.

Berr Clay came up short off the north road at the outer edge of the well, short and solid, with the red wool cord of his office folded under his coat instead of worn across it and the green of tasted glaze at one corner of his mouth. Hot clay came off him over the smell of nothing at all, which is the most expensive smell in Grath. His hands were large and clean. He wiped the thumb twice on his apron before touching nothing, and held a narrow slate unopened against his forearm, and passed a finger over the figure on it, and let his eyes rest on the writing a breath longer than the writing required — the way a man checks a dish he has set down already to be sure it will not slop. He did not raise his voice. A man who does not raise his voice in a yard makes the yard lean toward him, and the women at the bucket leaned without appearing to, the ones with wet sleeves drawing back a little to give him the dry place.

“One in twelve, as the notice says,” he said, low, into the top of the leaning yard. “A number goes into a slate and not into a strip. If your house wants it again, there’ll be a slate at the watch.”

Your house. Not my account. He took himself out of his own sentence as neatly as a hand goes out of a field before the dressing goes on, and the sentence went on standing in the yard with nobody in it. The boy had heard that grammar in a midwife’s mouth and a clerk’s and a neighbour’s and now in this man’s over the worked ground: the record before the person, the figure before the face, the hand to the slate before the eyes to the stalls. Four record-first orders on four trays, and he set this one beside them without calling any of them the same hand. The twice-wiped thumb went on a tray of its own. It was the thumb of a man who would not let a slate be spoiled by a print, which is a kind of care, and care spent on the wrong object is the most expensive kind. Berr did not look into the basket. The keeping of that not-looking went on its own place and was joined to no figure.

A man come up from the strips with a number in his fist brought it toward the well and the bucket-round, and held it out where a man might put a number, and looked at what his fist had made of it. He thought better of giving it. The fist went back into his sleeve and he went off up the road with the number still in it, and the figure arrived nowhere. It was the district’s whole arithmetic done in the open air — from a fist to a mouth to a shoulder, and nowhere — and the women went on drawing as though a man had not stood there at all. The boy kept the shape of the fist on a place of its own beside the clay jar’s coldness and the reaching hand at the shelf: a thing withheld that had never been entered and could not be, because the entering of it would have made the hand that withheld it answerable. He wrote no cause across the space from the fist to the slate. A number that arrives nowhere is still a number that was carried, and a man who carries one and sets it down where nobody will take it up has done the same work as a clerk who writes one where nobody will read it: the figure is entered in the carrying, not in the receiving. The boy had watched a figure go from a fist to a mouth to a shoulder for as long as he could remember, and had never once seen anybody hold it at the end. He did not call the man a thief or a fool, because the man had put the fist in his sleeve with the care of a man putting a tool away where it can be found, and a tool put away is a tool kept. In this district everything withheld is kept and everything kept is owed, and the boy kept the shape of the withheld thing without entering it, which was the only form of ownership available to him.

The arguers put their case to the boards and the well, as they had put it for a month, the ones who wanted the thaw early and the ones who wanted it late, neither of them asking the bucket or the pot and both of them speaking to the ground.

“Early’s best,” a woman said to the stone lip. “Get it over. A body can plan on a thing that’s done.”

“Late,” said a man to the boards. “Late puts it off.”

Tarn came and took the broom only after Sella had given it its instruction, and he said “Yes, house” to the one and “Yes, house” to the other, and agreed so entirely that the argument had nowhere to put its foot. He went on wanting the late because a late thaw puts off the paying, though the paying had stood in the ruts since the water went and would stand there whatever the moot said. His chest had been tight since the dark and there was a small interval at the top of each reach where the breath should have gone in, and the breath did not go in. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line, and no scrubbing lifted it; the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back. A breath stopped against a clamp is a breath spent whether it goes in or not, and the price of it is in the hand that holds the iron, not in the vessel it holds. The small bench knife lay where it could be checked and the seventh year lay where it could not, at the very edge of the sill, and the sentence that would call the loan stayed under this roof unspoken. The boy set the held breath beside the half-check at the damper and laid no finger across the two. A loan that can hear is a loan that can be called, and a man who keeps an ear worth more than his debt keeps it where no clerk can spell it. Two keepings under one roof had stood for a hundred and eleven days and had not yet made a third thing, and the strain of it showed nowhere in the yard except in the small interval at the top of a reach where a breath should have gone in.

Berr went off up the clay road toward High Kiln with the narrow slate under his coat and the box locked in his house behind him and the true subtraction inside it. He believed he had done the district a kindness; a reasonable man with a ledger in his hand goes home clean, and the reason he carries is the part that will be remembered against him. He had not looked back at the well and had not once said I. Garl’s debt against the firing of tiles went around the space where a name might have stood, neither paid nor settled nor done with, the way the yard went around the clean dark line and the new finer grey that the house called the thaw: a thing left where it lies is a thing kept, and a thing kept is a thing that will one day be asked for. Garl Vail had not been at the well to answer for it and could not be, and his salt-stiff coat kept his broader shape on its peg under the roof where no man had taken it down.

The boy turned out what the afternoon had given and laid it where it belonged. Berr’s twice-wiped thumb went on its own tray and did not join to the neighbour’s reaching hand at the shelf, or to Sella’s hand moving along the board, or to the clerk’s hand going to a slate before its eyes went to a person; the figure read aloud did not become the figure paid, and the figure put into a slate did not become a figure put into a strip, and a figure let fall from a fist toward a well arrived nowhere. Three likenesses on three places, and no line drawn through them, because a line drawn through three absences is a cause with a clean hand at the foot of it. The light went off the yard and the cold came up off the Ilm in its known order, cheek, then temple, then the bare wrist where the wool had ridden back from the joint, and under the roof the fold kept its one stiff crust and the empty place beside it in a room the well did not touch. His small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down. One hundred and eleven stood where one hundred and ten had stood, exact, unspent, spoken by nobody. Berr’s large clean hand wiped the thumb twice on his apron before touching nothing, and the narrow slate went up the clay road under his coat toward High Kiln with the box locked behind him, the figure read aloud already separating from the figure paid.

The river wind got under the north bridge before the market did and went among the outer boards of the fish-trade shade with a cold that had been in the water all night. It found the wicker of the creels, the slime drying on the boards, and the folds of wool in the basket set at the very end of the shade where the shadow thinned and no slate lay within reach of a hand. Sella Vail had set the basket there before she set anything else, and had chosen the place before she chose the shade. She had carried the boy down from Low Kiln at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, and gone around the space under the lowest rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. The instruction went out ahead of her hand, first to the wool and then to the water running in the gutter, and never to the basket. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. “It’s the river wind.” To the water she said, “Go where you’re going.” Only then did she set him down. A basket set down was a pot set down and left; a shade, a strip, a shelf and a bed are not openings, and she stood a half-pace from the nearest open slate as though the half-pace were a wall she had built herself and knew the thickness of.

He was one hundred and twelve days old, three months and twenty-two days, and nobody in the shade knew it and there was no word in Grathic that would have carried the saying if they had. Nobody had told him. He had stopped expecting to be told, which was not the same as not wanting it; the want went on under the not-expecting the way a coal goes on under blind ash. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and nobody turned it back because the instruction had gone to the wool and not to the wandering. Cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, and the wool did less against the river wind than the wind wanted, riding back from the bare joint so the cold found the place it had come for. His small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down. He had reduced a thousand shoulders in a room that was not this one and he could not close two inches on the basket’s rim.

Mira Silt worked the eels at the outer boards where the shadow ended, with pale slime dried on her sleeves in the long shape of her forearms and river silt dried pale in the hollow of her neck, her head held a little to the right where the top of her left ear had been, her small smile kept on her face the way a door is kept on the latch: it could be shut without anybody seeing the movement. She had a knife in one hand and a string of eel-bones in the other, and she flicked a bone against her thumb while she decided. The boy watched the order of the two hands, bone first and thumb second, and set it beside the order he had watched in other rooms — the hand to the chart before the hand to the body, the hand to the slate before the eyes to the face, the hand past the bare board before the eyes went to the child. Here the record was a bone and the account was a cord, and neither of them could be entered by any Court thumb that ever washed and went home. Two women in two districts kept two records in wool and knot, and he set the keeping on a place of its own and wrote no cause across it. Another hand going to another kept place was the third shaping of a thing he had refused to name for three days; he held it where he held the rest, advanced, exact, and worth nothing to anybody who could have paid for it.

A woman come down the steps with salt dried white at her wrists asked whether the run would be thin now the hands had gone into the strips. Mira answered the question and then her own answer, which gave her the room to choose which of the two the listener got.

“Thin?” she said. “Who’s to say thin? It comes when it comes. Ask the river.”

She moved two eel-bones against her thumb, one over and one back, while the deciding went on behind the small smile, and put one away when the deciding was done. The deciding and the not-saying were one movement of the same hand; the boy had watched a man decide the same way once with his fist shut around a number and his sleeve waiting for it, and the figure had arrived nowhere. Here the figure was not even spoken, and nothing arrived, which was the same arithmetic done more cleanly. He kept the bone-hand and the fist-hand on two places and laid no finger across them. A third likeness would have made a cause with a clean hand at the foot of it, and a clean hand at the foot of a cause was the one object in this world he had spent two lives declining to become.

She opened no slate, and the errand that had carried her three doors down the Clay Yard stayed an errand to a room; she did not turn it into a walk to Slate Row, though most of what this room held would have read better there. A likeness laid on a likeness will grow a cause whether or not anybody put one there; he had learned that in a better-lit room and had spent his whole life in this one keeping his finger off the places where it would grow.

She went out as she had come in, short, with the birth-slates under her arm, and Sella did not ask what had been written. A half-pace and a turned slate stood between the two women for the whole of the visit, which was the arrangement, and neither had put a word into it in three years. Sella lifted the basket level and carried him back out at the half-pace from every open slate, and did not look into it, and did not turn the errand into a walk to Slate Row on the way back either.

❦

The going of the light under the Vail kiln-house roof found him where it had found him a hundred and eleven times before, on his bed and not in the Debt Loft, with the fold put right at his ear and under his chin without a look at his face and the thread of drool at his mouth frozen and unwiped. Nobody had been given an instruction about the thread, and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. He turned out the private file the way a man lays instruments on a towel before he begins, and found every object where he had left it.

Six hearings of the full note stood beside and never among, with no seventh. Three near-full answers waited on their own tray, advanced and not paid. Three intervals lay on another. The word forty stayed unspoken, with the readable double turn beside it and the weight of a thumb set apart from both. Four low things kept their four places and the river’s note of water over water was a fifth on a place of its own and not a hearing. The two tellings of the shut counting room stood joined to no kiln. The damper’s double bite was filed as an object, exact and worth nothing, joined to no firing. The loan that could hear was still uncalled.

He added what the days had given and entered none of it. Mira’s hand going to the grey cord and staying longer than the figure required; the soft-cuffed clerk still set at the out-of-line landing with his slate to his forearm for a measure nobody had called; a neighbour reaching past the bare board in one breath and naming two absences as one; Berr gone up the road with the box locked in his house and the true subtraction inside; Jorra’s bent finger standing in the cold iron and Tarn’s broom checking half a stroke behind it, two silences in one room with no word between them. He set them on their places and laid no finger across any of them. A fourth likeness would have made a cause with a clean hand at the foot of it, and the clean hand was the one object in this world he had spent two lives declining to become.

The crust in the fold was one brown-edged thing gone stiff and past smelling of anything, with the empty place beside it still unnamed and no third. The shelf’s two gaps were in the other room, in a room that did not touch this one: the old space at the row’s end that came in with the planting, and the new palm’s breadth that had come in the night of Seed 6 with no scrape, crumb, print or word. Below the sill the dark foot stayed dark inside three turns of wool, cold to one woman and weather to the yard, uncured, uncaused and unnamed. He knew the order of it as he knew the back of his own hand — the extremity going before the core, the face putting on an almost-smile that was not peace, the afterdrop waiting on the far side of a warming nobody would perform — and the reading was perfect and bought nothing. There was no warmth in the house that would come and sit with that foot through the afterdrop, and if there had been, he had no hand to carry it there.

The clean dark line where the Ash ridge had turned its corner lay unswept, and the new finer grey beside it lay unswept too, and the house called both of them the thaw. The space under the lowest rack was gone around as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and entered by nobody. The carried fire lay under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees and giving back four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for a hundred and twelve days. Every warmth in the house had a payer and none of them stood in the room to be thanked. No opening had come, so the widow’s mark hung untouched where it hung, and Sella’s private roll stayed unshown.

Sella told the fire it was the thaw and turned the coals with the back of the broken pot and fed them. “The house will keep it,” she said where another woman would have said I, and did not look toward the bed. She stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand counting something under her breath that no man could have taken up as a figure, and the grey streak at her temple showed pale in the dying light off the coals, and she pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn, and set the pot where it could be found in the dark, and went on.

The door stood a finger’s width open. The river’s note came through the gap from the Ilm, water going over water under the bridge, and through the floor came the kiln’s breath, four, four and a gap under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, two sounds in two places that had not met in a hundred and twelve days and would not meet tonight. The boards overhead kept four nails, four, and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts lay side by side and did not become one.

Cold arrived in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, and the wool did less against it than the cold wanted, riding back from the bare joint so the cold found the place it had come for. He wanted the crust two inches from his ear, and the breast, and the bucket’s lip two paces off, and the number of his days said aloud by somebody who would know what a life counted, and he could reach or ask for none of them. He was a man who had reduced a thousand shoulders in a room that was not this one and he could not close two inches on the fold. The keeping bought him nothing and kept the places clean, and that was the whole of its worth: the crust in the fold, the two gaps on the shelf in the room that did not touch this one, the hollow in the wool three doors down, the pewter tin above it, the narrow slate under a floorboard he could not name, the date standing where a name should have stood, a cord and a second twist no Court thumb would count, a clerk’s uncalled measure, a sixth hearing that did not come, a word unspoken and a loan uncalled. All of it held where it held, advanced and unpaid, one day further on.

His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down. One hundred and twelve stood where one hundred and eleven had stood, exact, unspent, spoken by nobody, while the river went over water beyond the finger’s-width gap and the fire missed its fifth breath under the floor and the two counts did not meet. On the morrow one hundred and thirteen would stand in the same place and nobody under the roof would know a number had moved.

The Chronicle of Chapter 24

On the twenty-first of Seed the planting entered its last tend, the hands in the strips counted in glances and handfuls, never a figure. Sella Vail carried the boy at a half-pace from every open slate and gave the bucket, broom, pot and fire their instructions, never the basket. At the loaf shelf a neighbour reached past a bare board, paused, closed on the loaf beyond and went off saying the old space at the row’s end came in with the planting and this was the price of a wet thing set down wrong. She made a cause of two bare boards and never saw the empty place at the child’s ear. Sella gave nobody a figure and did not touch the widow’s mark.

At the going of the light he lay on his bed, the thread at his mouth frozen. He turned out his file and found it unchanged: six hearings beside and never among with no seventh, three near-full answers advanced and not paid, the word forty unspoken with its readable turn, four low things in four places with the river’s note a fifth, the shut-room tellings joined to no kiln, the damper’s bite filed as an object, the loan uncalled. One crust lay stiff in the fold; the shelf kept two gaps in a room that did not touch it. Sella told the fire it was the thaw and said the house would keep it.

On the twenty-second men came before first grey into the finger’s-width-open room off the wheel-shed, and Jorra Vail gave them no name. He came through with grey slate dust ground into both forearms past the bent finger and put that finger into the cold damper ahead of Sella’s unfinished sentence, so the household straightened around his silence. Tarn’s stroke checked half a beat behind him and went on; the two silences stood in one room with no hand joining them. At the well Berr Clay read one in twelve and said a number goes into a slate, not a strip; a man brought a number up out of a fist and put it back in his sleeve, and it arrived nowhere.

On the twenty-third, at the Fishstairs, Mira Silt answered her own answer to whether the run would be thin and put her thumb to the grey cord when a buyer named a figure; shifting a creel showed a second cord twisted twice, neither knot counted. The soft-cuffed clerk sat at the landing for an uncalled measure. Three doors down Alda Reed laid her birth-slates on the bench before turning to the fold, crossed two fingers at the birth-flower unknowing and covered the sign with a dead woman’s date; warm and breathing was exact and not true. At nightfall the file was unchanged. The river’s note came through the gap and the fire’s missing breath through the floor. His hand went up an inch toward the water and came down. One hundred and twelve stood unspent, spoken by nobody.
