The Figure over the Basket

Days 189–191 · Ember 10–12, Y.H. 813 · The Vail kiln-house, Low Kiln

The figure goes twice over a body that cannot answer. The bell goes four, four and a gap, and nobody crosses himself; the account stands as read.

— A saying at the Cross, on the first Account-day of Ember

— Day 189 —

Ember 10, Y.H. 813 (Tenthday)

Heat came into the Vail kiln-house before the grey did, as it had come every morning of the boy’s life, hot clay under tallow and wet wool under that and, below both, the sour mineral breath of the corner no broom in Low Kiln had been given an instruction about in two years. He lay on the bed with the hearth at his back and his face turned a little to the boards, one hundred and eighty-nine days old and six months and nine days, and nobody under any roof in Sill, in Grath, in all of Sarn, said so. The first sound was the kiln drawing breath in fours with a gap. It was not a bell. No bell had been rung under this roof in a hundred and eighty-nine days, and one would be rung today in the square, later, when the light had come and the pigs were over the board; that was still ahead of the morning and the boy did not borrow it. Ember 10 was an Account-day and a market-day, the first of the kiln month, and the district went on calling Tallow the firing month and Ember the kiln month without a word spent between the two names.

Through the floor the carried fire answered four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, and its payer had no name and had had none for a hundred and eighty-nine days. Through the finger’s-width door the river kept its note of water over water, a fifth low thing never counted among the six hearings of the full note and never made to stand up as a seventh, for an empty place kept empty is a finding and a finding filled to make a row come out even is a finding forged, and a forged finding carried into a room with a signature at the bottom is a finding that will be believed. Overhead the boards kept four nails, four, and a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts in three places, and none rose up and became the others.

The surgeon’s register laid them out the way it laid out everything that could not be touched: three towels, three instruments, nothing resting across anything else. A man who had once read a trauma bay in the ninety seconds before he chose which body to work on now read a ceiling from a basket, and read it correctly, and could do nothing with the reading. Four and a gap under the floor, five low over water through the door, four and a knot above his head. A likeness in a row will stand up and call itself a rule if a man leaves it standing, and a rule is an account that has found a payer, and no payer stood to any of these. He had spent a hundred and eighty-nine days taking such likenesses down one at a time with nobody to help and nobody to see, and the work had made him no friends and bought him nothing he could reach. The told bell of the Long Night lay on a shelf of its own among these, four, four and a gap spoken in the dark, kept apart from any bell the morning might ring; the two would stand side by side before the day was out and neither would be asked to account for the other. It was a keeping like the others, exact, complete and worth nothing at two paces.

The crust lay two inches from his ear, one stiff brown-edged barley cake gone leathery in the dry heat, with its empty place beside it unnamed and no third. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had wiped it, because nobody under any roof had been given an instruction about the thread. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and nobody had been given an instruction about that sound either. He knew the sound. He had once reduced a shoulder in the dark and counted the seconds between a breath and its consequence; now the consequence was his own ear going damp against the fold and he could not lift a hand to his own chin to stop it. The adult and the infant quarrelled in the dark, and the infant was louder. He could not roll. He could not reach the crust two inches off. He could not close two inches on the pot handle standing where it stood. He had put a thousand shoulders back where they belonged and could not keep a blanket on himself, which was a joke with a blade in it that cost him nothing and was not funny.

Cold had come in its known order to cheek, temple, the small bare joint at the wrist where the wool rode back, and stopped there as if it had reached the end of its own instruction; the afterdrop waited on the far side of a warming nobody in this house would perform. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water through the finger’s-width door and came down against the fold. He wanted the crust, wanted the breast, wanted the handle standing where it stood, wanted a number spoken by somebody who would have known what a number counted, and got none of them, and had stopped expecting to be told, which was not the same as not wanting it.

Sella Vail came in with the basket against her hip and gave the instruction to the wool before she gave it to anything with ears. “Keep it off his ears. It’s the river wind,” she said; and the fire got its own instruction, and the pot got its own, and the broom leaning by the door got its, and the running water at the well got its, and the basket got none. She said the house where another woman would have said I, and kept her half-pace from every open thing, and did not once look into the fold. Her eye went once to the clay jar standing cold under its glaze and her hand shut half over nothing and came away without touching it. She pushed coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn so the grey streak showed pale off the coals, and set the pot rim out on the loaf shelf with its handle standing two inches beyond the reaching hand, and let her hand rest a half-beat longer at the setting than the setting needed before it went away. It would have said, if a man could ask it, that the weight in the basket was the same on this first Account-day as on the first morning of his life, which was true, and that the cost was not the same, which was also true and entered nowhere. Her hands were never still; when they had nothing to do they counted spoons, nails, the breaths of a child who could not speak for himself, and gave nobody a figure.

At the hearth she told the fire it was the thaw, turned the coals with the back of a broken pot, said “The house will keep it,” and stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand counting under her breath toward no opening — the small intake at the top, the hold, the letting go. She did not look toward the bed. The widow’s mark hung on its cord at her throat where it had hung since the first firing, a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it. It had been touched once, before the kiln on the first morning of Ember, held one breath, let go, and at no shelf, well, rack, road, basket, afternoon or dusk since, because a morning and a market and a reading are not openings and the hand goes to the mark before the kiln and at no other hour. Nobody had praised the keeping and nobody had seen what it mended, and the fire still answered four pulls with the fifth missing. People noticed the burn first and the mark last, which was the order she preferred. She kept a roll of the house in a careful hand and showed it to no man come down from Slate Row, and what went into it and what stayed out was the same thing to the basket: a door without a handle on this side.

Tarn was at the kindling before the light with the small knife back at the edge of its sill, six years in the worn thumb-mark where the house could put a finger and verify them and the seventh at the lip where no thumb of the house had gone, Garl’s unforged collector sentence lying beside it and entered in neither. He took the broom only after it had been instructed; the sentence about an ear under this roof being worth more than the debt that held it came up to his teeth and stood there with no figure and no name, and he drew the breath he should have spent and did not spend it. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line, and no scrubbing lifted it; the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back. At the finger’s-width door of the Vail wheel-shed Jorra Vail kept dusty forearms and clean ones on one tray, and gave the wheel its instruction before he gave anything to a person, and did not look toward the basket. The two rooms under the roof went on not touching, and the boy in the fold heard the household going about its ordinary morning without being told what any of it cost.

She lifted him with the basket against her hip and gave the instruction to the wool and to the river wind and never to the basket. “Keep it off his ears. It’s the river wind.” Woodsmoke went before her in long low bars out of the chimneys and the river wind came up behind with the taste of water over water, the two passing on the stair of the morning as they had passed every evening of his life without taking each other’s colour or spending a word on either. The arm that had carried him a hundred and eighty-nine mornings kept the height of the handle, the give of the shoulder and the turn of the wrist at a step’s end, a record in muscle that remembered weight and gave no account of cost; it would have said the weight was the same and the cost was not, which were both true and neither entered.

At the edge of the Clay Yard she went around the round place on the wall where the shallow clay pan had stood mouth-up through Reed and was standing there no longer, with no scrape, no crumb, no print in the dust and no chip of a rim; around the trough at the well, bare and dark, whose water was called weather; wide beneath the lowest rack where the broken birth-slates lay, entered by nobody; and around the empty place in the returning line where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out. Each got a look that asked for nothing and found nothing, a wide step and no number and no explanation to the basket of why a pot set down here should be carried wide of there. The clean dark line where Ash had turned the corner for two years and the new finer grey called the thaw beside it lay unswept in the corner, neither with an instruction, and the household went around both. The yard kept its arithmetic in arms and loaves and glances: a woman with slip dried pale in the creases of her wrists lifted a pot and set it down, a man counted what he carried with his hands and not his mouth, and nothing in the walk needed a figure to be done correctly.

Down the clay road the market was assembling before the reading: two hundred people leaning toward the reading block, eight pigs going over the wet boards, a Court table set at the fringe of Slate Row spoken of as a direction and not as a place anybody meant to go that morning. The square smelled of wet boards and pig and turnip tops and woodsmoke flattened into the low air, and under all of it came the same mineral sourness the house kept in its corner, carried down in the wool. People brought figures to a heap of turnips and left them standing side by side without making them agree. Slates lay open on barrels and knees and the tops of walls, and she kept her half-pace from every one of them, so that the basket went through the square at the edge of the light where no figure could fall into it and none could be taken off it. The boy read the crowd the way he had once read a room he was not allowed into: by the exits, by which hands were full and which were empty, by the weight people carried and where they put it down. Two hundred bodies leaning the same way made one shape, and the shape had a hole in it where the answer would stand, and nobody in the square was looking into the basket to see whether the basket had an answer of its own. There would be a figure said twice before the afternoon, once for the moot and once for the households that came late, and the figure would go into a slate that knew nothing about the small humiliating warmth the boy had been carried out of. A reading is a thing said twice over a body that cannot answer; he knew the shape of that before the first stroke of the bell, and knowing it bought nothing.

The square was cold in a way the bed had not been cold. The bed had held a warmth he could not reach and did not want to leave; the square offered nothing and asked for attention. He gave it. The infant wanted the breast and the crust. The surgeon wanted the number. The two wants lay one over the other in the fold and did not become one want, because a likeness stood beside a likeness will call itself a truth if no hand holds it apart, and he had learned to hold them apart with hands that could not lift a spoon. Across the pot board a cup stood turned a quarter and set back where it had stood, and he called the colour at a mouth corner green and nothing else, and gave the shape no name and no ear. The bell would be kept where such bells are kept, beside the told bell of the Long Night, four, four and a gap with nobody crossing himself, and neither hearing would be made to answer for the other; that keeping belonged to later in the morning and he did not spend it before it came.

She had set him at the edge of every open slate for a hundred and eighty-nine mornings and had never once set him in one, and this morning was like the others in that respect if in none other: the reading would go over his head and not into him, and the house would carry him home and put him on his bed and say nothing about what had been said. She set the basket in the moot-post’s finger’s width of shade where no slate lay, with the cold iron of the post near his cheek, and stood half a pace off with her burn shut on nothing. She gave the wool its instruction and the river wind its instruction and gave the basket none and no reason for the setting. The shade was thin and did not hold; it was a place to set a pot and not a place to keep anything, and she had set him there as she set every vessel down, base first, where the light would not fall wrong on it. The bell went over the square, longer than a church bell, and the first stroke came down into the boards and the pigs and the open slates. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down against the fold. One hundred and eighty-nine stood exact, complete and unspent, spoken by nobody under any roof, and the fire’s fifth was still missing under the floor of a house up the clay road with its payer unnamed, and the river kept its fifth and the boards kept their knot, three counts in three places that did not become one. The hands of the square went on reaching for their slates, and the count went forward into the noise.

Berr Clay came up short off the clay road while the bell was yet going over the wet boards, and the two hundred at the Cross leaned the way a stood sheaf leans when a hand passes through it and then settled back into themselves. He had not hurried. A man who has carried clay learns not to arrive at a moot out of breath, because breath is a thing a crowd can hear and count against you. Under his coat the red wool cord of his office was folded square, not worn across his chest, and the green of tasted lead glaze sat at one corner of his mouth where the habit of tasting had put it and where no wiping would take it off before the reading. His hands were large and clean. He wiped his thumb twice on his apron before he touched anything, once for the slate and once for the figure that would be on it, and then let the hands hang as though they had finished their work and were answerable for nothing further.

He held a narrow slate unopened to his forearm with the writing turned toward his sleeve, and passed a finger over the figure without looking at it, letting his eyes rest on the place a breath longer than the writing required, the way a man tries a seam with the pad of a thumb before he puts his weight on it. Then he read. “One in twelve, as the notice says,” he said low into the top of the crowd, and the crowd had to lean in to hear him, which was the reason he never raised his voice. The figure went twice over the basket in the moot-post’s finger’s width of shade, once for the moot and once for the households that came late, and the boy lying there with cold iron near his cheek heard a number said over him and had no way to answer it and no hand to put a figure anywhere. A reading is a thing said twice over a body that cannot speak; he had known the shape of it before the first stroke, and knowing it bought him nothing.

“The levy is called,” Berr said. “It is not called by me.” To a man who had brought a number up out of a fist and held it toward the block, he said there would be a slate at the watch if your house wanted it again — your house, never I — and took himself out of his own sentence as neatly as a surgeon takes his hand out of a field before the dressing goes on. He did not say whose house wanted it or what the number had cost anybody. The printed figure stood where the notice had put it and nobody leaned in to challenge it; the month’s arithmetic changed where two hundred people stood and could not be changed back by dusk. A figure read aloud is not the figure paid. The true subtraction went away up the clay road in the locked box under his coat, and the boy could not have put a finger on which of the two figures was the true one, because no figure in the square knew the man who had been carried out of the Clay Yard the day before with the pale line at his gum, and no collector, no slate and no notice had entered what that carrying cost. The pale line lay where it lay, on a place of its own, exact, complete and worth nothing at two paces, and the reading went over the top of it without stopping.

Berr went off toward High Kiln with the cord folded and the box locked, believing he had done the district a kindness. The bell went four, four and a gap over the going of him, longer than a church bell, and nobody crossed himself. The boy kept the heard bell beside the told bell of the Long Night, four, four and a gap spoken in the dark, and neither was asked to account for the other; two hearings of one shape standing side by side will call themselves a rule if no hand holds them apart, and he had spent a hundred and eighty-nine days holding such hands apart with hands that could not lift a spoon. Under the boards of the house up the road the carried fire answered four low pulls and missed its fifth, its payer still without a name; through the finger’s-width door at home the river kept its note of water over water, a fifth low thing never counted among the six hearings of the full note; overhead the boards kept four nails, four, and a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts in three places that did not become one. The last stroke of the bell died into the pigs and the turnip tops and the wet boards, and the square went back to its own noise as water goes back to its own level in a pail that has been struck.

Sella Vail stood half a pace off with the basket set in the thin shade where no slate lay, her burn shut on nothing. She gave the instruction to the wool and to the river wind and gave none to the basket and no reason for the setting. The boy’s cheek kept the cold of the moot-post’s iron and his hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down against the fold. He wanted the crust, wanted the breast, wanted the pot handle standing two inches beyond his hand at home, and wanted a number spoken by somebody who would have known what a number counted, and got none of them. The figure that had gone over him was not his figure and did not know him; it would go into a slate in Greybank before noon and be entered in a hand that had never seen the inside of a basket. He had stopped expecting to be told, which was not the same as not wanting it.

Before noon Hessa Rale came through the square with the Thane’s narrow slate held edge-on to her forearm and the writing turned toward her sleeve. Her hand arrived at the slate before her eyes arrived at the crowd — the small flat correction of a strap, the turn of a wrist that brought the edge round, the settling of the second shorter strap let out for the long road — the whole order done before she had looked at a single face. Her coat was shortened at the skirt for the road’s mud and the mud had dried on it in a pale scurf she did not brush off, because a runner’s coat is a tool and nobody scours a tool for its own sake. The thumb lay along the grain of the frame and nothing pressed where the writing was. She was a runner and not a reader, and the figure on the slate remained a thing somebody else had set down.

A woman near the pigs asked what she carried. “I don’t read it. I carry it. There’s a difference, and the road doesn’t care which,” Hessa said, and went on at the pace of a person who has made a bargain with distance and keeps it without thinking.

“Then what’s on it?”

“Cross to Greybank. Reading goes up before noon. I’ve got a road to walk.” No rudeness, no apology. She answered the question put and not the one behind it, and left the second question standing where it had been set down, the way a man leaves a pot where there is no hand to carry it.

The boy filed the order of her reaching on a place of its own, the eighth such hand in the district, joined to none. Below the bridge another hand went to another slate before its eyes went to the stalls, and the two records going inward were two records going inward; a row of such hands will call itself an account if a man leaves it standing long enough to want an answer, and he would not leave it standing. No figure came off Hessa’s slate under any roof in Sill, and no figure came off it into the basket.

Below the north bridge the soft-cuffed clerk stayed set at the out-of-line landing for a measure nobody had called, his cuff grey from washing and grey again by midday, his slate to his forearm with the writing turned inward where no goer-by could take a figure off it. His hand went to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls; a buyer named a number and the clerk entered it before he looked up to find the man who had said it. Nobody stepped around him and nobody took him off. The market sold its pots and its eels over the top of him as water goes over a stone that lies where a stone lies. He was not Marra, was not the grey-fingered officer out of the Drawing, was not the soft-handed man at the Court’s table, was not the foundling; the boy kept him on his own place and did not make a fourth likeness stand up and call itself a cause.

Across the square at the pot board a woman stood looking at cups with the sweetish green of lead glaze at one corner of her mouth and clay worked under her nails, a pale line across the back of one hand where something had once been worn tight and long enough to leave its mark. She turned a cup a quarter without lifting it and set it back exactly where it had stood, said nothing, and went off with the light. The boy called the colour green and nothing else and gave the shape no ear: the sixth green at a mouth, a colour joined to no hearing, held where it was held and not counted again, not joined to the Thane’s green at his own mouth and not joined to anything that had ever been spoken in a room.

At the market’s edge a small shape in cast-offs worn like a uniform stood with silt dried pale in the creases of his wrists and a green sliver on a string at his throat that caught the light when he turned and went dull when he turned back. No loaf passed to him, no slate came near him, no sale was made, and no sound came out of him that any clerk could take down. The boy did not count him again. A shape is a shape, and the sixth sighting stayed the sixth and was not spent for effect; the green at the child’s throat was a colour and the green at the woman’s mouth was a colour, and the two colours standing in two places of one square were not joined by any finger of his and did not stand up together and call themselves a cause. He had stopped drawing the line long before he learned what a line was for, which was a small mercy and not a skill.

Sella carried him past the runner at the half-pace and gave the runner no name and asked nothing about the slate. The basket went through the square at the edge of the light where no figure could fall into it and none could be taken off it, and the morning’s arithmetic went on over its head in arms and loaves and glances, which was the only arithmetic the house would ever enter.

He had no towel and no instruments and no case, and laid them out anyway in the dark with hands that could not have lifted the least of them.

Six hearings of the full note lay beside and never among — five from four sources, a cup across a birth room, a pot in the Vail wheel-shed through a finger’s width of door, the district’s slate twice at the Cross, and a man giving at the Drawing — and the sixth, the small flat knock out of a woman as the men lifted, filed where a thing out of a person must go and never among the sources. No seventh came in a hundred and eighty-nine days and none was invented to fill the empty place, for an empty place kept empty is a finding and a finding filled to make a row come out even is a finding forged. None was promoted into the hearings and no pot was lost in the yard. Nine stoppings stood on the interval tray — Reed 5, 8, 11, 14, 17, 20, 23, 26 and 29 — objects and not a rule, intervals and not payments, no payer named to any, held apart by hand because a doctrine is an account that has found a payer and no payer stood to any of them. A tenth had not stood up before the seventh year was earned; the seventh year stood nearer its end than it had ever stood and had not given, the loan that can hear was uncalled, and the Seed 16 breath stood unspent at the top of every reach with the small interval where a breath should have gone in and had not.

— Day 190 —

Ember 11, Y.H. 813 (Firstday)

The account had gone up the clay road in Berr Clay’s locked box in the dark, and there was no hand under the Vail kiln-house roof that could reach into it and take the true subtraction back out. The printed figure stood where the notice had put it — one in twelve — and nobody in Low Kiln had leaned in to challenge it on the first Account-day of Ember. The levy had been called and denied in one breath, the calling and the denial going off up the road together with the man who made them, and the district’s arithmetic had changed where two hundred people stood and could not be changed back by any hour of this morning. A figure said over a basket is not a figure entered in a slate, and a figure entered in a slate is not a figure paid. Nothing in this house had been paid. The boy came into his hundred and ninetieth morning one hundred and ninety days old and six months and ten days, and nobody under any roof in Sill, in Grath, in all of Sarn, said so.

Heat came into the room before the grey did, as it had come every morning of his life: hot clay under tallow, wet wool under that, and below both the sour mineral breath of the corner no broom in Low Kiln had been given an instruction about in two years. He lay on the bed with the hearth at his back and his face turned a little to the boards. The knife lay at the edge of its sill with the seventh year standing at the lip where no thumb of the house had gone, and the count went forward one more day exact and unspent in the dark of the wool.

The afternoon thinned without ending. It lost its edges the way water goes off a stone that has held the sun all day, the heat going first out of the high air and staying longest in the low places, under the shelf, behind the pot, in the fold of wool where the boy lay. Hot clay came up under tallow and wet wool came under that and, below both, the sour mineral breath of the unswept corner that no broom in Low Kiln had been told to take. He lay at the low angle where the underside of the loaf shelf made a roof that did not reach the corner, and the afternoon came down that roof toward him in a slow slant and stopped at the edge of his reach as if it, too, had been given an order and had come to the end of it.

Sella Vail stood at the shelf with her back to the basket. She set the pot rim out on the board with its handle standing two inches beyond the reaching hand, let her hand rest a half-beat longer at the setting than the setting needed, and did not look toward the bed. At the going of the light she carried him home up the clay road. At the edge of the yard she went wide around the round place on the wall where the shallow clay pan had stood mouth-up through Reed and was standing there no longer, with no scrape, no crumb, no print in the dust and no chip of a rim; around the trough at the yard well, bare and dark, whose water is called weather; wide around the space beneath the lowest rack where the broken birth-slates lay, entered by nobody; around the empty place in the returning line where a hand went in on Seed fifteen and did not come out; and around the place the two carriers had crossed with the glaze-room hand between them. Each turn had a look and a wide step and no number, and she gave none of them to the basket and no explanation of why a pot set down here should be carried wide of there. A cost asked as a direction went to the pot and the weather and arrived nowhere, and the carrying-out of a man breathing went on being entered in no slate.

Under the roof she set him on his bed and not in the Debt Loft, base first and the weight settled the way a woman sets a vessel that must not rock. In the small hours he turned out the file the way a man lays instruments on a towel before a case begins, each thing going into its own hollow and nothing resting across anything else, the order of the laying being the whole of the discipline and the discipline older than any word in this country, older than the house and older than the woman asleep in the room that does not touch this one. He has no towel and no instruments and no case, and lays them out anyway in the dark with hands that could not have lifted the least of them.

Six hearings of the full note lie beside and never among — five from four sources, a cup across a birth room, a pot in the Vail wheel-shed through a finger’s width of door, the district’s slate twice at the Cross, and a man giving at the Drawing — and the sixth, the small flat knock out of Dessa Silt as the men lifted, filed where a thing out of a person must go and never among the sources. No seventh came in a hundred and ninety-one days and none was invented to fill the empty place, for an empty place kept empty is a finding and a finding filled to make a row come out even is a finding forged, and a forged finding carried into a room with a signature at the bottom is a finding that will be believed.

Three near-full answers stand on their own tray, advanced and not paid: a pot under the shed wall that answered nearly full with air under the note and a want in it; a rack pot lifted a hand’s breadth too high and set down again; and the knowing the wheel-keeper keeps back from a man come up on a wrong wind who said a pot singing before its time generally sings itself out early. The told bell lies beside the heard, four, four and a gap, with nobody crossing himself, and the told figure lies beside the paid figure, and neither pair was made one.

On a fifth place lies the pale line at the gum, exact, complete and worth nothing at two paces. The man had been read as a man with lead in him: the wet white line where the colour should have been, the hand held curled and then held limp by another man, the fingers gone the colour of old rope and the nails pewter-pale, the breath in short and out shorter with a small pause at the top where something hurt, the wrist that set the dipper down with care because the fingers had stopped telling the man where it was. Each thing lay in its own hollow of the register, and the whole of it was worth nothing without a hand to carry it. It is not a hearing, not a seventh, not a near-full answer, not a stopping, not one of the three held changes and not a fourth likeness stood up to call itself a cause, for a rule is an account that has found a payer and no payer stood to this. It is not joined to the sweet of lead glaze on the wrong wind, the dust ground small as meal, the pot that sang itself out early, the sixth green, the foundling’s sliver, the pan’s gone ring or any absence in the yard. The man went out of the yard breathing and went on breathing up the clay road; a breath kept is not an ending.

The sixth green is a colour joined to no ear and the foundling a shape with no sale, both held and not advanced. The sliver at his throat went dull when he turned and caught the light when he turned back, and his head went toward a set-down pot half a beat after the boy’s, after the knuckle and after the note, and then stopped in the middle of the turn. No loaf had passed, no slate had been seen near the basket and no sale had been made. The shelf’s two gaps, the fold’s crust with its empty place, the pan’s gone round ring and the field’s empty place in the returning line lay on trays that do not touch. No third gap came, no fourth likeness was made a cause and no finger of his drew the line; three likenesses in a row will stand up and call themselves a truth if a man leaves them standing, and he had spent a hundred and ninety-one days taking them down one at a time.

He wants the crust two inches from his ear, wants the breast, wants the pot handle standing two inches beyond his hand, wants a number spoken by somebody who would have known what a number counted, and gets none of them. He has stopped expecting to be told, which is not the same as not wanting it. Through the finger’s-width door the river keeps its note of water over water, a fifth low thing never counted among the hearings and never invented to stand up as a seventh; through the floor the carried fire gives four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer without a name for a hundred and ninety-one days; overhead the boards keep four nails, four, and a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts in three places, and none of them rises up and becomes the others, because a man who wanted them to agree would have to put a payer under them, and no payer stands to any of them. Ash lies thin along the skirting board in the colour of the clean dark line and the new finer grey called the thaw lies beside it, neither swept and neither with an instruction. Cold has stopped at cheek, temple and the small bare joint at the wrist where the wool rides back, and the afterdrop waits on the far side of a warming nobody in this house will perform.

One hundred and ninety-one stands exact, complete and unspent in the dark of the wool, spoken by nobody under any roof. Nothing has been joined, named, opened, called, spent or ended. The nine stoppings are still nine with no tenth, the Seed sixteen breath is still held, the charcoal still waits under the shelf for a hand that is not hers and a day that has not come, the mark was touched once before the kiln and at no other hour, and the cost of the man carried out stands entered nowhere. On Ember’s first Account-day and market-day, Sella Vail carried the boy to the Cross at the half-pace from every open slate and set him in the moot-post’s shade where no slate lay. Two hundred leaned to the reading block; eight pigs went over the wet boards; the bell went four, four and a gap, and nobody crossed himself. Berr Clay came up short off the road, office cord folded under his coat, glaze green at one mouth-corner, wiped his thumb twice before touching nothing, passed a finger over a figure and read one in twelve, as the notice says low into the crowd. The levy is called. It is not called by me --- your house, never I --- and he went off toward High Kiln with the box locked and the true subtraction inside, believing he had done a kindness. The month’s arithmetic changed and could not be changed back; a figure read aloud is not the figure paid.

Hessa Rale took the slate up the road to Greybank before noon, edge-on to her forearm: I don’t read it. I carry it. There’s a difference, and the road doesn’t care which.
