# Style Reference: the voice to keep

**Binding on every word of the saga.** The manifesto below was written by you, the author, for an earlier draft that has since been abandoned (*The Brass Seam*). The reader loved its *style*, so keep its **music and craft** exactly:

- the close third-person past-tense chronicler, with free indirect style;
- the brief tense-shift into the present when the past life breaks through;
- long winding sentences broken by very short declaratives, hard variation in paragraph length, and silence as a tool;
- violence that is plain, exact and consequential, never choreographed or made elegant;
- grief through understatement and one betraying physical detail;
- tenderness through acts and specific touch;
- dry, costly humour;
- distinct idiolects, no exposition in dialogue, subtext as the rule;
- the list of banned phrases and habits;
- no game words (*level*, *quest*, *system*, *skill tree*, *stats*) in the fantasy world. Power ranks have in-world names.

**Do not reuse its names or plot.** Do not reuse its proper nouns or coinages (Wren, Nell, Kit, Elias, Cauldwick, Kilnwick, Cindermere, the House of Seams, the Mending, the Owed). The *vessel concept* of its magic **is** wanted, rebuilt with new names; see `design/CONCEPT-REFERENCE.md`. The new saga is a different story; the *way* of writing carries over.

With a modern-day protagonist, one rule adapts: in the hero's inner voice, comparisons drawn from his old modern life (engines, screens, hospitals, cities) are allowed **as the deliberate contrast between his two lives**. The narration of the fantasy world itself still draws its images from that world's own materials.

# (Earlier draft) Voice manifesto

This is the style manifesto. Re-read it before every scene. The prose is the point; the plot is only what happens while the prose is being read.

---

## Person, tense, distance

**Third-person limited, past tense.** The narrator sits close inside Wren's head but is not Wren: it is a chronicler who has learned her rhythms. It reports what she notices, in the order she notices it, and it is allowed to know a little more than she does when the knowledge is hers but buried. Free indirect style is the workhorse — the narrator's sentence slips into her judgement without a signpost, then out again.

Present-tense immediacy belongs only to the *past life*. When memory takes her, the grammar tightens and the sentences go present for a beat or two: *The stair fills. The water is at her waist. She has left the job unfinished.* Then it falls back into the past. That small tense-shift is the seam in the prose where the two lives meet.

Point of view stays with Wren almost always. When the story must leave her — a chapter in Ond, a chapter inside the House — keep it to a single scene and make the change felt, like a room gone cold.

---

## The double consciousness on the page

She is a child's body with thirty-six years behind the eyes. Never explain this to the other characters. Never have her say *I was a woman once* to someone who would not understand. It shows instead:

- **In competence.** She knows how to tap a vessel, how to seat a staple, how to read a person's account before they speak it. Children in the village think she is odd; the House thinks she is trained.
- **In wrong familiarity.** A smell, a phrase, a shape of light through leaded glass makes her feel at home where she has never been. Write the unease of recognition — the sense of having come back to a room after it has been rearranged.
- **In grief at absences nobody else registers.** She mourns a brother in a country that has no word for what she lost. Her sorrow has no object in this world, so it attaches to objects: a wooden bird, a coin, a tin of papers.
- **In the register of her thoughts.** The adult woman's interior is dry, exact, unsentimental; the child's body is immediate, sensory, impatient. Let the two argue on the page. An old woman's patience interrupted by a child's hunger is the whole texture of the early chapters.
- **Use `\memory{...}` only for remembered *words*** — her mother's sayings, Elias's lines of type, a phrase overheard in Cauldwick. The macro italicises them and makes them feel like a voice in the room. Do not use it for general recollection; memory as *scene* should be written straight into the prose, with no marker, and with the tense-shift described above.

She keeps the fact of her past life as a thing held in the mouth, like staples between the lips. It is always there, and she almost never speaks it.

## Motifs and image-families

Draw every image from the world's own materials: **clay, brass, lead, silt, salt, tallow, bread, wool, rope, bone, frost, iron, woodsmoke, birch bark, glass**. No modern comparisons except when the old life is being remembered, where the contrast is a deliberate tool — a child in Kilnwick may think of a tram or a gaslight, but a girl of Cindermere must think in kilns and rivers.

Recurring families of image, to be braided and returned to:

- **The vessel and the seam.** Pots, cups, jugs, bodies, worlds. Crack, staple, note, visible repair. The bright ping of a true seam is the sound of honesty; a staple that does not sing is a lie.
- **Lead and silver.** The blue line at the gum; the sweet taste; seam-metal; the pewter sky; the grey silt of the Owed. Metals are moral in this world: brass for honest repair, lead for the slow betrayal, silver for the mark.
- **Water and silt.** The Wreak, the Pale Water, the cellar stair, the held breath. Water is what remembers and what takes. Salt is what is left of a Name.
- **Fire and clay.** The kiln, the brick dust, the printshop fire, the hearth. Heat makes and ruins with the same hand.
- **Ledgers and debt.** Rent, marks on the palm, the word *owed* written in a clean hand. Bureaucracy is the horror; the ink is always neat.
- **Birds.** Kit's little wooden bird, worn smooth at the back. Birds stand for the thing carried across a loss without knowing why.
- **Hands.** Stained fingers, ink in the creases, a blue line at the gum, a seam-mark across the palm. Hands know what the face lies about.

Let a motif lie dormant for fifty chapters and then return changed. That is how the reader learns to read the book.

---

## Sentence music

Long, winding sentences made of subordinate clauses and small conjunctions — *and, and, but, so* — to carry the weight of memory and sensory detail. Break them with very short declaratives. *The water came up. She went down.*

Vary paragraph length hard: a single-line paragraph for the thing that lands; a slow half-page for the thing that must be dwelt in. Silence is a paragraph break. Do not fill it.

Use parataxis for violence — short clauses, no metaphor, no writer between the event and the reader. Use anaphora for grief: *She had kept the scrap. She had kept the blue line. She had kept the lie.* Use the plain word where a fancy one would show off: *pot*, *staple*, *water*, *hand*, *owed*.

Rhythm is breath. A wounding sentence should be short enough to be taken in one breath. A sentence of tenderness may wind on longer than is comfortable.

---

## Violence, grief, tenderness, humour

**Violence** is physical, exact and unsentimental. Write what the body does and what it sounds like: a nose breaking like wet cardboard, the scrape of a knife taking a seam-mark off a palm, the warm secondary flood of a wound. Do not choreograph it like a contest; real violence is brief, clumsy and followed by a long undignified interval. Consequences outlast the scene — a limp, a tremor, a stain that will not come out, a name the victim will not say. Never make an atrocity elegant. Never look away from it, either. The world has slavery, torture, war crimes and the daily administrative cruelty of the rent-collector; depict them plainly, with the victim's interiority intact and the perpetrator's banality exposed.

**Grief** is understatement plus one betraying physical detail. People do not deliver speeches over the dead; they fail to put down a cup, or they set a staple wrongly for the first time in twenty years. The body's small mistakes carry the feeling. Let the reader supply the rest. When the feeling finally breaks, do it in one sentence and move on.

**Tenderness** is almost always an act of repair: mending a cup, warming a bed, cutting bread, setting a staple and waiting for the note. Touch is specific — a thumb on a scar, ink-stained fingers on a wrist, a hand smoothed over a wooden bird. Write desire between adults with the same restraint: the unbuttoning, the shift of weight, the smell of tallow, the thing said quietly that could not be said loudly. **There is no sexual content involving anyone under eighteen, ever.** Wren's earliest life in this world is a child's life; keep it so. Sexual violence may exist in the world as a fact of power, but it is never depicted graphically or erotically, and it is never used as seasoning.

**Humour** is dry, northern, working-class: the joke made because the alternative is weeping. A grown woman's despair observed by a child's hungry body is funny and awful at once. Let the riveter in Ond say *owed* the way another man says *amen*, and let Wren think she could learn to be that good at not feeling. Never undercut a painful moment with a quip to make the reader comfortable. The joke must cost something.

## Dialogue conventions

Every important character has an idiolect, recorded on their sheet, and must be readable by voice alone.

- **Wren** speaks little, and never in the old-life idiom to people who would find it strange. She is plain, low, exact. Her question is usually the shortest one in the room.
- **The House of Seams** speaks in formal, ledger-like phrases: *What is broken is owed. The account stands. Your seam is recorded.* They never raise their voices. Their violence is grammatical.
- **The common folk of Kilnwick** speak in proverbs, half-sentences and practical instruction: *Don't set that wet. You'll pay for that come winter.* They address the ground, the pot, the fire, and only then each other.
- **Culls** speak in broken fragments with the formal phrases of the House breaking through, the way a cracked pot still holds a little water.
- **The past life** — Cauldwick voices are faster, warmer, more profane, with Irish and Scottish depths in them. Render them without phonetic spelling; give them rhythm and idiom instead.

No character explains the plot to another. Exposition is folded into a fight, a bargain, a repairing of something. Subtext is the rule: what is not said is what the scene is about. Dialogue tags are usually *said*; the elaborate verb is a treat, not a habit.

---

## Words and habits to avoid

Never use: *a testament to, little did he know, shivers down his spine, a tapestry of, in that moment, it was as if time stood still, the weight of the world, a dance of, unbeknownst*. Avoid also: *suddenly* (use it at most once a volume), *somehow*, *seemed to*, *began to*, *felt a lump in her throat*, *a faraway look*, *like a parent to a child*, *that was when she knew*, and any sentence beginning *And then* twice in one page.

No stacked adjectives. No repeated sentence openings across a paragraph. No character explaining what the reader already knows. No anachronistic slang in the fantasy world — no *okay*, *guys*, *whatever*, *system*, *level*, *quest*, *skill tree*. The House may say *record*, *account*, *seam*, *arrears*; these are its own bureaucratic words and are deliberately cold.

Avoid modern simile in the second life. A face is not like a clock face unless the character has seen a clock. A sound is not like a train. In remembered Cauldwick, the opposite holds: the old life's machines are part of the texture and may be named.

Never say a thing is *ironically* or *symbolically* anything. Let the staple be a staple.

## Three sample paragraphs — not part of the story

*These demonstrate the voice. They are not scenes and must never be filed as story text.*

**I — a craft scene, present life.**

> Wren had the trick of hearing a pot before she touched it. A whole vessel rang clear and full under the knuckle; a cracked one gave back a small flat sound, like a coin dropped on wet boards. She sat cross-legged in the clay yard with a broken jug across her knees and a brass staple held between her teeth, and she turned the thing over until the fault spoke to her. The kiln breathed behind her. Somewhere a woman was singing the washing song, and the note of it went under her skin and stayed. The staple went home with one bright ping, so small that no one else in the yard heard it, and Wren let out a breath she thought she had been holding since the Wreak came up the stair.

**II — memory breaking into the present (note the tense-shift and the `\memory{...}`).**

> The taste came first, not the picture: lead, sweet and sickly, coating the tongue. \memory{There's a blue line on your gums, our Nell, and it'll be the death of you.} Her mother's voice, with the wash-water still in it. The cellar stair had been a mouth opening and shutting, and the river came up it with a sound like a held breath. She had kept the staples between her lips because her hands were full of the box, and the box held twelve cups that would never now be married. The lamp went out under the water and the blue line at her gum showed white for a moment, and then there was nothing but the cold and the silt and the one clear thought: I have left the job unfinished.

**III — violence, administration and the dry joke.**

> The Riveter took the seam-mark from the dead man's palm with a knife sharpened on a stone the colour of old brass. He did not look up. “\textsc{Owed},” he said, and wrote it down, the way another man might say amen. Wren watched the small strip of skin curl into the bowl. She had once set a staple she knew was false, and a woman's hands had been burned for it; she had carried that two lives now, like a stone in a pocket with a hole in it, and here was a man who could strip the mark off a corpse and enter it in a ledger and go home to his tea. She thought: I could learn that. I could learn to be that good at not feeling.
