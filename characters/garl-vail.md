## Appearance

He is known in the house by objects rather than by memory. The wheel in the shed has a seat worn hollow on the left side where he sat for twenty years, and Sella will not let anyone fill it. A coat of his hangs on a peg behind the door with the salt stains still in it, and she has never taken it down, though Jorra has twice reached for it in the dark and stopped. He was a big man with clay in the creases of his hands and a laugh that ended in a cough; his left thumb was shaped by the wheel into a permanent hook. People who saw him said he moved slowly and arrived early, which in a kiln-house is the same thing. He smelled of wet clay and banked coals. In the house the absent smell is stronger than the memory: when the kiln is cold and the Ash ridge is thick, the room smells the way the corner of a room smells where something was left too long.

## Voice & Manner

He is remembered in proverbs, which means his voice has been improved by use and is probably not as good as the versions of it that survive. He never said a hard word in front of the fire — a habit Jorra has inherited without knowing it — and when he was angry he went to the wheel. His sayings are all about repair and none of them are about completion.

- “If it's worth mending it's worth mending twice. The second time is the real one.”
- “Don't tell me the pot's ruined. Tell me what it was before it was ruined.”
- “Feed the fire before you feed yourself. It'll still be there in the morning. You won't.”
- “A man's hands know what his head's still arguing about.”
- “Leave the crack showing. People trust a thing they can see has been handled.”

## History

Fourth generation of Vails, born in the kiln-house and married to Sella, a slip-mixer's daughter, in Y.H. 796. He kept the wheel for twenty years, threw the tiles that went into the floor of the Cross market, and was offered a price for his measure by a Sere factor in Y.H. 809 which he refused and did not mention for a year. He had two children: Jorra, born Y.H. 796, and Enna, born Y.H. 802, who died at nine months of the second chill and whose death he sat through without being able to do anything that mattered. In Y.H. 810 he began to be what Low Kiln calls walking thin — a vessel leaking faster than a man of his trade should leak — and he said nothing about it, because a kiln-master who admits a flaw loses the wheel.

In the Long Cold of Y.H. 811 the Ilm lay under ice for ninety-four days and no collector could cross from Greybank. Garl's vessel gave in the first weeks of Stone. He died at home with Sella beside him and no iron tube in the room, and his warmth went where warmth goes when nobody takes it: into the Waste, unbanked, unrecorded, unreturned. In the morning there was grey Ash along the skirting board thicker than anyone had seen in that house. Tarn was sent to find a collector and turned back at the frozen ford. Jorra was thirteen and took the wheel a week later. The Vail household has been cool ever since, in a way that has nothing to do with the weather.

## Wounds & Fears

He knew he was leaking and kept throwing. That is the wound and it is also the answer to any question about why he is dead. He feared being banked thin and coming back as someone who would stand in his own house and not know the wheel; he feared more than that being spent whole and returned beautiful for somebody else's benefit, which is what he had seen happen to a man he would not name. His contradiction: a man who mended pots for a living would not let anyone mend him, and called the refusal *not wanting to be a bother*, which is the gentlest lie in the district and the most expensive.

## Desires

He wanted to leave the wheel to Jorra whole, with the seat still worn and the note still true, and to come back as himself one day and teach the boy what a full note feels like under the knuckle. He thinks he wanted a whole return for himself. What he needed was to understand, before he died, that a vessel can be repaired without first being emptied — the thing his son's household's foundling will one day prove in the face of every priest in Sarn. He did not live long enough to be wrong about it in an interesting way, which is why his absence still has work to do.

## Bonds

**Sella Vail** — his wife. She decided he would not be collected; he did not ask her to decide. A promise unkept gathers Ash in Sarn as surely as a debt, and the ridge in their room is the shape of theirs.

**Jorra Vail** — his son, who has his hands and not his patience. Garl never told the boy he was proud of him. The boy has spent three years building a life around the absent sentence.

**Corin** — he never met the boy, who now sleeps under his roof and uses a warm stone that was Garl's. There is a late recognition in this: the dead man's stone against a child's belly, and a promise that cannot be settled because it was never made.

**Berr Clay, the Thane of Clay** — owed Garl for a consignment of tiles and never entered it. Garl never called it in. The Vail house has been short of coin ever since, and the debt has gathered interest in the Court's books even though nobody in Low Kiln can read them.

**Enna** — his daughter, who died at nine months and whose second chill he could not stop. He has been uncollected for a year; she has been returned thin or not returned at all, which nobody in the house will say aloud.

## Arc So Far

No chapters are written yet. He exists as an absence with objects attached: a worn wheel-seat, a salt-stained coat, a ridge of grey Ash, a peg behind a door. The reader will know him by what the household does around the hole he left before the reader is told how the hole was made.

## Secrets (private)

1. He had begun to listen to his own warm-ways and had found them coiling where they should branch. He wrote what he heard on the back of a slate. Sella burnt it, not knowing what it was, and has never told him she did.
2. In Y.H. 808 he sold a day of his own warmth to an Ash-runner to buy Jorra a new wheel. He is the first Vail to pay for a repair with himself, and the only record of it is in a Fen runner's knot-stick three leagues downriver.
3. He had a brother who went south with a Sere factor and came back beautiful, whole and empty — a return that remembered enough to be exhibited and not enough to know his own family. Garl refused the factor's price in Y.H. 809 because of this, and gave no reason.
4. He did not ask Sella to let him go uncollected. The decision and the silence are hers, and the Ash is the joint property of the two of them.
5. His last coherent sentence was not about the fire or the wheel. It was a question about a child who was not born until after he died, and neither Sella nor Jorra has ever understood what he meant.

## Plans (private)

Use him as the house's uncollected debt: the reason the Vail rooms run cold, the reason the Ash ridge is thick, the reason Sella will not let anyone sweep. Dan's first heretical discovery — that a dying person's warmth can be left where it is and the world does not end — should come to him first as a story about a man who was left, and only later as an experiment.

The burnt slate is the plant. When Corin is old enough to find the edge of it in the kiln, he will be looking at the first record of the spiral in Sarn, written by a man who thought he was going mad and by a woman who thought she was burning rubbish. Garl never becomes a returning ghost or a convenient guide. He stays dead. The house keeps his peg, his coat and his worn seat, and the boy grows up with a father who is a flaw in the floor that nobody will patch.
