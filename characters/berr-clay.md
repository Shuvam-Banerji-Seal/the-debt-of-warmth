## Appearance

Forty-eight, heavy in the way a wagoner is heavy, with a back that has carried clay and a grey stubble he trims with a knife before market-day. He has a ring of lead-glaze green at the corner of his mouth from the old kiln-master's habit of tasting glaze, and he cannot stop doing it in front of people, which the masters read as thoroughness and Sella reads as nerves. He wears a cord of red wool across his chest when he sits as Thane and takes it off before he goes home, folding it into a drawer. His hands are large and clean; he wipes his thumb on his apron before he touches a slate so that the slate will stay clean. He smells of hot clay, lead and, on Court business, nothing at all, which is the most expensive smell in Grath.

## Voice & Manner

The kiln-master's proverb braided with the Court's passive grammar. He never says *I* when he can say *the district* or *the account*, and he never raises his voice, which in a moot is more frightening than shouting because the crowd has to lean in to hear him. He apologises in the form of an instruction. He uses the phrase *as the notice says* the way another man might cross himself.

- “The levy is called. It is not called by me.”
- “One in twelve, as the notice says. If your household has heard otherwise, your household has heard wrong.”
- “You can bring the slate to the moot. The moot will shout. The slate will not change.”
- “Sella. Your widow's mark is confirmed by the factor at Greybank. That is a kindness, and kindness is entered.”
- “I signed for the Hearth. I did not sign for a name. There's a difference, and you can take it to the Cross if you like.”
- “The figure stands as read. There'll be a slate at the watch if the house wants it again.”

## History

He was born in High Kiln, the son of a glaze-mixer, and worked up through the yards until the masters chose him Thane of Clay and the Court's factor at Greybank confirmed it, which is the only way a Thane is confirmed. He has kept the Sill kilns firing through two bad winters and a war of deductions that he describes, when pressed, as *a disagreement about arithmetic*. In Y.H. 811, when the Long Cold had the district calling itself walking thin, he raised the levy to one in three on the factor's instruction and announced it as one in twelve, because the printed notices say one in twelve and the notices are what a man can be seen reading. He has read the difference in a locked box in his own house. He has never destroyed the box.

In the first weeks of Stone, Y.H. 812, he signed the slate that took forty children of Low Kiln for a private account. The factor at Greybank showed him the purchaser's name and Berr has forgotten it on purpose, which is a thing a man can do if he is sufficiently determined and sufficiently afraid. He told the district the warmth was for the Hearth, and to a mother named Dessa Silt he said one in twelve and meant one in three. His daughter came back thin in Y.H. 800 and does not remember him; he has kept her name on a slate at home and has never corrected the deduction on her return, because the return is what the household was given and a household does not look a gift in the mouth.

## Wounds & Fears

A daughter who calls him by another man's name when she is feverish. He fears being seen clearly by Sella, who knew his wife and knows the difference between a levy and a theft. He fears that the locked box will outlive him and that his son will open it. He fears, most of all, that the district will one day ask him why forty children were necessary when one intact measure should have served, because he has an answer ready and knows it will not balance. His contradiction: the man who counts warmth as a public good has privately bought a whole return for his granddaughter and paid for it with the deduction from Low Kiln, and calls the purchase *a gift from the Hearth*.

## Desires

He wants the kilns firing, his name on the roll, and the factor at Greybank to write back in the same hand next year. He thinks he wants the Court's confirmation. What he needs is to learn that he is the most frightening thing in Sarn — not a dark lord, but a reasonable man with a ledger and a good reason — and that the reason is the part that will be remembered against him.

## Bonds

**Sella Vail** — he confirmed her widow's mark and has never called in the debt Garl left against a shipment of tiles. The uncalled debt is worse than a called one: she cannot pay it, cannot settle it, and cannot stop owing it. She has never asked, and he has decided this means she knows. *Ch 3:* he told her at the Cross that the mark was confirmed by the factor at Greybank, that it was a kindness, and that kindness is entered. She answered the wool and the boy, not him; he stood with his mouth open on the next part of what he had come to say, looked at the mark under her collar without asking to see it, and went back to the moot leaving the debt as carefully unentered as he had found it.

**Jorra Vail** — he suspects the young man is with the Broken Slates and lets him be. A district needs somewhere to put its anger, and Berr has calculated that a kiln-house with a safe place for anger is cheaper than a kiln-house without one. Jorra threw a stone at his door in Y.H. 810; Berr had the stone swept up and does not mention it.

**Garl Vail** — dead friend and rival. Berr let the Vail house carry a debt rather than call it in, and has spent a year being unable to decide whether this was mercy or the simplest way to keep a witness quiet. *Ch 3:* the debt against the firing of tiles went spoken around at the Cross, a space in a sentence shaped like the hollow seat up the yard; his care not to call it was another kindness entered nowhere.

**Alda Reed** — he pays her to keep the birth-slates and has never asked about the second notes. He has concluded that she must be paid enough not to keep them, which is an expensive conclusion and a wrong one. *Ch 3:* she kept the birth-slates in a row at the edge of the market, turning the writing down when a mother came near; he did not see the crossed sign she made at the boy's wrist or the date she covered it with, which is the whole point of paying someone to keep a record.

**Dessa Silt** — he took one in three from her wages and told her one in twelve. She spat at his door and denied it; he had the spittle cleaned before the market opened and never said who did it.

**Corin** — he watches the boy with a kiln-master's eye and has decided he is worth more than he costs, which is exactly how the Court will one day see him. This is not cruelty in Berr's account. It is an investment. *Ch 3:* the boy lay in a basket at the edge of his square all morning and he never once looked into it. The figure was read over the top of him twice; the district went back to the pigs. He has entered this nowhere, including in his own mind.

**Ch 5.** At the Fishstairs on Stone 14 he came down the steps with the red wool cord folded under his coat and wiped his thumb on his apron before touching his slate, the green of tasted glaze at one corner of his mouth. He said *the levy is called for the feast; it is not called by me*, accepted four eels where six should go without appearing to count them, and gave out *three days to the Five* from the top of the steps with a finger passed over the figure. He has paid her two marks short for six years and taken two eels short for six years, and the war has never been named by either of them. The box in his house stays locked; the true deduction stays in it.

**Ch 6.** On Stone 20, Account-day, he came to the block with the red cord of his office folded under his coat and the green of tasted glaze at one corner of his mouth, wiped his thumb twice on his apron before touching the slate, and read *one in twelve, as the notice says* low into the top of two hundred leaning people. He called the levy for the feast and said it was not called by him, passed a finger over the figure, confirmed the fire and the widow's mark under this roof, and went around the consignment of tiles where a debt might have stood, the way a woman goes around Ash left in a corner. He did not look into the basket and went back to the pigs by dusk believing his own sentences. The box stays locked and the true deduction stays in it.

## Arc So Far

He enters at the Account-day moot of Stone 10, Y.H. 812, wiping his thumb on his apron before touching the slate, and says *one in twelve, as the notice says* in a low voice that makes the shouting stop because the crowd has to lean in. He never says *I*; he says the district, the account and the notice, and apologises in the form of an instruction. He sets the slate down twice and twice it answers flat, and he does not hear what the boy hears. Later, at the Slate Row end of the Cross, he confirms Sella's widow's mark as a kindness that is entered, leaves Garl's debt against the tiles uncalled, and goes back to the moot with the red cord folded square. The locked box in his house has not been shown or named. He has been on the page for one chapter and is already the local face of the Court: reasonable, clean-handed, with a good reason for every cruelty and no idea that he has given any.

On Stone 14 the Thane comes down to the eel trade and is the same man in a market that he was at the Cross: the clean thumb, the passive grammar that takes the speaker out of his own sentence, the hand that counts a figure before he lets it go. He calls the levy for the feast and says it is not called by him, and takes four eels where six should go and does not seem to notice, which is how a man can be short and kind and reasonable in the same breath. The number of days to the Five is let out into the market air and is still nothing anybody can spend.

On Stone 20 he is the same man at the Cross that he was at the eel trade: the thumb wiped clean before the slate, the passive grammar that takes the speaker out of his own sentence, the figure passed over with a finger to make sure it is still where he put it. Two hundred people lean in because they have to; he reads one in twelve as the notice says, calls the levy for the feast and says it is not called by him, and goes around the uncalled debt against the tiles as carefully as Sella goes around Ash in a corner. He confirms the mark and the fire and sets the printed figure down where everyone can see it. The boy lies in the post's thin shade with cold iron at his cheek and hears a number he cannot spend, and Berr never once looks into the basket or enters what he has left lying where it lay. He goes home by dusk believing he has done the district a kindness.

## Secrets (private)

1. He knew the Forty were for a private whole return. The factor showed him the name; he has forgotten it deliberately and would recognize it if he heard it again.
2. He bought a whole return for his granddaughter with warmth deducted from Low Kiln and told her mother it came from the Hearth. The mother has thanked him twice.
3. His daughter's thin return is the reason he believes in the Keeping. He has never told anyone that she sometimes calls him by her dead aunt's name, which is a Second Want the Court's records do not contain.
4. He keeps the true deduction rate in a locked box and has never destroyed it, because a man who keeps a thing can one day produce it, and a man who produces it can decide when.
5. He will be the one to call the collection on Dessa Silt and will not be present when it fails. He will sign the resulting slate in a neat hand and will not read the note Alda writes in the margin.

## Plans (private)

He is the local face of the Court: not a villain, a man who can be defeated in no fight and only in an account. Keep him reasonable to the end. Give him a good reason for every cruelty and let the reason be the thing the reader remembers, because the reader will have heard it before in Vesci's warm voice saying that a chart closes a matter.

He will offer Corin a place among the kiln-masters — a good offer, made in good faith, in exchange for a silence about the boy's ear. When Dan refuses, Berr will not be angry; he will enter the refusal on a slate. Late, when the true deduction is made public, he will be the one who says *the account stands* and means it, and will still be unable to understand what he did wrong. His punishment is to survive his own accounting and be respected for it.
