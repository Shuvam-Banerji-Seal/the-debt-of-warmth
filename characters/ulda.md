## Appearance

Three years older than Corin and twice as easy in the world, with a narrow face that is always slightly amused and a set of clothes that are somebody else's cast-offs worn as if they were a uniform. He has a gap where a front tooth was knocked out at the Fishstairs and he grins through it, which makes people forgive the things he says before they have decided whether to be offended. He keeps a sliver of green lead-glaze in a string around his neck, picked up in High Kiln, and rubs it with his thumb while he decides what a person wants to hear, which is almost always the thing they have already said they want and a little more. He smells of river silt, small ale and the warm stone of other people's hearths, because he is always in somebody's kitchen and never in his own. The thing people notice first is the grin; the thing they notice last is that when Corin turns his head toward a pot, Ulda turns it too, a half-beat late, and has never once been able to explain why.

## Voice & Manner

Fast, warm, fluent in the proverb-form of Low Kiln and quicker than the adults at it. He is the only child in the yard who can make the kiln-hands laugh, and he does it by saying the thing the adults are thinking in a voice that makes it harmless. He never says *I want*; he says *they say*, *everyone knows*, *your house wouldn't mind*, which is a way of wanting without owning the want. When he is lying he gets affectionate, which is the tell that Mira Silt has learned to read and that Corin, for years, took for kindness. He has a habit of finishing another person's sentence for them, correctly, and then looking surprised that it was not what they meant.

- "—and a good deal," said at the well in Ch 16, finishing a kiln-hand's sentence in a quick, warm, affectionate voice. "That's the whole of the account. Everyone knows it. I'm only saying." This is his first plain sound in the book: a borrowed sentence, harmless on the surface, that makes the yard laugh before anybody has decided whether to be offended.
- "Everyone knows the Vail boy isn't Vail. I'm not saying it. I'm saying everyone knows it. There's a difference."
- "You heard it before I tapped it, didn't you? That's not a trick. That's a thing you should be careful with, Corin. I'm only saying."
- "I didn't tell the clerk. I told him a thing and he wrote it down. That's not the same, is it? You tell me, because I can never tell which part's mine."
- "The mark. Under your sleeve. I saw it at the Weir and I didn't say. I didn't say for three years, Corin. That's what I'm asking you to remember."
- "Sella's kind to you. She's kind to me too, when she's thinking of something else. Don't make it more than it is."

## History

He was born in Low Kiln to a mother entered in the name-pot and returned thin, and he has never known which of the several dead in him is his mother, which he describes as *a family, in a manner of speaking*. He was entered on a slate at three because the Court began counting foundlings in Y.H. 782, and has had a name on a roll ever since, which makes him, in his own account, a man of property compared with Corin, who for his first years had none. He attached himself to the Vail yard at five because there was alwaysbread there and because the boy with the flower was interesting in a way Ulda could not name. He became the foundling's friend in the way poor children become friends: by sharing a crust, by knowing which boards at the Fishstairs are safe, and by being the person who says the thing aloud that the other one is thinking.

He has sold small secrets at the Fishstairs since he was nine — which household has paid the levy, which collector takes his ale before a draw, which hearth-maid has begun to keep a second set of notes — and has never thought of it as betrayal, because a secret in Low Kiln is a thing that belongs to whoever can use it. In Y.H. 811 he told a Waiting Room clerk that a foundling under the Vail roof could hear the full note of a pot before it was tapped. The clerk wrote it down. Ulda was eleven and was paid in a loaf, and has entered the loaf in no account, which is the only thing about the whole business he has never been able to explain to himself.

Three years later he will tell the same clerk about the branching red mark under the boy's sleeve, which he saw at the Weir and has kept for exactly the right moment, and the clerk will write that down too. He will do it because he has begun to be afraid that Corin will one day be taken by the Court and Ulda will not be there to be useful, which in his vocabulary is the same as being abandoned. He will say afterwards that he did it to protect him. He will believe it for a while.

**On his age in these chapters.** In Y.H. 813, when he first comes plainly into the yard in Chapter 16, he is a small foundling of three or four in somebody else's cast-offs, not the eleven-year-old of the dialogue above. The sentences about the Waiting Room clerk, the paid loaf, the mark under the sleeve and the sale at eleven belong to years hence and are written here as the shape of the man he will become, not as words this child speaks now. In Chapter 16 he is given one borrowed sentence and nothing of his own; his sheet was reconciled to this on his first appearance. If he is given a second speaking appearance while the boy is still an infant, treat the later dialogue as years ahead of him and give him no secret to sell yet.

## Wounds & Fears

He has no house and has made a career of being the person who fits into other people's. His wound is that he cannot want anything in his own voice: every desire comes out as *they say* or *everyone knows*, so that he can deny it if it is refused. He fears the name-pot, because a foundling entered on a slate can be entered again, and fears more than that the day Corin looks at him with that clinical, quiet attention and sees the thing he did at eleven. His contradiction: the boy who tells everyone's secrets has kept one of his own for three years — the mark — and calls the keeping of it loyalty, when it is a thing he has been saving to spend.

## Desires

He wants a place in a household, any household, where the door closes on him and somebody says his name without a slate in front of them. He thinks he wants Corin to stay in Low Kiln and be his friend forever, which is the same as wanting the boy not to become what he is becoming. What he needs is to understand that a person cannot be kept by being useful, and that the loaf he was paid at eleven has gathered interest for three years in an account he cannot read. When he finally says the thing he did, it will be in the affectionate voice, and it will be the first honest sentence he has ever spoken, and Corin will not believe him at first, which is the correct response.

**The Waiting Room clerk** — a man whose name is not on any roll, who pays in loaves and ale and has taught Ulda that a secret is worth exactly what somebody will give for it. This is the man who will buy the mark.

He says what everybody is thinking in a tone that makes it harmless, and he never says *I want* --- he says *they say*, *everyone knows*, *your house wouldn't mind*. When a woman sets a pot down his head goes toward it a half-beat after the boy's, after the knuckle and after the note, then stops and comes back; he rubs the green sliver and cannot say why he turned, and is as surprised as anybody. When a man from Slate Row whose name is on no roll holds out a warm loaf, his mouth opens on a sentence with the shape of *everyone* or *your house* and stops where a want would have stood, because he has no form of words for a want that is his own and is too quick a child to say a thing that could be refused to him by name. The loaf goes into the pocket of the cast-off coat, which holds a bit of string and nothing any clerk could read, with no slate between them; the man says something low that does not carry and the child nods as though he had understood it. He is named on the sheet but not in the narration. ## Secrets (private)

1. He told a Waiting Room clerk at eleven that the Vail foundling could hear the full note, and was paid in a loaf. He has never told Corin and has entered the loaf nowhere.
2. He saw the branching red mark under Corin's sleeve at the Weir and has kept the sight for three years, waiting for a moment when it would be worth the most. He has begun to be afraid that the moment has already passed.
3. He cannot spell his own slate-name, which was written for him by a clerk and which he recognises by shape, the same way Orra Weir recognises the collector's name by shape. He has never told anyone this, not even Mira.
4. He has been to the Bone Orchard twice looking for a shallow grave with his mother's name on it and found none, which means her warmth was banked and is somewhere in an account, which means there is a thin little boy in the district who is partly him and does not know it.
5. He will sell the mark to the clerk, and when the collectors come for Corin he will discover that he has not protected the boy at all, and will spend the rest of his life trying to be useful to the person he betrayed, which is the only apology he knows how to make.

## Plans (private)

Let him be charming for a hundred chapters before the reader suspects him, and then let the suspicion land as a physical detail: the half-beat delay when he turns his head toward a pot. He is the hidden enemy disguised as a friend, and he is not a monster; he is a poor child who learned that a secret is property and that property is the only thing a foundling can own.

When he betrays Corin, do not give him a speech. Give him the affectionate voice and a sentence that is almost an apology, and let Corin, now older and much colder, finish it for him. He survives, and keeps being useful, and never once gets the door that closes on him with somebody saying his name without a slate in front of them. That is his account, and it stays open.
