## Appearance

Twenty-six, narrow, with the pressed, maintained look of a man who irons his cuffs before he goes to a district where there is no ironing. His hands are good hands — quick, clean, certain on a slate — and he has the student's habit of tapping the chalk once against the frame before he writes, which the hearth-maids of Low Kiln have learned to read as a signal that a number is about to be entered against somebody. He wears the College's black wool even in Silt, when a sensible man would wear clay-stained linen, and it marks him as a visitor from the Hearthseat in a way he is unaware of or has decided not to mind. His face is open and slightly eager, which is the least threatening expression in Sarn and, on him, the most dangerous, because it disarms people a moment before the figure lands. He smells of new chalk, clean paper and, when he has been in the Weighing Hall, the Hearth-fire, which he has never mentioned to anyone in Sill.

## Voice & Manner

Fast, precise, quietly delighted by his own accuracy. He says *the figure* where another man would say *the truth*, and he has a way of offering a correct number as if it were a gift, which it is, to the person who asked for it and not to the person it is about. He does not sneer. He does not need to; his certainty does the work. When he is beaten — and he will be beaten — he becomes curious rather than angry, which is worse for the victor, because curiosity is a hand that keeps hold. He has one verbal tic: he repeats the last figure of another person's statement back to them, quietly, as a way of checking both the figure and the person.

- "One in twelve. I've checked the notice, the roll and the factor's copy. They agree. Three copies agreeing is a kind of truth in my trade."
- "Forty. I keep coming back to forty. Why forty unspent measures for one intact one? I've asked the provost. He said the account stands."
- "You heard the flat note before I tapped it. That's a good trick. I'd like to know how you did it. Not to take it. I want to know whether it's a method."
- "I'm not your enemy. That's the thing nobody understands about me. I'm a man with a slate. The slate is the enemy, if there has to be one, and even the slate is only following the figure."
- "Seven. Six. Five — you've lost count, haven't you? I counted for both of us. That's my gift, I suppose."

## History

He was born in the Hearthseat and entered on the rolls at nineteen, which in the College means he has never had a house-name to be proud of or ashamed of. He proved early that he could add, which in the College of Assessors is the rarest of gifts: most officers memorise the figures, a few compute them, and almost none check the printed ones against the rolls. Rennick checked, at twenty-one, and found that one in twelve on the notice did not agree with the deduction on a Fen household's slate, and that the difference was not an error but a method. He asked Provost Wessen about it. Wessen said the account stands and gave him a figure for his trouble, which Rennick has entered in his private tally as the first kindness shown to him by the institution.

He was posted to Slate Row in Sill in Y.H. 810, at twenty-four, to value the lives of a district whose households he cannot pronunciation-proof and whose children he cannot keep track of. He has kept his own count of the Forty of Low Kiln, which is forty-one, because on the night of the levy he saw a covered bundle carried up the Clay Yard that did not appear on any list, and a collector's slate with a name written before a face. He cannot spell the name but he remembers the shape of it, and he has begun to look for that shape in every document that passes across his table. He has not told anyone about the forty-first. He has told Wessen about the forty, who told him to enter it as an irregularity and forget it, which he entered and did not forget.

He is good at his work and he likes being good at it, which is his great disqualification. He will meet the foundling Corin as a boy with an ear and no ledger, and will recognise in him a rival in the only sense Rennick understands: a person whose method produces correct answers by a route the College cannot teach. He will try to learn the route, honestly, by asking, and will be refused, and will keep asking, because a refusal is a thing with a shape and he wants to know what it is.

## Wounds & Fears

He can add, and nobody in his institution wants him to. His wound is a gift that his teachers have spent six years trying to correct out of him, and his fear is that he will succeed at last and become an ordinary officer who prints one in twelve and never asks why. He fears a person who can hear a vessel without a tool, because such a person makes his arithmetic look like a slow, clumsy way of doing the same thing. He fears the grey fingers of Collector Marra, whose reports are more exact than his and whose exactness he has begun to suspect is a kind of concealment. His contradiction: the young man who believes figures are a form of honesty has learned to hide the one figure he cannot explain, and calls the hiding *an irregularity entered in the proper form*.

## Desires

He wants to be right in a room where being right is noticed. He thinks he wants the foundling's method, because a method that hears a cracked vessel through a wall would make the College's work faster and truer, and perhaps make the true deduction public without anyone having to say who took it. What he needs is to learn that a figure is not a person and that adding a district correctly is not the same as being kind to it, which is the lesson the Warm Court has built eight hundred years on not learning. He will learn it late, and will become more dangerous when he does, because he will then be an assessor who can add and knows who pays.

## Bonds

**Corin** — the rival. Rennick admires the boy's ear without understanding it and has begun to suspect that the boy hears things he is not supposed to hear, which makes him want to check him the way one checks a slate. The tension is admiration curdling into a need to verify: Rennick will not be able to leave the boy's method alone, and the boy will not be able to explain it without lying.

**Wessen** — his teacher, who corrected the verb out of him and refused to correct the arithmetic, which Rennick has decided means the provost wants him to keep adding. He is right about this and wrong about why.

**Marra** — the collector whose reports are the standard he measures himself against and whose fingers are going grey. He has begun to watch her hands when she writes, which she has noticed and pretended not to.

**Berr Clay, the Thane of Clay** — who tells him one in twelve in a moot and shakes hands afterwards. Rennick has checked the Thane's figures three times and found them correct in the way a printed notice is correct, which is to say: agreeable and wrong.

**Alda Reed** — who answers his questions about births with the weather. He has begun to suspect the weather is an answer and has entered the suspicion nowhere, because an assessor who writes down what a hearth-maid means is an assessor who has started to add in the wrong direction.

**Steward Derren** — whom he has never met and whose signature he recognises on the private-authorisation of a whole return with no purchaser named. Forty. One. He keeps coming back to it.

## Secrets (private)

1. He has counted forty-one on the night of the levy and cannot account for the last one. He remembers the shape of a name written before a face and has looked for it in every document since.
2. He checked the printed deduction against the rolls at twenty-one and discovered the true figure. He has never told anyone except Wessen, who told him to enter it as an irregularity, which he did and then kept a second copy.
3. He has a private tally of his own acts — a thing no officer keeps — in which he entered Wessen's figure as the first kindness shown to him by the Keeping. He has not decided whether this is sentiment or evidence.
4. He will obtain a sample of Corin's method by asking for it as a favour, and will use it to compute something the boy did not intend, which will cost a household its warmth and teach him that a method is never only a method.
5. When he finally learns who paid for the whole return, he will add it correctly for the first time and will not be able to stop, because the figure will not balance and he has spent his life believing that figures balance.

## Plans (private)

Let him be the rival who is not evil: a gifted young man doing his job well, whose gift is the thing Dan most needs and whose job is the thing Dan most opposes. The tension should never be personal hatred. It should be the horrible ease with which an honest person can do a dishonest sum.

Keep the forty-first alive in him as a plant that looks like a small professional curiosity and is really the same thread Orra tied in knots and Marra entered in a forged slate. He will be the one who finally says the number aloud in Dan's hearing, years after Orra first said it to the cord, and Dan will be too old by then to pretend he does not know what it means. Rennick survives. He becomes an assessor who can add, which in the long run is more dangerous to the Court than any rebel with a slate, because a rebel burns the slate and Rennick reads it correctly.
