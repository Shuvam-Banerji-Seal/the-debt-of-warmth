## Appearance

Sixteen and built like a gate hinge: tall for a clay boy, narrow, with slip dried to the colour of old bone up both forearms and a permanent bend in his left little finger from a splinter off the wheel when he was eleven. He cuts his hair short so Ash cannot nest in it, which is a thing the district notices and he never explains. His eyes do not finish sentences; they go to the door, to the fire, to the wheel, and come back without reporting. He smells of damp clay, tallow and the sweetish edge of lead glaze carried up from High Kiln on a bad wind. The thing people notice first is his hands, which are the hands of a thrower and have been working since he was nine. The thing they notice last is that he never looks at Sella when she speaks to him, though he obeys her instantly.

## Voice & Manner

Short, flat, declarative. He asks almost no questions, and when he does it is the tool rather than the clay: *Is it dry? Did you fire it? Who wrote it?* He speaks to the wheel and to the pots as other men speak to animals, and only then to people. When he is angry he gets quieter, the way Sella does, and the household has learned to read the lowering of his voice as a weather signal. He never says *with* or *against* when *I haven't said* will do.

- “The wheel doesn't care whose hands. It cares if they're steady.”
- “You want me to say it. I'm not saying it.”
- “Sella. Don't feed him from your own dish. He's not Vail.”
- “A slate burns in four minutes. Four. I've timed it.”
- “I didn't say I was with them. I said I wasn't against them. That's different. In this district it isn't, but it's what I said.”
- “He's not Vail.” --- said in the kiln-house at first light, refused an answer, and sealed by the boy's arrival.
- “You'll do.” --- to the pot on the wheel, in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either.

## History

He is the son of Garl and Sella Vail, and he took the wheel at thirteen when Garl died in the Long Cold and did not come back and nobody came to draw him. The wheel had been Garl's for twenty years and the seat was worn hollow; Jorra has spent three years trying to fill that hollow with his own weight. He has thrown pots since he was nine, fired them since eleven, and knows the full note of a Vail pot before it leaves his hands, though he will not say so in front of the kiln-masters, who would call it early and make something of it. His house has remembered the Ash Rising since Y.H. 668, when children were taken and the rising was put down by a man with a list; the memory is kept not in stories but in the way certain doors are never closed and certain names are never entered. Since he was fourteen men from the Broken Slates have come into the back room of the kiln-house and spoken in low voices. He has listened to all of them and told none of them yes.

He watched Enna, his sister, die of the second chill at four, and has never forgiven the fire for being warm while she cooled. He watched his father die and was sent to find a collector and could not cross the ford, and has never asked whether he was sent too late or whether he was sent at all. When Corin arrived he said one sentence — *He's not Vail* — and has said nothing about it since, which in that house is a form of argument.

## Wounds & Fears

A father who went uncollected, a sister who went cold in a warm room, and a wheel he was given before he was ready. He fears Sella will die with something unsaid and leave him holding the house and the lie together. He fears the Broken Slates will decide for him what he is, because he has not decided and the longer he waits the more their decision looks like his own. He fears the boy most of all, because the boy hears things. His contradiction: he is the most careful keeper of the Vail fire in four generations and he is quietly helping the people who want to burn every slate in Sill. He has never once thought these two facts cannot live in the same man.

## Desires

He wants the wheel to stay in the house and the fire to hold and nobody to write the boy down as Vail without asking him first. He thinks he wants Sella to say that he has done well with the wheel. What he needs is to learn that burning a slate does not return a person to you, and that the anger he has been nursing for three years is not his alone — it has been lent to him by men who intend to collect on it.

## Bonds

**Sella Vail** — his mother. He believes she took Corin in to replace Enna and to keep the house's standing after Garl died. She has never contradicted him. Every obedience he gives her is therefore double: it is love and it is a correction neither of them will make. *Ch 2:* she announced the taking and he answered *he's not Vail*; she got quieter and gave him no answer at all. He went on working, did not look at her, and shifted the damper before she had finished telling him to.

**Corin** — the foundling. Jorra was the first in the house to notice that the boy turns his head toward a pot before anyone taps it, and that he does it to hear the note, not the noise. He has told nobody. He resents the boy for being given a bed in the house while the clay-bound hand sleeps in the loft, and he cannot stop watching him. *Ch 2:* the foundling arrived under the roof and nobody wrote him as Vail, which is what Jorra wanted and what he cannot bear. He spoke to the wheel, to the pots and once to Tarn about a rasp out of its place, and never addressed the boy.

**Alda Reed** — attended Enna's death and Corin's birth, and has never met Jorra's eye for longer than a moment. He knows she wrote something down the night Enna died and has never asked to see it, because asking would make it a thing between them.

**Tarn** — the Vail's clay-bound hand. Jorra treats him as a brother when the wheel needs him and as a servant when it does not. Tarn has decided to hate him for it and keeps failing, because Jorra once sat up all night with him through a fever and has never mentioned it. *Ch 2:* Tarn was given the loft while the foundling was given the bed, and said so in one sentence to Sella. Jorra said nothing; he has never defended the household's arithmetic and never contests it.

**Berr Clay, the Thane of Clay** — Jorra threw a stone at his door in Y.H. 810 and denied it. The Thane suspects the boy is with the Broken Slates and lets him be, which Jorra finds more frightening than being watched.

**Ch 4.** Sella's hand went to the mark before the kiln and not at the bank; he shifted the damper before she had finished and again at dusk, and did not take up her silence about the back room. The boy was at the edge of his notice as a thing that hears: he spoke to the pots and not to him, and came in from the back room with slate dust worked into his forearms --- grey and fine, not slip --- and said *the back room's cold* to nobody. He wanted the boy outside the name Vail and the hollow seat his, and did not say either.

**Ch 5.** The wheel took the house into its rhythm again with slip dried to old bone up both forearms and no slate dust on Stone 13 or Stone 15; the boy noticed the absence and could not ask which it meant. He told a pot *you'll do*, told the wheel it did not care whose hands were on it, and shifted the damper before Sella had finished telling him the fire was low. He did not say the back room was cold. *He's not Vail* stayed sealed as it had been on the hour it was spoken, and the foundling went on being carried under the roof without a Vail name, which is what Jorra wanted and what he could not bear.

**Ch 6.** On all five days he came in from the shed with bone-coloured slip dried to old bone up both forearms and the bent little finger, and no slate dust; the boy noticed the absence and could not ask what it meant. He told a pot *you'll do* and *the shoulder's thick*, told the wheel it did not care whose hands, only if they were steady, and moved the damper before Sella had finished telling him the fire was low, going in with the bent finger ahead of his hand. He did not say the back room was cold and did not look toward the basket or the bed. *He's not Vail* stayed sealed; the foundling went on being carried under the roof unwritten.

**Ch 7.** On Stone 23, after five mornings of clean bone-coloured slip, he came in from the back room with fine grey slate dust worked into the creases of his forearms past the bend of the little finger and said nothing about where he had been; the boy read the arranged face the way he had once read a resident who kept his eyes on a chart. He told the pot she would want a thinner slip, told the wheel it did not care whose hands, only if they were steady, and went to the damper before Sella had finished, the bent finger going in first. On Stone 25 the forearms were clean again and he kept dusty and clean as two entries; on Stone 29 the sweeping talk made him quieter and the yard straightened around it without an order. He knows the full note of a Vail pot before it leaves the wheel and would not say so in front of a kiln-master, who would call it early and make something of it; the boy knows this about him as he knows the bent finger, and keeps both where questions can last for years. He did not say the back room was cold and did not look toward the bed; *he's not Vail* stayed sealed through the year's last market.

**Ch 8.** Through the Five the wheel-shed gave him bone-coloured slip dried to old bone up both forearms and the bent little finger leading at the damper; he moved the damper before Sella had finished and did not say the back room was cold. No slate dust came after the Last Day and he never mentioned the back room; he did not look toward the bed or the basket. On the Last Day the grey in the corners was still where it lay and the baking went on around it, and he went quieter as the sweeping talk went around. On Ilmen 1 he worked the slip toward the thaw and the pits with the same forearms and the same silence, and the foundling went into the new year carried under the roof without a Vail name.

## Arc So Far

He enters at the wheel with his weight in the hollow his father wore and has spent three years failing to fill --- slip dried to the colour of old bone up both forearms, the left little finger bent from a splinter at eleven, a story about a fight he tells because a fight sounds more like him. In Chapter 2 he says one flat sentence about the foundling and is refused an answer, and the sentence is sealed by the boy's arrival: a child lies under the roof and nobody has written him as Vail. He does not look at his mother while obeying her instantly, speaks to the pots before people, goes quiet when the levy is spoken of, and has given none of the men who come into the back room a word they can carry away. He is the most careful keeper of the Vail fire in four generations and is quietly helping the people who want to burn every slate in Sill, and has never once thought the two facts cannot live in the same man.

On Days 5 and 6 the back room comes into the house on his forearms as slate dust, grey and fine, worked into the creases past the bend of the little finger, and not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel. He tells the pots *you'll do*, shifts the damper before Sella has finished telling him and shifts it again at dusk, and says *the back room's cold* to nobody in particular --- not an answer and not a question, shaped so nobody can take it further. Sella does not take it further. The boy watches the dust and wants to know which slate was broken, and whether it was a birth-slate, and keeps the questions where questions can last for years.

On Days 7, 8 and 9 the wheel keeps the house's time and the absence of dust is as much a fact as its presence was on Stone 12. He works in bone-coloured slip, speaks to the pots and the wheel and never to the basket, and moves the damper before Sella has finished: an obedience that is also a correction neither will name. He goes quieter when the levy is spoken around and quieter still at *the house's share*, and the yard straightens around him. The broken birth-slates under the drying rack and the men whose names are not spoken stay unshown; the back room is not entered in any roll the house shows, and its cold is not mentioned. He still wants the foundling outside the name and the hollow seat for himself, and has said neither.

On Days 10 through 14 the wheel keeps the house's time and the absence of dust is the fact of the five days: slip the colour of old bone on both forearms, no grey worked into the creases. He speaks to the pots and the wheel and never to the basket, says *you'll do* to a pot and that the wheel does not care whose hands are on it, and shifts the damper before Sella has finished --- an obedience that is also a correction neither will name. He goes quieter when the levy and the debt are spoken around and quieter still at the reading at the Cross. He does not say the back room was cold, does not take up Sella's silence, and does not look toward the bed where the foundling lies. The broken birth-slates under the drying rack and the men whose names are not spoken stay unshown; he still wants the boy outside the name Vail and the hollow seat for himself, and has said neither.

On Days 15 through 24 the wheel keeps the house's time and the dust comes and goes. Five clean mornings make its absence a fact; on Stone 23 it returns, grey and fine in the creases past the little finger, and the boy sets the two states of the same forearms on separate places on a tray. Jorra speaks to the pot and the wheel, never to the basket, moves the damper before Sella has finished, and goes quieter as the Last Day sweeping talk goes around. He sets a pot down on the bench and does not turn to its interval, and the boy does not turn after him. On Stone 29 a sweeping that will leave the grey where it lies until it is burned and called dealt with makes the house straighter and him quieter; on Stone 30 he has moved the damper and not looked toward the bed. The full note is in a Vail pot before it leaves the wheel, which he will not say before a kiln-master, and the boy knows he knows. The broken birth-slates under the drying rack and the men in the back room stay unshown; the foundling goes into a new year carried under the roof without a Vail name.

On Days 25 through 30 the wheel keeps the house's time and the back room is not once mentioned. The forearms carry bone-coloured slip dried to old bone and the bent little finger goes in ahead of the hand at the damper, which he moves before Sella has finished; no grey dust appears after the Last Day, and the boy, who once set the dust and the clean slip on separate places on a tray, has nothing new to file. He speaks to the pots and the wheel and never to the basket, does not look toward the bed, and goes quieter when the sweeping talk and the burned Ash come around. The full note is in a Vail pot before it leaves the wheel and he will not say so before a kiln-master; *he's not Vail* stays sealed through the turn of the year, and the foundling goes into Y.H. 813 carried under the roof unwritten.

## Secrets (private)

1. He has broken three birth-slates in the last two years and buried the pieces under the drying rack. He has never told the Broken Slates, because he wants them to believe he is still deciding.
2. He knows where the district's second set of birth notes is kept — Alda Reed's tin under a floorboard — and has not burned it because he is afraid of what is written there about the year of the flower.
3. He has promised a slate with Corin's name on it to the Broken Slates. If the boy has no house, his name is easy to give; it is the one thing Jorra has that costs him nothing except, perhaps, the boy.
4. He does not want the wheel at all. He wants to leave Low Kiln, which he would say is impossible, which is what he says whenever he is asked anything real.
5. The bent finger came from the wheel, not from a fight. He tells people it was a fight because a fight sounds more like him.

## Plans (private)

Make him dangerous by the smallness of the act, not the size: he will hand Corin's name to people who burn slates, and he will do it while believing he is protecting the house. He is not a villain and will never be given a villain's speech; he is a young man with a wound and a wheel and a grievance that has been lent to him.

He becomes the one who tells Corin, years later and cruelly, that the woman who bore him was Dessa Silt and that she has been dead since the Slip Brook took her — a truth used as a weapon because he cannot say the thing he actually means, which is *I was here first*. He stays in Low Kiln, keeper of the wheel, a man who has never said which side he is on until the day he says it and it costs him the house. His arc is the saga's first proof that a person can be both wounded and wrong without being either one exclusively.
