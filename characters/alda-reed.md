## Appearance

Forty-two and built like a woman who has spent her life lifting other people's children: broad shoulders, a permanent stoop from leaning over births, and hands so steady that a mother will grab her wrist rather than her arm when the pain comes. Her nails are scrubbed to the quick and still have clay under them. She wears a grey wool shawl in every season and a little tin box on a cord inside her bodice, which she touches when a question is asked and never opens in company. She smells of soap, warm milk, tallow and the faint mineral tang of the Ash she has promised herself she will sweep tomorrow. The thing people notice first is her calm; the thing they notice last is the crossed sign she makes with two fingers at the corner of a birth-flower, which she does not know she does in front of strangers.

## Voice & Manner

Practical instruction in proverbs and half-sentences. She addresses the newborn, the stone, the slip, the mother, and only then the father, in that order. She does not say *please* and she does not say *sorry*, and she does not consider this coldness; she considers it the correct amount of words for a room where somebody is in pain. She has a habit of answering a question with the date. When she is lying she becomes unusually exact.

- “Warm the stone first. A cold stone is a stone, not a kindness.”
- “Don't set that near the fire. You'll pay for it in the thaw.”
- “He grew worse on the third day. So did the one before him. That's all I've written.”
- “The Court struck the name out. They didn't strike the child out.”
- “I crossed myself. It's what my mother did. Don't make it more than that.”

## History

She was born in Low Kiln to a family of reed-cutters and became a hearth-maid at nineteen, which in Grath means midwife, keeper of the birth-slate, and the person a household calls before it calls a collector. She has attended something over four hundred births. The first of the second notes was not hers. In Y.H. 651, long before she was born, a hearth-maid in Low Kiln attended an infant who grew worse on the third day and spoke words in a language no one in the district knew. The Court's physician at Greybank wrote it down as a settling of the graft, struck the phrase *Second Want* from his own report, and paid the maid for her trouble. That note passed down the order, from hand to hand and tin to tin, until it came to Alda. She has added to it every year since she took it up: every infant who grew worse on the third day, every word that did not belong to Grathic, every birth-flower shaped like a branch instead of a rose. The notes live under a floorboard in her house, in a second hand that the Court has never seen. Alda Reed's is the fullest set in the order.

She attended Enna Vail's death in Y.H. 804 and sat with Sella through a night in which nothing she did mattered. She attended Corin's birth on Stone 7, Y.H. 812, crossed herself at the branching red flower across his ribs, and told nobody what she had seen. Three days later she told Dessa Silt that her child had gone in the levy, which was a lie told to keep a whole-return infant off a slate that every officer in Slate Row would have wanted to read. She has not slept properly since.

## Wounds & Fears

Four hundred births and one she cannot account for. She fears collectors — not their tubes, which she has held, but their slates, which are always in a better hand than hers. She fears that the second notes are a treasonous thing and that keeping them has made her a person who waits for children to get worse. She fears Dessa's face in the street. Her contradiction: the woman who insists that everything be written down within three days has spent thirty years keeping a second record that cannot be shown to anyone, and calls it *not wanting trouble*.

## Desires

She wants one of the Court's physicians to read her notes and say the words she struck out herself. She thinks she wants the boy she delivered to grow up ordinary, so that she can stop looking at him. What she needs is to understand that she has been a keeper of the truth in the only way available to a hearth-maid: badly, in secret, and at a cost to the woman she lied to.

## Bonds

**Corin** — the child she delivered and crossed herself over. She has watched him for three years without once saying what she saw, and has begun to suspect he knows that she knows.

**Sella Vail** — they have traded a silence since the boy was three days old. Sella has never asked what Alda wrote; Alda has never asked why Sella took the child. This is not friendship. It is an arrangement with a door in it that both women have agreed not to open.

**Dessa Silt** — she lied to this woman about her own child and has taken her barley money from Sella's house ever since. When Dessa speaks to her in the street, Alda answers with the weather and hates the sound of her own voice.

**Jorra Vail** — he knows she wrote something down the night Enna died and has never asked to see it. She has begun to be afraid of the day he does, because the boy in his house is the one thing Jorra could be persuaded to burn for.

**Berr Clay, the Thane of Clay** — pays her to keep the birth-slates and has never asked about the second notes. She has decided this is because he knows and does not want a copy of anything, which may be true.

**Orra Weir** — was in the room on Stone 7 and tied a knot she cannot account for. They never discuss it. Orra is the only person Alda has ever let near the subject of the third day, and even she has not seen the tin.

## Secrets (private)

1. The tin under her floorboard contains the fullest record in Grath of the Second Want, including Corin's third day. It began in Y.H. 651 in another maid's hand; Alda has added to it every year since she inherited it and has never missed a year.
2. She told Dessa the boy was among the Forty. She knew perfectly well he was not; she knew the Forty were being banked for a private whole return and that the boy was the reason.
3. She recognized the birth-flower from an old rubbing in her master's notes, taken in Y.H. 651 from a child who grew worse on the third day. There have been others. She has never written Corin's name beside that rubbing.
4. When Enna Vail died she wrote in the second notes that the child's warmth had not cooled properly in the last hour. She has never told Sella, because it would mean telling Sella that the ordinary explanation was wrong and that her girl might have been held.
5. She has begun to dream that Dessa comes to her with a baby made of ash, and that the baby laughs in a language Alda understands in the dream and forgets on waking.

## Plans (private)

Let her die late, of a lifetime of other people's children and a leak she has ignored like Garl Vail, and let Dan find the tin only after she is gone. The notes are the plant: a second record, kept by a woman who was paid by the Court, that will one day prove the Court struck a word out of its own file because the word was true.

Make her lie to Dessa the moral knot of the early chapters. It was told for the boy's safety; it cost Dessa three years of believing her child was spent; it cannot be undone by any return. When Dan learns it, he will recognize the gesture — the kind answer, the note not read, the institution's version preferred because the truth would make a scene — and hear Ostrowski's voice in it, because the saga keeps translating his old life into this one.
