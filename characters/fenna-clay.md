## Appearance

Twelve when she first appears and in the years after a woman of moderate height with the pale-green staining of lead glaze at the corners of her mouth that the district calls *the green* and that she has never tried to hide, because a glaze-mixer without the green is a glaze-mixer who does not taste her work. Her hands are stained blue-green to the second knuckle and the stain is in the creases permanently, so that when she holds a cup at the Cross people look at the cup and not at her. She has a single lock of hair that never takes the dye of the kiln smoke and stays the colour of dry straw, which the High Kiln children say means she will marry someone from the lowlands, which she has spent years trying to disprove. She smells of lead glaze, crushed galena, vinegar and the sweetish heat of the Glaze Room in Ember, which is a beautiful smell and a poisonous one, and she is the only person in Sill who will tell you so in the same breath. The thing people notice first is the green at her mouth; the thing they notice last is that she taps everything — cups, tables, the wheel-shed frame when she visits — with a knuckle, and listens, and has never been able to stop.

## Voice & Manner

Practical, dry, with the glaze-mixer's habit of naming a thing by what it is made of rather than what it is: not *a cup* but *a lead cup*, not *a lie* but *a glaze over a crack*. She has a way of saying the dangerous thing in the vocabulary of the workshop, so that it can be taken as instruction if the listener prefers, which is the same defence Dessa Silt uses with different materials. She does not giggle and does not lower her eyes, which in High Kiln marks her as odd and in Low Kiln marks her as strange in a way that draws the boy from the yard across the river. When she is frightened she becomes technical, giving proportions and temperatures, because a recipe is a thing that holds still. She will not say *please*, because her mother taught her that a glaze-mixer who begs gets the lead order wrong.

- "It's lead. The pale line at your gum is lead. I can tell you when it started, and you won't thank me for it."
- "You tapped that before I did. Why did you tap it before I did? Nobody taps before the glaze-mixer."
- "A good glaze hides the crack. That's its whole purpose and its whole defect. Ask a potter which he wants and he'll lie to you."
- "I'm from High Kiln. You're from the yard. If anybody writes it down it becomes a thing with a number on it, and I don't want a number."
- "Don't set it near the fire. I know you know. I want to hear you say it in our house's words, not theirs."

## History

She was born in High Kiln to a family of glaze-mixers who have supplied the Vail kiln-house and half the tables of Sill for four generations, which means her household is comfortable and quietly hated. She learned the trade at nine by tasting the mix the way her mother did and by tapping a fired pot with a knuckle to hear whether the glaze had filled the flaw or only covered it. She is good at it — better than her mother, which nobody in High Kiln will say aloud — and has a habit the masters call *the ear*, which she has inherited from a grandmother nobody remembers and which she considers a recipe like any other: a thing that can be learned by doing it wrongly often enough.

She met Corin at the market when she was twelve and he was nine, when he turned his head toward a cup on her family's table before she tapped it, and said, in the flat way of the yard, that it would crack at the handle in a season. She tapped it. It answered flat. She has never forgotten the exact sound, or the fact that he did not wait to be asked. They have spoken at the Cross, at the Fishstairs, at the edge of the Clay Yard which she is not supposed to cross, for years, in the intervals when a child from High Kiln and a foundling from Low Kiln can be seen together without anybody making a number of it. She has never called him Corin in front of anyone; she calls him *the one who taps early*, and he has never corrected her.

When they are both older she will be the person who can tell him what a glaze does to a crack and why the Court's fine ware is beautiful, which is the physical answer to the question of why the rich are beautiful and repeated. She will also be the person who refuses to be his if it means being entered on a slate, which is the first time he will meet a love that will not be booked. She is, in her own account, a woman who has spent her life covering flaws and has never once mistaken the covering for the repair.

## Wounds & Fears

The green at her mouth is the beginning of sweet-sickness, which she knows, and she has told no one in her house, because a glaze-mixer who admits the sickness loses the wheel and her mother's table. She fears that the boy from the yard will one day be entered in a Court roll and become a person with a number, and that she will be unable to look at him in the same way. She fears her own talent: she is better at hiding cracks than anyone in Sill, which is a useful trade and a way of life built on a lie that kills slowly. Her contradiction: the girl who insists that a glaze hides nothing has spent every day of her life hiding the pale line at her own gum, and calls it *the green*, which is what everyone calls it, which is how Sarn makes a sickness into a trade-mark.

## Desires

She wants to mix a glaze that does not poison the mixer, which is an impossibility in Sill and the reason she will eventually leave for Vhalen, where the glass men use different fluxes and do not taste their work. She thinks she wants the boy from the yard to say the thing he never says. What she needs is to understand that she cannot be kept whole by someone who is himself cracked, and that loving a mender is not the same as being mended, and that the crack that is visible is the only honest part of either of them. Their romance can only begin in adulthood, when both have survived their first firings and the green at her mouth has become a thing they can speak of plainly.

## Bonds

**Corin** — the boy who tapped early. The bond is a shared ear and a river between two districts; she has the note by inheritance, he has it by a life he cannot explain, and neither has ever asked the other where it came from, which is how they have kept it safe. The tension is class and the slate: she has a house-name and he does not, and a marriage between them would be an entry in a roll, which is the last thing either of them can afford.

**Sella Vail** — buys her family's glaze and has never once paid the full price, which Fenna has decided is a kindness from a woman who cannot afford to be kind, which makes it worse. Sella has also never once asked why the girl comes to the edge of the yard, which Fenna has decided is the same kindness from the opposite direction.

**Jorra Vail** — whom she has known all her life and who would sooner burn a slate than let a High Kiln girl into the wheel-shed. Jorra has never said why, and Fenna has decided it is because he is afraid she will hear the wheel too, which is true and not the whole of it.

**Mira Silt** — the Fishstairs woman who sells her eels and knows exactly what a High Kiln girl is doing at the edge of the Clay Yard. Mira has never said a word to her about it, which Fenna finds more frightening than if she had.

**Her mother** — who taught her to taste the mix and never once asked whether she had begun to taste it too often. The mother's silence is the trade passed down: a woman who stops asking questions is a woman whose daughter will keep the wheel.

**Veno Brine** — the Greybank factor who buys High Kiln ware and once offered her a place in Vhal, which she refused without knowing why, and has since understood had something to do with the lead and a man who wanted her ear for a different trade.

## Secrets (private)

1. She has the beginnings of sweet-sickness and has entered it nowhere. She has begun to put less lead in the Vail order, which is a small treason against her household's price and the first honest work she has ever done.
2. She can hear the full note as well as Corin and has known since she was nine that it is not a recipe, because recipes can be taught and this cannot. She has never told a kiln-master, because a guild that learned what she is would enter her in a roll and spend her.
3. She saw the branching red flower under the boy's sleeve when he rolled it up to reach a cup, and said nothing, and has spent years deciding whether the not-saying was love or fear, which in Sarn are the same act with different names.
4. She refused Veno Brine's offer because he wanted her to mix a glaze for a whole-return exhibit in Sere, and she understood that a beautiful return is a crack covered with lead, which is her trade and the thing she most wants to stop doing.
5. She will one day tell Corin the composition of the Court's fine ware, and it will be the first time anyone has given him a physical answer to a political question, which is the kind of answer he trusts.

## Plans (private)

Let her be the romance that cannot be booked. She meets him as a child with an ear like his, and the reader will want them together immediately, and the saga will keep them apart for years for the best of reasons: class, the slate, the green at her mouth, and the fact that neither of them can afford to be entered. When they finally come together as adults, write it with restraint — the unbuttoning, the shift of weight, the smell of tallow, the thing said quietly that could not be said loudly — and let the thing they say be about a crack.

Use her to put the theme of the beautiful return into a woman's hands: a glaze that hides the flaw, a trade that poisons the mixer, a household that calls the green a mark of skill. She is the person who can tell Dan that repair is not restoration without ever using those words, and she is the person he cannot keep, because keeping her would be another covering. No sexual content involving her before adulthood; she enters at twelve and the romance begins when both are grown and the green can be named.
