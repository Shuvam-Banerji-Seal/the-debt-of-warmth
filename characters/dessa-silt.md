## Appearance

Twenty-four and worked older: a thin woman with slip-grey nails and a cough that comes up from the pits in cold weather, which is most of the year. She has a way of smiling when she is angry that her sister Mira calls *the door shut*, because the smile means nothing further will be said. Her left forearm carries a long pale scar from a broken sieve-wire, and she rubs it when she is deciding whether to tell the truth. She smells of wet clay, sour milk and, on the days she has been at the pits, the mineral edge of water from the Lead Run that nobody in Low Kiln will drink twice. Her hands are small and quick and never clean. The thing people notice first is the smile; the thing they notice last is that she counts under her breath when she walks — the nineteen steps from the slip pit to the drying rack, which she has counted every day for six years.

## Voice & Manner

Short, practical, with a joke that costs her. She says the difficult thing in the proverb-form the district uses for saying anything at all, so that it can be taken as advice if the listener prefers. She never calls her child by his name; she calls him *the little hot one* or *the flower*, and has done this so long that the habit has become a way of not saying what she means. When she is frightened she gets specific — dates, steps, the number of loaves — because specifics are a handle on a thing that is otherwise too large.

- “Nineteen steps. I counted them when I was waiting for him to come back. He didn't come back.”
- “A body can be warm and still be gone. You learn that in the pits.”
- “I didn't give him away. I gave him heat. There's a difference, and I'll hear it from a collector if you like.”
- “Don't set that near the fire. It'll crack, and then you'll have a crack and a pot.”
- “He's not a Vail. Don't let anybody write him as one and call it kindness.”

## History

She was born in Y.H. 788 to a mother who was banked and returned thin, which means Dessa came into the world with a faint interest in a life she cannot name. She learned early that this is considered an advantage in Low Kiln, where a person with some of somebody else left in her is a person who can work two shifts. She works the slip pits for six bits a day, shares a room with her sister Mira, and at nineteen had a child by a quarry hand from Stonemouth named Nils, who went upriver in the autumn and never sent for her. She carried the child through the Long Cold with nothing but a stone warmed at the Vail fire and the kindness of Alda Reed, who attended the birth on Stone 7, Y.H. 812, and crossed herself at the branching red flower across the baby's ribs.

On the third day Sella Vail took him. Alda told Dessa the boy had been entered to a house and then, when Dessa asked which, said the thing that has been a lie for three years: that the child had gone in the levy, that he was among the forty children of Low Kiln banked that month for a private account, and that a mother with a thin return would only draw attention to a slate that was better left alone. Dessa believed her. She has spent three years believing her son was spent to buy somebody else's wholeness, and has never once been able to say the number forty without touching the pale scar on her forearm.

## Wounds & Fears

A mother who returned thin and reached for a life that was not hers; a child she believes was banked; the nineteen steps she counts because counting keeps her from thinking. She fears that if she ever learned the boy was alive she would want him back, and that wanting would make her a thief in a district where a woman with no house can be accused of anything. She fears the collectors, the name-pot, and the Second Want in herself — the shape that sometimes comes over her in the night and leaves her reaching for a cradle that is not in the room. Her contradiction: she tells everyone she has stopped thinking about the little hot one, and has kept a scrap of cloth from his birth in a tin above the bed for three years.

## Desires

She wants to be warm once without anyone entering it against her account. She thinks she wants Nils to come back, though she would not take him if he did. What she needs is to know that the boy lived; and beyond that, to understand that the shape in her that reaches for him is not the defect of a thin return but her own love, which is the one thing the Court has never learned to deduct.

## Bonds

**Corin** — her son, who lies three doors away under another roof and does not know her. She calls him the flower when she thinks nobody is listening, and once, at a market, stood behind him for a quarter of an hour while he slept in a basket and did not touch him.

**Sella Vail** — the woman who took him and paid her in barley and slip-clay to stay away. Dessa has taken the barley every winter and hated herself for it each time, because taking it is a kind of agreement.

**Alda Reed** — who lied to her and has not slept well since. They avoid each other in the street; when they must speak, they speak about the weather, which in Low Kiln is never the subject.

**Mira Silt** — her sister, her room-mate, the person who knows the boy is alive and has not said so. Dessa suspects it. She has never asked, because an answer would be a thing she would have to carry, and she is already carrying one.

**Berr Clay, the Thane of Clay** — took the levy from her wages in Y.H. 811 and called it one in twelve. She spat at his door in Grain and denied it when he asked, which is the whole of their relationship.

**Nils of Stonemouth** — the quarry hand who left before the child was born. She has not heard from him, and has begun to hope she never does, because a man who returns after three years is a man who will ask questions about a boy.

## Secrets (private)

1. Corin is her son. She has never told anyone, and has told herself he is dead so often that the belief has the weight of a fact.
2. She was not one of the Forty, and neither was her child. The levy took her wages and her sleep, not her warmth; the boy was given away before the collectors came.
3. She once walked to Slate Row intending to tell an officer that a foundling had been entered under a false house. She got as far as the Waiting Room door and went home, because a woman with a thin return who makes a claim on a whole-return child will be believed about neither.
4. She has a stain on the ceiling above the bed where she kept a warm stone for the baby for a year after he was gone. She has never moved the bed.
5. She is already leaking when the story opens. The cough is not the pits; it is a vessel going thin, and she has treated it as weather for the same reason Garl Vail treated his as nothing.

## Plans (private)

Kill her early — Day 38, Ilmen 9, Y.H. 813, in the clay-heavy rise of the Slip Brook — and let her die uncollected, so that Dan's first intimate encounter with the Waste is a woman he loved without knowing her. The collector's clerk is at the Fishstairs; Alda arrives after the vessel has given; a one-year-old with a surgeon's ear is held on the bank and hears the small flat knock of a body that has cracked all the way through. He cannot speak. He will remember the exact shape of her hand on the basket handle.

Alda's lie is the thing he cannot forgive, and he will not learn it until long after the grave. When he learns that she was his mother, he will have no face to put to the knowledge — only nineteen steps, a pale scar, and the way a woman smiled when she was angry. Dessa never becomes a quest. She becomes an account that cannot be settled, which in this world is the only kind that lasts.
