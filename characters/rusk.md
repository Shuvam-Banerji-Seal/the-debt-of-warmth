## Appearance

Seventy and thin as a stripped willow, with hands that have spent fifty years in cold water and have the pale, dangling look of things pulled from a weir. He wears a coat lined with ice-mouse fur, which is a joke the collectors make and which he has never acknowledged, and a hat whose brim is stiff with old salt. One eye waters in the wind; he says it is the river, and it is not. He walks with a sideways hitch from a winter he spent on the plank walk when the Weir froze and he would not come in. He smells of wet rope, cold fish, tallow smoke and, in his room, of the iron he keeps in a drawer. The thing people notice first is that he never sits down indoors; the thing they notice last is the drawer, which has a small lock and which he touches with his knuckles when a certain kind of question is asked. He has a habit of turning a question back on the asker before answering, so that the answer, when it comes, sounds like a correction of somebody else's mistake.

## Voice & Manner

He speaks in questions, and answers his own questions badly. He never says what he wants. He asks what *you* want, then corrects the answer until it resembles what he decided you should have wanted all along. He is exact about vessels and vague about himself, and in this he is the mirror image of Marra, who is exact about other people's warmth and vague about her own in the opposite way. His questions are not socratic; they are a hand feeling along a wall for a switch. When he is frightened he becomes gentle, which is the most alarming thing about him.

- "What do you hear? No. Listen again. What do you hear now that you've stopped trying to be clever?"
- "You want to know what's in the drawer. Everybody wants that. Nobody wants what it costs to open."
- "I taught you to listen. Did I teach you what to do with what you heard? No. That's the part I kept."
- "Who told you a clamp was mercy? I did. Was I wrong? Ask me again in twenty years."
- "Don't thank me. Thank the boy you'll be when you've decided whether to say *leave it* out loud. I'm only the old man who held the iron."

## History

He was born at the Weir and has lived within a league of it all his life, which in Grath is not narrowness but a kind of depth. He kept the traps for thirty years, sold eels at the Fishstairs, and was known as a man who could tell a collector's boat by its oars before it came round the bend and who would not say what he had heard through the ice. He learned to read a vessel the way a trapper reads a weir: by where the water slows, by what has been chewed, by the note a thing makes when tapped. He has no name for the ranks and does not use them. He calls the full note *the note that holds*, and a crack *a place that has been used*.

In Y.H. 675 a man named in a Court file as a person of the sealed opinion came to the Weir and asked Rusk whether he had ever noticed that a man's ways run in spirals. Rusk said he had. The man asked what Rusk wanted to do about it. Rusk asked what the man wanted. Neither answered. Three years later Rusk opened the drawer for the first time and found a copy of a paper that had been read by nine people in Y.H. 702 and suppressed in 710. He has read it once a year since, on the Last Day, when the ash in the corners is not swept.

He saw the foundling Corin at the Fishstairs when the boy was small, turning his head toward a pot before anyone tapped it. He began to teach him not because the boy was gifted but because the boy was willing to decide: he had once watched a drowned eel-trapper be left where he lay and had not cried or prayed, only looked. Rusk recognised the look and has spent the years since shaping it into an instrument. He has never told the boy why he teaches him. He has never once asked where his memory came from, because he already knows.

## Wounds & Fears

A life spent in cold water has left his hands almost without feeling, which means he can no longer find the full note by touch and must hear it instead — a humiliating reversal for a man whose whole craft was the knuckle. He fears the Weighing, where whole returns are announced in a clean voice, because he has paid for one and knows what the payment was. He fears that he has taught a boy to decide without teaching him what a decision costs when the person deciding is the instrument. He fears Marra, who is the only person who has ever asked him directly whether he still believes in the Final Banking, and who does not wait for the answer. His contradiction is the whole of him: the teacher who warns against every clamp has kept the key to one in a drawer for thirty years, and calls it *not having decided yet*.

## Desires

He wants the sealed world — a vessel that leaks nothing, owes nothing, and can give nothing away, which he has called paradise for thirty years and has never once described in plain words to a living soul. He thinks he wants a mender cold enough to do the sealing. What he needs is to understand that the boy he is shaping is not a tool but a man with a dead daughter on another planet, and that the willingness to say *leave it* is not the same as the willingness to let the world stop wanting. When he finally opens the drawer for Corin it will be too late to be a lesson and just in time to be a betrayal.

## Bonds

**Corin** — his student, his instrument, the only person he has ever taught without being asked. He loves the boy in the only dialect available to him: a question, a correction, a hand on the wrist moved aside at exactly the right moment. The tension is that every lesson is a lie by omission. Rusk knows where the boy came from and what paid for him, and teaches him anyway, so that one day the boy will be able to look at a dying world and say what Rusk has never been able to say about his own life.

**Marra** — the collector who wrote the name before the face. They are the two halves of the same purchase: she pays with her fingers, he pays with his silence. They have not spoken plainly in twelve years. She has begun to go grey and he has begun to be gentle, and each knows the other is nearer the end of the work than either will admit.

**Orra Weir** — he walks her traps at dusk and has never once asked permission, which in a Weir household is a declaration of something. She keeps forty-one knots and he keeps a drawer, and both have decided the other is keeping the wrong count.

**Alda Reed** — they were in the room on Stone 7. She crossed herself at the birth-flower; he looked at the collector's slate. Neither has said a word about it to the other in three years, which is how neighbours in a small district show the deepest possible respect.

**Sella Vail** — he has never entered her house and has never explained why. She has never asked. They have an understanding built out of not meeting: he teaches her foundling to listen, she lets him, and both pretend this is a coincidence of the Weir and the Clay Yard.

**Hald Iron** — the Keldish clamp-master who once offered Rusk a place on the duelling floor and was refused. Rusk says he refused because of the cold. Hald says he refused because he was afraid of what he would learn there. Both are right.

## Secrets (private)

1. He is Unbanked and has been since Y.H. 675. The Final Banking is his life's work and he has never spoken its name to Corin.
2. He knew on the night of Stone 7 that a whole return was being bought with forty children's warmth, and he knew the name on the slate before he saw the child. He did not try to stop it. He saw in it the instrument he had been waiting thirty years for.
3. The locked drawer holds a copy of Haldre's *The Sealed Vessel*, a list of nine names, eight of them struck out, and a rubbing of a branching red mark taken from a forearm in the First Seam in Y.H. 588. He has never shown the rubbing to anyone.
4. He has slowed Corin's understanding of the spiral on purpose, twice, by giving him a wrong answer to a question about a dead bird. The boy corrected him both times, which delighted him and frightened him more.
5. He will betray Corin in the middle war by handing him to the people who intend to seal the world, and will believe until the last moment that he is doing it for the boy's own good.

## Plans (private)

Let him be warmth with a blade inside it. Every lesson he gives is true and every lesson is a lever. He is the saga's great argument about teachers: a man who loves the boy he is ruining, and who cannot distinguish the two because he has mistaken the shape of his own wanting for the shape of the world's need.

Keep the drawer closed for hundreds of chapters. When it opens, the reader should find not a villain's manifesto but an old, practical paper about stopping leaks, written by a man who vanished, and a rubbing of a mark that the reader has already seen on Corin's ribs. Rusk does not become a monster in that scene. He becomes a tired man who has decided that a sealed world is worth one more spent life, and who asks Corin one last question before he hands him over — and then answers it himself, badly, as he has always done.
