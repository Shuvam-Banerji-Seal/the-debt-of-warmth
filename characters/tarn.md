## Appearance

Twenty-two and bent at the shoulders from six years in the slip pit, which makes him look older than his term. He has grey Ash in the creases of his knuckles that no amount of scrubbing lifts, and a pale ring round his left wrist where an iron clip sat in Y.H. 806, when he was bound. He is a narrow man with a narrow face and the habit of putting the smallest tool on the bench into his pocket when he thinks nobody is looking, because a man who owns nothing should know where the small things are. He sleeps in the Debt Loft above the wheel-shed and keeps his few possessions in a clay pot with no mark on it. He smells of wet wool, tallow and the sour tang of a corner that has not been swept. The thing people notice first is how quickly he moves; the thing they notice last is that he stops moving altogether when he is told he is doing well.

## Voice & Manner

Deferential with a nail in it. He calls everyone *house*, including Sella, and says *I owe* where another man would say *I am*. He never argues; he agrees so thoroughly that the argument has nowhere to go. When he is frightened he becomes helpful, which in a kiln-house means doing three things at once and quietly putting the fourth right. His jokes are not jokes; they are statements of fact made in a tone that invites someone else to laugh so that he does not have to.

- “Yes, house. I'll sweep it. I swept it this morning. I'll sweep it again.”
- “Six years. One to go. I've counted every one, and I won't count the next.”
- “He's a foundling. I'm a debt. Don't put us in the same sentence, Sella.”
- “I took the small knife. I put it back. You can check.”
- “You can't return a man to himself. They tried with my mother. She came back with somebody else's hands.”
- “I took the small knife. I put it back. You can check.” --- said of his own accord, to nobody, in the tone of a man stating the weather.

## History

His mother was banked in Y.H. 792 and came back thin to the district's name-pot, with fragments of a life she could not name and hands that frightened him because they were not her hands. He was born of that return, which means he carries a little of somebody else's interest in a life he cannot place, and has been told all his life that this makes him useful. In Y.H. 806, at sixteen, his mother's account was entered against him and he was bound to House Vail for seven years, a term he did not choose and has counted every day of. He has worked the slip pits, swept the yard, banked the fire and carried the household's small accounts in his head for six years.

He was sent to fetch a collector for Garl Vail in the Long Cold and turned back at the frozen ford. Sella never asked whether he had gone. Jorra, who was thirteen, never asked either, which Tarn has decided is worse. He was in the house when Corin arrived and watched the boy be given a bed while he slept in the loft, and he has never said the thing he thinks about it, because a clay-bound hand who complains about beds is a clay-bound hand who can be returned to the slate.

## Wounds & Fears

He is a debt with a body, and one year of a seven-year term left. He fears that when the term ends there will be no house to take him and he will be entered again for vagrancy, which is how the district keeps people. He fears the Second Want in himself — the shape that comes over him some nights and leaves him reaching for a tool he has never used. He fears Sella's kindness more than Jorra's command, because the kindness is the thing he cannot enter in any account and therefore cannot pay back. His contradiction: the man who owns nothing is the one who notices when a tool is missing and the one who keeps the house's accounts most exactly.

## Desires

He wants a slate with his own name on it and no mark against it. He wants to be called by something that is not a house's possession. He thinks he wants freedom; what he needs is to learn that freedom in Sarn is another category of account, and that a man can be unowned and still be cold, and that the thing to want is not an empty column but a way to keep warm without being spent.

## Bonds

**Sella Vail** — she holds his debt and once, in the winter Garl died, gave him a warm stone for the loft which he has kept in his clay pot for two years. He cannot reconcile the stone with the debt, and has stopped trying, which is not the same as forgiving it. *Ch 2:* he calls her *house*, says *I owe* where another man says *I am*, and agreed with everything so thoroughly that there was nowhere for an argument to go. She gave the foundling a bed and him the loft, and he put the one sentence into the room that nobody had asked for.

**Jorra Vail** — son of the house, who treats him as a brother when the wheel needs him and as a servant when it does not. Tarn has decided to hate him for it and keeps failing, because Jorra once sat up all night with him through a fever and has never mentioned it. *Ch 2:* Jorra noticed a rasp left out of its place and Tarn answered him; the kindness and the command arrived in the same voice and Tarn did not separate them.

**Corin** — the foundling who arrived and was given a bed in the house. Tarn was the first to notice that the boy turns his head toward a pot before anyone taps it, and has told nobody, because a clay-bound hand who can hear the full note is worth more than his debt and he does not want to be bought. *Ch 2:* he said *He's a foundling. I'm a debt. Don't put us in the same sentence, Sella.* His resentment is not envy of the bed; it is a man keeping the one thing he owns, which is the exact account of what he is owed and what he owes.

**Dessa Silt** — he has carried messages between her and the house, and barley from her to Sella, and has hated being the thin part of that chain. He knows her cough is not the pits and has never said so.

**Mira Silt** — she has suspected for a year that he is more than he says, and has not asked. He has noticed her noticing, and has answered her with the weather every time.

**Garl Vail** — he was sent for a collector and turned back. The dead man's coat still hangs behind the door and Tarn has never once touched it.

## Arc So Far

He enters already sweeping, before anyone wakes, a bent man of twenty-two with grey Ash in the creases of his knuckles and a pale ring round his left wrist where the clip sat in Y.H. 806. Six years of a seven-year term lie behind him and he has counted every one and will not count the next. In Chapter 2 he pockets the small bench knife and then says of his own accord that he took it and put it back and they can check; he stops his broom for two strokes when a pot is set down and gives its flat note, turns his head a degree and says nothing; he banks the coals without looking at the fire. When the foundling is given a bed and he is given the loft he makes one short sentence of it and goes back to sweeping. His deference has a nail in it, and the nail is the one possession the house cannot check.

## Secrets (private)

1. He has kept the small bench knife for six years and used it to scrape Ash from his knuckles. The mark on the handle is worn almost flat.
2. He turned back at the frozen ford and told Sella the collector had been sent for. She did not ask, and he has carried the sentence she did not say for two years.
3. He can hear the full note of a pot before anyone taps it, and has never told a soul. It is the same gift the kiln-masters will later find in Corin, and Tarn has spent six years being afraid of his own ear.
4. He has begun to dream in a language that is not Grathic — the shape of his mother's returned life arriving as a Second Want. He writes the words on the inside of his clay pot with a nail and scratches them out in the morning.
5. He will be offered a whole return in the middle war and will refuse it. The refusal will be the first time anyone in Low Kiln refuses the Court's best gift, and it will happen before Dan's more famous refusal, which is why almost nobody remembers it.

## Plans (private)

Let him be the household's conscience and its cheapest labour — the person through whom Dan first sees how a house decides who is worth warming. When Corin is given a bed and Tarn is given the loft, the boy should notice it with the part of him that was a surgeon: a triage performed by a poor household on its own members.

Keep him poor, keep him mostly kind, and never let him become a mender. He is what mending is for. His refusal of the whole return is the plant that prepares Dan's refusal to collect; Dan will hear about it second-hand years later and not connect it to the man who swept the yard. When his term ends he will still be in Low Kiln, which is its own answer to what freedom costs in Sarn.
