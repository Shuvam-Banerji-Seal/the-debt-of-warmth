# Walk-ons and figures too small for a sheet

*One line each: who they are, where they are seen, and in which chapter. Named characters with a real role get a full sheet instead. This file is private and is not printed in the book.*

- **The child under the dashboard** — the nine- or ten-year-old in the minivan on the expressway, whom Dan went back for and got out before the fire took the van; never named in the book. Seen in the Chicago break, Ch 1. She lives and grows on Earth, and it is the hot trace attached to her, not to Nina, that one day offers a way home (T020); his last warmth went into her and he never knew her name.
- **The woman singing below the birth room** — a neighbour in Low Kiln whose low song comes up through the floor of House Silt with its words worn flat by work; heard, never seen or named, Ch 1. Her song is the room's second pulse, counted against Dessa's breathing.
- **The woman at the tub** — an unnamed neighbour in the Clay Yard who looks up as Sella carries the covered bundle up the yard, then goes back to her work without appearing to have seen it; glimpsed through the fold of the wool, Ch 2, and again on Stone 22, Ch 7, when the basket was carried two doors down for a neighbour's collection; she looked up, went back to her work without appearing to have seen it. The district's ordinary witness, who saw a woman carrying a bundle and nothing more.

- **The Sill assessor** — an officer of the Warm Court at the Account-day moot at the Cross; narrow clean collar and hands that have never touched a pot, pig or carrying cord, so that his work can be read off his grip before his collar. Gave one line, *The figure stands as read*, to the slate rather than to the crowd, and settled his shut ledger under his arm; Ch 3. He is an officer with a reason, not a monster, and he closes a matter before it has properly opened. If he speaks a second time, give him a full sheet. On Seed 10, Ch 20, Account-day, he was at the Cross again, his hand going to the slate before his eyes to the rail and then his mouth, writing the eight pigs down before anybody could ask which eight he meant.

- **The collector's clerk at the Cross** — a narrow man at the Court's table at the Slate Row end of the Cross, with a cuff gone soft from washing and ink in the creases of two otherwise ordinary-coloured fingers. Put his hand on the top slate before he looked at the woman waiting and said, in a soft voice with no edge, *It's entered as it stands. There's nothing in it the household need correct.* Ch 3, and the same one sentence again in Ch 6 (Stone 20), with the cuff turned back twice from washing, and a third time at the market's edge on Stone 30, Ch 7, where the neat upper line carries the small stop a fifth time. He is not Marra and must not be joined to the grey coat or to grey fingers; the neat upper line on his slate is unread to the boy, who sees only the pause between strokes, now seen five times. He stays the walk-on with no sheet: the sentence is a formula of the table, not a character. The woman waiting at his table, who drew her hand back from her own stone and went off toward the row with her fist shut, is also a walk-on, Ch 6; she is at the market's edge on Stone 30, Ch 7, and goes away with her fist shut after the same sentence and the fifth sighting of the pause in the neat upper line.

- **The collector at the Hearth Drawing** — an unnamed officer who came in out of the cold with a flat leather satchel, took a cold iron tube from a roll of cloth, found the way in a dying neighbour's forearm by touch and held it without pressing; cuff soft from washing and folded back twice, fingertips grey at the tips the colour of skimmed milk gone off. Set his hand on the slate before he looked at the bed and spoke once, to the slate: *The measure is banked. The account stands. The return is recorded.* He has a reason --- he believes he keeps the world alive --- and is not Marra and not the clerk at the Cross; the neat ordinary hand he writes in has no pause in it. Ch 4. If he speaks a second time, give him a full sheet.
- **The neighbour at the Drawing** — a man of Low Kiln whose age the grey had taken off, lying on a board-bed against the far wall with his hands crossed on his belly; gave under the cold iron tube with the small flat knock and was left with a pale round place on his arm where the iron had been, not a wound and no blood. Ch 4.
- **The neighbour mother of the rekindled child** — came under the Vail roof on Stone 11 with a child three days out of a thin return and sat later beside him on a quilt in the death-house with a cup of warm milk she never drank; asked whether he would know her if he came through and was told a child knows warmth before it knows a face. Ch 4. Her child, grey at the fingertips and worse on the third day, with a red mark at the hairline, gave before the day was out; the room called it a graft settling. Ch 4.
- **The death-house women** — the woman at the wrist who counted a pulse and began the count again when it was interrupted; the woman who pressed a fold flat over a chest as a hand might keep warmth in by holding it down; the girl at the hearth who moved the pot out of the draught and spoke to it about the thaw; and the older one who said *warmth goes in slow; you can't hurry a graft; my mother knew it and her mother knew it*. They corrected the boy without knowing he had spoken. Ch 4.
- **The woman from two houses down** — put her head in at the Vail threshold in the middle of Stone 11 and asked after the boy without asking after him; got *warm* from Sella, said to the fire, and went away with the answer she had come for. Ch 4.
- **The woman at the salted-perch tub** — an unnamed stall-keeper at the Fishstairs who asked whose the basket was, was told *warm* by Mira Silt, laughed, and went back to the perch; she made nothing of the answer and carried no figure onto any slate. Ch 5.
- **The short-changed eel buyer** — an unnamed woman at the Fishstairs who laid down marks for six eels and was given five and did not count them; the sixth part of Mira's six-year war with Berr Clay, done in the ordinary work of a dawn market. Ch 5.
- **The man come up from the iced river** — an unnamed neighbour who came into the Clay Yard with water on his legs and told the yard, when asked how long it was to Kindling, *not so far as a man would want*, then went into his own house; the number of days went around afterward as a closed fist. Ch 5.

- **The neighbour at the threshold with a slate to her chest** — an unnamed woman with pale dried mud on her hem and hands red from the tub, who stood on Stone 18 with the open writing turned toward her own chest, asked for a number, spoke around Garl's uncalled debt against the tiles without naming it, and called the sharing *a kindness, and it's entered where it's entered*; got *the house* and *ask the pot* from Sella and went away with her fist still shut. Ch 6.

- **The neighbour at the yard well** — an unnamed woman who came to draw with a slate tucked under her arm and a child of four or five at her skirts, and put the slate down on the stone lip with the writing facing her before she put a hand out to keep the child from the water; *Keep off the lip. It'll go in.* Her order of reaching broke the boy's present-tense break into a hospital at four in the morning, a chart with its note turned down and a hand going past it to a signature. Ch 6.

- **The man come up from the Ilm with the shard of ice** — an unnamed neighbour with water frozen pale on his boots who came up the Clay Yard on Stone 19 cupping a palm's breadth of grey ice in which a dark hair ran from one edge and stopped a thumb's breadth short of the other, held it up into the tallow light, refused to set it on the bench, and said the water under it had gone different. He was still in the doorway at the end, neither asked to stay nor told to go. Ch 6. Not the same man as the iced-river neighbour of Ch 5.

- **The neighbour back from the ice** — an unnamed man, face unseen by the boy, who came to the Vail door on Stone 21 with the cold in a small grey cloud and a number shut in his fist with the thumb tucked under it, said *It's come a hair further* to the door and thought better of the rest; Sella answered *It's a cold* and fed the fire. Ch 7. The number came as an absence and was not given.
- **The collector fetched for a neighbour** — an unnamed officer called from two doors down on Stone 22, cuff soft from washing and turned back twice, fingertips the colour of skimmed milk gone off, flat satchel; set his hand on the slate before he looked at the bed and said *The measure is taken and the account stands as entered* to the slate. Ch 7. Not Marra and not the clerk at the Cross; the grey fingertips were filed on a tray of their own.
- **The woman on the board-bed two doors down** — a neighbour of Low Kiln from whom a measure was taken on Stone 22 while the basket sat at the edge of the light; she made a small sound and did not look up, and two of them carried something out in a flat satchel. Ch 7.
- **The woman at the racks with the cracked pot** — an unnamed woman working the drying racks on Stone 25 who set down a pot that cracked all the way through with the small flat knock, kept her hand on the rim a moment too long, said *That's a pot gone wrong* and swept around the fault. Ch 7. She named the vessel and not the sound.
- **The three neighbours at the yard well with the changing figure** — women on Stone 26 who passed the number for the Five from mouth to mouth as *three*, *two* and *whatever the moot gives out*, then ended the argument in weather; one wanted a late thaw because late puts off the paying. Ch 7.
- **The child with the ice-mice patch** — a child two doors down whose hand grew a spreading thumb-wide grey numb patch on Stone 27, held by a woman who looked at it and did nothing, called ice-mice, a thing the cold does and therefore weather. Ch 7.
- **The woman at the tubs with the Last Day date** — an unnamed woman who told the girl beside her that the grey would be left where it lay until the Last Day and burned the morning afterward and called dealt with, as though a thing called dealt with had ever been a thing finished. Ch 7, and again on the Last Day, Ch 8, when Sella would not let anyone sweep the grey early.

- **The woman from High Kiln with the sweetish mouth** — an unnamed glaze-room woman who came down the path on Stone 19 with the sweetish lead edge of glaze on her coat and a smear of it at one corner of her mouth, looked at the shard of ice as a woman looks at a pot she has been asked to price, and said *It'll go early. Ice with a hair in it goes before its time. I've seen it.* Ch 6. Her green at the mouth is the mark Fenna's will one day be read beside (T051), but she is not Fenna and does not speak twice; a woman with the same sweetish glaze at one corner of her mouth says on Stone 27, Ch 7, that the thaw will come early because ice runs thin where the water is fast, and is contradicted by those who want it late. On Ilmen 7, Ch 9, a woman come up from High Kiln with the sweetish lead on her breath says the thaw will come early and that the Warm rising under the stone is the whole of it.

- **The neighbour who wanted a late thaw** — an unnamed woman who came to the Vail door on Stone 19 with a slate held flat against her apron and said the ice would hold because there was the levy in the thaw and *a late thaw puts off the paying; a body wants its weeks*. Ch 6.

- **The neighbour back from the Court's room** — an unnamed woman who came along the yard path from Slate Row on Stone 19 with her shawl across her chest and her slate turned inward, and described the room she had been in since morning: a bench by the door, a pot on the bench, a draught under it going along the floor to the corner, and a place in the figures where a line should stand and did not, every time. Said the figure was read twice, once for the moot and once for those who came late. Ch 6. The boy kept her room in four separate places and made nothing of the likeness. A similar room two doors down the Clay Yard receives the basket on Stone 22, Ch 7, with a board-bed, a bench and a door a finger's width open, where a neighbour gives under a collector's flat sentence; still not recognised as the same room.

- **The two hundred at the Cross** — the Account-day market crowd of Stone 20 who leaned in to hear the figure and then went back to the pigs, carrying figures and fists no slate could enter. Ch 6. Walk-ons en masse; none named, none looked into the basket. The crowd of the year's last market on Stone 30, Ch 7, leans in to one in twelve and goes back to the pigs, passing the figure for the Five from fist to fist. On Seed 10, Ch 20, Account-day, eight pigs went over the wet reading block and the crowd leaned in to one in twelve twice and went back to the pigs, nobody crossing himself at the bell's four, four and a gap and nobody looking into the basket.

- **The neighbours who hand the year's fire from hearth to hearth** — the unnamed households along the Clay Yard who, before first light on Kindling, put their own fire to the coal in a clay dish and passed it on; each house says it kept the flame, and nobody says where it was first lit or what it cost. Ch 8. The collector who takes the year's first measure that morning is offstage, unnamed and unseen; the boy learns of it only from the order of a door closing and a woman's fist shut.

- **The officer who reads the measures in a clean voice** — an unnamed officer at the Weighing who reads the year's measures, a whole return for a person not in the room and a name the household will not repeat, in a clean voice wearing the grammar of the Cross in a temple's clothes, with the speaker taken neatly out of his sentences. Ch 8. He is not Berr, not the Sill assessor and not the collector's clerk; if he speaks a second time, give him a full sheet. The neighbours who come back from the reading and repeat the name no further are walk-ons with him, Ch 8.

- **The neighbours who speak the names of the dead along the yard** — unnamed neighbours on the Remembering who pass the names of the dead from house to house along the Clay Yard while the Vail house keeps its own quiet and does not say the one name; the boy counts the spaces between the sounds without knowing which are names. Ch 8.

- **The neighbour who calls the burned grey dealt with** — an unnamed neighbour who says on Ilmen 1 that the Ash burned that morning is gone and dealt with; Sella does not say it, and the boy keeps the called-gone ridge beside the left-in-place one. Ch 8.

- **The woman at the well who tastes the water** — an unnamed neighbour who lets the bucket down before light on Ilmen 2 and says *Tastes different*, tasting a finger and making a small sound that is not a word; she passes the man's *honeyed* around the well with the bucket. Not the slate-and-child woman of Ch 6. Ch 9.

- **The man come up from the Ilm with the honeyed word** — an unnamed neighbour with his boots still wet from below the sill, who says the ice is *honeyed under the mud* and that he has said it twice already that morning and will say it again. Not the shard man of Ch 6 and not the fist man of Ch 7. Ch 9.

- **The woman at the racks with the nearly full pot** — an unnamed woman working the drying racks on Ilmen 6 who sets a pot down that answers nearly full under nobody's knuckle, does not tap it or hear it, says something practical about the slip and goes back to her work. The boy files the sound as the first earthly answer to an interval and cannot ask her to set the pot down again. Not the woman whose pot cracked on Stone 25. Ch 9.

- **The man come back from the ice with the hair's further figure** — an unnamed neighbour who comes to the well on Ilmen 7 with a figure shut in his fist and the thumb tucked under, says *It's come a hair further*, sets the fist on the stone lip, looks at it and thinks better of the rest; the number goes around as a shape nobody gives out. The same recurring figure as the Ch 7 neighbour back from the ice, kept on its own day. Ch 9.

- **The old man at the tubs** — an unnamed neighbour who says on Ilmen 7 that the levy will come whether the ice holds or breaks, and that a late thaw gives a man two more weeks of his own fire, which is the only argument for cold weather the yard needs. Ch 9.

- **The girl with her sleeves rolled past the elbow** — an unnamed girl at the well on Ilmen 7 who says her mother wants the thaw early so the pits can be worked, and is told to hold her tongue about the pits. Ch 9.

- **The women at the racks on Ilmen 8** — unnamed rack-workers, one of whom says the pits were worked early and that is the colour of the water, another calling it weather, both going on with their hands in the slip while the brook runs clay-heavy a little higher than it should. Ch 9.

- **The rack-women and the man from the pits on Ilmen 9** — unnamed yard-workers who leave their work and come down to the Slip Brook before light: two women in the water to their knees, a third holding a rope, and a man from the pits who comes down the nineteen steps at a run and goes in without taking his boots off. They lift Dessa Silt out with their hands and no words, wipe clay from a face that will not be wiped clean, and go back to the ordinary work at the yard's edges. Ch 10.

- **The collector's clerk at the Fishstairs** — an unnamed officer set at the steps with a slate and a cuff gone soft from washing, posted for a measure nobody has called for. Word comes up with the neighbours that he has been set there; when Sella carries the basket down past the edge the boy sees the hand go to the slate before the eyes go to the stalls. Not Marra, not the grey-fingered Drawing collector, and not joined to the soft-cuffed clerk at the Court's table at the Cross, who appears again on Ilmen 10 saying *It's entered as it stands. There's nothing in it the household need correct.* Ch 10. He is still set there on Ilmen 16 and Ilmen 18, Ch 11: on the first morning the boy saw the hand go to the slate before the eyes went to the stalls; on the third word came up that he had not been taken off and the eels went on being sold over the top of him. No sixth sighting of the neat dark hand came on either day. He is still set on Ilmen 20 and Ilmen 24, Ch 12, hand to the slate before his eyes to the stalls, filed apart from Marra, the Drawing collector and the Court-table clerk; no sixth sighting came in the seven days. On Ilmen 30, Ch 13, he was glimpsed at the Fishstairs with his hand going to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls and did not speak; the boy filed the order apart from the other record-first hands and On Silt 2, Ch 14 he was glimpsed again at the Fishstairs with his cuff gone grey from washing, hand to his slate before his eyes to the stalls, and did not speak; on Silt 7 word came up that nobody had taken him off. No sixth sighting of the neat dark hand came. On Silt 20 and Silt 24, Ch 17, he was still set at the landing, now four steps, four, and a landing a hand's breadth out of line that a whole market walked over carefully without a fall, hand to slate before eyes to stalls for a measure nobody called; no sixth sighting came. On Silt 29 and Silt 30, Ch 18, he was still set at the landing with his cuff gone grey from washing and his slate to his forearm for a measure nobody called, hand to slate before eyes to stalls, filed apart from Marra, the Drawing collector and the Court-table clerk; no sixth sighting of the neat dark hand came.

- **The neighbour scrubbing in House Silt on Ilmen 13** — an unnamed woman working in the room three doors down while Sella sets the basket just inside the finger's-width-open door; she goes on with her cloth, says to the pot *It's up again behind the racks. It'll be in the yard by night*, and does not look up when Sella takes the clay jar from the shelf. She does not name the woman who lay in the room or the tin above the bed. She was scrubbing in the back room again on Silt 5, Ch 14, while Sella set the basket just inside the door and Alda wrote the sixth note; she struck her pot once against the stone and went on, and a low song came up through the floor. Ch 10, Ch 14.

- **The weir women at the Cross who speak of Orra** — unnamed women at a stall on Ilmen 14 who speak of Orra Weir as a woman speaks of a roof she has stood under in a storm: not fondly, but with the exactness of somebody who knows which boards will hold. Ch 10. A girl beside them with salt dried white at her wrists does not once look down toward the shaded edge where the basket lies.

- **The old weir woman who says the saying** — an unnamed trap-worker with her apron wet to the knee, who says on Ilmen 15 at the blessing: *The brook takes what no slate will enter; the knot keeps what no clerk can spell; the eels run whether anybody blesses them or not.* She says it to the boards, not to the boy, and goes on with her salt. A man behind the basket says the last clause too and somebody laughs, and nobody joins the laugh; the hearth-maids and the women at the racks are otherwise unnamed. Ch 10.

- **The woman with a slate under her arm on the river road** — an unnamed woman met on the walk back from the Weir on Ilmen 15; Sella holds the boy level at her hip with a half-pace kept and does not set him down where the slate might lie open. Ch 10.

- **The brine-gull with the coal** — a grey bird that went off upriver below the north bridge on Ilmen 16 with a coal from somebody's banked fire that was not yet cold; the warmth left the carried fire's account with no slate entered and no hand put out. It is a taking with a payer and no entry, and the boy kept the subtraction nobody else kept. Ch 11. Not a person and not given a sheet; it is the bird of the glossary's **Brine-gull**.

- **The weir woman who asked Mira about the run** — an unnamed woman at the eel racks below the north bridge on Ilmen 16 who asked whether the run would come now the blessing was done, and got Mira's question and Mira's own answer (*It comes when it comes. Ask the river*). On Silt 2, Ch 14 she asked again whether the run would come now the month had turned (*Does it come now? It comes when it comes. Ask the river*) while Mira moved eel-bones and decided whether to lie. Ch 11, Ch 14.

- **The woman with her sleeves rolled at the well** — an unnamed neighbour on Ilmen 18 who let the bucket down and knocked the heads off three frost fronds, said the ice would go soft before its time, and said the water had been tasting of the Warm all week; the woman at the tubs said no, it would hold, because the nights were still cold and frost kept, and the argument ended in weather without anyone owning it. Ch 11. Not the slate-and-child woman of Ch 6 or the water-tasting woman of Ch 9.

- **The man who carried a number from fist to mouth to shoulder** — an unnamed neighbour on Ilmen 18 who brought a figure up from the road below in his fist, passed it to a man at the racks, then onto the shoulder of a woman shifting slip, and, when asked at the end of the chain, said nothing at all; the number arrived nowhere and was given to nobody. The woman who lifted a shoulder at him is with him. Ch 11.

- **The weir woman with salt white at her wrists who said the board would not bear a man** — an unnamed trap-worker come up from the river road on Ilmen 18 with salt dried white at her wrists and the cuffs of her sleeves, who said of the plank walk at the Weir, *One board in the walk won't carry a man. Not when the river comes up. They'll step on it and they'll find out*, and did not say which board. Ch 11. Not the girl with salt at her wrists of Ch 10; the weakness is spoken of in the third person and left where it is.

- **The neighbours who brought word the clerk was still set** — unnamed neighbours on Ilmen 18 who came up the yard saying the soft-cuffed clerk was still at the Fishstairs with his slate for a measure nobody called, with no sign of his being taken off; the boy filed the order on its own tray. Ch 11.

- **The two neighbours who had argued the ice** — the woman at the tubs who had said all winter that the ice would hold, and the man who had come up from the river in the warm spell and called it honeyed under the mud; on Ilmen 19, Ch 12, the first stood with her wrists in the wash and said nothing at all and the second kept his counsel and went off toward the road with his hands in his sleeves. The argument that used to end in weather had no arguer left to end it; the boy set their silence beside the frost-bloom and the slack frame. They are the same quarrel as the well-neighbours of Ch 9 and Ch 11, kept on their own day.

- **The man who passed the yard door with a carried vessel** — an unnamed neighbour who went past the Vail yard door on Ilmen 21, Ch 12, carrying a vessel that gave its note as it went; Tarn's head did not turn and neither he nor the boy said anything about it, and the boy did not call it a seventh hearing. The sound stays unnamed in Grathic. Not the man with the cracked pot of Ch 7 or the rack-woman of Ch 9.

- **The rack-woman who set down the second nearly-full pot** — an unnamed woman working the drying racks who set a pot down in the Clay Yard out of her shadow on Ilmen 23, Ch 12, and, under nobody's knuckle, the vessel answered nearly full with air under the note and a want in it; she did not tap it or hear it and went on with her wringing. The boy filed it as a second earthly answer to the interval carried since Stone 13, advanced and not paid, with no hand to tap either pot. Not the woman of the first nearly-full answer on Ilmen 6, Ch 9, and not the woman whose pot cracked on Stone 25, Ch 7.

- **The eel buyer who said a figure at the Fishstairs** — an unnamed buyer at the dawn eel trade on Ilmen 24, Ch 12, who named a figure for the eels; Mira answered a question about the run with her own answer and her thumb went under her apron to the grey cord and stayed while she decided whether to lie, showing the second cord twisted twice beneath it. Nothing was said of the thin child upriver and no door was knocked.

- **The weir woman who said the weak board was still there** — an unnamed trap-worker with salt dried white at her wrists and the cuffs of her sleeves who, on Ilmen 25, Ch 12, said plainly of the plank walk at the Weir that the weak board was still there and would not carry a man, and that nobody had trodden it. The same kind of figure as the weir woman of Ilmen 18, Ch 11, now saying the thing in the present tense.

- **The man who brought a number in his fist and thought better of giving it** — an unnamed neighbour come back from looking at the river who set his fist on the stone lip of the yard well on Ilmen 25, Ch 12, looked at it a breath, put the fist in his sleeve and went off toward the road; the number for what the thaw would cost went from fist to mouth to shoulder and arrived nowhere. - **The man come down from Greybank** — an unnamed traveller with road-mud on his boots and the salt-and-hot-clay smell of the east road on him, who waited his turn at the yard well on Ilmen 26, Ch 13, and told the bucket-round that in the last war a counting room was shut and the men inside died of the breath with the damper closed, eighteen leagues east. - **The bucket-round at the yard well on Ilmen 26** — unnamed neighbours who received the Greybank man's story as history: a woman said the war did for the men, a man said a room shut up is a room nobody has counted, and somebody said the frost would take the pits early; the argument went off into the thaw and none of them joined the sealed room to any kiln in Low Kiln.
