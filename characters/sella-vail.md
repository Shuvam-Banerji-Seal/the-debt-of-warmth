## Appearance

A broad woman of thirty-three with forearms the colour of dried slip and a burn across the back of her left hand shaped like a little door --- two straight sides, a lintel and a threshold, the skin inside it puckered and pale --- which she keeps half-closed when she carries a cup, and which closes on nothing when her hand has nothing to hold. She is not tall and she does not make herself tall; she takes up room by being impossible to move. Her hair is coal-black with one grey streak at the temple that she says came from the winter Garl died and will not discuss further. She smells of woodsmoke, wet wool, and under it the faint sour mineral tang of the Ash ridge along the skirting board, which she has not swept in two years and does not intend to sweep. Her hands are never still. When they have nothing to do they count: spoons, nails, the breaths of a sleeping child. She wears her widow's mark on a cord at her throat, a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and she touches it before she opens the kiln and never at any other time. People notice the burn first and the mark last, which is the order she prefers.

## Voice & Manner

Grathic proverbs and half-sentences, practical instruction addressed to the pot or the fire before it is addressed to a person. She says *the house* where another woman would say *I*, and *the boy* where another would say *my son*. She never finishes a sentence that would require her to say what she feels, and she is not unaware of this; she has simply decided that a thing said aloud becomes a thing the Court can enter. Her humour is dry and costs her something. When she is angry she gets quieter, and the quieter she gets the more the household straightens up.

- “The fire doesn't know you're sorry. Feed it.”
- “Don't set that near the door. You'll pay for it in the thaw.”
- “He's a boy. He's warm. That's the whole of what I know.”
- “You can ask me what you like. I won't answer it. Ask the pot instead; it's more use.”
- “A slate-name with no house is a coat anybody can wear. I've seen it before.”
- “The boy comes under the roof on the morrow. The house will keep him.” --- the taking announced without an *I* in it, and never explained.
- “Don't set that near the door. You'll pay for it in the thaw.” --- instruction to the pot, not to a person, given with the kiln already open.

## History

She was born in Low Kiln to slip-mixers, married Garl Vail at seventeen, and kept the Vail fire ever since in the way a widow keeps a wound clean: daily, without comment, with a hand that knows exactly how much pressure the thing will bear. She bore two children. Jorra, who lived, and Enna, who died at nine months of the second chill in Y.H. 804, after Alda Reed had warmed the stone and done everything the craft knew. Sella sat up with that child for three nights and learned what the midwives already knew: that a vessel can cool while everybody is being kind to it. In Y.H. 811, in the Long Cold, Garl died with the river iced and no collector able to cross, and his warmth went where unkept warmth goes. The Ash in the Vail corner is thicker than in any other house in the district. Sella has never swept it and has never said why.

On Stone 7, Y.H. 812, a woman in the district bore a child with a branching red flower across his ribs, and Sella took him in on the third day. She gave as her reason the one she has given ever since: a foundling with no house-name can be used by anyone, and a boy entered under a roof is a boy nobody can take for a levy without answering to that roof. This is true. It is not the whole of it. Alda Reed, who delivered him, crossed herself at the birth-flower and told nobody what she had seen, and Sella, who saw the same look on the midwife's face, asked her nothing at all. She has kept the boy close for three years and has never once asked where his memory came from. She is waiting to see what he will become.

## Wounds & Fears

Enna's second chill; Garl going uncollected; the Ash ridge that has grown an inch since. She fears another Long Cold, and fears more than that the day somebody from Slate Row notices that one child in her house listens to the fire before he looks at it. She fears Jorra, who has begun to disappear into the back rooms with men whose names are not spoken, and she fears that she has already lost the wheel to him while he still stands at it. Her contradiction is the whole of her: the woman who feeds every hungry child in the yard has never once said she loves one. She would sooner swallow the widow's mark.

## Desires

She wants the fire to hold, the wheel to stay in the house, and a Vail name written on the birth-slate of every child born under her roof. She thinks she wants a whole return for Garl, so that he might come back as himself and sit where he used to sit and say nothing in his own voice. What she needs is to admit that she did not take Corin in for the house at all. She took him because he was the only warm thing in a room full of people pretending not to be cold, and because she had already lost one child to the second chill and could not bear to lose another to an explanation.

## Bonds

**Jorra Vail** — her son, the keeper of the wheel. He believes she took the foundling in to replace Enna and to keep the house's standing while he did the work. She has never told him otherwise, which is how he has come to believe it. They speak about the kiln, the levy, the weather; they do not speak about Garl, or Enna, or the boy. *Ch 2:* he said *he's not Vail* when she announced the taking, and she did not answer him; she grew quieter and the house straightened up around it. He went on turning the wheel without looking at her and obeyed her before she had finished. The sentence stands and the boy is under the roof and still unwritten.

**Corin** — the foundling she took in and has never questioned. She watches him with a hearth-maid's attention and a widow's refusal to name what she sees. Her silence is its own kind of Court: kindness that does not want an answer, and that therefore cannot be refused. *Ch 1:* her hand came in ahead of her, felt for the heat at his belly, found none, opened and shut once over the covered bundle, and withdrew. She did not look at him, or at any of the women, and said nothing. *Ch 2:* the hand that hovered now takes. She crossed to the bench first and put her hand near the slate as near a sleeping animal, then lifted him, the little-door burn closing first on nothing and then on his weight. She said *the boy* and *the house*, carried him up the yard without looking back, laid him on fresh straw, and gave instruction to the pot and the fire. She has not written him as Vail and has said nothing of payment or purpose. *Ch 3:* she carried him out under the Vail roof for the first time, in a basket under a fold of wool, like a pot filled that must not be slopped. She kept a half-pace from every open slate, put the fold back without looking, answered a question about whose house with *the house*, and answered a question about the mother with *Account-day*. All morning she spoke to the wool and the wind: *Keep it off his ears. It's the river wind.* She did not look into the basket on the walk home and said nothing of what had been read over him.

**Alda Reed** — delivered Enna and Corin both, and crossed herself at the second birth. They have traded one silence for three years. Alda has the fuller notes; Sella has the fuller knowledge of what a silence costs when a household must eat. *Ch 1:* Alda laid the slate flat on the bench as Sella's hand went back out the door; the trade of silences begins in that room. *Ch 2:* Sella put her hand near the writing on the bench as she took the child and did not touch it, and Alda did not look at her. The arrangement holds without a word passing between them.

**Dessa Silt** — the woman who bore the boy. Sella knows it and has paid her in barley and slip-clay to stay away, and has never entered the payment in any account, not even the private one she keeps in her head. *Ch 1:* Dessa's pulse rose under her *door shut* smile while the hand hung above her child; neither looked at the other. *Ch 2:* Sella took the boy up the yard without once looking at the woman standing at the end of the nineteen steps, and Dessa did not follow.

**Berr Clay, the Thane of Clay** — confirmed her widow's mark and has never called in the debt Garl left against a shipment of tiles. The uncalled debt is worse than a called one, because she cannot pay it and cannot settle it and cannot stop owing it. *Ch 3:* at the Slate Row end of the Cross he told her the mark was confirmed by the factor at Greybank, that it was a kindness, and that kindness is entered. She did not answer him about the mark; she pulled the wool up over the boy's ear and said it was the river wind. He stood a moment with his mouth open on the next part, then left the debt where it lay as carefully as he had found it. She has never asked and has never thanked him, which he has decided means she knows.

**Garl Vail** — her husband, uncollected in the Long Cold. She decided he would not be collected; he did not ask her to decide. The Ash in the corner is the shape of that decision and she has lived beside it for a year. *Ch 2:* she keeps his seat empty and his coat on its peg, will not let anyone sweep the ridge, and touches the widow's mark only before she opens the kiln. The house keeps a roll of its own in which he is entered, and does not show it to a man from Slate Row.

**Ch 4.** The boy was carried to the Drawing and back like a pot filled that must not be slopped; she placed him where he could see and not join and stood a half-pace off, her burn shut on nothing, and would not be the one to tell him what the room was doing. She did not answer Jorra's *the back room's cold*; she shifted the damper. With Alda, a half-pace and a turned slate stood in for the arrangement, and neither asked what the other had written. Dessa at her threshold got no look from her and no word, and the barley between the two houses went on being unentered. Garl's mark was touched before the kiln and not at the bank, and his coat, seat, ridge and proverb kept the house in his shape while the debt stayed uncalled.

**Ch 5.** The rule of the mark held exactly: touched before the opening on Stone 13 and at no other time, not at the bank that night or on Stone 15. She fed him barley gruel from the house's own dish, holding his head with two fingers behind the ear, and gave every instruction to the fire, the pot and the wool --- *keep it off his ears; it's the river wind* --- never to the basket. She carried him down to the Fishstairs twice and to the market's edge once, kept her half-pace from every open slate, laid marks for two pair of eels and counted them with her eyes, and said nothing about the collector's cuff, the cord or where they had been. At dusk on Stone 15 her hand did not go to the mark; she counted loaves and bits with her lips and came back to the same place twice, keeping the shape of the days to Kindling without giving the number. She neither saw nor refused the crust Mira put in the fold, and where she is concerned these are the same thing.

**Ch 6.** On Stone 16 she touched the mark before the lid and at no other hour, moved her lips along the loaf shelf so the days to Kindling stood as a shape, and told the neighbour at the threshold to ask the pot. She carried him down to the Cross on Stone 20 and back, kept her half-pace from every open slate, set the basket in the moot-post's shade and did not look into it; at dusk she put the fold right without his face, banked the coals with the back of a broken pot and did not touch the mark, because a reading is not an opening. On Stone 19 she slid the basket a hand's breadth out of the draught and would not have called it love, measured the ridge with her thumb --- near two thumbs here, a finger more two doors down --- and said leave it until the Last Day. ## Arc So Far

In Chapter 1 she is a hand in a doorway and a shadow, not a speaking part. Woodsmoke and wet wool arrive ahead of her; then the burn shaped like a little door reaches for the heat at the child's belly, finds none, hovers over the bundle and withdraws. In Chapter 2 she is inside the house for the first time on the page and is known by its objects before she is known by her words: the hollow seat, the salt coat, the Ash ridge a thumb and then two thumbs wide, the charred edge of a slate nobody picks up, the roll the house keeps and does not show. She announces the taking, is answered by her son, refuses the answer by getting quieter, and on the third day performs the taking with a hand that knows how much a thing will bear. She lays the boy on a bed and tells the fire what to do. In Chapter 3 she takes that keeping out of the house for the first time and into a market that explains him as a figure. She carries him in a basket, keeps her half-pace from every open slate, refuses to say whose house he is beyond *the house*, refuses to answer Berr about the mark, and walks him home without a word about where they have been. The private roll and the Court's slate are seen to be the same shape of account, both entering a child who has not agreed to either, and her little-door hand is at one end of it. She has not explained any of it. Her silence has stopped being a pause and become a wall, and the wall has acquired a second face in the district's grammar of entered kindness.

On Days 5 and 6 the keeping goes on without an entry. She touches the widow's mark before the kiln on Stone 11 and at no other time, banks the coals with the back of a broken pot at dusk on Stone 12 and does not touch the mark then; she feeds the boy from her own dish and puts the fold right without looking at his face. She carries him to the Drawing and places the basket where he can see the work and cannot be part of it, never where a slate lies open, and stands a half-pace off with her little-door burn shut on nothing, refusing by her stillness to tell him what is being done. She says nothing about where they went, what was taken or from whom; when Jorra says *the back room's cold* she shifts the damper instead of answering. The wall has acquired a door she will not open and a room she will not enter, and the fire inside the rule of the mark goes on holding.

On Days 7, 8 and 9 the keeping goes on without an entry and adds no explanation. She opens the kiln under a hand that touches the mark and lets it go, feeds the foundling from the house's dish and says *the house* where another woman would say *I*, and puts the fold right without looking at his face. She carries him down twice through the cold off the Slip Brook and sets him in the bridge's shade on a patch no slate has reached, the same care she used at the edge of the light at the Drawing; she stands a half-pace from every open slate and buys eels without counting them with her fingers. At the bank she does not touch the mark, and at the end of Stone 15 she goes around the days to Kindling in the arrangement of loaves and bits and comes back to the same place twice, so that the number need never be said. The wall has grown a second face: it keeps out the woman at the eels, the crust in the fold, the cuff on the bridge and the count her son will not speak, and it keeps the boy warm without ever entering the warmth.

On Days 10 through 14 the keeping goes on without an entry and the shape of the number becomes a household's whole arithmetic. She touches the mark only before an opening --- not at a bank, not at the reading on Account-day --- and moves her lips along the loaves so the days to Kindling are a shape no collector can take up. She stands a half-pace from every open slate in the yard and at the Cross, refuses a neighbour a number with *the house* and *ask the pot*, and carries the basket down and back saying nothing about where they have been or what was read over him. On Stone 19 she moves the basket out of a draught without looking at his face and calls it nothing; she measures the Ash by thumb and leaves both ridges for the Last Day. The crust in the fold goes cold and stays where it was put. The wall has acquired no door: it keeps out the neighbour with the slate, the clerk's soft sentence, the figure at the moot and the number her son will not speak, and keeps the boy warm without ever entering the warmth.

On Days 15 through 24 the keeping takes the measure of a tend and a year. The rule of the mark holds before each opening and at no bank or reading; the household's number lives in loaves moved along a shelf, in the space at the end of the kindling and in a fist shut on a door-latch, and she will not say it even when the district gives out three and then two. She sends Tarn for a collector and carries the boy to the neighbour's room, setting him where he can see and not join, with a half-pace kept from the open slate. At the Last Day's sweeping she measures the ridge against the death-house's with a thumb laid flat first, and lets the woman at the tubs say the grey will be left and burned and called dealt with. On Stone 30 she carries him down to the year's last market for a reading and not an opening, stands a half-pace off with her burn shut, and brings him back under the roof to his bed, banked coals and two crusts in the fold she has neither named nor removed. ## Secrets (private)

1. She decided not to call a collector for Garl. He did not ask. The promise to keep his warmth at home was unkept and became the Ash ridge; she has not swept it and will not let Jorra sweep it.
2. She knows Dessa Silt is Corin's mother, and has paid her to keep away and to believe the child died in the levy. The barley has gone from the house all three winters; Jorra thinks it is eaten by mice.
3. She knew on the third day that the boy was not ordinary. He did not cry at the second chill; he lay with his eyes on the fire and his fingers opening and closing as if taking the measure of something. She has told nobody, not even Alda.
4. She has a second household roll, not shown to the Court, in which Garl and Enna are entered and Corin is entered as *the fire's*, not *the house's*. The distinction is deliberate and will matter.
5. The widow's mark is held on a condition: the Vail fire must never go out. She has banked coals under the ash every night for two years, including nights when there was nothing in the house to eat.

## Plans (private)

Let her be the mother who never says the thing, so that the boy learns from her that love is maintenance: a stone warmed for a cot, a coat held against a draught, a fire banked when there is no reason to bank it. She dies neither early nor violently but by slow leakage, which she will treat as a draught in the room until it is too late to treat it as anything else.

When Dan learns that she knew his mother and paid her off, all that tenderness must curdle in the retrospect without becoming false: she did it to keep him, which is the same reason the Court does everything it does. Her refusal to ask where his memory came from is the first wall in the book. He will climb it for years, and when he finally gets over it there will be nobody on the other side to explain, only an old woman who fed him from her own dish and told him not to set the cup near the door.
