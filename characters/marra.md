## Appearance

In the death scene she is a grey shape at the edge of the flare light in a coat the colour of wet ash, holding a slate, writing; the writing does not stop when the van takes him. In Sarn she is a woman in her forties with the neat, unremarkable face of an officer who has spent her life at tables rather than in rooms. Her fingertips are the colour of wet ash and have been since Y.H. 803, which she attributes to cold rooms in a voice that invites no question. She wears her hair pinned tight and her cuffs buttoned to the wrist, partly from the trade and partly because she does not want the hand seen. She has the collector's habit of touching the slate before she touches the person, and a smaller habit — noticed only by people who have watched her for years — of touching her own wrist afterwards, where the iron clip would go, as if checking that it is not there. She smells of cold iron, slate dust and nothing else, which is the most expensive smell in Sarn.

## Voice & Manner

She apologises in clauses she does not finish. She is precise about other people's warmth and vague about her own, and the vagueness is not evasion but a genuine absence: she has entered so many measures that she has lost the habit of entering herself. She speaks the Ledgerhand passive as naturally as breathing — *the measure is banked, the return is recorded, the account stands* — and then, rarely, a plain sentence breaks through, usually about a child, and is immediately corrected into the grammar again. When she lies she becomes even more exact, giving dates and figures that nobody asked for.

- "The measure was drawn at the fourth beat. I'm sorry, I — the account stands. That's all that's required."
- "I don't remember my fingers. I mean, I remember that they were — they were warm once. That isn't in the report."
- "You were written before you were seen. That's an irregularity. It isn't a cruelty. There's a difference, and I've entered it."
- "Forty measures. One return. If you add it and it doesn't balance, you're adding it wrong. I've added it. I know what it balances to."
- "I've been paying since. Don't ask me with what. It isn't in the report."

## History

She entered the Keeping as a collector's assistant in Greybank in Y.H. 790, arriving from the Hearthseat with a slate and an unusual care for the measurement of other people's warmth. Her early reports were models of the craft: exact, unembellished, quiet about her own deductions. Within four years she was collecting in the Sill district; within twenty her fingertips had begun to go grey; within twenty-five she was the collector of the Low Kiln levy and the woman the district crosses the street to avoid. She has never been reprimanded and has never been promoted, which is a distinction she has thought about more carefully than either.

In the winter of Y.H. 812 she was standing at the edge of a flare light on an expressway in a country she could not have named, writing because the trace in front of her would not cool. She did not cause the crash and did not touch the child. She wrote the measure of a man who gave at the moment of breaking and whose promise was too heavy to bank. She wrote a name — Corin — before she had seen the infant's face. Then she went home to Sarn and forged the assessment: a whole return with memory intact, booked at twelve hundred marks and forty unspent measures, with the deduction entered as zero. The money came from forty children of Low Kiln. The collector's clerk who carried the slate has never been able to spell the buyer's name.

She has been paying for the return ever since with her own fingers, a little warmth at a time, drawn without a slate and booked nowhere. In Y.H. 803 a slate in Greybank was found with a line that did not balance; it was corrected in a second hand and filed without comment. The first hand was hers. The second was also hers, written with the other hand so that the file would show two officers.

## Wounds & Fears

Her fingers. And underneath them, the forty. She can add and she has added, and the difference between what a whole return costs and what the Hearth was told it cost is a number she carries the way Dan carries Tasha's chart — as a small, hard, private knowledge of the shape of her own conduct. She fears the Weighing, where the year's measures are read aloud in a clean voice, because one of them is hers and it is not on any roll. She fears that the boy will one day look at her with that clinical attention and add her up the way she has added up everyone else. Her contradiction: the woman who invented an officer's exactness to hide a forgery has made the forgery the most exact thing in her life, and calls it *the account standing*.

## Desires

She wants the sealed world too — the Unbanked's paradise, where nothing leaks and nothing is owed — and she wants the instrument they sent for to be whole enough to do the sealing. She thinks she wants the boy to live. What she needs is to understand that she has already spent him: forty children bought one man, and no amount of careful handwriting makes that a balance. When she finally confesses it, it will be in a clause she does not finish, and the boy will finish it for her, in the plainest words, and the world will not end.

## Bonds

**Corin** — the return she paid for and has never watched grow. She knows his name before she knows his face, and when she meets him in Slate Row she will look at him the way a surgeon looks at a chart: to check whether her own work held. He will be the first person to ask her about the fingers, and the first to hear her say *I don't remember* and understand that she means *I do*.

**Rusk** — the other half of the purchase. He taught the boy; she bought him. They have not spoken plainly in twelve years and have arranged their lives so that they never have to. She has begun to be afraid that he is gentler with the boy than the work permits, which in her vocabulary is the same as saying he has begun to love him.

**Berr Clay, the Thane of Clay** — she took his signature on the levy of forty and let him forget the purchaser's name, which he did deliberately and with relief. She has never thanked him and never will; a debt acknowledged is a debt entered.

**Alda Reed** — Alda crossed herself at a birth-flower and wrote nothing in the Court's book. Marra has read the Court's book. She has never asked Alda what else she wrote, because a hearth-maid's second note is the one thing even a collector cannot lawfully want.

**Haldre** — whose paper named the ending she has been paying for. She has never read it. She has heard it described once, by Rusk, and has spent the years since doing the work without the theory, which is a more Sarnish arrangement than either of them will admit.

**Steward Derren** — her head of office, whom she has never contradicted and never trusted. He has praised her exactness twice. She has decided that his praise is the most dangerous thing in the Hearthseat.

## Secrets (private)

1. She forged the assessment for Corin's whole return and set the deduction at zero. The forty children of Low Kiln paid; no slate in the Hearthseat records the true destination of their warmth.
2. She drew the trace on the expressway and wrote the name before she saw the face. The name came to her from the shape of the warmth, not from any document.
3. The grey fingers are not cold rooms. They are the payment, drawn a little at a time over twelve years, booked nowhere, and the true reason her reports have never mentioned her own deductions.
4. She corrected the unbalanced slate of Y.H. 803 with her own other hand so that the file would appear to have been checked by two officers. The trick has never been found and she has never been able to decide whether it was craft or theft.
5. She is Unbanked and has been since before Corin was born. She wants the Final Banking and she has bought the man who may refuse it.

## Plans (private)

Make her the saga's most ordinary monster: a woman with a neat hand, a good reason, and fingers going grey. She is the Court and the Unbanked in one body — the collector who collects and the conspirator who wants collecting to stop. When Dan learns what her fingers mean, the discovery should arrive as a physical detail, not a confession: he takes her wrist to check a pulse and finds the colour underneath.

Keep her apology unfinished her whole life. Let the sentence always break at the same place — *I'm sorry, I —* — and let Dan, years later and much older, supply the words she cannot. She never becomes a friend. She becomes an account that was kept so carefully it was almost love, and that is the cruellest thing the Court has done to her, which is saying something in a world where the Court is cruel by profession.
