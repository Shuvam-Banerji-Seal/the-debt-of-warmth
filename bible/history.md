# A Reckoning of Years

*From the common chronicles, the border surveys, the songs of the flats, and the fragments the Synod has allowed to be copied. Where these disagree, the disagreement is set down rather than smoothed over. The years are counted in the **Ashen Reckoning** (AR); events before the count are called **before the Ash**.*

---

## On the sources

There are four sorts of record, and each lies in a different way.

- **The Synod's Ledgers** are kept in Ond and are beautiful. They record every Mending, every debt, every name struck through. They do not record anything that was not done by the House, and they are copied often enough that whole reigns have been rewritten in a clean hand.
- **The border surveys** were made by men who wanted roads and bridges and had no reason to flatter the priesthood. Their maps are plain, their measurements are exact, and they sometimes note things they cannot explain. These are the most trustworthy records left.
- **The canton songs** are the history of the Salt Marches sung at low water. They preserve dates badly and grievances perfectly.
- **The Weald counts** are oral, kept by the Gloam-mothers, and are said to go back before the Ash. No outsider has ever been taught to check them.

The priesthood teaches that history began with the First Crack. The border surveys show roads that predate the oldest recorded Mending by more than four hundred years, cut through three distinct layers of grey silt. The surveys do not say what made the silt.

---

## The deep ages

### The Uncracked Years (before the Ash)

The common teaching is that there was a time when nothing broke. A vessel did not crack, a body did not fail, a promise did not curdle. The world was a whole pot with no seam. This ended, say the priests, through pride: someone tried to make a thing that would not need mending, and the attempt split the world.

The oldest rocks of the Rift Marches tell a different story. They are veined with seams, and some of those seams are not seam-metal at all but a dark impressed script, set into the stone as if the world had been *written* at its making. The border survey of AR 312 notes a seam in the Glasswold "of a width beyond any staple, and bearing marks resembling sentences." The page was later stamped *examined, no consequence*.

### The First Crack and the Year of Ash

No date is given for the crack. The Reckoning begins not with the breaking but with the repairing: AR 1 is the year of the **First Mending**, when the Great Seam is said to have been stapled shut by the Farriers of the First Light. The Year of Ash is not counted as a year at all. It is named as a wound is named.

The Farriers are said to have been seven, neither men nor gods, who worked from the edges inward and left the finish to mortals. The **First Ledger**, held in the Synod vault, is their only record. It is a copy of a copy. Two leaves are missing from the middle and one from the end, and the surviving marginal hand is not the same hand as the text. What is left of it says only: *the seam was set and it sang, and the debt remained.*

### The Age of Farriers (AR 1–83)

Almost nothing survives that is not a later copy. The age is remembered for three things: the seven Farriers, the first staples, and the **Sealing** — the holy day on which the year's dead are Mended together. The border surveys find no Mending sites before AR 84. The first grey silt in the Vey valley appears in layers dated AR 88–92, abruptly and in quantity. It is generally taught that the Mending was given to relieve the world of Owed. The silt arrived after the Mending was practised, not before. No one has explained this in print.

### The Mending Peace (AR 84–231)

The long settled age. The rite spread from Ond down the Vey and out to the March. Villages built Mending chapels; the household Hearthrite absorbed one prayer to the Seam without giving up its older gods. The price of a cheap Mending, recorded at Veyford in AR 140, was one florin — the same price the Riveter charges at Kilnwick today. A pot was mended for a groat; a body, for a year's wages. The peace ends with the **War of Broken Vessels**. It is called the Mending Peace in Ond schools. The border surveys call those years "the quiet silt," because the grey deposits treble.

## The wars that made the borders

### The War of Broken Vessels (AR 236–251)

Ond claimed that the un-Mended dead of the Wold gathered Owed enough to split the world, and that the Wold was therefore an offence against all living things. Harrow answered that the dead were not the property of clerks. Fifteen years of burning along the upper Gyle ended with the **Treaty of the Iron Ring** (AR 251): Harrow kept its dead and its hills; Ond kept the Vey and the right to collect toll at Veyford. Eleven border forts were built; nine are still standing and two are not spoken of.

This war made the northern border and the Wold's hatred, which has not softened in a hundred and eighty years. The treaty is taught in Ond as the end of the Mending wars. There were three more.

### The Salt Schism (AR 288–300)

Ond held the salt of the Pale Water and demanded, in the **Tithe of Names** (AR 288), that the dry cantons surrender the given names of their un-Mended dead "for the completion of the Ledger." The cantons refused; nine clans took the flats; twelve years of evasion and quiet killings followed. The **Compact of Low Water** (AR 300) left Ond the north shore and gave the cantons the flats and the right not to be named.

The Ond schools describe the Tithe as a voluntary gift freely made. Every canton song describes it as theft. Both cannot be true, and the surviving tax rolls support the songs.

### The Short War of the Weald (AR 372–374)

Ond sent Riveters into the Gloam Weald to "survey the Bone Orchard." Three Factors entered the forest and came out unable to say their own names. A fourth came out and could not be identified by anyone, including himself. The House withdrew and declared the campaign a success. The Weald kept a debt against the House and has never said what it is.

### The Harbour War (AR 331–349)

The Nine Harbours objected to Ond's monopoly on fine Mending and to the price of seam-metal. Eighteen years of privateers, blockades, and ruined shipping ended with the **Articles of Verran** (AR 349): Ond kept fine Mending, the Harbours won the right to buy scrap metal and keep their own Ledger, and a clause permitted "the private repair of household vessels by the householder or his nominee." The House has spent the years since interpreting that clause as narrowly as language allows. The Harbours have spent them widening it. Smuggled seam-metal goes through Verran in wine casks.

### The Cull Rising (AR 410–414)

The worst year in the memory of the living. Over-mended souls in the Cinder Quays and the Cull Warrens rose, not to destroy the House but to be entered in it properly. Their leader is known only as **the Bird-Carver**, because the surviving petition carries a small carved bird where a name should have been. The rising was crushed at the **Sealing of AR 414**. The leaders were re-hollowed and their names struck through.

The official history calls it a riot of the ungrateful, stirred by southern merchants. One copy of the Culls' petition survives in a Rift Marches chapel. It asks for three things: their names back, an end to the beating of those who remember too much, and the return of small personal effects. It does not ask for freedom. This is the detail the Ond schools leave out.

After the rising the Synod opened the file of the **Grand Seam**: a final, permanent repair, "so that no soul shall ever again be owing and no vessel shall ever again break." Work began that year and has continued without public debate.

## The last two hundred years (AR 231–431)

- **AR 236–251** — The War of Broken Vessels. Border forts raised. The Gyle headwaters burned twice.
- **AR 251** — Treaty of the Iron Ring. Veyford toll confirmed to Ond.
- **AR 258** — First recorded use of the word *Cull* in a Synod document, in a list of labour "beyond ordinary remembering."
- **AR 264** — The first Cull Warren built under the Cinder Quays. Three hundred souls housed below the salt works.
- **AR 271** — The Quiet Census. Four hundred and twelve persons in Ond are recorded as "Mended three times or more." The census is not repeated for a hundred years.
- **AR 288–300** — The Salt Schism and the Tithe of Names.
- **AR 305** — A physician of the flats writes of a new sickness among salt-workers: *memory without a self; the man recalls his wife's face and cannot recall that it is his.* The House calls it a weakness of the imagination.
- **AR 312** — The border survey maps the Glasswold and the Singing Staple. The surveyor times the staple's note at dawn and records it as "one pure ping, no more."
- **AR 322** — A clay-reeve of Kilnwick is fined for mending a neighbour's water jug "without entering the break in the account." The fine is three groats. The jug is still in the family.
- **AR 331–349** — The Harbour War. Verran blockaded twice.
- **AR 349** — Articles of Verran signed. The clause on private repair is inserted late, in a hand that is not the chief clerk's.
- **AR 362** — A fragment of typeset paper in a script no scholar can read is found in a ruined print-house in the Rift Marches, sent to Ond, and filed. Four words are legible as shapes. The file is stamped *wait*. No translation is recorded.
- **AR 372–374** — The Short War of the Weald. Three Factors lose their names. The debt is not settled.
- **AR 388** — The Synod orders the **Reckoning of the Living**, a survey of the Owed in persons still alive, "for the maintenance of the seam." This is the first time the living are entered as debtors in bulk. The grey silt in Ond increases sharply after this year.
- **AR 396** — A Riveter named **Master Halloway** performs a celebrated fine Mending in Ond and is entered in the roll of the Synod. His origins are given as "elsewhere." He is the only Riveter of that rank whose earlier accounts cannot be found.
- **AR 401** — The heir of Harrow disappears from Harrowstead. The Wold says the south took him. Ond says nothing. A wooden ring is kept in a drawer in the Synod archive.
- **AR 410–414** — The Cull Rising. The Bird-Carver's petition. The Sealing of AR 414, at which the leaders are re-hollowed.
- **AR 414** — The Grand Seam file is opened. The first pre-staples are drawn from the living. The works begin below the House.
- **AR 415** — A Cull is entered in the House roll as number 44; his name is struck at the owner's request. A small carved bird is listed among his effects. He is assigned to a workshop where fine repair is done.
- **AR 421** — The Pale Water rises three feet in a single night and eats away the north silt shelf. The priesthood declares a washing of names. The border survey records the actual height and says nothing about names.
- **AR 425** — The Synod votes to "accelerate" the Grand Seam works. Cracking in the Salt Marches increases measurably from this year.
- **AR 428** — **Ildra Voss**, a Seam-priest of the veiled order, is granted leave to travel beyond the survey "with a purse of Ond coin and a commission to find an honest hand." Her face is not in any register, by her own request.
- **AR 430** — A silver florin of Ond bearing the unreadable script turns up in a clay village on the Gyle. No one in the village can read it, so it is put in a board by the bed and kept for luck.
- **AR 431** — The present year. In **Silt**, the planting month, the Gyle comes up early after a wet Brook and takes the bridge at Mire End. In the same month a child is born in **Kilnwick** to a clay-digger and a washerwoman, with a silver-white line across the left palm. The Riveter's next visit to the March is due in seven weeks.

## Lies, and how to tell them

These are the received versions and the objections to them. None of the objections has ever been printed in Ond.

1. **The world is healing.** Taught every Sealing. The border survey of AR 421 measures the Great Seam at the north shelf and finds it wider than the survey of AR 312. The measurements are in a public archive.
2. **The Mending relieves the world of Owed.** The silt arrived after the rite, not before.
3. **The First Ledger is the Farriers' own book.** It is a copy of a copy in at least three hands, and two leaves are gone from the middle.
4. **The Tithe of Names was freely given.** The tax rolls of AR 289 support the canton songs.
5. **The Cull Rising was a riot for freedom.** The surviving petition asks for names and small effects.
6. **The salt-sickness is imaginary.** It is entered in the House's own medical register with its symptoms plainly listed.
7. **The crack is a single event.** The Glasswold shows at least nine distinct firings. The oldest seam in the stone is not made of seam-metal and is shaped like writing.
8. **No mender has ever been called from elsewhere.** The file stamped *wait* since AR 362 contains four words in a script no Cindermere scholar can read. A second such scrap is said to be kept by a priest who does not show it.
9. **The Grand Seam is maintenance.** It has never been described in public. The vote of AR 425 was taken in closed session and entered in the Ledger in a clean, narrow hand.

## Seeds left in the record

Set down here without explanation, because a chronicle ought to leave room for what has not yet been understood:

- The missing leaves of the First Ledger. The surviving end-page has a single word scratched into the vellum beneath the text, in the same sharp script as the coin and the Rift scrap.
- The nine firings under the Glasswold, each with a seam laid over the last.
- The unreadable four words, carved over the House door and found also on a scrap of typeset paper before the House door was built.
- Master Halloway, whose earlier accounts do not exist.
- The wooden ring in the drawer, and the heir of Harrow who is not spoken of.
- Cull number 44, name struck, carved bird in the pocket, assigned to fine work.
- The petitioner called the Bird-Carver, whose name was already gone before the rising began.
- The fact that the Singing Staple gives out one note, and that the surveyor of AR 312 heard it as a true note, and that the House forbids drilling it.
- The florin in the board by the bed in Kilnwick, which is worth more than a year's wages and was paid for work that was never delivered.
