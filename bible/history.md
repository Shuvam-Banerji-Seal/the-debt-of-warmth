# The history of Sarn

*Sarn keeps its past in three forms: on slates, in the temple tellings, and in the mouths of old people. The slates are neat, the tellings are comforting, and the old people are usually right about small things and wrong about large ones. Where they disagree, this account sets the disagreement down rather than settling it.*

Years are counted from the lighting of the Hearth, in the form **Y.H.** Dates before that are reckoned **B.H.**, before the Hearth, which the temple calls the Broken Years and the people of Keld call the Long Want. The present year is **Y.H. 812**.

---

## I. The deep ages

### The First Breaking

The temple tells it this way. In the beginning the world was a vessel, fired whole and perfect, with the Hearth at its centre and the sky laid over it like a lid. It was broken in a single moment of anger. The Warm went out through the crack and ran away into the dark outside, and that dark is the Waste. The Nine Who Kept came after the breaking. They collected what had escaped, drove the first repairs, and left behind the rite of the Return so that mortal hands might go on with the work until the crack was closed. This is the first story a child hears and the last thing a dying person is told.

The story has the shape of a well-kept account: one loss, one repair, one institution to do the repairing. It is too tidy.

The surveyors of Keld, who spend their lives against the Listening Stones in the deep shaft, say the rim-seam carries many repairs laid one over another, some of them older than any Hearth that can be lit. The oldest of all are not stone and not clay. In the surveyor's language a repair is described by what it is made of, and these are entered as *that which was left unsaid made solid*. The Keldish translate it as Ash. The Court's own copy of the survey, held in the Hearthseat and shown to no one below the rank of assessor, translates it as *language*. Both translations are guesses.

### The chain of broken worlds

There is a doctrine, not taught anywhere, that Sarn is not the first vessel of its kind. It appears in three places: a crossed-out line in the Keld survey of Y.H. 588, a blank column in the Hearthseat's oldest ledger, and a sentence scratched into the back of a slate found in the Old Kiln at Sill. The sentence reads: *Another kept badly, and we inherited the crack.*

No temple acknowledges this. The Keld surveyors will not discuss it in the presence of an officer. The slate from the Old Kiln is in the possession of the Vail kiln-house, which does not know what it is.

### Before the Hearth (B.H. 400 to 1)

Almost nothing is recorded, and what is recorded was copied by men who did not understand the original. The temple calls it the Long Want: a time when the dead went into the Waste and did not come back, when vessels broke and nothing mended them, when the Cold had no place to put what it took. The people of that age are said to have been without the Return and therefore without hope. This is the temple's worst lie, because it makes the Court a mercy rather than a choice.

The few objects that survive from B.H. times are bowls and slates. The bowls are well made. The slates are accounts, and the accounts are in a hand that no living scholar can read, though the Vorr claim to recognise the numbers.

## II. The making of the Keeping

### Y.H. 1 to 120 — the lighting

The Hearth was lit in Y.H. 1, and the first Returns were recorded in Y.H. 3. The temple says the Nine Who Kept lit it with a coal brought from the first fire. The Hearthseat ledger says only: *The chamber was found already warm.* The entry has been copied four times, and each copy is smoother than the one before.

For a hundred and twenty years the Court did little but count. It established the three offices that remain: assessors to value a life, collectors to draw the measure, bankers to press it into an infant. It fixed the deduction at one part in twelve and printed that figure on the Weighing notices, where it still stands. It sealed four of the Nine Wells in the Keel and kept the keys. It opened the first bank in the Hearthseat and the second in Greybank, which is why Greybank has always been more Court than Grath.

The cost of those first whole returns was paid by a province. The **Empty Cantons**, sixty leagues south of Grath, appear in the ledger as a line of figures and then as a line of empty squares. The temple says the people were returned elsewhere. No record says where.

### Y.H. 120 to 300 — the quiet centuries

The Rite of the Return spread through the bowl in these years, mostly by being made compulsory. Villages that had buried their dead were made to bank them instead. The lead-glaze of the Keel began to be used on fine ware, and the sweet sickness followed it downriver, though nobody connected the two for two hundred years. The first Keld survey of the rim-seam was made around Y.H. 260 and is the oldest document that still has its original numbers. It notes a repair *of unkept words*, and says the repairs lie in layers, the newest over the oldest, *as a pot is mended again and again until nothing of the first pot can be seen*.

This passage does not appear in any Court history. It does appear, in a different hand, on the back of a slate in the Old Kiln.

### Y.H. 300 to 420 — the Glaze Wars

The Glaze Wars made Grath's eastern border. They began as a quarrel over lead workings in the lower Ferr valley and became a war of towns when Vhalen salt-masters bought the mines from Keldish contractors and the men of Sill found their clay roads blocked. There were no great battles. There were burned kilns, poisoned wells, and a winter in which three hundred households of High Kiln were made thin by drinking from the Ferr.

The war ended with the **Treaty of the Blue Cliff** in Y.H. 418. Grath kept the clay towns and the right to fire its kilns; Vhalen kept the coast and the salt; Keld kept the lead and the right to sell it to anyone. The treaty fixed the eastern boundary at Greybank and made the Clay Road a Court road, which is why an assessor can still stop a cart there without asking the thane.

### Y.H. 420 to 560 — the Bowl Wars and the deduction

Sere grew north in these years, not by conquest but by purchase. Its agents bought measures in Grath and the Fenmarch at prices no poor household could refuse, and returned them as whole, beautiful, remembering people in the southern capital. The Court's answer was the **War of Deductions**, a war of paperwork and collectors that lasted thirty-one years and killed fewer men than a single bad thaw, but left more of them thin.

The result was the figure everyone in Sarn now knows and nobody in Low Kiln believes: one in twelve for a citizen of a recognised house, one in three for a person of the fen districts, the clay-bound and the unhoused. The notices in every Court room still say one in twelve. The difference has been entered nowhere, which is how the Court likes its cruelty to be kept.

The Fenmarch became a confederacy under Court protection in Y.H. 612. Its cheap thin returns date from the same year, and have never been investigated.

### Y.H. 588 — the man with the mark

In Y.H. 588 a surveyor in the First Seam at Keld recorded a returned man whose forearm and ribs carried a branching red mark that the tattoo did not explain. The man had come to the mine four years before, could not say where from, and had been returned whole with his memory intact. The surveyor wrote down the shape of the mark and then crossed the whole entry out. The crossed-out page is still in the Keld file. The Keldish call such marks *birth-flowers* and consider them unlucky.

A second such mark appears in a Court record of Y.H. 803, in the margin of a slate concerning a levy in Low Kiln. It is not mentioned in the body of the slate.

### Y.H. 651 — the Second Want

In Y.H. 651 a hearth-maid in Low Kiln noted that a rekindled infant grew worse on the third day and spoke, in its fever, words in a language no one in the district knew. The Court's physician at Greybank wrote the condition down as a settling of the graft, which is the ordinary explanation of the second chill and is usually true. The word **Second Want** appears once in his report and is struck out. From that year onward the Court's medical records speak only of the afterdrop. The hearth-maid's note is not in the file.

### Y.H. 668 to 700 — the Ash Rising

The **Ash Rising** began in Low Kiln in Y.H. 668, after a levy took ninety children's warmth for a whole return that no household in Sill could account for. The rising lasted eleven weeks. It was put down by Assessor-Marshal **Pell Varr**, who arrived from the Hearthseat with twelve collectors and a list. The accounts of what followed disagree only in degree. Varr's own report says forty-one dead, all of them "spent in the keeping of the peace." The hearth-maids of Low Kiln say nothing and do not sweep their corners on the Last Day.

From the survivors came the people the Court calls **Unbanked** and who call themselves nothing at all. Their argument is old and simple: if the world must be kept, then let it be kept perfectly, so that nothing leaks, nothing is owed, and no one can ever give anything away. A world of unbreakable vessels would be a world of the walking dead. The Court's public answer is that the Unbanked are mad. The Court's private answer is kept in a sealed file in the Hearthseat.

### Y.H. 702 — Haldre's paper

In Y.H. 702 a keeper of the Hearthseat named **Haldre** wrote a paper called *The Sealed Vessel*. It proposed that the Final Banking be accomplished not by more Returns but by making every vessel perfectly retentive: no leakage, no deduction, no Want, no return. The paper was read by nine people and suppressed in Y.H. 710. Haldre disappeared in Y.H. 704. The Court records that he died and was banked. The Unbanked records that he crossed first. There is no grave.

What the two accounts agree on is that he wanted the same ending. They disagree only on whether he was the first to reach it.

### Y.H. 741 to 747 — the Rim War

The Rim War made Grath's northern border. Keld's Warden **Karric VII** claimed that the true Hearth lay under his mountain and shut the Cold Pass to Court traffic. The Court answered with assessors, then with mercenaries, then with a levy of Grathish clay-men who had no interest in the argument. Seven years of fighting ended at the **Cold Pass agreement** of Y.H. 747: Keld keeps the pass and the survey shaft, Grath keeps the road, and the Court guarantees the Hearthseat as neutral ground. The treaty is engraved on a slate in Slate Row in Sill. The local thane is not consulted about it, which is the Grathish view of all important treaties.

### The last thirty years, in detail

**Y.H. 782 to 790.** The Court extends its counting to foundlings. Before this year a child with no house was simply given a warm name at the moot; after it, every birth in Grath must be entered on a slate within three days. The hearth-maids object. The hearth-maids are paid by the Court. The objection stops.

**Y.H. 790.** Marra arrives in Greybank from the Hearthseat, a junior with a slate and an unusual care for the measurement of other people's warmth. Her early reports are models of the craft: exact, unembellished, and quiet about her own deductions. Within four years she is collecting in the Sill district. Within twenty her fingertips have begun to go the colour of wet ash, which she attributes to cold rooms.

**Y.H. 794 to 803.** The price of a whole return rises from nine hundred marks to twelve hundred, and the number of unspent measures required with it is fixed at forty. No explanation is given for why forty is necessary when one intact measure should serve. The assessors answer the question with the phrase *the account stands*, which is the Court's way of closing a subject. In Y.H. 803 a slate in Greybank is found with a line that does not balance; it is corrected in a second hand and filed without comment. The first hand is Marra's.

**Y.H. 806.** The Keld survey of the First Seam is sealed. The crossed-out entry of Y.H. 588 is copied into the sealed file at the Hearthseat, and the copy is made by a clerk who cannot have known why he was asked.

**Y.H. 811, the Long Cold.** Ice came on the Ilm in early Stone and did not break for ninety-four days. The barley crop of the previous summer had been thin; the peat in Mud End did not dry; fuel prices doubled and then stopped being quoted, because there was none to sell. In Low Kiln, households began to be called *walking thin* — people with the measures of the returned poor, who keep moving because stopping is the same as cooling. The Court raised the levy to one in three across every Low Kiln household and announced that the additional warmth was for the Hearth. The printed notices still say one in twelve. Nobody in Low Kiln reads them.

**Y.H. 812, the year of the book.** In the first weeks of Stone, forty children of Low Kiln were banked for a private account. The entry in the Greybank slate gives no name for the purchaser and no destination for the warmth. It gives a name for the return: **Corin**. The collector who wrote it had not yet seen the infant's face. She wrote it from the shape of a measure that would not cool, on the same night that a woman in Low Kiln bore a child with a branching red flower across his ribs and forearm, and crossed herself, and said nothing more.

## IV. Irregularities in the record

These are gathered here because they are awkward to place anywhere else. They are not explained in this account. A reader who meets them again later should not be surprised.

1. The oldest repairs in the rim-seam are recorded as neither stone nor clay. The Keldish say Ash. The Court's copy says language.
2. The Empty Cantons have roads, wells and doorways but no recorded population after Y.H. 40.
3. In Y.H. 588 a returned man in the First Seam carried a branching red mark. The entry was crossed out.
4. In Y.H. 651 the Second Want was struck from the medical record by a physician whose own report used the phrase.
5. Haldre wrote a paper proposing a sealed world in Y.H. 702, vanished in Y.H. 704, and left no completed return.
6. In Y.H. 717 a Keld surveyor described the oldest repair as *words that hold* and was removed from the shaft.
7. In Y.H. 803 an unbalanced slate in Greybank was corrected in a second hand, and the first hand was Marra's.
8. In Y.H. 812 forty children of Low Kiln paid for a whole return whose purchaser is not named, and the purchaser wrote the infant's name before seeing his face.
9. The Ash is said to gather where promises are unkept. It has never been weighed. It has never been weighed because it is not always visible in daylight.
10. The temple teaches that the world was made whole and cracked once. The seam shows layers. A pot mended many times is not the pot it was.
