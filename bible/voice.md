# Voice manifesto — *The Cold That Remembers*

Built faithfully on `design/STYLE-REFERENCE.md`. That document's music is binding: keep its person and tense, its tense-shift, its sentence rhythm, its handling of violence, grief, tenderness and humour, its dialogue discipline and its banned list. Re-read it before every scene. What follows is only what is new to this saga; where the two disagree, the reference wins.

---

## Person, tense, distance (inherited, unchanged)

**Third-person limited, past tense.** The narrator sits close inside Sten's head but is not Sten: a chronicler who has learned his rhythms. It reports what he notices, in the order he notices it, and is allowed to know a little more than he does when the knowledge is his but buried. Free indirect style is the workhorse — the narrator's sentence slips into his judgement without a signpost, then out again.

**Present-tense immediacy belongs only to the past life** — the Chicago life, the “first life.” When it takes him, the grammar tightens and the sentences go present for a beat or two: *The expressway fills with brake-light. The water is at the door. He has left the call unanswered.* Then it falls back into the past. That small tense-shift is the seam where the two lives meet. It is the only licence for modern imagery; the narration of Sarn itself draws every image from Sarn's own materials — Hoar, coal, flue, peat, wool, tallow, iron, bone, snow, rope, turf, frost.

Point of view stays with Sten almost always. When the story must leave him — a chapter in Verran, a chapter inside the Warm Court — keep it to a single scene and make the change felt, like a room gone cold.

## The double consciousness on the page (inherited, then sharpened for an infant)

He is an infant's, then a boy's, then a man's body with thirty-nine years behind the eyes. Never explain this to the other characters. Never have him say *I was a man once* to someone who would not understand. It shows instead:

- **In competence.** He knows how to tell a fever's curve by the heat of a brow, how to stop a bleed with pressure and patience, how to read a room's warmth before anyone speaks. Villagers think him odd; the Kept think him trained.
- **In wrong familiarity.** A shape of frost on a window, the exact blue of a Spent child's nails, the sour-sweet smell of a nearly-spent coal makes him feel at home where he has never been. Write the unease of recognition — the sense of having come back to a room after it has been rearranged.
- **In grief at absences nobody else registers.** He mourns a daughter in a country that has no word for what he lost. His sorrow has no object here, so it attaches to the small things of this world he can pretend were tokens of the last — a smooth iron button found in the hearth-ash, a scrap of tin from a broken flue — and, above all, to the branching birthmark on his own ribs that is the shape of the burn he died with.
- **In the register of his thoughts.** The dead man's interior is dry, exact, unsentimental; the infant's body is immediate, sensory, impatient, and humiliating. Let the two argue on the page. A grown man's patience interrupted by a child's hunger is the whole texture of the early chapters.
- **In the silence.** For the first chapters he cannot speak at all. He understands every word and can produce none. An adult mind locked behind a mouth that only cries is the earliest, cruellest form of the double life. Do not narrate this as pathology; narrate it as courtesy — he *chooses* the correct babble, because the wrong sound gets a baby killed.
- **Use `\memory{...}` only for remembered *words*** — Nina's lullaby, an attending's line in the trauma bay, a dispatcher over the radio. The macro italicises them and makes them feel like a voice in the room. Do not use it for general recollection; memory as *scene* is written straight into the prose, with the tense-shift described above.

He keeps the fact of his past life as a thing held in the mouth, like a cold lozenge. It is always there, and he almost never speaks it.

## Sentence music (inherited, unchanged)

Long, winding sentences made of subordinate clauses and small conjunctions — *and, and, but, so* — to carry the weight of memory and sensory detail. Break them with very short declaratives. *The coal gave out. The room went blue.*

Vary paragraph length hard: a single-line paragraph for the thing that lands; a slow half-page for the thing that must be dwelt in. Silence is a paragraph break. Do not fill it.

Use parataxis for violence — short clauses, no metaphor, no writer between the event and the reader. Use anaphora for grief: *He had kept the token. He had kept the badge. He had kept the lie.* Use the plain word where a fancy one would show off: *coal, flue, stone, frost, breath, cold, kept, spent*.

Rhythm is breath. A wounding sentence should be short enough to be taken in one breath. A sentence of tenderness may wind on longer than is comfortable.

## Violence, grief, tenderness, humour (inherited, then applied here)

**Violence** is physical, exact and unsentimental. Write what the body does and what it sounds like: a cheek caving like a rotten turnip, the wet click of a dislocated shoulder going back, the soft rush of blood into a boot. Do not choreograph it like a contest; real violence is brief, clumsy and followed by a long undignified interval. Consequences outlast the scene — a limp, a tremor, a cough that will not stop, a name nobody will say. Never make an atrocity elegant. Never look away from it either. The Hoar, the Warm Court and the Keeper's trade have slavery, torture, the cold cough, the sale of children's warmth and the daily administrative cruelty of the coal-reeve; depict them plainly, with the victim's interiority intact and the perpetrator's banality exposed. A Keeper who drains a man is a tired civil servant doing a tiresome job and going home to his tea.

**Grief** is understatement plus one betraying physical detail. People do not deliver speeches over the dead; they fail to put down a cup, or they bank a coal wrongly for the first time in twenty years. The body's small mistakes carry the feeling. Let the reader supply the rest. When the feeling finally breaks, do it in one sentence and move on.

**Tenderness** is almost always an act of warming: a stone heated against a belly, a flue stopped with a rag, bread torn so the soft part goes to the other person, a hand held a moment too long over a banked coal. Touch is specific. Write desire between adults with the same restraint: the loosening of a coat, the shift of weight on a narrow bed, the smell of wet wool and tallow, the thing said quietly that could not be said loudly. **There is no sexual content involving anyone under eighteen, ever.** Sten's earliest life here is a child's life; keep it so. Sexual violence may exist in the world as a fact of power, but it is never depicted graphically or erotically, and it is never seasoning.

**Humour** is dry, northern, working-class: the joke made because the alternative is weeping. A grown man's despair observed by a hungry child's body is funny and awful at once. Let a Spent man say he was “warmed” by a beating the way another man might say he was taught a lesson. Never undercut a painful moment with a quip to make the reader comfortable. The joke must cost something.

## Dialogue conventions (inherited, with this saga's idiolects)

Every important character has an idiolect, recorded on their sheet, and must be readable by voice alone.

- **Sten** speaks little, and never in the old-life idiom to people who would find it strange. He is plain, low, exact. His question is usually the shortest one in the room. As an infant he says nothing at all that matters.
- **The Keepers and the Warm Court** speak in careful, warmer-than-necessary phrases: *The coal is recorded. The room is kept. What is spent is gone.* They never raise their voices. Their violence is grammatical.
- **The Spent of Grath** speak in proverbs, half-sentences and practical instruction: *Don't bank that wet. You'll cough come Still.* They address the stone, the flue, the fire, and only then each other.
- **Rusk** speaks in corrections — he was a Keeper once and cannot stop editing the world: *Not drawn. Taken. There's a difference, and the difference is a man.*
- **The past life — Chicago voices** are faster, warmer, more profane, with Midwestern plainness and Italian-family volume underneath. Render them without phonetic spelling; give them rhythm and idiom instead.

No character explains the plot to another. Exposition is folded into a fight, a bargain, a warming of a stone. Subtext is the rule: what is not said is what the scene is about. Dialogue tags are usually *said*; the elaborate verb is a treat, not a habit.

## Combat and magic in this voice

Never game-like. No numbers, no bars, no "cast," no list of spells. Ranks are social facts spoken by other people, earned slowly and paid for visibly.

Write the Kept Art as an exact transfer with three parts: **the source, the path, the destination.** Where the warmth comes from, how it travels, where it ends up, and what leaks to the Hoar on the way. A drawing is described by its physical signs: the room's breath going white, the frost creeping up the inside of a window, the sour-sweet taste in the mouth, the way a coat freezes where it touches a wall, the red mark on the source's skin where warmth was taken. The cost is always named. If a boy is warmed, someone else is colder; say who, and say what happens to them.

Combat is brief and ugly and followed by the long undignified interval. A knife goes in; there is a sound; then there is bleeding, shivering, and the business of stopping it. Dan's modern knowledge makes him good at that business and at nothing else — he can clamp an artery and will still lose the duel because his hands shake. Write the shake. Never make a fight elegant, and never let a victory arrive clean.

## This saga's image-families — new, none from the reference

- **The Hoar and the kept coal.** Cold is the world's default and its memory; warmth is a stolen, stored, countable thing. Images: rime feathering a door-lintel, the click of a bank-stone taking heat, the sour-sweet smell of a coal near its end, a warm room seen from outside at night like an animal's den. A kept coal is a life held back from the cold by one act of theft.
- **Breath and its white ghost.** In Sarn, breath is visible — a small cloud that leaves a body and is gone. It is the cheap, constant tax of being alive and the sign of the last moment: the breath that goes up and does not come back. (Imagery of mortality only; never a technique, never a path to power.)
- **The Melt and what it uncovers.** Truth is seasonal. The thaw brings up bodies, old roads, bogs, kept stones, unmarked graves. Layers — ice-rings, peat-strata, the brown water in a boot-print — are how the world remembers, and Dan reads them the way a pathologist reads a wound.
- **The Spent and the Kept written on the body.** Warmth is written on skin: the ruddy faces and soft hands of the Kept, the blue nails and scrofulous cough of the Spent, the branching red map of a bad drawing, the dull eye of a man whose coal has gone out. Bodies are evidence. Nobody needs a ledger; they have faces.
- **Iron and the cold bite.** Iron drinks warmth faster than flesh. Cold iron rings, door-bands and knives are the tools of the Keeper's trade and the poor man's defence; a cold ring laid on a coal kills it. Iron is the anti-warmth metal, the thing that makes theft physical.
- **The birthmark and the two fires.** A branching burn-map on Sten's ribs that he did not get in this life, matched later on Orin Casset. The old life written on the new body; proof that a wound can travel farther than a man.

Let a motif lie dormant for fifty chapters and then return changed. That is how the reader learns to read this book.

## Words and habits to avoid (inherited, unchanged)

Never use: *a testament to, little did he know, shivers down his spine, a tapestry of, in that moment, it was as if time stood still, the weight of the world, a dance of, unbeknownst*. Avoid also: *suddenly* (at most once a volume), *somehow*, *seemed to*, *began to*, *felt a lump in his throat*, *a faraway look*, *like a parent to a child*, *that was when he knew*, and any sentence beginning *And then* twice in one page.

No stacked adjectives. No repeated sentence openings across a paragraph. No character explaining what the reader already knows. No anachronistic slang in the fantasy world — no *okay, guys, whatever, system, level, quest, skill tree*. The Court may say *record, kept, spent, arrears of warmth*; these are its own cold words and are deliberate.

Avoid modern simile in the second life. A face is not like a screen unless Sten has seen a screen, and in Sarn he has not. A sound is not like a siren. In remembered Chicago the opposite holds: the old life's machines are part of the texture and may be named.

Never say a thing is *ironically* or *symbolically* anything. Let the coal be a coal.

## Three sample paragraphs — not part of the story

*These demonstrate the voice. They are not scenes and must never be filed as story text.*

**I — daily life in the new world.**

> The stone had been warming since before \place{Grath} woke, and still \person{Sten} could not keep his fingers from it. It sat in the ash at the edge of the hearth, a common grey bank-stone with a long crack down one side, and his mother had rubbed tallow into the crack every Still since he was born, the way other women rubbed a child's chest. Snow sifted through the turf roof and lay in a soft line on the floor inside the door. His mother did not seem to see it. She was counting coals into a box, and each coal made a small sound, like a knuckle tapped on wet wood, and when she had six she covered them with cloth and said, “That's the week, then,” as if a week were a thing a body could hold. Sten wanted to tell her that six would not do, that the draught under the door would eat two of them by morning and the cough would take the rest by the Melt. He was four years old and the words came out as a breath that fogged and went. She put her hand on his head. It was a warm hand. That was all she had to give, and she gave it.

**II — the past life breaking through (note the tense-shift and `\memory{...}`).**

> The taste came first: diesel, and the sweet rotten-egg stink of a fuel line, and under it the metallic flatness of a February mouth. \memory{Medic, we've got a patient on the roof. Medic?} The cab is on its side. The snow is up to the door handle. He is doing something he has done a thousand times — find the airway, find the bleed, find the end of the problem — and his hands know the order even though his head has already left it, because the van's engine is cooking and there is a woman inside still moving. \memory{Nina, I'm gonna call you back. I swear.} His phone is lit on the passenger seat, the screen going grey in the cold, and the call is the one thing in the world he cannot put down and cannot pick up. The light is sodium orange on the snow. Then it is not sodium anything, and the cold he knows is a different cold, and the only clear thought is: I have left the job unfinished.

**III — violence and magic.**

> \person{Halven Rusk} took the warmth from the boy's leg without looking at the boy. He laid a cold iron ring on the skin and drew, and the room's breath went white all at once, the way a mirror clouds, and frost ran up the inside of the window in branching lines that reminded Sten of the mark on his own ribs. The boy made one sound. It was short and went up at the end. Rusk did not stop. There was a red branching place on the boy's shin where the warmth had left, and his foot went from blue to the colour of a dead fish's belly, and he began to shake so hard his teeth clicked against the cup someone had given him. “A drawing,” Rusk said, and set the ring on the table, where it took the heat out of the air around it. “Not a cure. There's a difference, and the difference is a man.” He wiped the ring on his sleeve and went to the door. Outside, the snow was coming down over Grath, and someone else's window went dark, two streets over, at the exact moment the boy stopped shaking.
