# Peoples, Names and Customs

*A physician of Veyford wrote, in AR 389, that Cindermere has no such animal as a human being, only vessel-bearers of several descriptions, all of whom leak. The book was not widely read and is not often quoted. What follows is the common account, with the local particulars kept separate.*

---

## The peoples of the world

There is no single people of Cindermere. The old genealogy in the Synod vault divides all persons into **seven vessel-kindreds**, but the border surveyors counted nine and the Weald-born refuse to be counted at all.

- **The March-folk** (clay-bloods) — the majority of the Clay March and the river villages. Short, sturdy, broad in the hand, with clay ground permanently into the knuckles. They are called slow by the Wold and honest by themselves. Wren Sedge is of this kindred.
- **The Ond-born** (Ledger families) — pale, narrow-shouldered, careful of their fingers. Generations of indoor work and repeated Mending have made them a people of fine hands and watchful eyes. The very greatest families have had the seam-mark removed so often that their palms are smooth as paper.
- **The Harrow** (Wold-men) — tall, fair, heavy-boned, loud in argument and silent in pain. They do not trust a man who will not look at a horse before he looks at a person.
- **The Canton folk** (salt kin) — bleached by the flats, squinting, with a particular way of holding the breath at low water. Their dead are not Mended, and they are the only people who speak of the lake as though it could hear.
- **The Weald-born** (the Gloam) — a dim, long-limbed people of the western forest. They cast faint shadows at noon, which the surveyor of AR 312 noted and the Synod stamped *no consequence*. Their true names are not known to any outsider.
- **The Harbour folk** — a mixed seafaring people, dark from the Cinder Sea, who speak Trade in every port and something else at home. They are the only people who consider a receipt a kind of prayer.
- **The Rifters** — not a kindred but a variety: the broken, the hired, the scavengers of the Rift Marches. They are made rather than born.
- **The Silt-born** — said to live under the salt shelves and to be very patient. No surveyor has measured them. The canton songs say they are the ones the lake did not keep, and that they are more numerous than the living.
- **The Culls** — not a people, though they are treated as one. A Cull is a person who has been Mended so often, and beaten down so thoroughly, that only fragments of the original remain. They are the House's labour. They speak in broken pieces with the formal phrases of the Ledger breaking through, as a cracked pot still holds a little water. Whether a Cull is a person, a possession, or an account is a question the House has never had to answer in public.

## Languages

- **March speech** — broad-vowelled, slow, proverb-heavy. A clay-marcher addresses the ground, the pot, the fire, and only then other people. It has no word for *mourning*; grief is described as a thing that has not been swept. Wren thinks in this tongue and remembers in another.
- **Sharp Script** (Ledger Ond) — the formal hand of the House, carved over its door and stamped on its coin. It is written left to right in strokes that end in smallhooks, like staples. Ordinary Ond folk can read a price and an instruction and no more. The sharp script is also found on the Rift scrap of AR 362, where no scholar can read it.
- **Wold tongue** — guttural and short, rich in iron, frost and kinship. It has eleven words for snow that are all the same word said with different feelings.
- **Canton trade** — salt jargon with a sign-language used at low water, when saying a name aloud is thought to be dangerous. Cantons are the only people who can bargain without speaking.
- **Weald Cant** (Gloam speech) — a language of half-words and trailing endings; the Weald-born claim it predates the Ash. Speakers say that a sentence begun in it can be finished years later by someone else.
- **Harbour Trade** — a merchant patois, understood from Verran to Veyford. It is the language of bargains and therefore of lies told politely.
- **Rift slang** — a thieves' cant full of trade names that change every season.
- **The Old Script** — the unreadable sharp-angled writing on the Ond florin and the typeset scrap. Scholars call it Old Script for want of a name. No Cindermere person can read it and none admits it matters.

## Naming conventions

Names in Cindermere are not decoration. They are the part of a person that the Ledger keeps when the body is returned, and getting one wrong is an offence in nearly every realm. The forms below are the ones to use. Keep them consistent; a name invented in one chapter must mean the same thing in the thousandth.

### Clay March (Wren Sedge's people)

A **given name** from a small pool of short, useful things — birds, plants, tools, weather — followed by a **house-name** taken from a trade, a place or an ancestor. There is no rigid patronymic, but the formal style uses *-daughter* or *-son*: a person may be "Wren Hob-daughter of Kilnwick" in court and "Wren Sedge" everywhere else. Spelling is what the clerk makes of it.

- Given names: **Wren, Hob, Maud, Tamsin, Wat, Garrick, Dilla, Ebba, Fen, Joss, Bee, Aldis, Rook** *(Nell is a name of the other shore and is not given to March children)*.
- House-names: **Sedge, Clayholt, Mire, Barrow, Rook, Eelhook, Thistle, Wick, Garr, Ashby**.
- Examples: **Hob Sedge** (Wren's father, clay-digger); **Maud Rook** (her mother, washerwoman, born at Barrow Wick); **Wren Sedge** (their daughter); **Tamsin Barrow** (midwife); **Garrick Mire** (clay-reeve); **Wat Eelhook** (fisher at Eelhook); **Bee Clayholt** (potter); **Dilla Rook** (Maud's sister); **Joss Thistle** (wheelwright); **Ebba Wick** (the reeve's wife).

### Ond and the House of Seams

A **given name** plus a **seat-name**; the seat-name is not inherited so much as assigned by the Ledger and may be changed by the Synod. Officials are addressed as *Master* or *Mistress* plus the seat-name alone. A person Mended more than once gains a second name in the Ledger, written "Name-Second," "Name-Third," and so on; the suffix is a mark of standing among the great and a mark of shame among the small.

- Given names: **Elias, Ildra, Corvin, Sera, Odel, Regis, Hester, Aldis, Marten, Vale, Ysuld**.
- Seat-names: **Thorne, Voss, Halloway, Vane, Marche, Fenn, Dorne, Correy, Wren** *(not to be confused with the March child), **Seam**.
- Examples: **Elias Thorne**; **Ildra Voss** (a veiled priest, no face on file); **Master Halloway** (Riveter of record; no first name is entered anywhere); **Sera Vane** (Factor of the Third Ledger); **Odel Marche** (chief clerk); **Corvin Fenn** (Riveter of the Cinder Quays).
- Culls are entered by **number**. The given name is struck through and a number written above it: "44," "19-Sedge," "the second of the Bird." The struck name is kept in a separate file. Culls do not know theirs.

### The Wold

A **given name** and a **clan**, often said "given of the clan": a person is "Sigrith of the Rime" in company and "Sigrith Harrow-daughter" to her kin. Clan names come from iron, weather and kin, never from trades. A Wold person who has been Mended is given a **wooden ring** and thereafter uses no clan name at all.

- Given names: **Haldren, Sigrith, Ovek, Runn, Ysuld, Garr, Tor, Eithne, Brand**.
- Clans: **Harrow, Rime, Bone, Iron, Frost, Thane, Wold**.
- Examples: **Sigrith of the Rime**; **Haldren Harrow-son**; **Ovek of the Iron**; **Ysuld Runn-daughter**; **Brand of the Frost**.

### The Salt Cantons

A **given name** plus the **flat** a person was born on. Names pass through the mother; a child takes its mother's flat-name. At low water a canton will sometimes use a **dead-name** instead, a name abandoned after a wrong, and will not say which is which.

- Given names: **Nara, Orrin, Dun, Vess, Hara, Kell, Mira, Oss** *(Rill is a name of the other shore and is never used on the flats)*.
- Flat-names: **Salt, Crye, Wane, Dun, Husk, Mere, Low-Shelf, Grey-Reach**.
- Examples: **Nara Salt of Crye's Flat**; **Orrin Crye of the Low Shelf**; **Dun Wane of the Grey Reach**; **Vess Husk**; **Hara Mere**; **Kell of the Low Shelf**.

### The Nine Harbours

A **given name** and a **house-name**, used with *of House*: "Valen Correy of House Correy." A person's credit is entered under this name in the harbour Ledger. Women keep their house-names after marriage and add the other house's name after a slash. A receipt carries the full form.

- Given names: **Valen, Iona, Sera, Marten, Hobb, Fenn, Dorne, Isen, Brekk, Orewell, Tamsin**.
- House-names: **Correy, Brekk, Dorne, Vale, Isen, Marche, Orewell, Fenn, Verran**.
- Examples: **Valen Correy**, **Iona Brekk**, **Sera Dorne of House Dorne/Vale**, **Marten Isen**, **Hobb Vale**, **Orewell of Verran**.

### The Gloam Weald

Every person has a **true name** given at birth and never spoken aloud, and a **common name** taken from something done, at sixteen. To learn someone's true name is a serious gift or a serious theft. Outsiders are given a common name immediately and are not told it has been chosen for them.

- Common names: **Thist, Yspe, Brier, Moth, Nettle, Wisp, Rood, Ash, Hollow, Fen**.
- Examples: **Gloam-mother Yspe**; **Thist of the Hollow**; **Brier the Listener**; **Moth**; **Nettle-Rood**; **Wisp of the Weald**.

### The Rift Marches

Given names are discarded in favour of a **nickname** describing the worst thing survived or the trade followed. Nicknames change when the wearer does. Formal documents in the Rift are therefore nearly useless.

- Examples: **Scrap-Jen**, **Pike**, **Rattle**, **Stub**, **Half-Flare**, **Kiln-Jen**, **Copper-Nell** *(Nell is never used; see the March note)*.

### A rule for the author

A person's name is a small, exact thing. Use the canonical form on first mention in a scene, inside the indexing macros. Use the form the character would actually hear in dialogue. A Cull's number is never spoken by the Cull. Master Halloway is always *Master Halloway* in Ond and *Halloway* in private. Ildra Voss has no given name on file, and no one has ever successfully asked her for one.

## Class structure

- **Ond:** Synod (twelve) and Seam-Master; Factors and senior Riveters; clerks and working Riveters; free householders; debt-servants; Culls. A person's class is his account, and the account can be changed by a clerk in an afternoon.
- **The Clay March:** reeve; potters and clay-diggers; fishers and washers; seasonal debt-servants; the very old and the very young, who are not counted as workers. There is no hereditary class and very little hereditary wealth; the kilns and the wash-house pass from mother to daughter in some families and are held in common in others.
- **The Wold:** crown; thanes; freemen (each with a tool and a weregild); thralls (few, household-bound). A Mended Wold-man drops out of the structure entirely and wears a wooden ring.
- **The Salt Cantons:** Cryers; flats-families; those who have lost their names; outsiders. A stranger cannot become a canton person, though he may live on the flats for forty years.
- **The Nine Harbours:** Factors; house-merchants; factors' clerks and shipmasters; sailors; slaves. Credit, not birth, decides the order; a ruined house falls in a season.
- **The Gloam Weald:** Gloam-mothers; Weald-born; those who have forgotten their true names (pitied, not punished).
- **The Rift Marches:** company lords; hired men; captives. A captive becomes a hired man by surviving long enough to be paid.

## Gender customs

The Clay March is practical about gender. Women dig clay, take in washing, mind midwifery, and in some households fire the kilns; men haul, fish, and build; the washing-line and the wash-house usually pass down the female line. No one in Kilnwick finds this remarkable. Ond keeps no gender in its Ledger at all — a soul is entered as a vessel, neither man nor woman — and this exactness is one of the coldest things about the House. The Wold expects women to fight, smith and rule and considers a man who will not take an opinion from a woman half a person. The Cantons pass names through mothers and will not let a man speak a dead-name at low water. The Harbours allow women to hold bonds in their own names, which is why the wealthiest Factors in Verran are often women. The Weald is governed by mothers and nobody there thinks it worth commenting on. In the Rift Marches nobody has time to notice.

Across all realms, the one near-universal custom is this: the person who *mends* is honoured above the person who *breaks*, regardless of sex. A woman who sets a true staple is a person of consequence in the March, and the House recruits such women carefully.

## Coming of age

- **Clay March — the clay-mark, twelve.** A child is given a mark at the wrist in clay-slip and counted a worker. The mark washes off in a year; the status does not. A child of twelve can be sent to the pits.
- **Ond — the Sealing, sixteen.** Every citizen is Mended for the first time. The poor are done in a line in the Cull Warrens yard; the rich are done in the House chapel with a fine staple and a feast. Everyone receives the seam-mark, whether or not they wanted it.
- **The Wold — the Iron Take, sixteen.** A young person is given a tool and told to make something that will outlast him. The thing is kept and shown at his death.
- **The Salt Cantons — the Naming of the Flat, fourteen.** The child is taken to low water and told the name of the flat. A name spoken on that day is kept for life.
- **The Nine Harbours — the Line, eighteen.** A line of credit is opened and a set of weights given. A young person who has no credit is not yet an adult in the eyes of the harbour.
- **The Gloam Weald — the Common Name, sixteen.** The birth-name is set aside and a name is taken from a deed. The old name is never said again by anyone who loves the person.
- **The Rift Marches** — not celebrated. It is simply noted that the person is now worth hiring.

## Death rites

- **The Clay March — the upside-down pot.** The body is laid under a cairn of kiln-wasters and a broken pot is set upside down over the face. Hearthrite words are said to the fire and the threshold. The grey silt in the house is swept out on the day after the funeral and not before. A cheap Mending may be bought at Veyford; most families let the dead go unmended and say the pot holds.
- **Ond — the Mending and the Sealing.** The body is taken to the House, the quick is drawn, the staple is set, and a new vessel is prepared. The account is carried forward. The year's dead are Mended together at the Sealing. A person who was Mended finely enough may have the seam-mark removed and be entered again as new.
- **The Wold — the Quiet.** No Mending. The body is burned with iron, and the tool made at the Iron Take is laid on the pyre. A person who has been Mended wears a wooden ring and is burned apart, in a different place, without a name spoken.
- **The Salt Cantons — the low-water name.** The dead are named once, at low water, from the shore, and left to the lake. A name spoken a second time is said to call something up. Cantons are therefore careful with the dead and angry at being asked to be more careful.
- **The Gloam Weald — the Bone Orchard.** The body is hung in the trees, where the pale shapes are said to be Names waiting to be called. The common name is spoken once; the true name is not spoken at all.
- **The Nine Harbours — the receipt.** The dead are given a receipt for their debts, which is sealed and filed. A rich person's receipt is read aloud. A poor person's is put in a drawer.
- **The Rift Marches** — whatever the company does. Usually the weapon is buried with the person and the nickname is crossed off the roll.
- **The Un-Mending** — the worst punishment in the known world. The soul is not repaired. It is left to the silt. No family will speak the person's name, and in Ond the account is closed with a single word written in a clean hand: *owed*.

## Crime and punishment

- **The Clay March — restitution.** Pay for what you break, or mend it. A broken pot is mended or paid for in kind; the honesty of the repair matters more than the loss. A man who mends a neighbour's jug without entering the break in the account is fined three groats (AR 322). Theft of a staple is six groats. There is no prison; the stocks at Veyford are for people who will not pay, not for people who cannot.
- **Ond — arrears.** An offence is a debt entered in the Ledger, with a tenth added at each Sealing. Striking a Riveter is forty florins. Taking seam-metal is a hundred. Refusal to pay leads to service in the salt quays, then to the Cull Warrens, then to the Un-Mending. Punishment is administered by a clerk who never raises his voice. The violence is grammatical.
- **The Wold — weregild.** A price is set on every bone and every insult, paid in iron. A man's full weregild is a good horse and thirty groats' weight of worked iron. A person who cannot pay is held until his kin does.
- **The Salt Cantons — the taking of a name.** The wrongdoer's given name falls out of use until the wrong is paid. This is considered worse than death, and the cantons are generally believed to be right.
- **The Nine Harbours — the bond.** Anything written and sealed is binding, including a bond of service. Fraud is punished by the loss of credit, which in Verran is a form of death.
- **The Gloam Weald — the return of names.** A wrong is settled by taking a name and giving it back when the wrong is righted. The Weald-born keep a debt against the House that has not been returned in four hundred years.
- **The Rift Marches — the contract.** A company enforces its own agreements. Punishment is short, final, and unwitnessed.
- **On Mending as punishment:** in every realm except the Wold and the Weald, a repeat offender may be Mended without consent. This is called *correction*. It is done cheaply, in bulk, and the offender is not asked. Among the poor of Ond it is an ordinary Tuesday.
