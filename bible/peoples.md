# The peoples of Sarn

*This account records how Sarnish folk name themselves, how they count their ranks, and how they bury their dead. Names are set down with care, because a name in Sarn is a vessel of a kind: it holds a person, and a person returned thin may find it does not hold as well as it did.*

---

## I. Peoples and races

### The Sarnish stock

The common people of the bowl are one stock, divided by country and trade rather than by blood. A Grathic clay-hand and a Vhaleni salt-master can have children together, and the children are simply of whichever hearth raises them. What separates them is the tongue, the food, and the way they hold a slate. The six great cultures are:

- **Grathic** — the clay country, river valleys, peat and kilns. Low, guttural speech, short names, long grudges.
- **Vhaleni** — the Sallow ports. Sibilant, practical, salt-soaked; a people of contracts that rot in the rain.
- **Keldish** — the high Rim. Clipped, numerical, suspicious of warmth they did not dig out of stone.
- **Sere** — the southern empire. Long vowels, open courtyards, public feasting, and the largest slave population in the world.
- **Fenfolk** — the peat and reed country. Slow nasal Cant, boardwalk villages, cheap returns.
- **Wastelings** — the cold outside. A handful of borrowed words and no grammar worth the name.

### The Vorr

The Vorr are not Sarnish and do not claim to be. They are tall, slow, long-lived, and hold their measure in the bone rather than in the blood, which makes them difficult to collect and impossible to bank in the ordinary way. Their skin has the pale grey of old warm-stone and their fingers are doubled at the last joint. They do not undergo the Return. When a Vorr dies, the others seat the body upright facing the direction it came from and leave it. They say: *We were never owed.*

The Vorr keep the Listening Stones in the high Rim and sell nothing but numbers. They claim to be able to read the oldest slates, and have never been induced to translate one in full. The Court employs them as surveyors and watches them carefully. There are perhaps four thousand Vorr in the world, most of them in Keld, and almost none in Grath.

### The blent

Where Sere experiments with grafting beast-clay into human vessels, or the Court patches a wall with material taken from a donor of another kind, the result is called **blent**. Some blent pass as people. Some do not. They are strongest in the southern empire, where they are exhibited, and commonest in the back rooms of the Hearthseat, where they are made. The law says a blent person has the measure of the vessel its donor had, which means in practice that a blent person has no measure at all.

### The Kept

The Kept are not a people but a condition. There are fewer than twenty in Sarn. Their vessels have been made perfectly retentive, so that nothing leaks in and nothing leaks out. They do not feel cold, hunger, pain or affection. They are spoken of with pity and are generally left alone in rooms with good fires, which they cannot enjoy. The Unbanked intend to extend their condition to everyone.

## II. Languages and scripts

| Language | Where | Sound | Written on |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Grathic** | Grath, and as a common tongue everywhere | Short vowels, hard *k* and *thr*, little inflection | Slate with chalk; birch bark for accounts that must travel |
| **Ledgerhand** | The Warm Court | Grathic grammar with the verbs removed | Slate, in a neat upright hand; the only script all officers read |
| **Vhaleni** | Sallow ports | Sibilant, many tide-words, endings slurred | Salt-paper, which rots in damp |
| **Keldish** | The Rim | Clipped consonants, numbers before nouns | Tally cuts in bone and stone |
| **Sere** | The south | Long open vowels, elaborate politeness | Glazed tablet and paper with gold ink |
| **Fen Cant** | The Fenmarch | Nasal, slow, half-sung | Not written; marked sticks and knots |
| **Waste Cant** | Beyond the Rim | Grunts and borrowed Grathic | Nothing |
| **The Deep Hand** | Nowhere living | Unknown; the Vorr recognise the numbers | The oldest slates and the seam records |

Ledgerhand is a deliberately cold language. It has no word for *please*, no word for *sorry*, and eleven separate words for the different kinds of deduction. Its sentences are all in the passive: *the measure is banked*, *the return is recorded*, *the account stands*. Nobody in Sarn says such things at a fire. Officers say them at a table.

The Deep Hand appears on the oldest objects and on nothing else. It is taught in no school. When the Vorr look at it they say only *the numbers are correct*, which is not a translation.

## III. Naming conventions

### Grathic

A Grathic name has a **given name** of one or two syllables and a **house-name** taken from a trade, a place, or a material. A person is entered in the slate as *given plus house*. Foundlings receive a given name from whoever writes the birth-slate and no house-name until a hearth claims them. This is why **Corin**, written by a collector before she had seen his face, was simply Corin for the first years of his life.

Given names are often materials, tools or small domestic things: clay, ash, reed, stone, coal, a pot, a hand, a bird. They are not poetic. A Grathic child is named for what the household has plenty of.

| | Examples |
|---|---|
| Given names | Alda, Berr, Corin, Dessa, Ennit, Fenna, Garl, Hesta, Ivo, Jorra, Kess, Ludo, Mira, Nils, Orra, Rusk, Sella, Tarn, Ulda |
| House-names | Vail (kiln), Reed, Clay, Silt, Tallow, Marsh, Ash, Sill, Weir |
| Full names | Sella Vail, Jorra Vail, Alda Reed, Mira Silt, Tarn Vail, Hesta Clay, Rusk of the Weir |
| Foundlings | Corin, Ennit, Ulda — one name only, no house |

The Vail house of Low Kiln is a kiln-house, and has been Vail for four generations. The hearth-maid who attended Corin's birth was **Alda Reed**, who crossed herself at the birth-flower and told nobody what she had seen.

### Vhaleni

Vhaleni given names are short, open and usually taken from tides, winds, birds or salt. A house-name follows, taken from a boat, a quay or a trade. A Vhaleni who has no house-name is assumed to be a guest, which in Vhalen is a precarious condition.

| | Examples |
|---|---|
| Given names | Avis, Cella, Dolf, Eda, Gull, Havi, Jeva, Kett, Lona, Mav, Neri, Orro, Pia, Ravi, Tavi, Ulla, Veno |
| House-names | Wake, Hull, Quay, Brine, Sail, Salt, Net |
| Full names | Avis Wake, Kett of the Quay, Mav Hull, Dolf Brine, Neri Sail |

### Keldish

Keldish names are blunt and frequently begin with a hard consonant. The number of a man's measure is tattooed on his forearm and is considered part of his name; a Keldish person asked who he is will often give the number first. There are no house-names in the lowland sense. A person is known by the mine or the pass.

| | Examples |
|---|---|
| Given names | Arl, Bret, Caul, Dorn, Ekk, Farr, Gred, Hald, Ivar, Jarn, Karr, Lod, Marn, Orv, Pike, Ragn, Siv, Tord, Ulf, Vek |
| Designations | of the First Seam, of the Cold Pass, Iron, Rime, Vein |
| Full names | Siv of the First Seam, Tord Rime, Karric of the Cold Pass, Hald Iron |

### Sere

A Sere child may accumulate seven names by adolescence and keep whichever the household prefers. Names are long, vowel-heavy and often taken from heat, light or generous acts. House-names are grand and frequently false.

| | Examples |
|---|---|
| Given names | Alessa, Bastian, Dalia, Feria, Halia, Ilyra, Joren, Liria, Maris, Oriana, Rial, Solene, Talia, Valen, Zeri |
| House-names | Sunbowl, Ember, Gilded, of the Ten Bowls, Openhand |
| Full names | Alessa Sunbowl, Rial of the Ten Bowls, Ilyra Gilded, Valen Openhand |

### Fenfolk

Fen names are short, thick and usually nouns from the bog. A Fen person is known by a given name and a reed-name; the two are often the same word said twice. They dislike written names and will not sit at a table where a slate is open.

| | Examples |
|---|---|
| Given names | Bogga, Dib, Eech, Gorr, Hobb, Jow, Keg, Lull, Mudge, Nib, Ork, Pog, Quill, Rube, Slog, Tuss, Uck, Vobb |
| Reed-names | Peat, Reed, Eel, Mud, Rope |
| Full names | Mudge Peat, Bogga of the Reed, Slog Eel, Hobb Mud |

### Wastelings

Wastelings take a single word as a name and change it when it stops fitting. Nobody has a surname in the Waste. A Wasteling asked for a second name will usually point at the cold.

| | Examples |
|---|---|
| Names | Ash, Bone, Cold, Dregs, Grey, Husk, Knuckle, Loam, Mote, Nub, Pith, Rind, Skag, Thistle, Vole |
| Forms | Grey of the Waste, Husk, Knuckle Who Counts |

### The Vorr

Vorr names are long-vowelled and given one at a time, many years apart. A Vorr is known by the stone he keeps rather than by a family.

| | Examples |
|---|---|
| Names | Ao, Eru, Iyn, Oun, Uro, Aen, Oev, Itha, Elo, Unn |
| Forms | Ao of the Third Seam, Eru Who Counts, Iyn of the Listening Stone |

### The Court and the naming of officers

An officer of the Keeping has no house and no kin. On entering the service she is given a **slate-name**, which is entered on the rolls in Ledgerhand and is the only name she has. Some are one word, some two, and the second word is usually an office gone stiff: Varr, Rennick, Haldre, Marra.

| | Examples |
|---|---|
| Slate-names | Marra, Derren, Haldre, Rennick, Varr, Wessen |
| Titles set before | Steward Derren, Assessor Pell Varr, Collector Marra, Keeper Haldre |
| Rule | The account is her family; a question about her parents is a question about nothing |

### Slaves, foundlings and the returned

- **Slaves** are given a clay-name by the household that owns them and their old name is forbidden. In Sere the forbidden name is sometimes written on the inside of a bowl, which the slave is never allowed to look into. Grathish kept hands are usually named for the house that holds them: *the Vail's Tarn*.
- **Foundlings** receive a given name from the writer of the birth-slate. In Grath this is the hearth-maid or the collector, whichever arrives first. A foundling without a house-name cannot inherit a kiln, a boat or a debt, which is usually the point.
- **The returned** keep their name if the return was whole. If it was thin, the district's name-pot supplies a new one at birth and the old name is entered in a separate column of the slate that the family is not shown. A person banked five times may have five names and remember the wrong one.

## IV. Class structure

| Rank | Who they are | How they are spoken of |
|---|---|---|
| **The Kept** | Fewer than twenty; vessels made unbreakable by the Court | With pity, and from a distance |
| **Court officers** | Steward, assessors, collectors, bankers, keepers | *Clean hands*. They do not carry fuel |
| **Held** | Guild masters, factors, ship-owners, successful mercenaries | *A vessel that carries more than its share* |
| **Whole free** | Craftsmen, farmers, potters, would-be scholars | *A whole man*, meaning one who holds |
| **Thin** | Labourers, the returned poor, the walking thin | *Thin*, never to their face in Grath |
| **Clay-bound** | Debt-servants working off a term, commonly seven years | *Owed out*; their children are born to the debt |
| **Bought breath** | Chattel slaves; the owner holds their measure in law | *A kept hand* in Grath, *a bowl* in Sere |
| **The uncollected** | Those who die without being banked | Nobody. Their warmth goes into the Waste |
| **The Unbanked** | A secret faction inside the Keeping, not the same as the uncollected | Not spoken of outside sealed rooms |

A person may be high in one column and low in another. A Held merchant in Vhalen may be thin in the Court's books and clay-bound to a Sere factor. Sarn's classes are not a ladder but a set of overlapping accounts, and the accounts are never reconciled.

## V. Gender customs

- **Grath.** Both men and women throw clay and fire kilns. A widow can hold a kiln in her own right, which is called *a widow's mark* and is the only Grathish institution nobody argues with. The house-hearth passes to the eldest child regardless of sex; the slip-pits pass to whoever has the stronger hands. A woman who is banked thin may still keep the house but not the wheel.
- **Vhalen.** Boat shares pass through mothers. A Vhaleni man who marries into a boat-owning family is described as *come aboard*, and his opinion on the boat is counted at half. Sailors of either sex are common; captains are mostly women, because the sea does not care and the Council has learned not to either.
- **Keld.** Only men go down the deep mines, because a woman's measure is said to be *closer to the surface* and would draw the sweat out of the stone. Women do the counting above ground and hold the mine-reeve's keys in some families. A man who returns thin cannot descend, and is given the keys instead. This is considered a kindness and is not one.
- **Sere.** Inheritance goes to the eldest surviving child regardless of sex. Concubinage with returned slaves is open and is a public measure of a household's standing. A woman of a great house may own bowls, slaves and a husband's debt; she may not enter the counting room, where her numbers are read by a man.
- **Fenfolk.** Reeves are chosen by who can stand longest in a bog, and about half are women. A Fen mother names her children before the collectors do, which is the closest thing the Fenmarch has to a declaration of war.
- **The Vorr.** The Vorr do not divide work or inheritance by sex. They divide it by whether one has sat against a Listening Stone long enough to hear it, which takes most of a life and is not permitted to the impatient.

Across Sarn, a person who has been banked more than three times is considered an unsafe marriage partner, regardless of sex, because the Second Want may arrive in a child's body wearing the wrong face.

## VI. Coming-of-age

- **Grath, the First Firing, twelve years.** A child makes a pot, fires it, and knocks it with a knuckle. A clear note means the household holds a feast; a flat note means the child serves a year in the yard and tries again. Corin, being a foundling with no kiln of his own, will be judged on a pot made in the Vail yard.
- **Vhalen, the Salt Walk, fourteen.** The young walk the pans barefoot at low tide and name a tide they have never seen. Salt is considered a sufficient teacher.
- **Keld, the Descent, thirteen.** The young go down the mine for one day. Some come back with their number changed. A few do not come back, and the family is paid in iron.
- **Sere, the Bowl, sixteen.** A public feast. The young person empties a bowl of warm wine and names a debt he intends to carry. The applause is for the debt, not the drinker.
- **Fenfolk, the Reed Cut, twelve.** The child cuts a bundle of reed alone, in the dark, and brings it back without speaking. If the bundle is wet, the child is not yet of the bog.
- **The Vorr, the Long Sitting, forty.** The Vorr sit still against a stone for a season and listen. What they hear is not recorded, but their numbers change afterwards.

## VII. Death rites

**The common rite.** The collector arrives with a cold iron tube and a slate. The measure is drawn, booked, and deducted. The body is washed and laid with its feet toward the nearest water. A pot is broken at the door to let the heat out. A year later, the return is announced at the Weighing. The rich receive themselves. The poor receive a child who is faintly interested in a life it cannot name.

- **Grath.** Feet to the river. A broken pot at the door. The ash in the corner is left on the Last Day and burnt the next morning. A household that cannot pay the deduction keeps the slate and pays in labour, usually by a child sent to the kiln.
- **Vhalen.** The dead are sealed in a salted boat and pushed out on the ebb. The boat is watched until it is out of sight. A Vhaleni who dies at sea is considered already returned.
- **Keld.** The dead are walled into the mine, facing the deep seam. The mountain keeps its own, they say, and the surveyors add numbers to a slate that nobody reads aloud.
- **Sere.** Cremation in a public bowl, with wine and music. The warmest send-off in the world. The Court taxes the ash, and the family buys it back at the price of quiet ash, which is how a Sere funeral can ruin a house.
- **Fenfolk.** The body is laid on a peat raft and pushed into the reedbed. The mud keeps what it wants. Fenfolk do not object to thin returns; they expect them.
- **The Vorr.** They stop. The others seat the body upright facing the direction it came from and leave it uncovered. There is no slate, no deduction, no return. When asked why, a Vorr will say: *We were never owed.*

## VIII. Crime and punishment

| Crime | Common penalty | Who profits |
|---|---|---|
| Theft of goods | Compensation in marks, plus a day's labour for the moot | The wronged household |
| Theft of warmth from another's cup | Cold iron brand on the wrist; deduction from the thief's own measure | The Court |
| Refusing a lawful collection | *Unclosed account*: clip-held by the wrist until consent | The Court |
| Assault | Compensation for the damage to a vessel; a cut hand paid in marks | The injured person |
| Murder | The murderer is banked out; warmth divided to the victim's house; the killer returned thin as a debt-child | The victim's household and the Court |
| Bearing false slate | In Ledgerhand, *a false measure*: the hand is struck from the rolls and the fingers greyed by cold work | The Court |
| Speaking the Waste (denying the Keeping) | Fine, and a gag of ash worn for a market-day | The Court |
| Running from bought breath | Returned thin and set to the mills; in Sere, publicly exhibited first | The owner |
| Breaking a birth-slate | In Grath, a year in the clay yard and the household's name entered in a second column | The hearth-maids |

Sexual violence exists in Sarn as a fact of power, most openly in the southern empire and the Keldish mines, and most quietly in households where the victim has no account worth damaging. It is punished when it damages a household's standing, and usually not otherwise. It is not a subject for decoration. It is entered here because a reader should know what the law is worth before he is shown a court.

The cruellest penalty in Sarn is the one that sounds kindest: banking out. A murderer is not killed, because a life wasted is warmth wasted. He is emptied, returned thin to the household he wronged, and made to work the debt down. He may remember nothing of the crime. The debt remains. This is what the Court calls *nothing lost that we have kept*.
