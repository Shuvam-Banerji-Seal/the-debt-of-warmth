# Glossary of Sarn

*Entries for the reader. Every coined name set down in the Compendium so far is gathered here, in alphabetical order. Where a thing is known by different names in different countries, the common Grathic name is given first. A term whose deeper meaning is will be found in its own place in the Compendium; this glossary gives only what a traveller may hear in a market or a hearth.*

## A

**Account-day.** The tenth, twentieth and thirtieth day of every tend, when the slate is read aloud. In Grath these are also market-days. On Tallow 10, Y.H. 813 (Day 159), the first Account-day of the firing month, Berr Clay read *one in twelve, as the notice says* at the Cross, called the levy and denied it with *your house*, never *I*, and went off with the box locked and the true subtraction inside, believing he had done a kindness; a figure read aloud was not the figure paid. On Tallow 20, Y.H. 813 (Day 169), the second Account-day, he read the same figure again under the moot-post, called the levy and said it was not called by him, and sent a man with a number in his fist to a slate at the watch with *your house*, never *I*; the printed figure stood unchallenged, the true subtraction went back up the clay road in the locked box and Hessa Rale carried his narrow slate up the road edge-on without reading it. On Tallow 30, Y.H. 813 (Day 179), the third Account-day and the last market of the firing month, he read *one in twelve, as the notice says* for the third time, said *The levy is called. It is not called by me* and *your house*, never *I*, and went off toward High Kiln with the box locked and the true subtraction inside, believing he had done a kindness; the bell went four, four and a gap with nobody crossing himself, and a figure read aloud was still not the figure paid.

**A firing opens the kiln. A week of tending settles nothing.** A saying of the drying racks in Low Kiln, told after the first firing of a month: *A firing opens the kiln. A week of tending settles nothing. The hand goes to the mark before the fire, and to no shelf, no bed, no basket after it.* It is a rule about the widow's mark and a refusal to turn an opening into a doctrine: the first firing of Frost on Frost 2, Y.H. 813 (Day 271) was done with Sella Vail's hand to the mark for one breath and at no other hour, and through Frost 4–6 nobody made a lesson, sign, mending or payer of it. Four openings in four months are four and not a rule; the next stands in a later month's first firing, far off (T058/T079). Ch 74.

**The Afterdrop.** The worsening before recovery in a rekindled or rewarmed vessel. The ordinary cause is a graft settling; midwives call it the second chill.

**The Ash.** Grey silt that gathers where promises are unkept, in corners and under beds, invisible in daylight and plain by tallow-light. The poor leave it on the Last Day and burn it next morning. On Stone 29, Y.H. 812 a woman at the tubs told a girl beside her that the grey would be left where it lay until the Last Day and burned the morning afterward and called dealt with.

**Ash-mite.** A small grey mite that lives in the Ash and drinks the thin edge of a sleeper's measure. Invisible by day; troublesome in poor rooms.

**Ash-runners.** Criminal smugglers of the Fenmarch who move Warm and sell quiet ash without a Court licence.

**Ash Rising.** The rising in Low Kiln in Y.H. 668 after ninety children's warmth was taken for a private account; put down by Assessor-Marshal Pell Varr.

**Ash-sickness.** The condition of dreaming in another person's voice after long exposure to an Ash-wraith.

**Ash-wraith.** A grey shape seen by tallow-light where many have been spent; it follows the Ash ridge from room to room.

**Ask the arm that carried it.** A reed-cutter's answer to a girl at the Clay Yard who asked what a bundle held: *Ask the arm that carried it. It'll tell you.* The arm judges by the weight settling into the forearm and tells what was carried and not what it cost; the cutter lifted the next armful before a second question could be got out. It is the same answer as **A bundle's counted in the arm that carries it** in another mouth, filed by the boy on the tray of directions answered with directions. On Reed 7, Y.H. 813 the girl went off with the creel on her hip and no figure went with her. On Reed 13, Y.H. 813 a cutter told a girl with a creel at the Clay Yard to lift her bundle and let her arm tell her what went into it, *and that's the whole of what it knows*; to a man who asked what the work would want he gave the weather and the river, never a figure. See **A bundle's counted in the arm that carries it** and **A record kept in muscle**.

**Ask the pot.** An answer given to a question about cost in the Vail kiln-house of Low Kiln: *Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there. The house has said it.* The question is carried away held level, the way a woman carries a full pot, and arrives at a thing that has no answer in it; nothing is priced and nothing is refused. On Reed 4 and Reed 6, Y.H. 813 a woman asking what the reeds would cost now the hands were out of them was sent to the pot with these words, and a man asking what the beds would want was told to ask the beds and the weather. **Birth-flower.** The branching red mark some infants are born with, shaped like frost on a pane or a burn gone into the skin. Hearth-maids call it a flower, cross two fingers over it and say nothing about the sign. Alda Reed crossed herself over Corin's on Stone 7, Y.H. 812, and covered the act with the word.

**Birth-slate.** The flat slate a hearth-maid keeps within reach at a birth, on which the child and the day are entered. In Low Kiln it lies on the bench, and a midwife's hand goes to it before it goes to the child. A second hand may have written on it before anyone looked at the face.

**The Bowl.** The Sere coming-of-age feast at sixteen, where a young person empties a bowl of warm wine and names a debt they intend to carry.

**Bought breath.** A person sold for a term, usually a season or a year, to repay a debt. The common form of slavery in the Fenmarch.

**The Breaking.** The spring flood, when the Ilm comes up over the Fishstairs and takes the low houses; also the Silt thaw itself. The year's flood of Silt 9–15, Y.H. 813 came up in a thin brown sheet, stood on the Fishstairs and went around both the Vail corner and the burned ridge two doors down, then went off into mud; the district called it weather and then the thaw that was. It is not the great Breaking of later years, when the low houses are taken and the town's thin place is named.

**Brine-gull.** A Sallow-coast gull that steals anything warm: a loaf, a warmed stone, a coal not yet out. **Weighing.** The second of the Five, when the year's measures are read aloud and the rich learn how whole they are.
