# The first life — Daniel “Dan” Marchetti (private)

*Everything here happened on Earth, in the 2020s. It is the private property of the saga: nothing in this file is told to the reader at once. It surfaces in flashes, under `\memory{...}` for remembered words, and with the present-tense shift described in `voice.md`.*

## The man in brief

Daniel Marchetti was forty years old when he died and had been a surgeon for twelve. He worked the night shift in the trauma bay of a Level I centre on the near South Side of Chicago, where the bullets and the knife wounds and the crashes came in by the ambulance load, and where the job was not to save everyone but to decide, quickly and with a cold part of his mind he had trained for years, who could be saved and who could not. He was good at the work. He was bad at everything around it: marriage, fatherhood, sleep, silence, money, and the simple business of calling his daughter back.

He was a large man gone a little soft in the middle, with forearms like a butcher's and a left knee that clicked in the cold. He had a surgeon's hands — steady, short-fingered, with a pale line where a ring had been — and a face that people read as anger when it was only fatigue. His hair was going early at the temples. He shaved badly on post-call days and did not care. He smelled of hand sanitiser, old coffee, and the faint metallic tang of a hospital that has been cleaned too many times with the same mop.

## Hermosa, and the house on the block

He grew up in a two-storey brick bungalow in Hermosa, on a block where the el trains ran high and iron over Pulaski and the summer smelled of charcoal and wet pavement. His father, Aldo, was a machinist who had emigrated from a small town near Bari at nineteen and never learned to say what he felt in English or Italian; he showed it by fixing things. His mother, Theresa, worked part-time as a nurse's aide in a nursing home and came home with her uniform smelling of bleach and old people and lilac soap. There were three children — Dan, his brother Jimmy, who became a patrol officer and took after his father in the matter of silence, and a sister, Lisa, who left for Florida and did not come back often.

The house was loud. Everyone talked over everyone. There was always a pot on, always a relative in the kitchen, always a television murmuring in the next room with the volume too high. Sunday dinner was a duty and a pleasure and a trial: his mother making lasagne in a glass dish with the brown edge she considered the only true part, his father falling asleep in a chair after eating, his aunt saying something cutting in a dialect Dan understood but would never speak. From these people he learned volume, profanity, the habit of finishing everything on his plate, and the certainty that love is expressed by doing something practical for someone while refusing to discuss it. He would carry all four into a world where warmth is a material substance, and would be less surprised by that world than he should have been.

He was not a scholar. He went to Lane Tech because his mother insisted and because the school had a shop floor where he could take things apart, and he was good at biology and bad at everything that required remembering dates. At the University of Illinois Chicago he discovered the human body the way other boys discover engines — by taking it apart, being forbidden, and doing it anyway in the cadaver lab with a particular kind of excitement he would never quite lose. He liked anatomy because it did not argue. It had a right answer, and the answer was under your hands.

## The work, exactly

He trained at Cook County, the county hospital, where the trauma bay is a large room with ceiling-mounted lights and a trauma team that moves with the rehearsed speed of people who have done the same thing four thousand times. He became an attending. He worked nights — seven nights on, seven off — because nights paid more and because days were when the administration was awake.

What he knew, precisely:

- **Airway.** He could intubate a patient in the dark, in blood, with the patient vomiting. He could do a cricothyrotomy with a knife and a tube if the mouth was destroyed. He knew the feel of a difficult airway before he saw it.
- **Bleeding.** He knew the difference between blood that pulses and blood that wells, where to put a tourniquet and when a tourniquet is a lie, how to pack a cavity with gauze and hold pressure long enough for the body to catch up. He had done thoracotomies in the emergency department and knew how much blood a chest cavity can hold before the heart has nothing to pump. He knew that the third rib is the one that tears the lung, and why.
- **Bones.** He could reduce a dislocated shoulder, pin a femur, and set a forearm well enough that a patient would keep the hand. He knew that a deformed limb in the cold is worse than a deformed limb in a warm room, because the swelling has nowhere to go.
- **Burns.** He could estimate a burn by the rule of nines, calculate fluids by the Parkland formula, do an escharotomy when a chest could not expand. He had seen what fire does to skin and knew that the injury keeps arriving for days after the flame has gone.
- **Cold.** He knew hypothermia the way a priest knows sin. He knew the afterdrop, the moment a rewarming patient gets worse before getting better. He knew that a cold patient cannot clot, that a cold patient's heart will stop for no reason at all, that the grimace of severe cold looks like a smile. He knew frostbite by degree and had learned the hard way that you do not rub a frozen foot. He knew the exact temperature at which a patient becomes a problem you cannot fix.
- **Infection and dirt.** He knew sterile field, antibiotic choice, the smell of a wound that has turned, the fact that a clean-looking knife is not a clean knife. He knew how to irrigate, debride, and leave a wound open to close later.
- **Reading the dying and the dead.** A surgeon learns to look at a body the way a mechanic looks at a seized engine. He could tell a ruptured spleen from a bruised abdominal wall by the way a patient held their breath. He could tell how long someone had been dead by the lividity and the temperature, and he noticed, automatically and without being able to stop, the small evidence a body carries: the track marks, the old fracture, the fingernail split at the same angle on both hands, the wedding-ring tan line on a man who was not wearing a ring.
- **Triage.** He could walk into a room with twenty injured people and, in ninety seconds, sort them into the ones who would live, the ones who would live only if he chose them, and the ones who would die whatever he did. He had made that choice hundreds of times and had never once found a way to make it feel clean.

What he did **not** know mattered as much. He was a clinician, not a physicist: he had an intuitive, clinical sense of heat and loss — the body as a furnace that can be banked or bled — but no training in thermodynamics beyond what a bright undergraduate had once failed to find interesting. He could not make antibiotics. He knew penicillin existed and knew nothing about growing it; the idea of culturing a mould in a basement was the kind of thing he would have laughed at in a lecture and then, in Sarn, would try with terrible patience and partial success. He could not image a body without a machine. He could not anaesthetise anyone safely with what grows in a field. He knew no useful plants, no survival craft, no languages, no politics, no history before his own. He had never made a fire with his hands, never tracked an animal, never negotiated a price for food. He was an amateur boxer from his twenties — enough to keep his chin down and take a punch without panicking, nothing more. He was, in short, a man who knew exactly how a body fails and was almost helpless when the body in question was his own.

## Elena, Nina, Marisol

He married Elena Rossi in 2007, in a church in Elmwood Park with too many relatives and a photographer who cried. She was a teacher's aide, patient in a way he admired and could not imitate. For a few years it was good. Then the shifts ate the marriage: he was absent for the dinners, the birthdays, the small ordinary evenings that turn out to be the whole of a life. Their daughter **Nina** was born in 2000. By the time she was nine she had learned not to ask when he would be home, and by twelve she had stopped leaving a light on for him.

The divorce was quiet and worse for it. Elena did not rage. She simply stopped waiting. Dan moved into a small apartment near the hospital with a bed, a chair, a television, and a fridge containing four beers and a container of food he never ate. On the wall he kept a photograph of Nina at six, missing two front teeth, squinting in summer light on the edge of Lake Michigan. He looked at it every night without seeing it.

He had one later relationship, with **Marisol Vega**, an emergency-room nurse who was smart enough to see what he was and kind enough to try anyway for eleven months. She left because she was tired of being the woman who waits. Her leaving was temperate and final. He thought about her for years in the particular way a man thinks about a door he did not open.

Nina grew up. She went to college, then worked with animals somewhere outside the city, then moved. They spoke on birthdays and not otherwise. He kept meaning to call. He was always meaning to call.

## Des, Ostrowski, Vesci

His closest friend was **Deshawn “Des” Carter**, a paramedic with a great, unembarrassed laugh who had seen more of Chicago at 3 a.m. than most people see in a lifetime. Des knew Dan was falling apart and made jokes about it instead of asking. They had a ritual: after a bad shift they would sit in Dan's car in the hospital car park and eat bad pizza and say nothing for ten minutes. That was the whole friendship.

**Dr. Lena Ostrowski** had been his senior resident and then his mentor, a Polish-American woman with a voice like a file and a habit of correcting people by saying their full name. She taught him that the first duty of a surgeon is to decide, and the second is to carry the decision. She was the closest thing he had to a mother in the hospital, and when she retired he sent flowers and did not go to the party.

His rival was **Dr. Paul Vesci**, a plastic surgeon with a beautiful manner and a talent for taking credit. They had been friends in residency and then not. Vesci had once left a junior alone with a bleeding patient; Dan had reported it; Vesci had never forgiven him and spent fifteen years referring to Dan, in a warm, amused voice, as “the butcher.” Dan pretended not to care. He cared.

## The failure that stayed

When Dan was thirty-one, a twenty-two-year-old named **Tasha Broussard** came into the emergency department after a fight, was examined, had a normal blood pressure and a bruised abdomen, and was discharged with a diagnosis of sprain and instructions to return if she felt worse. She returned four hours later in cardiac arrest. A ruptured spleen the size of a fist had emptied her into her own abdomen. The junior doctor who had seen her first had missed the sign. Dan, who was covering the shift, had signed the chart without reading the nurse's note that said she had been vomiting.

At the review, the hospital called it a systems failure — a crowded department, an understaffed triage, a busy night. That was true. Dan let it be the whole truth. He said nothing about the nurse's note. He kept his job, his licence, his reputation, and a small, hard, private knowledge of the shape of his own conduct that he carried for the rest of his life and never confessed to anyone, not to Elena, not to Des, not to Nina. It became the thing he could not put down, the professional equivalent of the call he never returned. In Sarn, where every warmth is accounted for and every institution calls theft “keeping,” he will recognise the gesture instantly and be sickened by it, because he has performed it himself.

His father died of lung cancer in the spring of that same year, while Dan was on a thirty-six-hour shift. He learned about it in the car park afterwards, with the sun coming up over the hospital roof. He did not cry. He went to the funeral late, sat at the back, and noticed that the casket was slightly crooked in the aisle and that nobody corrected it. He thought about that for years, which is how he thinks about most things: as a small physical detail that should have been otherwise.

## Habits of mind

He counted without deciding to. Pulses per ten seconds, ribs under a hand, seconds since the last call, minutes until the next coffee, the number of steps from the bed to the door in any room he entered. He inventoried exits and load-bearing walls. He read bodies for evidence and people for the same, and he could not stop; at a family dinner he would note that his brother's knuckles were new and that his aunt had cut herself on a glass and wiped the blood on her skirt before anyone saw. He had a surgeon's tolerance for gore and almost none for mess. He finished everything on his plate, including food he did not like, because waste offended him in a way he could not explain.

His humour was fast, profane and withering, and he used it the way other men use a handrail. He was kind in specific, clumsy ways: a coffee bought at 4 a.m., a glove left in a pocket for a patient who would be cold in the recovery room, money slipped into a drawer for a colleague's rent with no note attached. He could not say the simple words. He could do the simple things, and did them, and this is what everyone who loved him remembered.

## The exact death

It was February, in a blizzard, on the expressway, at 2:17 in the morning. He had finished a double shift and was driving home in a snow that came down so thick the sodium lamps made orange tunnels of it and the wipers could not keep the windshield clear. On the radio, a dispatcher's voice said something about a pileup on the northbound lanes near the split. He did not remember deciding to stop. He remembered the flasher, the smell of antifreeze, the cars stacked at angles across the lanes like a child's game gone wrong, and a woman in a minivan still moving in the back seat.

He was working the scene the way he had been trained: airway, bleeding, the end of the problem. Snow went up his sleeves. His fingers stopped being reliable and he got angry at them. There was a fuel smell that turned, over several minutes, into something sweeter and worse. A man who had been helping him said something he did not hear. He went back to the minivan for the last time, reached through the broken window, and got the child out; and then the fire took the van and took him with it, a quick, enormous heat against a cold that had been there all night.

The last things he knew were orderless. The hiss of the fuel. The orange light on the snow. The vibrating weight of his phone in his coat pocket — a missed call, the screen lit, the name on it a thing he would not look at for another thirty years. The taste of metal. The heat, arriving from every direction at once, so much heat that it was like being held. The cold leaving his hands. A thought that had no words in it and was nevertheless a sentence: *I have left the job unfinished.*

He was wrong about the thirty years. It was less than a second before the warmth reached wherever it was going.

There had been a woman at the edge of the flare light, in a coat the colour of wet ash, holding a slate. He had noticed her the way he noticed exits: without interest, and with the certainty that he would not see her again. She had not come into the light. She had not touched the child. She had been writing, and the writing had not stopped when the van took him. What he could not have known, with the heat coming up through his sleeves and the cold leaving his hands, was that the warmth going out of him did not cool. It went into the child under the dashboard, and a thread of it stayed behind, too hot to settle, too tangled with an unpaid promise to be banked. In another world a woman in a grey coat would look up from her slate at that thread and write down a word she had not hoped to write in twenty years.

## What was left unfinished

The call. He had promised Nina he would call her on the drive home; he had meant to; he had even composed the first line of it in his head, something about the lake in the summer and a boat, a thing he had promised for nine years and never delivered. There was Tasha Broussard's chart, which he had signed and never reopened. There was a letter he had started to Elena and never sent, in a drawer in the apartment, containing one honest sentence. There was his father's casket, slightly crooked in the aisle. There was Des, who would get the call at 3 a.m. and would laugh about it for exactly one minute before putting the radio down. There was a half-read book on the bedside table and a coffee gone cold beside it.

All of it is finished now in the only world that knew what it meant. None of it is finished in him. He carries the whole ledger into an infant body in a country where no one has heard of Chicago, and where the only way to pay a debt is in warmth.

## Secrets of the first life that will matter

1. **Tasha's chart.** His private conviction that an institution can absorb a man's guilt and hand him back clean. He will recognise the Warm Court's accounting instantly and distrust his own reflections in it.
2. **The missed call.** It gives the saga its first `\memory{...}` line and its pattern: every important death in Sarn will arrive with something unsaid, and he will reach for a phone that is not there.
3. **The burn on his own body.** He dies with a branching red map across his ribs and forearm. In Grath that map is a birthmark — the only inheritance he brought, and the reason the midwife crosses herself and calls it a birth-flower. The woman with the slate writes his new name, Corin, before she has seen his face. It is the proof, when he finally sees the same branching pattern on another man's forearm, that the theft was real and that he is not the first.
4. **The boxer's chin.** Enough training to take a punch and wait. It will save him once and cost him once, and he will never be a fighter, only a man who knows what a fight feels like in the mouth.
5. **The clinical eye.** He reads a body faster than anyone in Sarn and has no explanation for it that he can give. This is the thing that makes people frightened of him and the thing that makes Rusk teach him. What they want is not his knowledge but his willingness to decide: a man who can look at a body and say *this one is gone; leave it* without saying a prayer first.
6. **His scientific habits.** He tests, records, repeats. In a world of sacred craft, method is heresy. He will do it anyway, in secret, with a charcoal stick and a slate, and this will eventually be the lever that moves the whole world.
7. **His contempt for his own comfort.** He worked until his body broke and called it duty. In Sarn, where comfort is stolen from the poor, this habit makes him dangerous to himself: he will accept cold the way a penitent accepts a hair shirt and will not notice that others are paying for it.

8. **The hot trace.** He died giving, not being robbed. Warmth that leaves a vessel in an act of giving does not cool into the bank; it stays hot and keeps the shape of the promise it was tangled with. This is what made him portable across worlds, and part of it is attached still to the child he pulled from the minivan.

9. **The missed call as mechanism.** The words of the call never cross. What crosses is their weight: a promise nine years unpaid, heavy enough at the moment his vessel broke to make him unbankable. The Warm Court calls such a man an unclosed account. A faction inside the Court has been looking for one for centuries.

## A sensory ledger of the first life

These are the details that will break through, whole and present-tense, at unlikely moments. Keep them exact.

- Sodium streetlight on wet snow, the orange so complete it makes the night feel indoors.
- The wipers: a wet slap, a wet slap, a rhythm you could set a watch by.
- Hospital corridors at 4 a.m.: the buffed floor, the smell of sanitiser, the vending-machine hum, the distant `sound` of a page over the speakers.
- The phone's glow under a sheet: a small cold rectangle of light with a name on it.
- Coffee from a machine that has not been cleaned: bitter, metallic, always too hot at the first sip.
- The el train over Pulaski in summer, the whole block shuddering, the smell of hot brakes and rain on dust.
- His mother's kitchen: oil in a glass dish, browned edges, the sharp smell of oregano from a jar with a faded green label.
- The trauma bay: the crack of a rib being forced back, the suck of a chest tube, the flat beep that means a heart has stopped and nobody has said so yet.
- A fan in a window in July, moving hot air from one part of a room to another and accomplishing nothing.
- Snow going up a sleeve, and the exact moment the cold stops being a sensation and becomes a fact about the body.

## The hinge

A man dies with too much warmth in him and wakes with too little. The last thing he sees is a fire; the first thing he will know in Sarn is a stone that has been warmed against a baby's belly and is already going cold. That is the hinge the whole saga turns on. Everything after it is a long, exact argument about who paid for the heat, and whether a life can be returned to the people it was taken from without destroying the man who is trying to return it.

The woman at the crash was Marra, a collector of the Warm Court, and the faction she serves is called the Unbanked. They did not make the blizzard and they did not put the minivan on the ice. They wanted a vessel that gave at the moment of breaking, and there was one, in a country they could not have named, on a night when a man who had spent his life deciding who was beyond help finally ran out of decisions to make. They paid for a whole return. They paid to have his memories left in him, which is the most expensive single thing a banker in Sarn can buy. The child in the minivan lived, and grew, and is still warm on a planet the Court has never heard of. To go back to her, Dan will one day learn, he would have to draw the warmth back out of the woman she became. That is the last account, and it cannot be settled by dying again.
