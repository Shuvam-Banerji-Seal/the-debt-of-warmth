# The Past Life — Nell Rill

**PRIVATE. This file is not printed. Everything here is canon, and much of it is a spoiler.**

---

## Era and place

She was born in the year 1847 in **Cauldwick**, a port town that does not exist on any map a reader will recognise, though it sits in the same cold northern latitudes as Hull and Leith and has the same weather: a low pewter sky, a wind that comes off the estuary with salt in it, and a fog that gets into the lungs and stays. The river that serves the town is called **the Wreak**, a broad tidal ditch of brown water that rises twice a day and has drowned the lower quay more often than anyone cares to count. Cauldwick smells of hot clay, glue, coal smoke, wet rope and, at low water, something sour and vegetable that the people of the town have learned not to name.

It is a town of brickworks, lead-glaze potteries, rope-walks, a glue factory, and two printshops. There is a great deal of work and very little of it pays. Women go into the potteries young and come out of them with blue lines at the gums. Men go into the brick dust and come out coughing. The town has the particular northern habit of making a joke of everything, including its own slow poisoning.

Nell Rill lived in two rooms above a cooperage on Silt Lane, within smell of the tide. She never left Cauldwick. She never saw a railway. She died there in the autumn of 1883, thirty-six years old, in the cellar of her own workshop, with her pockets full of brass staples and river water coming up the stairs.

---

## Family

**Josiah Rill**, her father, laid bricks for a living and died of the dust at fifty-one, when Nell was twelve. He was a quiet man with ruined hands and a habit of rubbing his chest as if he could wipe the ache away. He taught her how to hold a trowel and how to tell, by knocking on a wall, whether it had been built honestly. That skill — the ear for truth in a thing that has been put together — never left her, and in the new world it will be the first sign that something is wrong with her.

**Sarah Rill**, her mother, took in washing and drank when she could afford it and was not cruel when she was sober. She died at forty-four of a winter fever and a lifetime of other people's cold water. Nell was nineteen. There was a washing line in the yard behind their house, and the sheets on it snapped in the wind like sails. Nell could never hear a sheet snap afterwards, in either life, without being nineteen again. Her feeling for her mother was a braid of love and resentment that she never untangled, and after the second life begins it will be years before she can think of the woman without shame at the relief she felt when the fever took her.

**Christopher Rill**, called **Kit**, her brother, was nine when their father died and eleven when their mother did. Nell raised him. She fed him, scrubbed him, taught him his letters from the backs of old invoices, and made him a coat out of a cut-down skirt of her own. He was a thin, solemn boy with a talent for whittling: he carved small wooden birds out of offcuts, no longer than a thumb, and gave them to people he liked. He went to sea at sixteen on a ship called the *Marrow*, out of Cauldwick, bound for the Baltic timber ports. Letters came for a year and then stopped. He was nineteen when Nell drowned. Whether he was alive then, she never knew. That not-knowing is the hook on which half the saga hangs: in Cindermere he is alive, and the House has his name on file.

There were no other relations worth the name. There was an aunt who wrote once and did not write again, and a cousin who died in the glue factory when a vat burst. The poor in Cauldwick were not so much orphaned as thinned.

## Her work, and the skills that crossed over

At fourteen Nell was apprenticed to an old man named **Pell**, who mended crockery for the neighbours. The trade was called **riveting** or **stapling**: when a pot or jug or dish cracked, you drilled small holes on either side of the fault and drove a U-shaped staple of brass or iron across it, hammering the legs flat against the inside of the vessel. Done well, the staple held and the pot carried water again. Done badly, it showed. A repaired pot was never made new; the staple and the dark line of the crack stayed visible, and a woman who cared about her cups could tell at a glance that they had been broken and was not ashamed of it. Nell loved that about the work. She used to say that a pot which pretends it was never broken is a liar, and that a pot which holds water honestly is better company than most people.

She learned to hear a crack before she saw one. Tap a sound vessel and it rings clear and full; tap a cracked one and the sound dies flat in your hand, like a coin dropped on wet boards. She learned to set a staple so that it sang when it seated — one bright small note, no more — and she trusted that note more than her eyes. If the staple did not sing, she pulled it out and did it again. People thought this fussiness odd. It was the whole of her competence. In Cindermere, centuries and a world away, that note is the sound of a true seam, and she is the only person alive who can hear it.

She also worked with **leaded glass**, repairing the small coloured windows in the chapels and the front rooms of the more respectable houses. She cut the lead came, cleaned the channels, reset the glass, and soldered the joints. The work gave her the first taste of the lead that would help to kill her: a sweet, sickly sweetness on the tongue when she licked her fingers, and after a few years a thin blue line along the gum where the tooth met the flesh. She knew what the line meant. Everyone in the trade knew. They rubbed their gums and made jokes about it.

For extra money she **wrote letters** for people who could not write: to sons at sea, to wives in other towns, to the Board, to landlords, to the dead in the formal little phrases that the living use when they want to pretend the dead can still be addressed. She had a good, plain hand and a feeling for what a person meant rather than what they said, and she would sit at her table in the lamplight while a neighbour dictated and the kettle went cold, and she would write the letter the neighbour actually wanted, which was usually not the one they had come in to dictate. This habit — of hearing the unsaid word and putting it down — becomes, in the new world, the thing the House of Seams most fears in her and most needs.

She could read a little Latin from the labels on the apothecary jars, and she could read the strange script on the coin Ildra Voss gave her only as shapes, not as words. That impossibility haunted her. It will be the first overt clue that other worlds are real.

## Love — Elias Thorne

**Elias Thorne** was a typesetter at the larger of the two printshops, a narrow young man with ink permanently in the creases of his fingers and a habit of straightening the cuffs of a coat that had no cuffs to straighten. He set type all day in a room that smelled of hot metal and ink and put the same care into a line of worthless election notices that a jeweller would put into a stone. Nell met him because she mended the window in the printshop and stayed to watch him work, and because he was the only person in Cauldwick who asked to see the staples in a repaired pot rather than turning it over to hide them.

They were together six years, from her twenty-eighth to her thirty-fourth. They never married; there was never money, and Nell was vaguely afraid of the formal word as if it were another kind of staple. They lived in the two rooms above the cooperage, and at night, after the press stopped, he would come in smelling of ink and set a line of type on the kitchen table to show her — a line from a poem, or a scrap of the news, or something he had made up himself. She kept each scrap in a tin beneath the bed. There were more than a hundred.

He changed in the last year. He began to speak of the crack. He said that everything in Cauldwick had a fine hairline fault in it, and that if you listened at the right moment you could hear the town groan. He said it half as a joke, in the northern way, but his hands shook and he could not set a clean line. One morning he did not come to the printshop at all. There was a fire there that night — a trayside fire, the papers said, from a carelessly dropped lamp — and Elias was listed among the men who did not get out. The body was never identified. Nell kept the tin of scraps under the bed and did not look at it for two years.

On the last scrap he ever set, in the week before he vanished, there is a line of type in a script Nell did not know: four words, sharp and angled, which she could not read. She carried that scrap in her apron pocket until the day she died. In Cindermere, four words in that same script are carved above the door of the House of Seams: *What is broken is owed.* Elias did not die into nothing. He crossed first, and he has been building the Grand Seam ever since, and he does not know — or will not know — that the woman he loved is coming.

Their love is the slowest reveal in the saga. It must be planted in objects and habits and in the small exact details of typesetting long before it is spoken.

## Enemies, debts and the shape of ordinary cruelty

**Mr. Vetch** owned the glue factory and half the houses on Silt Lane, including Nell's two rooms. He was a fleshy man with a clean handkerchief and a way of speaking to women as if they were slightly deaf. He raised the rent after Elias died, giving Nell six days to find the money, and when she asked for more time he said that a woman who kept a dead man's room was keeping a luxury, and that the town was full of people who would be glad of it. She paid by taking in washing on top of everything else. The blue line at her gum got longer. She hated him with a clean, cold hatred, the kind you can carry for years without its spoiling. Whether Vetch was Mended into an official in Ond is left open for the middle arcs; the House keeps men like him, and men like him flourish there.

There were smaller debts. She owed Mrs. Ainsley three shillings for a month of washing she had not done. She owed the apothecary for a tonic she did not believe in. Mrs. Ainsley's daughter, a girl of eight with a loud laugh, used to come to the workshop and watch Nell set staples, and Nell liked the child more than she was willing to admit, and would have missed her if she had thought to miss her at all. That girl is one of the first people Wren will recognise in Cindermere, grown and Mended and owing, though the recognition will take two hundred chapters to become certain.

The true enemy, though, is not a person. It is the quiet arithmetic of a poor life: rent, lead, cold water, the tide. Nell's world trained her to believe that everything must be paid and that nobody ever pays you back. The world of Cindermere will turn that belief into a literal law of physics, and she will spend the rest of her long life learning that it was a lie her poverty told her.

## Failure — the jug, and the guilt she invented

When Nell was thirty-one, a neighbour named **Mrs. Garr** brought her a water jug with a long crack running down one side. It was a good jug, the best thing Mrs. Garr owned, and she was going to use it at her daughter's wedding. Nell was tired that week. Her mother's cough had come back, Kit had written from Riga and the letter had been returned unopened, and the lead had made her hands unsteady. She set three staples. The third did not sing. She heard the silence of it, knew the staple was false, and let it stand anyway because she wanted the job off her table.

Two months later the jug cracked wider at the wedding breakfast. Scalding water went over the hearth and onto Mrs. Garr's hands. The daughter was not badly hurt. It was the mother who was burned, and her hands healed badly, and she could not work for a season.

Nell never confessed. She mended the jug again, properly, and would not take money for it, and for the rest of her life she carried the thing like a stone in her pocket. She told the story to herself in the worst possible light: that a child had been scalded, that she had done it on purpose through laziness, that she was a woman who mended crockery and had lied in the one place where honesty mattered. Almost all of it was true enough to hurt, and almost all of it was exaggerated. The child survived. The burn was to the hands. The guilt grew in the dark, the way the Owed grows in the dark, until it was heavier than the actual deed.

This is her great secret and her first real test. In Cindermere, where guilt has weight and becomes a crack in the vessel, the invented part of her remorse is as dangerous as the true part. When she finally learns — very late — that the story she has carried is not the story that happened, she will have to do something she has never done before: repair a memory by telling the truth about it, and leave the staple visible.

## The death, exactly

In the third week of October 1883, a great tide came up the Wreak. The quay was under by noon. Nell's workshop was on the ground floor of a building on Silt Lane, with a cellar where she kept her clay, her glaze powders, her finished work and a tin box of commissions waiting to be collected.

Among the commissions were **twelve wedding cups**, ordered six weeks earlier by a veiled woman who had come into the workshop at closing time, paid in advance with a coin Nell did not recognise, and asked, in a voice with no accent Nell could place, that the work be ready before the first frost. The woman had known about the tide. Nell was sure of it afterwards — the woman had looked at the door, and at the sky, and said nothing. Whether that is true or the mercy of hindsight is another of the slow questions.

At dusk, with water coming under the door, Nell went down the cellar stairs for the box. She could have left it. Nothing in it was hers. She went down because work left unfinished was a debt, and she had been raised to believe that a debt must be paid even to people who would never know. She had the box under one arm and a handful of brass staples held between her lips, the way a riveter does when both hands are busy. The stairwell filled behind her. The water came up to her knees, then her waist, then her chest. It was cold enough to be a physical blow. It tasted of silt and iron. The blue line at her gum showed white in the lamplight as the lamp went out.

She thought, clearly and without drama: *I have left the job unfinished.*

Then the water went over her head, and the sound of the river at the cellar door became the sound of a held breath, and the held breath became everything.

She was thirty-six. Her death was not a battle, not a sacrifice, not a prophecy. It was a tired woman going down a stair for a box of cups. That is the point. That ordinariness is what makes her Owed heavy enough to be recalled instead of lost; that ordinariness is what the House, in its blindness, mistook for the mark of an honest mender.

## The people left behind

- **Kit Rill**, nineteen, at sea or dead, carver of birds, carrying a small wooden bird in his coat pocket that he had meant to give her when he came home. He never came home. In Cindermere he is a Cull in the House of Seams, and the bird is still in the pocket of a coat he no longer remembers owning.
- **Mrs. Ainsley** and her loud-laughing daughter, who would have missed her, and did, briefly, before the rent and the winter took over.
- **Mrs. Garr**, whose hands had healed into two pale ropes, and who said at the funeral that Nell had been a good woman, which was more than Nell would have claimed.
- **Mr. Vetch**, who took the two rooms back within the week and rented them to a family of four.
- **The tin under the bed** with a hundred and four scraps of typeset paper in it, none of which anyone ever found, and which will surface in Ond two thousand chapters later.

She left no will, no letters of her own, and no name on a stone. That last matters: in a world where Names are what survives, the woman who was recalled had, by the time she died, already been half forgotten by everyone she knew.

## Secrets of that life that matter in the new one

1. **The coin** Ildra Voss paid with bore a script Nell could not read. It was a coin of Ond. She kept it in her apron pocket; it should have gone down into the cellar with her. It did not. It is the first object to cross worlds after her, and it will turn up in Kilnwick before she does.
2. **The typeset scrap** with four words in the Cindermere script. Elias set it days before he vanished. It is the same line carved over the House of Seams.
3. **The note a true staple makes** — the small bright ping. This is the only "power" she brings: an ear for honest work.
4. **The blue line at the gum**, the sweet lead taste, the unsteadiness in her hands: these will surface as sensory memories at the worst possible moments, in a body that has never been poisoned and cannot understand why it remembers being.
5. **The guilt of the jug** is partly self-made. The truth will one day set her free and break her heart in the same hour.
6. **She chose to go down the stairs.** Nobody pushed her. She must eventually face the fact that her death was her own workmanship, as much as any pot she ever mended.
7. **She once wrote a letter for a man going to sea** — a letter that was never sent — and the unsaid words in it are, in Cindermere, a real Owed with Kit's name on it. The House has been trying to collect from him for twenty years.

## Sensory ledger — memories that will surface later

Keep these exact and reuse them sparingly. They are the threads between the lives.

- **Taste:** lead-sweet on the tongue, like a coin held too long; river silt, iron and cold in the flood water; the metallic tang of a brass staple kept between the lips.
- **Sound:** the small bright note of a staple set true; the flat dead tap of a cracked pot; sheets snapping on the washing line like sails; the press thumping in the printshop; the river at the cellar door like a held breath.
- **Smell:** hot clay and damp plaster in the kiln-yard; the sour vegetable reek of the Wreak at low water; printer's ink — hot metal and oil; tallow candles; wet wool drying on a body.
- **Touch:** the blue line at the gum felt with the tongue; a staple's legs pressed flat inside a pot; Kit's wooden bird worn smooth at the back; Elias's ink-stained fingers on her wrist; cold water against the small of the back as a cellar fills.
- **Sight:** the colour of old brass on a workshop wall; the pewter sky over the Wreak; a lamp going out under water; a blue line showing white at the edge of a dead woman's gum; a hundred scraps of type in a tin under a bed.
