\section*{Sarn — the world, the lands, and the nations}

\emph{An account set down for the reader of the Compendium. What is written here is what Sarn believes about itself, together with such measurements as a traveller may check with his own feet. Where the belief and the measurement disagree, the measurement is given first.}

\CRrule

\section*{I. The shape of the world}

\section*{The Warm}

Sarn is not a world that contains heat. It is a world made of \textbf{the Warm}: a living, stored heat that runs through clay, water, bone, beast, hearth and star. The poor call it \emph{the warm}, as they call a fire \emph{the fire}. The learned call it \textbf{the kept heat}. It is not flame. Flame is what the Warm looks like when it is escaping. Fire is the world bleeding upward.

The Warm has pressure, currents, sources and sinks, the way a body has blood pressure, arteries, a heart and a wound. It can be measured, drawn off, poured, pooled, banked, spent or lost. Everything that holds it is a \textbf{vessel}. A person is a vessel. So is a beast, a tree, a river, a town, a kiln, and the world itself. A vessel has walls, channels called \textbf{ways}, a capacity, flaws, old patches and a rate of loss. A vessel that holds is called \textbf{whole}. A vessel that leaks is called \textbf{thin}. When the walls give way the Warm goes out, and where it goes is the question every religion in Sarn exists to answer.

The great source lies beneath the world: a chamber of stone called \textbf{the Hearth}, which holds what the world had when it was made. The great sink lies beyond the cracks at the edge of all things: \textbf{the Waste}, a cold country outside the world, where everything unheld goes. The whole of Sarn is the space between the two, and every vessel in it is a small, failing dam.

\section*{The common account}

Every child in Sarn is taught that the world was a vessel, fired whole and cracked in a single moment of anger. The crack is called \textbf{the First Breaking}. The sky is the lid set upon the clay body, and where lid and body meet at the horizon runs the rim-seam, riveted with stars. Beneath the ground lies the under-seam, and below that the dark where nothing is kept. The temple teaches that \textbf{the Nine Who Kept} drove the first repairs and left behind the rite of \textbf{the Return}, by which the dead come back in new bodies through the mercy of the Hearth. This is the first thing a child learns and the last thing a dying person hears.

There are surveyors in the mountain kingdom of Keld who will tell a different story, if they have drunk enough and no officer of the Court is in the room. They say the rim-seam shows many repairs laid one over another, and that the oldest of them are not the work of any god that can be named. Some are not made of stone or clay at all. In the records they look like \emph{language}. The surveyors do not say what language. They stop there, and turn the slate face down.

\section*{The ranks of vessels}

The ranks of the Warm are trades, not prizes, and a man works years at one before he is called by its name. \textbf{Whole} is a vessel that holds without leaking. \textbf{Held} is a vessel that can carry more than its natural measure. \textbf{Drawn} can shape and direct a flow. \textbf{Banked} can store Warm outside the body, in clay or iron. \textbf{Hearthward} can draw from the Hearth itself and is therefore never cold. \textbf{Kept} is a vessel the Court has made effectively unbreakable, and there are fewer than twenty in the world. The Kept are spoken of with pity. They no longer feel the cold.

No one discovers these ranks by wishing. A vessel is opened, a way is cut, the pressure is raised, and something is paid. The price is always heat taken from somewhere: a chill in the practitioner, grey fingers, a memory burned thin, a district left cold for a season.

\section*{The bowl, the rim, and the sea}

Sarn is a great shallow bowl. Its western and northern edge is \textbf{the Rim}, a chain of cold mountains, broken at a few passes by roads that never quite freeze and never quite dry. Beyond the Rim lies the Waste, and no map is honest past the last pass. The bowl’s north-eastern lowlands drain into \textbf{the Sallow Sea}, a cold inland sea the colour of skimmed milk, whose tides run against the wind and are said to follow the Warm rather than the moon. Its coasts are salt, glass and fog. The centre of the bowl is a plain of clay and old river gravel, warm by Sarnish standards and therefore crowded. Across the south-east runs \textbf{the Keel}, a low ridge of stone under which the Hearth is said to lie, and on the Keel stands the Hearthseat, the Court’s city.

Distances in this account are given in \textbf{leagues}. A Sarnish mile is one thousand paces, roughly nine-tenths of an English mile; a league is three miles, a little under three kilometres. A loaded cart makes three leagues in a day on good road, four if the horse is fresh and the driver cruel. A man on foot makes four on the Clay Road and two in the bogs. A day’s walk is the common measure of neighbourhood: \emph{three leagues downriver} means a long morning and a sore back.

\section*{II. The lands and their climates}

The seasons are counted by the months of the Sarnish year, which are set out in `bible/calendar.md`. In Grath, ice comes on the river in Stone and breaks in Ilmen. Silt is the month of mud, when no cart can reach the high road. Seed and Tallow are the planting and lambing months. Ember is the short fierce summer in which every kiln in Grath is fired at once, so that the whole country smells of woodsmoke for thirty days. Smoke and Grain are the harvest and the second firing. Frost and Bone are the hunting months. Ash and Stone are the dark.

\section*{III. Wonders and wastes}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]

\item \textbf{The Hearth-fire.} In the Weighing Hall of the Hearthseat a flame is kept behind a grating of cold iron. It is said to be a tongue of the Hearth drawn up through the Keel. It has never gone out, and no priest has ever been allowed to touch it.

\item \textbf{The Singing Sill.} At Sill, the river Ilm crosses a ledge of hard stone before the falls. When the Warm rises in the spring the stone gives a low note under the water, audible from the Fishstairs. The people of Sill set their clocks by it and do not explain it.

\item \textbf{The Glaze Cliffs.} On the Vhaleni coast, cliffs stained blue-green by old lead workings. They smell faintly sweet in the heat. Children are warned not to lick them.

\item \textbf{The Listening Stones of the Vorr.} Standing stones in the high Rim, said to be older than the Return. The Vorr sit against them for days and come away with numbers.

\item \textbf{The Nine Wells.} Hot springs in the Keel where the ground gives back more than it takes. The Court seals four of them and keeps the keys.

\item \textbf{The Empty Cantons.} A province south of Grath with roads, wells and doorways but no people. The temple says the people were taken in the first Returns. Nobody says who paid.

\item \textbf{The Old Kiln.} A collapsed kiln one league south of Sill. The clay around it is grey and will not take a glaze. The ash in its throat is deeper than a man is tall.

\item \textbf{The Waste.} The country beyond the Rim. Travellers who go far enough come back older and thinner, and do not remember what they went for. The Court sends no collectors there.

\end{itemize}

\section*{IV. The nations and realms}

\section*{1. Grath — the clay country}

\textbf{Seat and government.} Grath is not a single state but a string of river towns under a \textbf{Thane of Clay}, who is chosen by the kiln-masters of Sill and confirmed by the Court’s factor in Greybank. Each town has a moot of householders that meets on market-day and settles small debts by shouting. The thane’s real power is the right to call a levy of clay and labour; the Court’s real power is the right to call a levy of warmth. Everyone in Grath knows which levy is the heavier.

\textbf{Coin and trade.} Grath works in copper and silver: the \textbf{bit}, twelve to the silver \textbf{mark}. Coarse pots, tile, peat and eels go downriver to Greybank; salt and iron come back up. A kiln hand earns one mark a day, a journeyman two, a child sweeping the yard six bits, and a collector’s clerk four marks. Rent on a two-room house in Low Kiln is eight marks a month, fuel four, food twelve. A man who misses three days of work is a man who has begun to owe.

\textbf{Faith.} The Return is taught by the \textbf{hearth-maids}, who are midwives, collectors’ assistants and keepers of the birth-slates in one person. A hearth-maid crosses herself at a birth-flower and says nothing about it. The poor of Grath pray to the Warm the way a man presses a coin flat: not for mercy but for a little more of it.

\textbf{Customs.} On the Last Day of the year the ash in the corners is left where it lies. It is swept up on the morning after, and burnt. A pot is broken at the door of a death-house to let the heat out. A foundling is named by whoever writes the slate, and has no house-name until a hearth claims him.

\textbf{Outsiders.} A man without a kiln-mark is a man without a debt, and Grath does not trust him. Travellers are lodged at the Cross and watched. Vhaleni salt merchants are tolerated because they pay. Sere traders are disliked because they buy measures. A Wasteling is not so much disliked as pitied in a way that closes the door.

\textbf{Slavery and law.} Grath knows \textbf{clay-bound} service: a debt paid in years of work, seven years being the common term, with interest gathering as grey Ash in the household accounts. Chattel slaves exist in the thane’s house and in the greater kilns, called \textbf{kept hands}, and are returned thin when they die. The moot punishes theft by compensation in marks; theft of warmth from another’s cup is punished by a cold iron brand on the wrist and a deduction from the thief’s own measure. Murder is not answered with death, which would waste a measure. The murderer is banked out: his warmth is drawn and divided among the victim’s household, and he is returned thin as a debt-child to work the debt off. Refusing a lawful collection is the crime called an \textbf{unclosed account}; the offender is clip-held by the wrist until he consents.

\textbf{What they fear.} The Breaking, when the Ilm comes up over the Fishstairs and takes the low houses; thin ice; the kiln’s breath, which kills a man in his sleep; the sweet sickness from bad glaze; the second chill in a rekindled child; and the grey ridge along the skirting board that grows when a debt is not paid.

\section*{2. The Warm Court — the Keeping}

\textbf{Seat and government.} The Court sits in the \textbf{Hearthseat} on the Keel and rules every vessel in Sarn in theory and most in practice. Its head is the \textbf{Steward of the Hearth}. Below him are the assessors, who decide what a life was worth; the collectors, who draw the measure with a cold iron tube; and the bankers, who press it into newborn vessels. The Court calls all of this \textbf{the Keeping}. It has no army of monsters and no dark lord. It has slates, clerks and a soft way of speaking.

\textbf{Coin and trade.} The Court does not trade in bits and marks; it books warmth in \textbf{embers}. One ember is booked at five marks, a rate no market has ever seen. The trade is in whole returns, thin returns, deductions, and \textbf{quiet ash}, a medicine sold to the rich who want their previous life to stop arriving in dreams. A whole return is booked at twelve hundred marks and forty unspent measures. A thin return costs the district thirty marks and is paid in labour. The deduction is taught as one part in twelve. In Low Kiln it is one in three.

\textbf{Faith.} The Keeping is both bank and church. Its priests say: \emph{The measure is banked. The account stands. Nothing is lost that we have kept. Your return is recorded.} They never raise their voices. Their violence is grammatical.

\textbf{Customs.} Every month’s tenth day is account-day, when the slate is read aloud. At the Weighing, in the Five Cold Days, the year’s measures are announced and the rich learn how whole they are. The Court keeps its dead on the rolls forever; an account is never closed, only carried forward.

\textbf{Outsiders.} There are no outsiders to the Keeping. A foreigner is a subject whose account has not yet been opened. An assessor can open one with a question and a slate.

\textbf{Slavery and law.} The Court owns no slaves, which it considers a mark of civility. Its \textbf{Kept} vessels belong to their accounts instead of to a master, which the Court considers kinder. Law is the slate: what is written stands; what is not written did not happen. An officer of the Keeping has no house and no kin. The account is her family.

\textbf{What they fear.} The Waste growing inward. A hot trace that will not cool. An unclosed account heavy enough to crack a slate. And above all, the day someone looks at a dying body and says, plainly, \emph{leave it}.

\section*{3. Vhalen — the salt republic}

\textbf{Seat and government.} Vhalen is a ring of port towns on the Sallow Sea governed by a \textbf{Council of Tides}, elected by ship-owners and salt-masters for a year at a time. The council meets in a room that smells of tar and wet rope. Its decrees are written on salt-paper and rot in damp weather, which is considered a virtue: a law that lasts too long is a law that has stopped earning.

\textbf{Coin and trade.} Silver marks and copper bits, with Venetian-style tallies kept by the factor. Salt, glass, rope, dried fish and ships are the staples. A sailor’s share is two marks a month and keep; a salt-worker gets one mark and a binding promise. Glass from the Glaze Cliffs is sold as far as Sere. Vhaleni shipwrights will build a boat for thirty marks and curse every hour of the work.

\textbf{Faith.} Vhaleni say the Sallow has a Hearth of its own beneath the salt, and that the Court’s Hearth is only its chimney. Their priests are called \textbf{dippers} and bless nets by trailing them in the sea at dawn.

\textbf{Customs.} Children are named for tides and winds. The dead are sealed in a salted boat and pushed out on the ebb; the boat is watched until it is out of sight, which is held to be enough. A stranger’s coin warms before his face, they say.

\textbf{Outsiders.} Welcomed if they pay, ignored if they do not. Vhalen has no word for a guest and no word for a friend, only for a man whose account is current.

\textbf{Slavery and law.} Condemned criminals are sentenced to the \textbf{pulled} benches, rowing the salt galleys for a term of seven years or until their hands give out, whichever comes first. Slaves can be bought in Vhalen but cannot be returned there: the Council will not pay for a thin return and will not house one. Law is contract; a contract written on salt-paper in wet weather is no contract at all, and half the port’s fortunes have turned on that.

\textbf{What they fear.} Cold tides that take warmth out to the Waste, being cut off from the Sallow in a hard winter, and the Court’s assessors arriving with a list of deductions on the salt works.

\section*{4. Keld — the mountain kingdom}

\textbf{Seat and government.} Keld holds the high passes of the Rim. Its ruler is the \textbf{Warden of the Rim}, a hereditary office, though a Warden who cannot hold a pass is removed by his own captains. Below him are the mine-reeves, who count every ember of iron and every life that goes down.

\textbf{Coin and trade.} Silver is scarce and iron is common: a good iron axe is worth two marks in the lowlands and half a mark in Keld. The kingdom lives on iron, lead, coal and the stone called \textbf{warm-stone}, which holds heat longer than any other substance in Sarn. Keldish mercenaries are hired by the Court and by Sere and are trusted by neither.

\textbf{Faith.} The Keldish claim the true Hearth lies beneath their mountain and that the Court’s fire is a candle lit from it. Their observatory is a deep shaft lined with Listening Stones, where surveyors record the repairs in the rim-seam. What they record is not published.

\textbf{Customs.} A Keldish person’s measure is tattooed on the forearm in numbers at thirteen. A man who returns thin has the old numbers cut out and new ones put in. Duels are fought with iron clamps, and the first man to open a way in his own forearm usually wins, because the other is busy bleeding.

\textbf{Outsiders.} Treated as tribute-bearers or spies, often both. A lowlander in Keld must state his measure before he states his name. If he lies, the mine-reeve checks him.

\textbf{Slavery and law.} The deep mines are worked by \textbf{under-vessed} slaves, condemned men and bought breath whose lives are reckoned at eleven months. The Warden’s word is law, tested by hot iron: a man who holds a clamp against his palm without flinching is presumed honest, which has produced a great many honest men with dead hands.

\textbf{What they fear.} The mountain sweating. In the deep shafts the Warm sometimes comes up through the stone in a lethal flood, and the only warning is the smell of hot lead. Keld also fears the Court’s assessors, who have never accepted the claim about the true Hearth and have never bothered to argue it.

\section*{5. Sere — the empire of bowls}

\textbf{Seat and government.} Sere is a hot southern empire of grain and great houses, ruled by the \textbf{Sun-Emperor} through a Council of Bowls. Each bowl is a province, and each bowl sends grain, slaves and warmth to the capital. The emperor’s acts are announced by the emptying of a public bowl of wine, which is read as a sign that a debt has been forgiven or a province has been ruined.

\textbf{Coin and trade.} Gold \textbf{crowns}, twenty silver marks to the crown, are the Sere currency. Grain, oil, wine, fine glazed ware and slaves go north; wool, iron and warm-stone come south. Sere buys poor measures openly and at a good price, which is why Grath hates Sere traders and sells to them anyway.

\textbf{Faith.} Sere teaches \textbf{the Spending}: a vessel was made to be emptied gloriously, and a life hoarded is a life wasted. Their temples are open courts with a fire in the middle. At the end of a feast they burn the debt-slates of one household, chosen by lot. Then they make new ones.

\textbf{Customs.} Name-days are public and generous; a Sere child may have seven names by adolescence and keep whichever the household likes. A young person comes of age at the Bowl feast: he empties a bowl of warm wine, names a debt he intends to carry, and is applauded. Inheritance goes to the eldest surviving child regardless of sex.

\textbf{Outsiders.} Welcomed as customers, enslaved as labourers, and rarely allowed to be both at once. A northerner with a thin return is a curiosity in Sere, exhibited at feasts and given small coins.

\textbf{Slavery and law.} Chattel slavery is open, extensive and legally simple: a slave’s warmth is the owner’s holding, and a slave has no measure in law. Beautiful house-slaves are given whole returns and exhibited as proof of the owner’s generosity. A slave who runs is returned thin and set to the grain mills. Justice is the emperor’s decree; the phrase \emph{measure for measure} is used to mean whatever the speaker has already decided.

\textbf{What they fear.} The Court’s deduction on their grain, the northern habit of calling them spendthrifts, and a slave rising who remembers enough to want something.

\section*{6. The Fenmarch — the reed confederacy}

\textbf{Seat and government.} A scatter of bog-villages governed by \textbf{reeves} chosen for their ability to stand in a bog longer than anyone else. The reeves settle feuds and collect fines; actual power lies with the families who control the peat-dries and the smugglers’ paths.

\textbf{Coin and trade.} Barter, copper bits, and marked sticks. Peat, reeds, hemp, eels and smuggled warmth. Fenfolk will sell a thin return for a third of what the Court charges and ask no questions about the source.

\textbf{Faith.} The \textbf{Marsh Mother} keeps warmth in the mud, they say, and gives it back to whoever can reach it. Their rites involve lying face down in peat until the cold becomes ordinary.

\textbf{Customs.} The ash in the corner is not left in the Fenmarch; it is gathered, bottled and sold. Children are taught to identify a collector by his boots before they are taught their own names.

\textbf{Outsiders.} \emph{A dry man is a dangerous man.} Outsiders are put up on the raised boardwalks and never told which boards are safe.

\textbf{Slavery and law.} \textbf{Bought breath}: a person sold for a term, usually a season or a year, to repay a peat-debt. Law is fine and feud; a fine unpaid becomes a term, and a term unpaid becomes a measure. Sexual violence exists in the Fenmarch as it does elsewhere in Sarn, and is dealt with by the reeve only when it has damaged a household’s account.

\textbf{What they fear.} The draining of the fens, the Court’s collectors arriving with a list, and a winter in which the peat does not dry.

\section*{7. The Waste — the country outside}

\textbf{Seat and government.} None. Bands of \textbf{Wastelings} move between the cold seams beyond the Rim, taking what the world loses. They have no banks, no Returns and no word for a debt. They are not Unbanked in the political sense; they are simply the spent.

\textbf{Coin and trade.} Nothing is traded that can be counted. A Wasteling may give a stone, a name or a day’s warmth, and expect nothing back.

\textbf{Faith.} None that a visitor can recognise. They say the cold is not empty; it is full of what nobody kept.

\textbf{Customs.} They leave their dead sitting upright facing the way they came. They do not cover them.

\textbf{Outsiders.} There are no outsiders, only vessels that have not yet leaked.

\textbf{Slavery and law.} No slavery, because no one in the Waste has enough warmth to own another. Law is the immediate appetite of the stronger vessel, which lasts about as long as the appetite.

\textbf{What they fear.} Nothing that survives the telling. A Wasteling asked what he fears will usually answer with a question about whether the visitor is warm.

\section*{V. Grath in detail — the birth country}

Grath lies in the north-west of the bowl, between the lower spurs of the Rim and the cold north-eastern reach of the Sallow Sea. It is a country of clay and water rather than stone: the hills are soft, the roads are rutted, and every house has a crack in it that the householder knows by name. The river \textbf{Ilm} rises in the Rim a hundred and twenty leagues upriver of Sill, runs east through the clay towns, and reaches the Sallow Sea another eighty leagues below Greybank. Its water carries fine grey silt, and in summer the shallows smell of iron and mud.

\section*{The town of Sill}

\textbf{Sill} stands where the Ilm crosses a ledge of hard stone and drops four feet. The town takes its name from that ledge. At low water the stone shows brown; when the Warm rises in Ilmen it gives a low musical note under the current, and the old people say the year has properly begun. Sill has just over four thousand souls, two bridges, three kiln-yards and a Court room.

Its districts:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]

\item \textbf{Low Kiln}, on the south bank below the sill, where the kilns stand and the smoke lies low. Houses here are two rooms, clay-daubed, with floors of trodden ash. This is where Corin is born and where the Vail kiln-house keeps its fire. The ash ridge along the skirting board is thicker here than anywhere else in Grath.

\item \textbf{High Kiln}, on the terrace above, where the master potters live and the glaze is mixed. The streets are steeper and smell of lead.

\item \textbf{the Cross}, the market square where the Clay Road and the river road meet. On market-day it holds perhaps two hundred people, eight pigs and one assessor.

\item \textbf{Slate Row}, east of the Cross: the Court’s room, the collector’s table, the assessor’s narrow house, and the Waiting Room where the collected are kept overnight before the banking.

\item \textbf{the Fishstairs}, the landing steps below the north bridge. Eels are sold here at dawn and rumours are sold here at noon.

\item \textbf{the Bone Orchard}, outside the north gate, a league from the Cross: the burial ground, the thin-return children’s graves, and the place where pots are broken for the dead.

\item \textbf{the Clay Yard}, in Low Kiln: the slip pits, the wheel shed, the drying racks and the kiln. It is the whole world of a child until he is old enough to walk to the Cross.

\end{itemize}

\section*{Roads, rivers and the cost of travel}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]

\item \textbf{the Clay Road}, Sill to Greybank, eighteen leagues, packed clay over corduroy in the wet places. A cart takes six days in Silt and five in Grain.

\item \textbf{the Salt Road}, Sill to the Vhaleni ports, eighty leagues by Greybank and the coast pass. Twenty days on foot at four leagues a day, more in a thaw.

\item \textbf{the Rim Road}, Sill north-west to Keld Keep, a hundred and twenty leagues of improving road and worsening weather. Only fools and factors travel it without a warm-stone.

\item \textbf{the Hearth Road}, Greybank south-east to the Hearthseat, a hundred and forty leagues. Merchants take it; Grath folk speak of it as if it were a fever.

\item \textbf{the Ilm}, the great river: frozen from Stone into Ilmen, broken up in the Breaking during Silt, low and silty in Ember.

\item \textbf{the Ferr}, a colder tributary from the south-west, forty leagues long, joining at Stonemouth. Its water carries lead dust and is said to make a man thin if he drinks it through a season.

\item \textbf{the Slip Brook}, a narrow clay-coloured stream through the Clay Yard into the Ilm. Children in Low Kiln learn to read the weather by its colour.

\end{itemize}

\section*{Seasons, food, smells}

The year begins in the dead of winter. \textbf{Stone} is the deep cold: the Ilm under ice, bread kept in a warmed box, every household counting its fuel. The \textbf{Five Cold Days} come at the turn of the year, and on the Last Day no ash is swept. \textbf{Ilmen} is the thaw and the first fishing; \textbf{Silt} is mud and the Breaking; \textbf{Seed} and \textbf{Tallow} are planting and lambing; \textbf{Ember} is the kiln month, short and fierce; \textbf{Grain} and \textbf{Smoke} are harvest and second firing; \textbf{Frost} and \textbf{Bone} are hunting and repair; \textbf{Ash} is the dark before the ice.

A day’s eating in Low Kiln is barley-oat bread with a brown edge, a pottage of turnip, chard and eel, salted perch in brine, dried peas in winter, sour-milk cheese when the cows are in, and small ale watered until it barely counts. At the fattening before the Five a household kills a pig and nothing is wasted, not the trotters, not the bladder. Kiln apples are baked in the embers and eaten with the outside burnt. Children are given the browned crust. This is considered kindness.

The smell of Grath is woodsmoke under wet wool, with peat smoke underneath that, and clay dust in the throat, and the sweetish edge of lead glaze from High Kiln, and tallow, and river silt when the wind comes off the water. In a Low Kiln room after dark there is one more smell: the sour, faintly mineral tang of the Ash in the corner, which everybody knows and nobody names.

\section*{Trades and prices}

Grath’s trades are clay-digging, slip-mixing, throwing, glazing, firing, peat-cutting, reed-cutting, eeling, salting, tallowing, carting, and the Court’s own trade of minding the dying. A child of eight can earn six bits a day sweeping the yard. A kiln hand earns a mark. The price of an ordinary life in Low Kiln, counted in coin rather than warmth, runs like this:

\section*{The dangers}

The Breaking takes the low houses every few years and leaves the silt waist-deep in the Fishstairs. Thin ice kills two or three children a winter. The kiln’s breath kills a man in his sleep and is called a merciful death by people who have not had to identify the body. The potter’s cough shortens breath and life. The sweet sickness from bad glaze shows first as a pale line at the gum and ends with a man who cannot hold a cup. There are wolves in the reedbeds east of Mud End. There are feuds between the kiln-houses that last longer than the men who started them. And there is the collector, who comes not with a threat but with a slate, a cold iron tube, and an account already written down.

\section*{VI. Measure, coin, and what a life books at}

\textbf{Coin.} Twelve copper \textbf{bits} make one silver \textbf{mark}. Twenty silver marks make one gold \textbf{crown}, which is rarely seen in Grath and common in Sere. The Court books warmth in \textbf{embers} and will tell you that an ember is worth five marks. No one has ever exchanged one for the other.

\textbf{The measure of a vessel.} A newborn vessel holds twelve to fifteen embers. A child holds twenty to thirty. A labourer in good case holds forty to sixty. A Held vessel holds a hundred or more. A Kept vessel holds three hundred and feels nothing at all. When a person is collected, the measure is drawn into the bank and pressed into one or many infants. A \textbf{whole return} keeps one person’s warmth intact and brings back the same person with memory. A \textbf{thin return} pools many measures and divides them; what comes back is a stranger with a faint interest in a life it cannot name.

\textbf{The deduction.} Every banked person is told that one ember in twelve is kept back for the Hearth. This is taught to children, printed on the Weighing notices, and written in the collector’s own hand. In Low Kiln the true figure is one in three, and the difference is not a tax but the mechanism by which the poor are spent.

\textbf{The Ash.} Grey silt gathers where promises are unkept: in corners, under beds, in the joints of the world. It is invisible in daylight and plain by tallow-light. The poor of Grath leave it on the Last Day. The Court bottles it and sells it to the rich as quiet ash. It is not dirt. It is what is left of a self when the Warm has been taken.

\section*{The peoples of Sarn}

\emph{This account records how Sarnish folk name themselves, how they count their ranks, and how they bury their dead. Names are set down with care, because a name in Sarn is a vessel of a kind: it holds a person, and a person returned thin may find it does not hold as well as it did.}

\CRrule

\section*{I. Peoples and races}

\section*{The Sarnish stock}

The common people of the bowl are one stock, divided by country and trade rather than by blood. A Grathic clay-hand and a Vhaleni salt-master can have children together, and the children are simply of whichever hearth raises them. What separates them is the tongue, the food, and the way they hold a slate. The six great cultures are:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]

\item \textbf{Grathic} — the clay country, river valleys, peat and kilns. Low, guttural speech, short names, long grudges.

\item \textbf{Vhaleni} — the Sallow ports. Sibilant, practical, salt-soaked; a people of contracts that rot in the rain.

\item \textbf{Keldish} — the high Rim. Clipped, numerical, suspicious of warmth they did not dig out of stone.

\item \textbf{Sere} — the southern empire. Long vowels, open courtyards, public feasting, and the largest slave population in the world.

\item \textbf{Fenfolk} — the peat and reed country. Slow nasal Cant, boardwalk villages, cheap returns.

\item \textbf{Wastelings} — the cold outside. A handful of borrowed words and no grammar worth the name.

\end{itemize}

\section*{The Vorr}

The Vorr are not Sarnish and do not claim to be. They are tall, slow, long-lived, and hold their measure in the bone rather than in the blood, which makes them difficult to collect and impossible to bank in the ordinary way. Their skin has the pale grey of old warm-stone and their fingers are doubled at the last joint. They do not undergo the Return. When a Vorr dies, the others seat the body upright facing the direction it came from and leave it. They say: \emph{We were never owed.}

The Vorr keep the Listening Stones in the high Rim and sell nothing but numbers. They claim to be able to read the oldest slates, and have never been induced to translate one in full. The Court employs them as surveyors and watches them carefully. There are perhaps four thousand Vorr in the world, most of them in Keld, and almost none in Grath.

\section*{The blent}

Where Sere experiments with grafting beast-clay into human vessels, or the Court patches a wall with material taken from a donor of another kind, the result is called \textbf{blent}. Some blent pass as people. Some do not. They are strongest in the southern empire, where they are exhibited, and commonest in the back rooms of the Hearthseat, where they are made. The law says a blent person has the measure of the vessel its donor had, which means in practice that a blent person has no measure at all.

\section*{The Kept}

The Kept are not a people but a condition. There are fewer than twenty in Sarn. Their vessels have been made perfectly retentive, so that nothing leaks in and nothing leaks out. They do not feel cold, hunger, pain or affection. They are spoken of with pity and are generally left alone in rooms with good fires, which they cannot enjoy. The Unbanked intend to extend their condition to everyone.

\section*{II. Languages and scripts}

Language — Where — Sound — Written on\par

\textbf{Grathic} — Grath, and as a common tongue everywhere — Short vowels, hard \emph{k} and \emph{thr}, little inflection — Slate with chalk; birch bark for accounts that must travel\par

\textbf{Ledgerhand} — The Warm Court — Grathic grammar with the verbs removed — Slate, in a neat upright hand; the only script all officers read\par

\textbf{Vhaleni} — Sallow ports — Sibilant, many tide-words, endings slurred — Salt-paper, which rots in damp\par

\textbf{Keldish} — The Rim — Clipped consonants, numbers before nouns — Tally cuts in bone and stone\par

\textbf{Sere} — The south — Long open vowels, elaborate politeness — Glazed tablet and paper with gold ink\par

\textbf{Fen Cant} — The Fenmarch — Nasal, slow, half-sung — Not written; marked sticks and knots\par

\textbf{Waste Cant} — Beyond the Rim — Grunts and borrowed Grathic — Nothing\par

\textbf{The Deep Hand} — Nowhere living — Unknown; the Vorr recognise the numbers — The oldest slates and the seam records\par

Ledgerhand is a deliberately cold language. It has no word for \emph{please}, no word for \emph{sorry}, and eleven separate words for the different kinds of deduction. Its sentences are all in the passive: \emph{the measure is banked}, \emph{the return is recorded}, \emph{the account stands}. Nobody in Sarn says such things at a fire. Officers say them at a table.

The Deep Hand appears on the oldest objects and on nothing else. It is taught in no school. When the Vorr look at it they say only \emph{the numbers are correct}, which is not a translation.

\section*{III. Naming conventions}

\section*{Grathic}

A Grathic name has a \textbf{given name} of one or two syllables and a \textbf{house-name} taken from a trade, a place, or a material. A person is entered in the slate as \emph{given plus house}. Foundlings receive a given name from whoever writes the birth-slate and no house-name until a hearth claims them. This is why \textbf{Corin}, written by a collector before she had seen his face, was simply Corin for the first years of his life.

Given names are often materials, tools or small domestic things: clay, ash, reed, stone, coal, a pot, a hand, a bird. They are not poetic. A Grathic child is named for what the household has plenty of.

Examples\par

Given names — Alda, Berr, Corin, Dessa, Ennit, Fenna, Garl, Hesta, Ivo, Jorra, Kess, Ludo, Mira, Nils, Orra, Rusk, Sella, Tarn, Ulda\par

House-names — Vail (kiln), Reed, Clay, Silt, Tallow, Marsh, Ash, Sill, Weir\par

Full names — Sella Vail, Jorra Vail, Alda Reed, Mira Silt, Tarn Vail, Hesta Clay, Rusk of the Weir\par

Foundlings — Corin, Ennit, Ulda — one name only, no house\par

The Vail house of Low Kiln is a kiln-house, and has been Vail for four generations. The hearth-maid who attended Corin’s birth was \textbf{Alda Reed}, who crossed herself at the birth-flower and told nobody what she had seen.

\section*{Vhaleni}

Vhaleni given names are short, open and usually taken from tides, winds, birds or salt. A house-name follows, taken from a boat, a quay or a trade. A Vhaleni who has no house-name is assumed to be a guest, which in Vhalen is a precarious condition.

Examples\par

Given names — Avis, Cella, Dolf, Eda, Gull, Havi, Jeva, Kett, Lona, Mav, Neri, Orro, Pia, Ravi, Tavi, Ulla, Veno\par

House-names — Wake, Hull, Quay, Brine, Sail, Salt, Net\par

Full names — Avis Wake, Kett of the Quay, Mav Hull, Dolf Brine, Neri Sail\par

\section*{Keldish}

Keldish names are blunt and frequently begin with a hard consonant. The number of a man’s measure is tattooed on his forearm and is considered part of his name; a Keldish person asked who he is will often give the number first. There are no house-names in the lowland sense. A person is known by the mine or the pass.

Examples\par

Given names — Arl, Bret, Caul, Dorn, Ekk, Farr, Gred, Hald, Ivar, Jarn, Karr, Lod, Marn, Orv, Pike, Ragn, Siv, Tord, Ulf, Vek\par

Designations — of the First Seam, of the Cold Pass, Iron, Rime, Vein\par

Full names — Siv of the First Seam, Tord Rime, Karric of the Cold Pass, Hald Iron\par

\section*{Sere}

A Sere child may accumulate seven names by adolescence and keep whichever the household prefers. Names are long, vowel-heavy and often taken from heat, light or generous acts. House-names are grand and frequently false.

Examples\par

Given names — Alessa, Bastian, Dalia, Feria, Halia, Ilyra, Joren, Liria, Maris, Oriana, Rial, Solene, Talia, Valen, Zeri\par

House-names — Sunbowl, Ember, Gilded, of the Ten Bowls, Openhand\par

Full names — Alessa Sunbowl, Rial of the Ten Bowls, Ilyra Gilded, Valen Openhand\par

\section*{Fenfolk}

Fen names are short, thick and usually nouns from the bog. A Fen person is known by a given name and a reed-name; the two are often the same word said twice. They dislike written names and will not sit at a table where a slate is open.

Examples\par

Given names — Bogga, Dib, Eech, Gorr, Hobb, Jow, Keg, Lull, Mudge, Nib, Ork, Pog, Quill, Rube, Slog, Tuss, Uck, Vobb\par

Reed-names — Peat, Reed, Eel, Mud, Rope\par

Full names — Mudge Peat, Bogga of the Reed, Slog Eel, Hobb Mud\par

\section*{Wastelings}

Wastelings take a single word as a name and change it when it stops fitting. Nobody has a surname in the Waste. A Wasteling asked for a second name will usually point at the cold.

Examples\par

Names — Ash, Bone, Cold, Dregs, Grey, Husk, Knuckle, Loam, Mote, Nub, Pith, Rind, Skag, Thistle, Vole\par

Forms — Grey of the Waste, Husk, Knuckle Who Counts\par

\section*{The Vorr}

Vorr names are long-vowelled and given one at a time, many years apart. A Vorr is known by the stone he keeps rather than by a family.

Examples\par

Names — Ao, Eru, Iyn, Oun, Uro, Aen, Oev, Itha, Elo, Unn\par

Forms — Ao of the Third Seam, Eru Who Counts, Iyn of the Listening Stone\par

\section*{The Court and the naming of officers}

An officer of the Keeping has no house and no kin. On entering the service she is given a \textbf{slate-name}, which is entered on the rolls in Ledgerhand and is the only name she has. Some are one word, some two, and the second word is usually an office gone stiff: Varr, Rennick, Haldre, Marra.

Examples\par

Slate-names — Marra, Derren, Haldre, Rennick, Varr, Wessen\par

Titles set before — Steward Derren, Assessor Pell Varr, Collector Marra, Keeper Haldre\par

Rule — The account is her family; a question about her parents is a question about nothing\par

\section*{Slaves, foundlings and the returned}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]

\item \textbf{Slaves} are given a clay-name by the household that owns them and their old name is forbidden. In Sere the forbidden name is sometimes written on the inside of a bowl, which the slave is never allowed to look into. Grathish kept hands are usually named for the house that holds them: \emph{the Vail’s Tarn}.

\item \textbf{Foundlings} receive a given name from the writer of the birth-slate. In Grath this is the hearth-maid or the collector, whichever arrives first. A foundling without a house-name cannot inherit a kiln, a boat or a debt, which is usually the point.

\item \textbf{The returned} keep their name if the return was whole. If it was thin, the district’s name-pot supplies a new one at birth and the old name is entered in a separate column of the slate that the family is not shown. A person banked five times may have five names and remember the wrong one.

\end{itemize}

\section*{IV. Class structure}

Rank — Who they are — How they are spoken of\par

\textbf{The Kept} — Fewer than twenty; vessels made unbreakable by the Court — With pity, and from a distance\par

\textbf{Court officers} — Steward, assessors, collectors, bankers, keepers — \emph{Clean hands}. They do not carry fuel\par

\textbf{Held} — Guild masters, factors, ship-owners, successful mercenaries — \emph{A vessel that carries more than its share}\par

\textbf{Whole free} — Craftsmen, farmers, potters, would-be scholars — \emph{A whole man}, meaning one who holds\par

\textbf{Thin} — Labourers, the returned poor, the walking thin — \emph{Thin}, never to their face in Grath\par

\textbf{Clay-bound} — Debt-servants working off a term, commonly seven years — \emph{Owed out}; their children are born to the debt\par

\textbf{Bought breath} — Chattel slaves; the owner holds their measure in law — \emph{A kept hand} in Grath, \emph{a bowl} in Sere\par

\textbf{The uncollected} — Those who die without being banked — Nobody. Their warmth goes into the Waste\par

\textbf{The Unbanked} — A secret faction inside the Keeping, not the same as the uncollected — Not spoken of outside sealed rooms\par

A person may be high in one column and low in another. A Held merchant in Vhalen may be thin in the Court’s books and clay-bound to a Sere factor. Sarn’s classes are not a ladder but a set of overlapping accounts, and the accounts are never reconciled.

\section*{V. Gender customs}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]

\item \textbf{Grath.} Both men and women throw clay and fire kilns. A widow can hold a kiln in her own right, which is called \emph{a widow’s mark} and is the only Grathish institution nobody argues with. The house-hearth passes to the eldest child regardless of sex; the slip-pits pass to whoever has the stronger hands. A woman who is banked thin may still keep the house but not the wheel.

\item \textbf{Vhalen.} Boat shares pass through mothers. A Vhaleni man who marries into a boat-owning family is described as \emph{come aboard}, and his opinion on the boat is counted at half. Sailors of either sex are common; captains are mostly women, because the sea does not care and the Council has learned not to either.

\item \textbf{Keld.} Only men go down the deep mines, because a woman’s measure is said to be \emph{closer to the surface} and would draw the sweat out of the stone. Women do the counting above ground and hold the mine-reeve’s keys in some families. A man who returns thin cannot descend, and is given the keys instead. This is considered a kindness and is not one.

\item \textbf{Sere.} Inheritance goes to the eldest surviving child regardless of sex. Concubinage with returned slaves is open and is a public measure of a household’s standing. A woman of a great house may own bowls, slaves and a husband’s debt; she may not enter the counting room, where her numbers are read by a man.

\item \textbf{Fenfolk.} Reeves are chosen by who can stand longest in a bog, and about half are women. A Fen mother names her children before the collectors do, which is the closest thing the Fenmarch has to a declaration of war.

\item \textbf{The Vorr.} The Vorr do not divide work or inheritance by sex. They divide it by whether one has sat against a Listening Stone long enough to hear it, which takes most of a life and is not permitted to the impatient.

\end{itemize}

Across Sarn, a person who has been banked more than three times is considered an unsafe marriage partner, regardless of sex, because the Second Want may arrive in a child’s body wearing the wrong face.

\section*{VI. Coming-of-age}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]

\item \textbf{Grath, the First Firing, twelve years.} A child makes a pot, fires it, and knocks it with a knuckle. A clear note means the household holds a feast; a flat note means the child serves a year in the yard and tries again. Corin, being a foundling with no kiln of his own, will be judged on a pot made in the Vail yard.

\item \textbf{Vhalen, the Salt Walk, fourteen.} The young walk the pans barefoot at low tide and name a tide they have never seen. Salt is considered a sufficient teacher.

\item \textbf{Keld, the Descent, thirteen.} The young go down the mine for one day. Some come back with their number changed. A few do not come back, and the family is paid in iron.

\item \textbf{Sere, the Bowl, sixteen.} A public feast. The young person empties a bowl of warm wine and names a debt he intends to carry. The applause is for the debt, not the drinker.

\item \textbf{Fenfolk, the Reed Cut, twelve.} The child cuts a bundle of reed alone, in the dark, and brings it back without speaking. If the bundle is wet, the child is not yet of the bog.

\item \textbf{The Vorr, the Long Sitting, forty.} The Vorr sit still against a stone for a season and listen. What they hear is not recorded, but their numbers change afterwards.

\end{itemize}

\section*{VII. Death rites}

\textbf{The common rite.} The collector arrives with a cold iron tube and a slate. The measure is drawn, booked, and deducted. The body is washed and laid with its feet toward the nearest water. A pot is broken at the door to let the heat out. A year later, the return is announced at the Weighing. The rich receive themselves. The poor receive a child who is faintly interested in a life it cannot name.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]

\item \textbf{Grath.} Feet to the river. A broken pot at the door. The ash in the corner is left on the Last Day and burnt the next morning. A household that cannot pay the deduction keeps the slate and pays in labour, usually by a child sent to the kiln.

\item \textbf{Vhalen.} The dead are sealed in a salted boat and pushed out on the ebb. The boat is watched until it is out of sight. A Vhaleni who dies at sea is considered already returned.

\item \textbf{Keld.} The dead are walled into the mine, facing the deep seam. The mountain keeps its own, they say, and the surveyors add numbers to a slate that nobody reads aloud.

\item \textbf{Sere.} Cremation in a public bowl, with wine and music. The warmest send-off in the world. The Court taxes the ash, and the family buys it back at the price of quiet ash, which is how a Sere funeral can ruin a house.

\item \textbf{Fenfolk.} The body is laid on a peat raft and pushed into the reedbed. The mud keeps what it wants. Fenfolk do not object to thin returns; they expect them.

\item \textbf{The Vorr.} They stop. The others seat the body upright facing the direction it came from and leave it uncovered. There is no slate, no deduction, no return. When asked why, a Vorr will say: \emph{We were never owed.}

\end{itemize}

\section*{VIII. Crime and punishment}

Crime — Common penalty — Who profits\par

Theft of goods — Compensation in marks, plus a day’s labour for the moot — The wronged household\par

Theft of warmth from another’s cup — Cold iron brand on the wrist; deduction from the thief’s own measure — The Court\par

Refusing a lawful collection — \emph{Unclosed account}: clip-held by the wrist until consent — The Court\par

Assault — Compensation for the damage to a vessel; a cut hand paid in marks — The injured person\par

Murder — The murderer is banked out; warmth divided to the victim’s house; the killer returned thin as a debt-child — The victim’s household and the Court\par

Bearing false slate — In Ledgerhand, \emph{a false measure}: the hand is struck from the rolls and the fingers greyed by cold work — The Court\par

Speaking the Waste (denying the Keeping) — Fine, and a gag of ash worn for a market-day — The Court\par

Running from bought breath — Returned thin and set to the mills; in Sere, publicly exhibited first — The owner\par

Breaking a birth-slate — In Grath, a year in the clay yard and the household’s name entered in a second column — The hearth-maids\par

Sexual violence exists in Sarn as a fact of power, most openly in the southern empire and the Keldish mines, and most quietly in households where the victim has no account worth damaging. It is punished when it damages a household’s standing, and usually not otherwise. It is not a subject for decoration. It is entered here because a reader should know what the law is worth before he is shown a court.

The cruellest penalty in Sarn is the one that sounds kindest: banking out. A murderer is not killed, because a life wasted is warmth wasted. He is emptied, returned thin to the household he wronged, and made to work the debt down. He may remember nothing of the crime. The debt remains. This is what the Court calls \emph{nothing lost that we have kept}.

\section*{The reckoning of days in Sarn}

\emph{The Court counts everything. It counts embers, deductions, returns and the years of a debt. It does not count the days a man is warm, which is the one count that matters to him. This file is the rule by which the days in the books are named.}

\CRrule

\section*{I. The year}

A Sarnish year has \textbf{365 days}: twelve months of thirty days, followed by five days outside the months called \textbf{the Five Cold Days}, or simply \textbf{the Five}. No day is ever added to make the sky agree with the slate. The Court teaches that the Warm holds the seasons in their places, and that the thaw has come in Ilmen for eight hundred years. The Keldish surveyors, who have counted both, do not say so in front of an assessor.

The months, in order, with the Grathic glosses that are used everywhere:

No. — Month — Gloss — Days of the year\par

1 — \textbf{Ilmen} — the thaw, the river rising — 1–30\par

2 — \textbf{Silt} — mud; the Breaking — 31–60\par

3 — \textbf{Seed} — planting — 61–90\par

4 — \textbf{Reed} — reed-cutting — 91–120\par

5 — \textbf{Tallow} — lambing — 121–150\par

6 — \textbf{Ember} — the kiln month, short fierce summer — 151–180\par

7 — \textbf{Grain} — harvest — 181–210\par

8 — \textbf{Smoke} — the second firing — 211–240\par

9 — \textbf{Frost} — first frosts — 241–270\par

10 — \textbf{Bone} — hunting and repair — 271–300\par

11 — \textbf{Ash} — the dark — 301–330\par

12 — \textbf{Stone} — deep winter, river ice — 331–360\par

The Five follow Stone and belong to no month:

Ordinal — Name — Observance\par

361 — \textbf{Kindling} — Every hearth in Sarn is lit from the Hearth-flame. Collectors take the year’s first measure.\par

362 — \textbf{Weighing} — The year’s measures are read aloud. The rich learn how whole they are; Low Kiln learns its deduction.\par

363 — \textbf{the Long Night} — No fire after dusk. People sit in the dark and tell the stories of the Return.\par

364 — \textbf{the Remembering} — Names of the dead are spoken. Whole returns are announced in a clean voice.\par

365 — \textbf{the Last Day} — Bread is baked. The grey Ash in the corners is \textbf{not} swept away. It is burnt the next morning.\par

Other named days:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]

\item \textbf{Account-day}, the tenth, twentieth and thirtieth of every month. In Grath these are also market-days; the slate is read at the Cross before the pigs are weighed.

\item \textbf{the Opening of the Slates}, Ilmen 1. The Court opens the new year’s accounts. The first thin returns of the year are announced at the Weighing, not now.

\item \textbf{the Hearth Drawing}, Stone 12. The first collection of the year; a collector may draw from the dying and from the newly born on the same day.

\item \textbf{the Fish Blessing}, Reed 15. The hearth-maids bless the weirs and the eel traps. In Sill this is done at the Weir, one league upriver.

\end{itemize}

\section*{Weeks}

A week is a \textbf{tend}, ten days long. Every month contains three whole tends: days 1–10, 11–20 and 21–30. The tenth day of each tend is Account-day. The tend-days are Firstday, Secondday, Thirdday, Fourthday, Fifthday, Sixthday, Seventhday, Eighthday, Ninthday and Tenthday.

\textbf{The Five are outside the tend.} They have names, not tend-days, and no market is held during them. On Ilmen 1 the count begins again at Firstday. This is why Account-days in the first weeks of a year fall on Ilmen 10, 20 and 30, and why there are three Account-days in the last ten days of Stone before the Five interrupt them.

\section*{Eras}

Years are numbered from the lighting of the Hearth: \textbf{Y.H. 1} onward. Earlier years are \textbf{B.H.}, before the Hearth; the temple calls them the Broken Years and the Keldish call them the Long Want. Scholars use informal names for stretches of the past — the Quiet Centuries (Y.H. 120–300), the Glaze Ages (300–420), the Bowl Years (420–560), the Ash Times (650–700) and the Settlement (after 747). The present year, \textbf{Y.H. 812}, is called the Thin Year in Low Kiln, after the Long Cold.

\section*{The Warm and the vessel — power in Sarn}

\emph{An account set down for the reader of the Compendium. The first part is what every child in Sarn is taught. \section*{Part I — What Sarn believes}

\section*{The common account}

The world was a vessel, fired whole and perfect, with the \textbf{Hearth} at its centre and the sky laid over it like a lid. It was broken in a single moment of anger. That break is \textbf{the First Breaking}. The Warm went out through it and ran into the dark beyond the Rim, and that dark is \textbf{the Waste}. The \textbf{Nine Who Kept} came after, collected what had escaped, drove the first repairs, and left behind the rite of \textbf{the Return}, so that mortal hands might go on with the work until the crack is closed.

Every vessel that holds without leaking is \textbf{whole}. Every vessel that leaks is \textbf{thin}. When the walls give way the Warm goes out, and the collector comes with a cold iron tube and a slate. The measure is banked. The account stands. Nothing is lost that we have kept.

This account is a piece of administrative poetry. It has one loss, one repair, and one institution to do the repairing, which is the shape of a well-kept ledger rather than the shape of a thing that happened.

\section*{The six named ranks of the common account}

The ranks are trades, not prizes, and a man works years at one before he is called by its name.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]

\item \textbf{Whole} — a vessel that holds without leaking.

\item \textbf{Held} — a vessel that carries more than its natural measure.

\item \textbf{Drawn} — a vessel that can shape and direct a flow.

\item \textbf{Banked} — a vessel that can store Warm outside the body, in clay or iron.

\item \textbf{Hearthward} — a vessel that can draw from the Hearth itself, and is therefore never cold.

\item \textbf{Kept} — a vessel the Court has made effectively unbreakable. There are fewer than twenty in the world. They are spoken of with pity. They no longer feel the cold.

\end{itemize}

No one reaches these ranks by wishing. A way is cut, the pressure is raised, and something is paid.

\section*{Part II — What is true}

\section*{I. The Warm: the world’s stored heat}

Sarn is not a world that contains heat. It is a world made of \textbf{the Warm}: a living, stored heat that runs through clay, water, bone, beast, hearth and star. The poor call it \emph{the warm}, as they call a fire \emph{the fire}. The learned call it \textbf{the kept heat}. It is not flame. Flame is what the Warm looks like when it is escaping. Fire is the world bleeding upward.

It behaves like a fluid under pressure and like a body under strain at the same time.

\textbf{Pressure and head.} A vessel’s Warm has a \textbf{head}, the height to which it would rise if it were water in a pipe. A labourer in good case has a head of forty to sixty embers; a Held vessel a hundred or more; a Kept vessel three hundred and feels nothing at all. Head is not the same as capacity. A cracked cup may hold a little water under a high head and empty itself in an hour. The Court books embers and pretends this is the whole of the matter. It is not.

\textbf{Currents.} The Warm runs through the world in veins. There is a slow \textbf{Rim current} draining toward the Waste, which is why Keld’s deep mines sweat and why warm-stone is found there. There is a \textbf{Sallow draw} seaward, which the Vhaleni dippers bless and the Court taxes. There are small local currents under every kiln, every body of water, every crowded room. Warm moves from the full to the empty, from the hot to the cold, from the many to the few, and never the other way without a tool and a price.

\textbf{Sources.} The great source is the \textbf{Hearth}, a chamber of stone beneath the Keel said to hold what the world had when it was made. The \textbf{Nine Wells} are smaller sources where the ground gives back more than it takes; the Court has sealed five of them and keeps the keys. Every living vessel is a small source while it burns food into warmth, and every hearth-fire is a small source that has been taught to sit still. Keldish \textbf{warm-stone} holds heat longer than any other substance in Sarn, which is a kind of source with the tap turned almost off.

\textbf{Sinks.} The great sink is the \textbf{Waste}, the cold country beyond the cracks, where everything unheld goes. \textbf{Cold iron} is a sink with a gate in it: it draws Warm out of a body and will not give it back. The \textbf{Ash} is a sink of a stranger kind: it does not hold Warm, it holds what is left when the Warm has been taken. The uncollected dead are a sink the Court cannot meter and therefore fears.

\textbf{Tides.} The Warm rises in Ilmen, when the Ilm breaks and the \textbf{Singing Sill} gives its note under the water; it falls in the Five Cold Days, when the year’s measures are read. A practitioner learns to work with the tide as a sailors learn to work with wind. Working against it costs more. There are also long tides nobody has measured: the slow inward creep of the Waste, the great seepage the Court calls the Keeping.

\textbf{Too much.} Pour more Warm into a vessel than its walls can hold and the head rises. The vessel cooks its own contents. Memory thins first, then appetite, then the sense of self, then the walls. This is \textbf{overfilling}, and the Court’s own physicians call the result \emph{a beautiful return}, because the person who comes back is calm and has ceased to want anything. A vessel pushed past its bursting gives way with a small flat knock, like a pot tapped where the crack is.

\textbf{Too little.} A vessel that loses more than it takes in goes thin. It cannot clot, cannot keep a thought warm, cannot sleep properly, and in the end cannot want. Whole districts of Grath live in this condition and are called \textbf{walking thin}: people who keep moving because stopping is the same as cooling. The Waste takes the rest. Travellers who go far enough into it come back older and thinner and do not remember what they went for.

\textbf{Conservation.} Warm is never created and never destroyed. It is only moved. This is the first law and the one the Court’s language is built to conceal. When an assessor says \emph{nothing is lost that we have kept}, he means that the measure has been entered somewhere. He does not mean it is still in the person. There is no “source” in the sense of something that gives without being drained, not the Hearth, not the Wells, not the sun. Every light somebody keeps burning is paid for by somebody.

\section*{II. The anatomy of a vessel}

A \textbf{vessel} is anything that holds a measure of Warm. A person is a vessel. So is a beast, a tree, a river, a town, a kiln, a bowl, and the world itself. Every vessel has:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]

\item \textbf{Capacity} — how much Warm it can hold, booked in embers.

\item \textbf{Walls} — the boundary between held and unheld. In a person these are not skin but something beneath it, which a surgeon’s hand can learn to feel as a resistance and a give.

\item \textbf{Ways} — the channels along which Warm moves inside the vessel. In people the ways run in slow spirals, not branching trees. In beasts they run close to the skin. In places they follow watercourses and seams. No native of Sarn has noticed the spiral in eight hundred years.

\item \textbf{Flaws} — a place where the wall was born thin. Every vessel has them. A flaw is not a crack until it is used.

\item \textbf{Cracks} — a flaw that has opened. Cracks carry the measure out. They can be heard.

\item \textbf{Scars} — a crack that has been closed and has closed badly. A scar holds less than the wall around it and is always the first thing to go next.

\item \textbf{Patches} — material grafted over a break: living clay, iron, salt, another vessel’s wall. A patch is never the wall it covers.

\item \textbf{Rate of loss} — the leak. It is measured by watching a full vessel cool and comparing it to a sound one.

\item \textbf{Head} — the pressure of the Warm within, which decides how fast it escapes through any opening.

\end{itemize}

\textbf{Repair is not restoration.} A mended vessel carries its break. The true test of a repair is the \textbf{full note}: tap a sound vessel with the knuckle and it answers full; tap a cracked one and it answers flat. A good repair gives a note nearly full and never quite. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something. The bottom of a cup, the wall of a house, the ribs of a man: all of them keep the history of their breaking under the glaze.

\section*{Vessels differ}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]

\item \textbf{People.} Ways run in spirals, deep, hidden, and slow. The measure sits in a shape; that shape is the self. A person is difficult to collect because the ways coil and must be followed inward, not cut across.

\item \textbf{Beasts.} Ways run near the skin and in a few long loops. This is why a hunter knows where to put a knife and why a beast bleeds Warm so quickly. A beast’s measure is small and simple; it cannot hold a complicated self, which is why a beast returned thin returns as almost nothing at all.

\item \textbf{Places.} A town’s ways are its streets and water; a river’s are its channel; a kiln’s are its fire. The capacity of a place is enormous and its walls are slow to fail, but when they fail a district goes cold for a season. The poor quarter of a town is always its crack: Low Kiln is the flaw in Sill that the rest of the town agrees not to mention.

\item \textbf{The Vorr.} They hold their measure in the bone rather than in the blood, which makes them difficult to collect and impossible to bank in the ordinary way. They do not undergo the Return. When a Vorr dies the others seat the body upright facing the direction it came from and leave it. They say: \emph{We were never owed.}

\item \textbf{The world.} Sarn is a cracked vessel, imperfectly repaired, perhaps not the first. Its ways are the great seam under the world. The spiral in every human body is a small map of that great crack. The surveyors of Keld have entered the oldest repairs in the rim-seam as neither stone nor clay; the Keldish translate the entry as Ash, and the Court’s copy translates it as \emph{language}. Both translations are guesses.

\end{itemize}

\section*{What can be done to a vessel}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]

\item \textbf{Strengthened} — by firing, salting, tempering, clamping, or grafting. Firing drives the flaws shallower; salting seals small leaks for a season; tempering is the practice of repeated small strains so that the walls learn to yield without opening. A vessel strengthened badly holds less than it did before.

\item \textbf{Broken} — by a cut across a way, by overfilling, by draining, or by the simple failure of an old scar. The sound of a vessel breaking all the way through is a small flat knock, not a crash.

\item \textbf{Mended} — with a clamp set across a crack, a staple of iron, a patch of living clay, or the great patch of the Return. The staple is mercy and mutilation in the same bite.

\item \textbf{Grafted} — wall taken from another vessel and set over the break. The donor pays. If the donor’s measure was shaped differently, the patch keeps wanting its old shape: this is the \textbf{Second Want}, and it arrives in the person who was mended, wearing a life that was not theirs.

\item \textbf{Drained} — a cold iron tube is set against a way and the Warm is drawn out. This is \textbf{collection}. The draw leaves grey fingertips in the collector and a bare account in the collected.

\item \textbf{Poured into another} — the measure is pressed into a newborn vessel. If one measure goes into one infant, the return is whole and the person comes back with memory. If many measures are pooled and divided among many infants, the return is thin: what comes back is a stranger with a faint interest in a life it cannot name.

\end{itemize}

\textbf{Death and rebirth.} Death is the walls giving way. Where the Warm goes is the question every religion in Sarn exists to answer and the bank answers first. Rebirth is a pouring, not a resurrection: the Warm is pressed into a new vessel and takes the shape of its new walls. The old shape tries to return in the \textbf{Afterdrop}, the worsening before recovery. Usually this is only the graft settling. In a few cases the Warm tries to resume its former owner, and the Court calls that the Second Want and strikes the phrase from its records. A vessel can be poured many times. Each pouring thins the shape. A person returned five times may have five names and remember the wrong one, and will be called unsafe to marry, because the Second Want may arrive in a child’s body wearing the wrong face.

\section*{III. Where personal power comes from}

A person’s power is the measure they hold plus what they can route. It is not a gift from the Hearth and not a mark of blood. It is built the way a callus is built: by small, repeated, deliberate injuries that heal into capability.

\textbf{Sensing.} A practitioner reads a vessel the way a cooper reads a barrel: by the unchanged note under the knuckle, by the feel of a temperature gradient through cloth, by the smell of Ash along a skirting board, by the colour of the fingertips after a draw. He hears a faulty vessel before he touches it and is right more often than he is wrong. The Vorr read the world through the Listening Stones and come away with numbers. Dan Marchetti reads a body with a surgeon’s hands and a \textbf{clinical ear}: he knows where the wall is thin because he has spent twenty years finding where the wall is thin in the old life.

\textbf{Training.} The first act is to stop leaking. The second is to cut a way and learn to move Warm within the self without losing it. The third is to carry more than the natural measure. Each stage is a procedure with a body and a tool and a scar. Masters teach slowly and badly, because a practitioner who knows the method is a practitioner who no longer needs the master. The disciplines are guild secrets, and the guilds are the gates.

\textbf{Spending.} Every working moves Warm from somewhere, and the caster pays first out of their own measure. A practitioner who opens a way in the forearm and draws heat up through it afterwards is cold, and the fingertips grey. A great working leaves a district cold for a season. The bill is never abstract: a name, a year, a hand, a memory, a season of cold in a street of houses that did not agree to pay.

\textbf{Exhaustion and recovery.} Spending past the measure produces grey fingers, a tremor, a chill that food does not fix, thinning memory, and finally the \textbf{hollow}: a vessel with a head so low it can no longer pull Warm from food. Recovery is warmth taken in from fire, food, or another vessel. There is no free recovery. Standing close to a kiln is theft from the kiln’s account; a warmed stone against a belly is theft from whoever paid to warm the stone. Sleep helps because a sleeping vessel leaks less. The poor recover by huddling. The Court books this as \emph{dwelling}, which is one of its eleven words for a deduction.

\textbf{The laws.} Warm moves from full to empty. A way once cut stays cut. A crack once made is never gone. Pressure equalizes unless iron holds it back. The shape of the measure is the self, and the shape is fragile. There is no law that says a vessel must be refilled, which is the one law the Court cannot abide.

Example procedures, from least to most:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]

\item \textbf{The full note} (Whole). Tap the vessel with the knuckle and hear whether it holds. Costs nothing. Requires the ear.

\item \textbf{The banked breath} (Whole to Held). Slow the loss by holding the chest and the ways quiet, the way a potter turns the drying rack away from the fire. Costs a little head; leaves the practitioner slightly cold.

\item \textbf{The first way} (Opened). Open a channel in the forearm by cutting across a surface way and keeping it open while it scars. Pain, a permanent mark, and a fortnight in which the arm is useless. Every Keldish duellist has one.

\section*{V. The named ranks of a vessel}

Nine ranks, each tied to what the vessel can hold and do. They are not achievements. A person works years at one and is called by it only when the work is done. The six in the common account are the ones the Court will admit to; the other three are known to craftsmen, Keldish duellists, and the people who live near the seams.

Rank — What the vessel can do — Rarity — How the breakthrough happens — What it costs\par

\textbf{Thin} — Cannot hold; leaks faster than it takes in. Most of the poor, the spent, and the returned poor never leave this rank. Barely a trade: a condition. — Common — perhaps half of Sarn — No breakthrough without first stopping the leak. Most never do. — Warmth taken in is lost again; a life of being cold.\par

\textbf{Whole} — Holds without leaking under ordinary strain. — Common among free craftsmen; rare in Low Kiln — Stop the leak. Find the flaw, clamp or temper it, and learn the full note. — Months of care; a scar; the flaw remains under the repair.\par

\textbf{Opened} — Has cut and learned to use a way; can move Warm deliberately within the self. — Uncommon; taught in the First Seam and a few lineages — Cut the first way and keep it open while it scars. — Pain, a permanent mark, a fortnight of uselessness; a way once cut is never closed.\par

\textbf{Held} — Carries more than its natural measure; can clamp and graft. — Guild masters, factors, successful mercenaries — Raise the head slowly by tempering; carry more than feels safe until the walls learn it. — Grey fingertips, shortened temper, an extra appetite nobody can satisfy.\par

\textbf{Drawn} — Shapes and directs a flow outside the body. — Rare; the working core of the collectors and the better duellists — Learn to push the Warm out through an open way without losing the self’s shape. — The caster is cold after every working; someone must pay to rewarm them.\par

\textbf{Banked} — Stores Warm outside the body in clay or iron and spends it later or through another. — Rare; essentially the Court’s bankers and a few Vhaleni factors — Make a vessel that holds outside the skin and keep it from leaking. — The store is always someone’s warmth; the banker lives off the account, and the account is never his.\par

\textbf{Welled} — Draws on stores held in the world itself — wells, seams, another’s bank — without owning them. — Very rare; almost never admitted to in the Hearthseat — Follow a way out of the body into the world and find a source that answers. — The level drops for everyone; a district pays in cold. The Vorr say the drawer has borrowed from a debt that was not theirs.\par

\textbf{Hearthward} — Draws from the Hearth itself; never cold. — Handfuls; the great keepers and the favorites of the Court — Cut a way that reaches the Keel and hold it against the Hearth’s head. — A memory each drawing. A Hearthward practitioner is a library burning itself to light a room.\par

\textbf{Kept} — Effectively unbreakable; nothing leaks in or out. — Fewer than twenty in the world — The Court makes it, not the practitioner: perfect retention imposed from outside. — Everything. The Kept no longer feel cold, hunger, pain, or affection. Perfect retention is perfect numbness.\par

The order of breakthrough is always the same: stop the leak, open a way, raise the head, route the flow, store it, find a greater source, and last of all be sealed. No rank is skipped and none is bought with a wish. A man who tries to take a rank before his walls are ready cracks, and what leaks out of him is the shape of his former self.

\section*{VI. What the world believes and what is true}

The world believes — What is true\par

The world was fired whole and cracked once, in anger. — It was made cracked and repaired in layers; the oldest repairs are not clay.\par

The Return is the mercy of the Hearth; the dead come back by grace. — The Return is an industry. Collectors draw, bankers press, assessors deduct.\par

The deduction is one part in twelve. — In Low Kiln it is one in three, and the difference is the mechanism by which the poor are spent.\par

The Kept are the most blessed vessels in Sarn; they no longer feel the cold. — They feel nothing at all. The Final Banking is their condition extended to every vessel.\par

A person’s ways branch like a tree, as blood vessels do. — They run in slow spirals. Nobody has noticed in eight hundred years.\par

Ash is dirt that gathers where promises are unkept. — It is what is left of a self when the Warm has been taken. It remembers.\par

Fire is a gift of the Hearth. — Fire is the Warm escaping. The world bleeding upward.\par

The Hearth is a reservoir that holds what the world had when it was made. — The Hearth is a wound. The whole economy is salvage from a failing repair.\par

A whole return brings back the same person. — It brings back the shape the new walls can hold. Memory is the most expensive thing to keep.\par

\section*{VII. Secrets about power that the world gets wrong}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]

\item \textbf{The shape of the ways.} Everyone who has dissected a body has seen the spirals and written them off as the wanderings of a vessel. They are a map of the great crack under Sarn. Every vessel is a miniature of the world’s wound. The discovery comes late, by dissecting a saint whose body is said to hold no flaw at all.

\item \textbf{The Hearth is a wound, not a reservoir.} The Court’s entire economy is a salvage operation: because Warm leaks into the Waste, it must be forced into new vessels faster than it escapes, and every life is conscripted as mortar in a repair nobody will admit is failing. The oldest repairs under the world are made of unkept promises made solid — Ash as structure.

\item \textbf{Perfect retention is death without the courtesy.} The Court calls the Kept blessed and the Final Banking paradise. Both are the destruction of want, which is the same as the destruction of self. A world of unbreakable vessels would be a world of the walking dead, and the Unbanked have wanted it for centuries.

\item \textbf{The Afterdrop has two causes.} The ordinary one is a graft settling. The other is the Warm trying to resume the shape of its former owner. The Court struck the phrase \emph{Second Want} from the record in Y.H. 651 and has spoken only of the graft ever since.

\item \textbf{A working does not need to be done.} The most heretical fact in Sarn is that a dying person’s Warm can be left where it is and the world does not end. Nothing happens. That nothing is the crack in the whole structure.

\item \textbf{There is no such thing as a clean return.} Every pouring costs a piece of the shape. The rich pay to lose less; the poor are divided and lose everything. No institution has ever returned a person whole, however neat the handwriting.

\end{enumerate}

\section*{VIII. Combat}

There is no spellcasting. A fight is anatomy under pressure.

A practitioner wins because they know where the ways run close to the skin, not because they are stronger. In Keld a duel is fought with iron clamps: the first man to open a way in his own forearm usually wins, because the other is busy bleeding. A knife goes into the thin place; a clamp stops a flow; a graft taken from the wrong donor makes an enemy want a life that is not his and hesitate at the wrong moment. An overfilled vessel can be made to cook from within. Ash in the eyes blinds the clinical ear as surely as a fist. A drawn flow can push a man off a ledge or burn the surface of his arm, but the range is a few paces and the caster is cold and shaking afterwards.

Fights are short and clumsy: a blade, a grip, a fall, and a long undignified interval of dragging and breathing and trying to stop the bleeding. Dan wins by knowing where the vessel is thin. His amateur boxer’s chin lets him take one punch and wait. He will never be a fighter, only a man who knows what a fight feels like in the mouth. Monsters are anatomical problems before they are adversaries: a thing with its ways in the wrong place, a beast overfilled by a banker’s error. The horror is clinical and therefore worse.

\section*{IX. What is forbidden, and why}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]

\item \textbf{The Second Want induced deliberately} — grafting a wall whose measure was shaped differently into a person, to make them want a life that was not theirs. Forbidden because it produces a person who is a stranger in their own body. The Court suppresses the very name.

\item \textbf{The Final Banking and the quiet seal} — making every vessel perfectly retentive. Forbidden because perfect retention is perfect numbness, and the Unbanked have been called mad for wanting it. The Court’s sealed file says something else.

\item \textbf{Theft of warmth} — drawing Warm from another vessel without a slate. Punished by a cold iron brand on the wrist and a deduction from the thief’s own measure.

\item \textbf{Blent-work} — grafting beast-clay into human vessels. Condemned in the Hearthseat and practised in the back rooms of it and openly in Sere. The law says a blent person has the measure of the donor, which in practice means no measure at all.

\item \textbf{Quiet ash sold to the poor} — the Court licenses it only to the rich. Uns licensed ash is one of the few crimes whose penalty is a greyed hand.

\item \textbf{Refusing a lawful collection} — an unclosed account. The offender is clip-held by the wrist until consent.

\item \textbf{Drawing from the Hearth without a keeper’s key} — the Nine Wells are sealed and the keys are kept. A keeper who drew without authorisation was hanged in Y.H. 618.

\item \textbf{Drawing from a saint} — a saint’s body is held to have no flaw. It is never dissected. What is found when it is dissected is not written down.

\end{itemize}

\section*{X. The protagonist’s edge and his programme of discovery}

\section*{The edge}

Dan Marchetti was a trauma surgeon in Chicago for twelve years. His body of knowledge maps onto vessels with a closeness that is almost indecent:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]

\item \textbf{Walls and pressure} become the vessel’s walls and head.

\item \textbf{Bleeding} becomes leakage; packing a cavity becomes clamping a way.

\item \textbf{Sutures and grafts} become the staple and the living patch.

\item \textbf{Infection} becomes Ash in the joint — except that Ash is not a living thing and does not respond to any drug.

\item \textbf{Shock} becomes the hollow — except that a cold vessel is not a dry one and fluid will not fix it.

\item \textbf{The Afterdrop} is the rewarming patient who gets worse before getting better; he recognised it on the third night of his own new life, in an infant’s body, and was corrected by every midwife in the district.

\item \textbf{Triage} — the ability to look at a body and say \emph{this one is gone; leave it} — is the one thing no priest of the Hearth can do. In a world whose founding doctrine is that nothing is ever beyond keeping, this is heresy. It is also the instrument he was recalled to use.

\end{itemize}

His method is the real weapon. He observes, dissects, records with charcoal on slate, repeats, is wrong, corrects the note, and is wrong again. In a world of sacred craft, method is heresy. He does it anyway. His \textbf{clinical ear} hears a faulty vessel before he touches it; what the world calls a gift is twenty years of finding the thin place under the skin.

The map fails in the important places. There are no tissue planes in the Warm. Ash does not culture. A vessel has no heart. The places where the surgical analogy breaks are the places where the great discoveries live.

\section*{The discoveries, in rough order}

Tagged as \textbf{(T)} true, \textbf{(D)} dead end, \textbf{(F)} forbidden, \textbf{(P)} partial.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]

\item \textbf{(T)} A person’s ways coil; they do not branch. He assumes he has made an error and repeats the dissection. He has not.

\item \textbf{(T)} The full note is a physical fact, not a craft mysticism. A cracked vessel answers flat. He can hear it through a wall.

\item \textbf{(D)} \emph{Warm is heat in the ordinary sense; I can measure it with a warmed stone and a clock.} It is not, and the stone measures only its own loss.

\item \textbf{(T)} A clamp stops a flow and a man can survive it — briefly. The limb below goes the colour of old rope. This is a tourniquet with a different name.

\item \textbf{(T)} A dying person’s Warm may be left where it is. The world does not end. Nothing happens. This is the first heresy.

\item \textbf{(D)} \emph{Ash is a miasma from bad clay.} He tries to drain a corner, seal it, and keep a room clean. The Ash returns in a single night, heavier.

\item \textbf{(P)} Mould grown in a dark cup kills the rot in a wound sometimes and kills the patient sometimes. He cannot grow penicillin and will spend years learning why.

\item \textbf{(T)} The Afterdrop has two causes; the second is the old shape trying to come back. The midwives call it the second chill; the Court struck the other name from the record.

\item \textbf{(T)} A graft takes best when the donor’s measure is shaped like the recipient’s. A mismatched graft takes anyway, and wants.

\item \textbf{(D)} \emph{If I can cut a vessel open I can mend anything inside it.} Opening a way is permanent. Some things let out more than they let in.

\item \textbf{(T)} Overfilling cooks memory before it cooks the body. The calm of a beautiful return is a burn.

\item \textbf{(T)} The deduction in Low Kiln is one in three. The notices say one in twelve. The difference is written on no slate, which is how the Court likes its cruelty kept.

\item \textbf{(D)} \emph{Anaesthesia can be found in a weed.} He will try many. He will kill two patients with a tincture that numbs without making safe.

\item \textbf{(T)} The Ash is what is left of a self when the Warm has been taken. It remembers, though nobody has asked it what.

\item \textbf{(T)} A town is a vessel; its poor quarter is its crack. Cooling Low Kiln cools the whole account, and the gateway house does not notice for a season.

\item \textbf{(F)} A man can be sealed so that nothing leaks in or out. He tries it once, on a dying enemy, and learns why the Kept are spoken of with pity.

\item \textbf{(T)} The spiral in the body and the crack under the world are the same shape, repeated. The discovery comes by dissecting a saint.

\item \textbf{(T)} The Hearth is a wound, not a reservoir; the Keeping is salvage.

\item \textbf{(F)} The hot trace that brought him across can be followed back. Going back means drawing the warmth out of the woman the child in the minivan became. The mechanism is real. The account cannot be settled by dying again.

\item \textbf{(P)} Fire is escaping Warm. He can reason about it correctly and still cannot make a fire with his hands. Knowing a thing and doing a thing are different vessels.

\item \textbf{(T)} The oldest repairs are Ash made structural — unkept promises, made to hold. Both the Keldish and the Court’s translations were guesses; both were near.

\item \textbf{(D)} \emph{If I can understand the Deep Hand I can read the first account.} He cannot. The Vorr say only that the numbers are correct, which is not a translation.

\item \textbf{(T)} A Vorr’s measure is in the bone. This is why they are never collected and why they say \emph{we were never owed}.

\item \textbf{(T)} The Unbanked and the Court want the same ending by different methods: a world in which nothing can be given away.

\item \textbf{(F)} Blent-work: a beast’s clay grafted into a human wall increases capacity. It also gives the recipient the beast’s want. He will be tempted when his own walls are failing.

\item \textbf{(T)} The Kept feel nothing. Perfect retention is the destruction of want, which is the destruction of self.

\item \textbf{(D)} \emph{A great enough draw can empty the Waste and end the leak.} He tries to reason the current backward. He nearly cracks himself doing it, and learns that the Waste is not a place a person can stand against.

\item \textbf{(T)} He is not the first mender recalled. Across the chain of broken worlds there have been others: one made the repair beautiful and lied, one made it honest and let the world break, one became the Court.

\item \textbf{(T)} A repair can leave the crack visible and the Warm free to move. This is the third thing, and it is not a better clamp.

\item \textbf{(T)} Attention is the one thing no institution can tax. He keeps the notes. The notes survive him.

\end{enumerate}

\section*{The hard limits}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]

\item He is a clinician, not a physicist. His sense of the Warm is a surgeon’s intuition, not instrumentation. He can tell that a vessel is failing; he cannot tell him exactly why in numbers the Court will accept.

\item He cannot create Warm. He can only move it, and every move has a payer.

\item He cannot grow penicillin reliably, cannot anaesthetise safely with what grows in a field, cannot make a fire with his hands, cannot track a beast, cannot bargain for food, cannot read a Sarnish ledger when he arrives, and cannot make a native believe that a clamped vessel might survive.

\item His own vessel began cracked and low-capacity. Every working costs him head, and he pays in grey fingers, a bad knee, thinning sleep, and memory.

\item Method is heresy. A false measure entered on a slate costs the hand that wrote it. His experiments are done in secret, with a charcoal stick and a slate, and a wrong answer costs someone a hand, a memory, or a life.

\item The world’s own rules are partly wrong. The common account is a founding invoice. He must discover the laws while every institution teaches the opposite.

\item Scale defeats him. He is one tired man with a clinical ear; the Court is a ledger with a thousand years of entries. He cannot out-work a bank.

\item Who gets mended is decided by power, not truth. His discoveries do not change that. Only politics and money change that, and he has neither.

\item He is almost always the oldest person in the room and never the most powerful. He will be wrong repeatedly, and each wrong answer will be carried by somebody else.

\item Triage is his gift and his condemnation. To say \emph{leave it} in Sarn is to deny the world’s founding mercy. He will say it, and the world will remember.

\item The last account cannot be paid. The debt of the unsent call can only be carried, and carrying it is the work of a whole life.

\item He never becomes a god. He stays a large, tired man gone soft in the middle, with a click in one knee, surgeon’s hands on a boy’s small wrists, and a habit of counting the exits in every room.

\end{itemize}

\section*{The bestiary of Sarn}

\emph{An account for the reader of the Compendium: vermin, beasts, blights, spirits and calamities, in the order in which a traveller is likely to meet them. Threat is measured against the named ranks of vessels, which are trades rather than prizes. Truth is what is found when the thing is opened.}

\emph{The rule of Sarn’s monsters is the rule of its vessels: a monster is a vessel whose ways are in the wrong place, or a vessel filled past its proper measure by somebody else’s error. The horror is clinical.}

\section*{I. Vermin and small nuisances}

\section*{Ash-mite}

\textbf{Habitat.} The grey ridge along the skirting board in every poor room in Sarn; thickest in Low Kiln and the Empty Cantons. \textbf{Behaviour.} Swarms in the Ash where promises are unkept. Invisible in daylight, plain by tallow-light, and most active on the Last Day when the Ash is not swept. \textbf{How it fights.} It does not bite. It settles in the seams of a sleeper’s clothes and drinks the thin edge of the measure over weeks. A household with mites is a household that is quietly getting thinner. \textbf{Threat.} Thin. It cannot kill a whole man, but it can keep a poor one from ever becoming whole. \textbf{Uses and price.} Ground into a paste and sold by Fenfolk as \emph{quiet paste}, a cheap cousin of quiet ash that makes a person forget a year. A thimble costs four bits in Mud End and sixty marks in the Hearthseat, where it is taxed. \textbf{Folklore vs truth.} Folklore says it is dirt that breeds in cold. Truth: it eats the residue of selves and passes the memory on in its own body. A man who has been mite-bitten for a year will sometimes dream in another person’s voice.

\section*{Cupworm}

\textbf{Habitat.} Clay yards, pottery hamlets, Old Kiln. Bores through unfired and fired clay alike. \textbf{Behaviour.} Follows the damp. A cupworm in a drying rack will hollow a pot from the inside so that it looks sound and breaks at the first pouring. \textbf{How it fights.} It does not fight. It ruins. A vessel with a cupworm has a hidden flaw that no knuckle-tap will find until it is too late. \textbf{Threat.} Thin to Whole, to vessels rather than people. A kiln-house can be ruined by one summer of cupworm. \textbf{Uses and price.} The silk it spins seals hairline cracks in green clay and is worth two bits a yard in Nine Pots. The worm itself is fed to pigs. \textbf{Folklore vs truth.} Folklore says a cupworm is the ghost of a badly fired pot. Truth: it is a animal, blind, and it prefers clay that has been handled by many people, which is why the pots of a busy household go first.

\section*{Silt-leach}

\textbf{Habitat.} The shallows of the Ilm and Ferr, in summer when the water is low and smells of iron and mud. \textbf{Behaviour.} Attaches to the ankles of waders and to the legs of eel-trappers. It is barely visible against silt. \textbf{How it fights.} Sets its mouth against a surface way and draws Warm slowly, the way a collector’s tube does. A wader feels only cold in one foot that does not go away when he leaves the water. \textbf{Threat.} Thin to Held, depending on the number. A child can be emptied by a handful in an hour. \textbf{Uses and price.} Dried leeches are used by collectors to make a draw without a slate, which is theft and punishable by a greyed hand. Three bits a dozen in the Fishstairs, no questions. \textbf{Folklore vs truth.} Folklore says the leach takes the warmth and gives it to the river. Truth: it gives it to the Sallow draw, and the river is only the road.

\section*{Ice-mouse}

\textbf{Habitat.} Frozen fields, river ice, the eaves of Grath in Stone. \textbf{Behaviour.} Lives under ice and in the thatch. Comes into houses in the deep cold and nests in clothing. \textbf{How it fights.} Its bite is cold and leaves a small numb patch that spreads if the wound is not warmed. Frostbite by another road. A sleeping infant bitten six times will not wake. \textbf{Threat.} Thin to Whole. \textbf{Uses and price.} The fur is warm beyond its weight and is sewn into the linings of collectors’ coats, which is considered a joke by the collectors and not by the poor. A skin is worth one bit in Sill and a mark in Keld. \textbf{Folklore vs truth.} Folklore says the ice-mouse is the cold made small enough to be killed. Truth: it is a field mouse whose ways have been pushed outward by living under ice; its bite steals head rather than causing it.

\section*{Tallow-wasp}

\textbf{Habitat.} The Tallow Works, rendering sheds, anywhere sheep fat is rendered. \textbf{Behaviour.} Swarms in the sweet smoke. Nests in the warm cracks of rendering houses. \textbf{How it fights.} The sting swells the ways near the skin so that Warm pools and cannot circulate. A man stung twenty times becomes feverish and then cold, in that order. \textbf{Threat.} Thin to Held; a swarm can drop a strong man. \textbf{Uses and price.} The nests yield a resin that holds a graft until the vessel takes, much valued by bowl-surgeons in Sere at four marks a nest. In Grath the nests are burnt. \textbf{Folklore vs truth.} Folklore says the wasp is drawn to the smell of rendered fat. Truth: it is drawn to the warmth of the rendering, and the fat is only where the warmth has been.

\section*{II. Beasts of the bowl}

\section*{Reed-wolf}

\textbf{Habitat.} The reedbeds east of Mud End and Reedholt, and the banks of the lower Ilm. \textbf{Behaviour.} Hunts in packs of five to nine. Does not howl; it makes a low clicking with the tongue that carries over water. \textbf{How it fights.} Goes for the throat and the inside of the elbow, where a person’s ways run closest to the skin. It does not worry a victim; it opens one way and waits. \textbf{Threat.} Thin to Held. A pack can pull down a Held man in mud, which is why the reed-cutters go in pairs. \textbf{Uses and price.} The teeth are set into clamp-handles, for a tooth remembers the shape of a way. A good tooth is two marks in Stonemouth. The pelt is worthless: the hair carries Ash. \textbf{Folklore vs truth.} Folklore says the reed-wolf is a drowned man’s grudge. Truth: it is a wolf whose ways have been pushed outward by living in water, and it kills the way a collector draws — quickly, at one place, and without malice.

\section*{Glass eel}

\textbf{Habitat.} The Ilm below the sill, the Ferr, the eel traps at the Weir. Spawns in the Sallow and comes upriver in Ilmen. \textbf{Behaviour.} Nocturnal, drawn to lamplight and to warm stone. Lies in the silt of the Fishstairs in the first warm days of the year. \textbf{How it fights.} A discharge through the water that stops a man’s ways for a moment, like a clamp with no iron. Long enough to drown a wader; not long enough to kill a man on dry ground. \textbf{Threat.} Thin to Opened in water; nothing on land. \textbf{Uses and price.} The eels are eaten, smoked, and sold for three bits at the Fishstairs. The skin, dried, is used by Drawn practitioners to carry a flow a little farther than the hand. A skin costs a mark in Greybank. \textbf{Folklore vs truth.} Folklore says glass eels are the shadows of the star-rivets fallen into the river. Truth: the eel is an ordinary eel that has learned to store a little Warm in a sac behind the head, and the Sallow draw does the rest.

\section*{Brine-gull}

\textbf{Habitat.} The Sallow coast, the Glaze Cliffs, Vhal’s salt pans. \textbf{Behaviour.} A thief of anything warm: a baked loaf, a warmed stone, a coal that has not gone out. Flies inland as far as Greybank in hard winters. \textbf{How it fights.} It does not fight. It steals the thing a man is holding against the cold and leaves him colder than before. A flock over a fishing boat is a real danger in a Sallow winter. \textbf{Threat.} Thin; a nuisance to any vessel above it. On the water in numbers it can empty a small boat’s store. \textbf{Uses and price.} The feathers hold salt and are used to salt a wound, which is a brute method and often works. A bundle costs a bit in Vhal. The meat is not eaten; it is said to make a man thin. \textbf{Folklore vs truth.} Folklore says the brine-gull carries the dead out to the Sallow. Truth: it carries anything it can lift, and the salt on its feathers is from the pans, not from the sea’s dead.

\section*{Kiln-hound}

\textbf{Habitat.} Around old kilns and the Old Kiln south of Sill. Feral dogs that have lived near firing for generations. \textbf{Behaviour.} Keeps to the smoke side of a kiln. Will not cross running water, which is why the people of High Kiln consider the river a wall. \textbf{How it fights.} Its breath is hot and carries the kiln’s own poison; a man overcome by it wakes with a headache and a lasting thirst, or does not wake. It bites at the face when the man is down. \textbf{Threat.} Thin to Held; the breath kills a sleeping man of any rank who has not learned to keep a window open. \textbf{Uses and price.} The tongue, dried, is used by the Fenfolk to test a room for kiln’s breath, since it discolours. Worth two bits. The hide is too smoky to sell. \textbf{Folklore vs truth.} Folklore says the kiln-hound is the kiln’s own breath given legs. Truth: it is a dog, altered by living where the air is bad, and the danger is the air and not the dog.

\section*{Lead-rat}

\textbf{Habitat.} The Lead Run, the Ferr valley, the old workings above Stonemouth, the lead-glaze stores of High Kiln. \textbf{Behaviour.} Greedy for the sweet taste of lead glaze; gnaws the lips of cups and the seams of glazed bowls. \textbf{How it fights.} Its bite puts a little lead into the wound and makes the ways sluggish over a season. A poor household with rats in the cupboard is a household whose children are going thin by degrees. \textbf{Threat.} Thin; cumulative. No single bite matters and a hundred do. \textbf{Uses and price.} None. Rat-catchers in High Kiln are paid by the tail, two bits a score, and the tails are burnt. \textbf{Folklore vs truth.} Folklore says the lead-rat is the sweet sickness made small. Truth: both come from the same lead, and the glaze is the carrier in each case. This connection was made in Y.H. 641 and then forgotten, which is how Sarn forgets things.

\section*{Bone-hound}

\textbf{Habitat.} The Bone Orchard outside Sill’s north gate, the burial grounds of Greybank, the thin-return graves. \textbf{Behaviour.} Scavenges the shallow graves of children. Follows the smell of Ash rather than the smell of decay. \textbf{How it fights.} Does not attack the living unless they come between it and a grave. Its bite carries Ash from the bodies it has eaten, and the wound refuses to close. \textbf{Threat.} Thin to Held; chiefly a danger to gravediggers and to those who return at night to the orchard. \textbf{Uses and price.} The Ash in a bone-hound’s stomach is sold as cheap quiet ash in the Fenmarch, and is the reason so many Fen returns are thin. Five bits a phial, no questions asked. \textbf{Folklore vs truth.} Folklore says the bone-hound guards the graves. Truth: it has learned that the children in the shallow graves still have a little Warm in them, which is true, and it eats that first.

\section*{Fen-leech / Bog-cow}

\textbf{Habitat.} The peat bogs of the Fenmarch and the marsh east of Reedholt. \textbf{Behaviour.} A large, slow, horned beast that stands in peat until only the head shows. The peat keeps it warm and it does not move for days. \textbf{How it fights.} It lowers its head and pushes. In a bog a pushed man cannot brace, and a man who falls into peat with a bog-cow standing over him drowns slowly. It does not eat him; it simply holds him there until he stops wanting. \textbf{Threat.} Thin to Held; to Drawn and above it is an obstacle, not a fight. \textbf{Uses and price.} The hide, dried over peat, makes the best water-proof coat in Sarn, worth three marks in Vhal. The horns are carved into count-sticks by the Fen reeves. \textbf{Folklore vs truth.} Folklore says the bog-cow is the Marsh Mother’s own cattle. Truth: it is a beast that has found a place where the Warm comes up through the mud and has learned to stand still and take it in through the hide.

\section*{Warm-stone grub}

\textbf{Habitat.} The deep mines of Keld, in the seams where warm-stone is cut. \textbf{Behaviour.} Eats warm-stone from the inside. A block that looks sound will crumble to dust in a man’s hands if the grub has been at it. \textbf{How it fights.} It does not fight. A mine full of grubs is a mine that sweats in the wrong places, and a Warm-flood follows. The Keldish call the grub \emph{the mine’s own appetite}. \textbf{Threat.} Indirect; a calamity’s harbinger. Keldish mine-reeves will stop a shift for one grub. \textbf{Uses and price.} The grub is fried and eaten by the under-vessed slaves, who say it is the only warmth they are given that they get to keep. No market price. \textbf{Folklore vs truth.} Folklore says the grub is sent by the mountain to punish greedy digging. Truth: it is a beetle larva that has learned to digest stone which has held heat for a thousand years, and it is older than the mines.

\section*{III. Spirits, blights and the half-returned}

\section*{Hearth-wisp}

\textbf{Habitat.} Hearth-fires, banked coals, the Weighing Hall’s grating of cold iron. \textbf{Behaviour.} A small light that appears where a fire has been banked and left. It moves toward warmth and away from iron. \textbf{How it fights.} It sits on a sleeper’s chest and draws the fire out of him while he dreams that he is warm. In the morning he is cold and does not know why. A hearth-wisp in a child’s room is a common cause of the second chill, or so the midwives say. \textbf{Threat.} Thin to Whole; usually a nuisance, sometimes a death. \textbf{Uses and price.} A wisp can be coaxed into a clay cup and carried as a light that needs no fuel. Such cups are sold in Vhal for two marks and go cold after a tend. \textbf{Folklore vs truth.} Folklore says a hearth-wisp is the soul of a fire that was put out unkindly. Truth: it is a small gathering of Warm that has learned a shape and keeps it, which is almost the definition of a vessel. The Court’s keepers consider wisps vermin and crush them.

\section*{Ash-wraith}

\textbf{Habitat.} Where many have been spent: the Old Kiln, the Empty Cantons, the corners of houses in Low Kiln after a levy, the Bone Orchard. \textbf{Behaviour.} A grey shape seen from the corner of the eye by tallow-light. It does not approach a person who is looking at it. It follows the Ash ridge from room to room. \textbf{How it fights.} It presses against the sleeper’s measure where the wall is thinnest and tries on the shape of the life that made the Ash. A man who dreams of someone else’s childhood wakes with that childhood available to him for a day. This is called ash-sickness in the Cantons. \textbf{Threat.} Thin to Held; a handful of wraiths can unmake a person’s memory over a winter. \textbf{Uses and price.} The Ash they leave behind is the strongest quiet ash there is, and a jar of it is worth twenty marks to the right buyer in the Hearthseat. The right buyer is always rich. \textbf{Folklore vs truth.} Folklore says the wraith is the ghost of a person who was returned thin and remembers being someone. Truth: it is what was left of the self when the Warm was taken, and it has learned to move. It remembers because selfhood does not evaporate.

\section*{The Second}

\textbf{Habitat.} Any house where a graft was set from a donor whose measure was shaped differently. Commonest in Sere and in the back rooms of the Hearthseat. \textbf{Behaviour.} A person who walks and speaks and has a name, who wants a life that was not theirs. The want arrives in the Afterdrop and does not leave. Sometimes the person is the donor and sometimes the recipient; the law does not ask which. \textbf{How it fights.} It does not usually fight. It hesitates at the wrong moment, reaches for a hand that is not there, and says a word in a language the household does not know. In a crisis it may open a way in its own forearm and not know why. \textbf{Threat.} Measured as Thin to Held, but the danger is to the idea of a self rather than to a body. The Court strikes the word from its records and calls the condition a settling of the graft. \textbf{Uses and price.} In Sere, a Second with a beautiful face is exhibited at feasts and given small coins. Its parts are not sold; a person is not a part, in law, if they have no measure. \textbf{Folklore vs truth.} Folklore says the Second is a person returned twice by mistake. Truth: it is a Second Want with a body, which is a different thing and worse, because it is someone who wants and cannot say what.

\section*{Seam-blight}

\textbf{Habitat.} Old repairs: the rim-seam, the under-seam, the walls of very old houses, the clay around the Old Kiln. \textbf{Behaviour.} Travels along a crack and widens it by a hair’s breadth each season. It is not alive in any ordinary sense. It is the repair failing. \textbf{How it fights.} It does not fight. A house with seam-blight loses heat through the wall in winter and is eventually condemned, which in Grath means the household is moved and the crack is left where it is. \textbf{Threat.} To places, not people, until the place fails. A seam-blight in the rim-seam is a calamity measured in years. \textbf{Uses and price.} The grey dust scraped from a blighted seam is used by the Vorr to mark counting-stones. No market price; the Vorr do not sell it, only numbers. \textbf{Folklore vs truth.} Folklore says the blight is the First Breaking continuing to happen. Truth: it is a patch coming loose, which is the same thing said by someone who has looked closely.

\section*{Frost-bloom}

\textbf{Habitat.} Frozen ground, the banks of the Ilm in Stone, the wall of a room where a thin person has slept for a winter. \textbf{Behaviour.} Grows overnight in the shape of a branching flower on any surface where Warm has recently left. It is ice, but ice in a pattern that no cold alone would make. \textbf{How it fights.} It does not fight. Where it grows, the wall holds less. A household that finds frost-bloom on the inside of its outer wall will be cold all winter no matter how much fuel it buys. \textbf{Threat.} To vessels that are places. A frost-bloom on a person’s skin around a graft means the graft is failing. \textbf{Uses and price.} The patterns are copied by potters onto kiln apples and by the Vorr into their counting, for a frost-bloom is a map of the leak that made it. No price. \textbf{Folklore vs truth.} Folklore says frost-bloom is the cold’s own flower. Truth: it is the shape of the Warm that has gone — the branching map of a loss. Dan will one day look at a frost-bloom on a saint’s cold skin and recognise the spiral.

\section*{The Hollow (thin-child)}

\textbf{Habitat.} The poor districts of every town; the waiting rooms of the Court; thin-return children’s graves. \textbf{Behaviour.} A child returned so thin that there is almost no shape in it. It eats, breathes, and looks at people, but does not want anything. It is not ill by any physician’s measure. \textbf{How it fights.} It does not fight. Its danger is to the adults around it: a household with a hollow child will spend warmth on it that the household cannot afford, for years, to keep something that does not know it is being kept. \textbf{Threat.} None to a vessel; a great deal to an account. \textbf{Uses and price.} None. The Court books a hollow child as a successful return. Its parents are told the account stands. \textbf{Folklore vs truth.} Folklore says a hollow child is one whose name was written wrongly. Truth: it is several people divided among one body, so that none of them is present enough to want. It is the poor person’s version of a whole return, and the Court’s cheapest product.

\section*{The Uncollected}

\textbf{Habitat.} Anywhere a person has died without a collection: battlefields, the Waste, the road between Sill and Greybank, the far bogs. \textbf{Behaviour.} A dead vessel whose Warm has not been drawn walks until the Warm leaks out into the Waste. It does not speak and does not seem to know it is dead. It walks in the direction it was facing when it broke. \textbf{How it fights.} It pushes. A man who is pushed by an uncollected thing feels a cold place on his chest for days afterwards and may find a little of his measure missing. \textbf{Threat.} Thin to Held; more frightening than dangerous. The Court sends no collectors into the Waste partly because there is nothing there worth collecting and partly because the uncollected there do not stop. \textbf{Uses and price.} There is no market. The Ash they leave is pure and is gathered by Wastelings, who give it away. \textbf{Folklore vs truth.} Folklore says the uncollected are the dead who were too poor to be returned. Truth: that is exactly right, which is why hearth-maids cross themselves at a birth-flower and say nothing.

\section*{Salt-thing}

\textbf{Habitat.} The cold tides of the Sallow, the Glaze Cliffs, the salt pans of Vhal in winter. \textbf{Behaviour.} A shape made of salt and cold water that follows a boat at night and taps the hull where the Warm is stored. \textbf{How it fights.} It draws through the hull, the way a silt-leach draws through an ankle. A boat’s store can be emptied in a night. Sailors who sleep on deck wake with grey fingertips. \textbf{Threat.} Thin to Banked, to stores rather than to people. A salt-thing cannot cross fresh water, which is why Vhaleni captains carry a barrel of rain. \textbf{Uses and price.} Salt taken from a salt-thing’s body is the finest curing salt in Sarn, worth four bits a pound in Vhal. It is said to keep a wound from turning. \textbf{Folklore vs truth.} Folklore says the salt-thing is a drowned sailor who could not pay the ferryman. Truth: it is a gathering of Sallow salt around a small knot of Warm that has forgotten how to be a vessel. Dippers bless nets against it, which is a reasonable thing to do.

\section*{Mire-fever}

\textbf{Habitat.} The Fenmarch; the marsh east of Reedholt; anywhere the peat has been drained badly. \textbf{Behaviour.} Not a creature but a blight of the water. A person who drinks or wades develops a fever that burns and then leaves them colder than before, each cycle taking a little head. \textbf{How it fights.} It fights by being endured. There is no treatment in Sarn beyond keeping the patient warm, which the poor cannot do. \textbf{Threat.} Thin to Held; a Held vessel can survive three cycles where a Thin one dies in the first. \textbf{Uses and price.} Fever-mud from a patient’s bed is used by Fenfolk to seal a peat wall, because it dries hard. No price worth naming. \textbf{Folklore vs truth.} Folklore says the Marsh Mother sends the fever to those who drain her peat. Truth: draining the peat lets something in the mud that was formerly held down come up into the water, and the fever is that thing’s appetite.

\section*{Sweet-sickness}

\textbf{Habitat.} High Kiln, the Glaze Cliffs, any house with lead-glazed cups. \textbf{Behaviour.} A blight of the glaze. The first sign is a pale line at the gum; the end is a man who cannot hold a cup. Takes two to five years. \textbf{How it fights.} It does not fight; it accumulates. Each year the ways run a little slower and the hands shake a little more, until the vessel cannot keep a grip on anything, including itself. \textbf{Threat.} All ranks; no vessel is immune, and the rich have the sweetest cups. \textbf{Uses and price.} None. The cure is to stop using the glaze, which no pottery household in Sarn can afford to do. \textbf{Folklore vs truth.} Folklore says the sickness is the price of beauty. Truth: it is lead, and the lead was noted in Y.H. 641, blamed on the glaze, and then quietly unlearned. The Court’s finest ware is still glazed with it.

\section*{Kiln’s breath}

\textbf{Habitat.} Enclosed kiln houses, cellars, any room with a fire that has not enough air. \textbf{Behaviour.} An invisible blight. It kills in sleep and is called a merciful death by people who have not had to identify the body. \textbf{How it fights.} It does not fight. A man goes to bed and does not wake; his lips are a little too red, which is all the evidence there is. \textbf{Threat.} All ranks equally, which makes it the most democratic danger in Sarn. \textbf{Uses and price.} None. Kiln-hounds are said to smell it, which is the only useful thing about them. \textbf{Folklore vs truth.} Folklore says the kiln takes a life in exchange for a good firing. Truth: it is the fire using up the air in a closed room, and the potters know this and leave the window open anyway, because a draft spoils the glaze.

\section*{IV. Monsters and calamities}

\section*{Seam-serpent}

\textbf{Habitat.} The under-seam; the deep shaft of the First Seam; the cracks beneath the Nine Wells. \textbf{Behaviour.} A long thing that moves along a crack the way an eel moves along water. It is rarely seen whole. Keldish surveyors have entered its passing as a change in the numbers. \textbf{How it fights.} It presses against a way from the far side of a wall and draws Warm out through the stone. A miner who has been “touched by the seam” will be thin by the end of the shift. \textbf{Threat.} Held to Welled; to a single man it is a cold place in the rock. To a mine it is a slow emptying. \textbf{Uses and price.} The shed skin is sometimes found in the First Seam and is said to be the oldest repair material nobody will name. The Vorr will look at it and say only that the numbers are correct. \textbf{Folklore vs truth.} Folklore says the serpent is the First Breaking still moving. Truth: it is a vessel that lives in the world’s crack and drinks what the world loses, which is a useful arrangement for everyone except the world.

\section*{The Sweating}

\textbf{Habitat.} The deep mines of Keld; the hot seams under the Rim. \textbf{Behaviour.} A calamity rather than a creature: the Warm comes up through the stone in a lethal flood, and the only warning is the smell of hot lead. \textbf{How it fights.} It fills the shaft. Men in the deep cannot climb out fast enough. The Keldish mine-reeves count every life that goes down and every ember of iron that comes up, and the two numbers are never the same. \textbf{Threat.} All ranks; Hearthward and above can survive it briefly by drawing from the flood instead of being drowned in it, which is one reason the Court employs them. \textbf{Uses and price.} The stone after a sweating is rich in Warm and is cut into warm-stone that holds heat for a generation. A single block is worth ten marks on the Keel. \textbf{Folklore vs truth.} Folklore says the mountain sweats when it is angry. Truth: it is the Rim current finding a thin place in the wall, and a warm-stone grub is usually found in the seam afterwards.

\section*{Cold-eater}

\textbf{Habitat.} The Waste, and the last passes beyond the Rim. \textbf{Behaviour.} A Wasteling shape, tall and thin, that moves toward any vessel with a head. It has no language and no appetite a visitor can recognise. It does not kill; it takes. \textbf{How it fights.} It comes close and stands. The visitor’s Warm goes out to it, slowly at first and then faster. Travellers who go far enough into the Waste come back older and thinner and do not remember what they went for. A cold-eater that follows a man back over the Rim can empty a house in a tend. \textbf{Threat.} Drawn to Hearthward; a whole village may be needed to drive one off. The Court sends no collectors into the Waste, and the Wastelings do not fight them, because there is nothing there worth owning. \textbf{Uses and price.} Nothing that can be counted. A Wasteling may give a stone, a name, or a day’s warmth and expect nothing back, which is how the Unbanked differ from the uncollected. \textbf{Folklore vs truth.} Folklore says the cold-eater is the Waste itself come to fetch what is owed. Truth: it is a vessel that has leaked so completely that it has become a sink, and what it takes goes nowhere. This is the closest thing in Sarn to a true monster.

\section*{Mender’s failure}

\textbf{Habitat.} Not fixed. Appears in the records of the chain of broken worlds, and occasionally in the present one. \textbf{Behaviour.} A person with a surgeon’s attention and no surgeon’s mercy, recalled in an earlier age to solve the same problem. One made the repair beautiful and lied about it. One made it honest and let the world break. One became the Court. \textbf{How it fights.} It does not fight; it builds. It makes clamps, slates, institutions, and worlds that hold. The mark it leaves is a branching red flower across the ribs and forearm, and the same mark appears on men who have never met each other. \textbf{Threat.} Beyond Kept in consequence, though its personal vessel may be no stronger than a Held man’s. \textbf{Uses and price.} None. The attempt to use one as a tool is the whole history of the Unbanked. \textbf{Folklore vs truth.} Folklore has no name for it. The Keldish call such marks birth-flowers and consider them unlucky. The truth is that the process has been run before, and the failures were not killed. They became the machinery.

\section*{Hearth-tongue}

\textbf{Habitat.} The Weighing Hall, behind the grating of cold iron, where the flame has never gone out. \textbf{Behaviour.} The Hearth-fire drawn up through the Keel. It has never been touched by a priest and has never gone out. When a keeper draws on it wrongly it reaches back. \textbf{How it fights.} It takes a memory from the drawer and gives nothing in exchange. A Hearthward practitioner who draws too often becomes a polite, empty person who no longer knows why they came into the room. The Court calls this a vocation. \textbf{Threat.} Hearthward and below; even the Kept are not asked to stand near it. \textbf{Uses and price.} The fire heats the Hearthseat and lights the Weighing. The price is paid by whoever holds the keys to the Nine Wells, and by the Kept, who are kept near it because they cannot enjoy it. \textbf{Folklore vs truth.} Folklore says the tongue of the Hearth is the breath of the Nine Who Kept. Truth: it is the leak made visible, and the room it burns in is the mouth of a wound.

\section*{Star-rivet}

\textbf{Habitat.} The rim-seam; the sky, where the stars are the rivet-heads of the great repair. One is said to have fallen in the Ash Times. \textbf{Behaviour.} A rivet that has worked loose and come down through the sky. It is old, bright with handling, and too hot to hold for long. \textbf{How it fights.} It does not fight. Where it lands, the ground holds more than it should for a season and then less than it should forever. The clay around the Old Kiln will not take a glaze, and the ash in its throat is deeper than a man is tall. \textbf{Threat.} A calamity to a place; nothing to a person who stays away. \textbf{Uses and price.} A fragment of a rivet would be the most valuable object in Sarn, if anyone could bear to look for one. No market has ever existed in it. \textbf{Folklore vs truth.} Folklore says the stars are rivets driven by the Nine Who Kept to hold the lid on the clay. Truth: the seam shows many repairs laid over one another, and the oldest of them are not made of anything that can be named. The rivets are part of a repair that failed before Sarn was made.

\section*{The factions of Sarn}

\emph{An account for the reader of the Compendium: the powers that teach the Warm, the powers that keep it, the powers that buy it, and the powers that have decided what should be done with it. Every faction is given its public face and its private one. Where a leader is named, the name is the one that appears on the slate.}

\emph{Sarn has no dark lord. Its most frightening institution is a reasonable body of men and women with a ledger and a good reason. The most dangerous faction in the world wants to save it by making every vessel unbreakable, and it has been called mad for eight hundred years.}

\CRrule

\section*{I. The powers that keep and teach}

\section*{The Warm Court — the Keeping}

\textbf{Kind.} Bank, church and government in one; the realm’s central power. \textbf{Seat.} The Hearthseat on the Keel; its nearest physical presence in Grath is the factor at Greybank. \textbf{Named leaders.} \textbf{Steward Derren}, head of the Keeping; \textbf{Assessor-Marshal Pell Varr}, who put down the Ash Rising in Y.H. 668; \textbf{Collector Marra}, who writes measures and has begun to go grey at the fingertips. \textbf{Goals.} To keep the world alive by banking warmth faster than it leaks into the Waste, and to record every measure so that nothing is lost that has been kept. \textbf{Methods.} Assessment, collection, banking, deduction, the Return. Its violence is paperwork: the slate is read aloud, the account stands, and a man who refuses is clip-held by the wrist until he consents. \textbf{Resources.} The Hearth, the Nine Wells (five sealed), the banks at the Hearthseat and Greybank, the Kept vessels, the rolls of the dead kept open forever, and the loyalty of officers who have no family but their account. \textbf{Enemies.} The Waste. The Unbanked, publicly described as mad. A hot trace that will not cool. A man who looks at a dying body and says, plainly, \emph{leave it}. \textbf{Hidden agenda.} To preserve the institution, which it has learned to identify with the world. The Court does not believe the world can be saved; it believes the counting can be continued. Those are different things, and the difference is the whole of its policy.

\section*{The College of Assessors}

\textbf{Kind.} Academy and gatekeeper; the school where the value of a life is taught. \textbf{Seat.} Slate Row in the Hearthseat, with an assessor’s house in Sill built in Y.H. 776. \textbf{Named leaders.} \textbf{Provost Wessen}, who teaches Ledgerhand and the eleven words for a deduction; the Sill assessor, unnamed on the local slate. \textbf{Goals.} To value lives accurately and to keep the valuation method in the hands of the College alone. \textbf{Methods.} A three-year apprenticeship in Ledgerhand; the passive voice; the annual Weighing; a slate-name entered on the rolls in place of a birth-name. Graduates are given no house and no kin. \textbf{Resources.} The rolls, the Weighing notices, the sealed files, and the exclusive right to write a lawful deduction. \textbf{Enemies.} The Vorr, who recognise the numbers in the oldest slates and will not translate them. The Deep Hand. Anyone who can add. \textbf{Hidden agenda.} The College knows the printed figure of one in twelve is false. It teaches the true method to its own and prints the false one for everyone else. The calculation that a poor district can be spent is taught as an exercise.

\section*{The Order of the Tenth Clamp}

\textbf{Kind.} Martial order and masters’ lineage; the gatekeeper of the art of Cutting. \textbf{Seat.} The First Seam beneath Keld Keep, with a duelling floor cut into the stone. \textbf{Named leaders.} \textbf{Hald Iron}, master of the floor; \textbf{Siv of the First Seam}, who keeps the crossed-out survey of Y.H. 588. \textbf{Goals.} To hold the passes, to keep the method of opening a way in the forearm within Keldish hands, and to make sure no lowlander learns it for free. \textbf{Methods.} Duels with iron clamps, a measure stated before a name, the tattoo of the number at thirteen, and a trial of hot iron for honesty which has produced a great many honest men with dead hands. \textbf{Resources.} The deep shafts, the warm-stone workings, Keldish mercenaries, and the only reliable teaching lineage for the first way in the world. \textbf{Enemies.} The Court’s assessors, whom they distrust without ever arguing with them. The Warm-stone grub. The Sweating. \textbf{Hidden agenda.} The Order has kept a copy of Haldre’s suppressed paper since Y.H. 710 and reads it annually. They do not intend to use it. They intend to be the only people who know it exists.

\section*{The Kiln-Masters of Sill}

\textbf{Kind.} Craft guild; the gatekeeper of Holding in Grath. \textbf{Seat.} The Cross and the drying racks of the Clay Yard, Low Kiln. \textbf{Named leaders.} \textbf{Berr Clay, the Thane of Clay}, confirmed by the Court’s factor; \textbf{Sella Vail}, who keeps the Vail fire and has a widow’s mark. \textbf{Goals.} To keep the kilns firing, the First Firing meaningful, and the knowledge of the full note within the families that have earned it. \textbf{Methods.} Apprenticeship, the knock of the knuckle on a pot, the levy of clay and labour, and a long local memory for which household owes which. \textbf{Resources.} Three kiln-yards, the slip pits, the clay roads, and the Vail household’s standing across four generations. \textbf{Enemies.} Nine Pots, in a feud over the road that outlives the men who started it. The cupworm. The Court’s levy of warmth, which is heavier than the levy of clay. \textbf{Hidden agenda.} The masters know that a child with a clinical ear is worth more than a child with a good hand, and they have quietly watched Corin since the First Firing. They do not know what he is. They know what he can hear.

\section*{The Hearth-Maids}

\textbf{Kind.} Female order of midwives, collectors’ assistants and keepers of the birth-slates. \textbf{Seat.} Scattered through every Grathic village; the birth-slate book is kept in the hearth-maid’s own house. \textbf{Named leaders.} \textbf{Alda Reed}, who attended Corin’s birth, crossed herself at the birth-flower, and told nobody what she had seen. \textbf{Goals.} To deliver children, record births within three days, and keep the small domestic knowledge of the afterdrop and the warmed stone alive. \textbf{Methods.} Practical instruction in proverbs and half-sentences; the stone against the belly; the slate; the crossed sign at a birth-flower. \textbf{Resources.} Access to every newborn vessel in Grath, the trust of households that would not let an officer into the room, and the memory of the second chill before the Court struck its name from the record. \textbf{Enemies.} The collectors, who arrive with a tube and take the mother’s attention. The College, which tried in Y.H. 782 to make every birth a Court entry and succeeded by paying them. \textbf{Hidden agenda.} The maids have kept a second set of notes since Y.H. 651: every infant who grew worse on the third day and spoke words in an unknown language. The notes are not in the Court’s file. Alda Reed’s is the fullest.

\section*{The Vorr of the Listening Stones}

\textbf{Kind.} Ancient people and keepers of numbers; a lineage rather than a school. \textbf{Seat.} The Listening Stones, five leagues above Keld Keep; a few seats at the Court’s survey table. \textbf{Named leaders.} \textbf{Ao of the Third Seam}, \textbf{Iyn of the Listening Stone}, \textbf{Eru Who Counts}. \textbf{Goals.} To sit until the stones give up their numbers and to keep the record of the repairs without selling it. \textbf{Methods.} The Long Sitting at forty; a life’s patience; answers of one sentence, usually \emph{the numbers are correct}, which is not a translation. \textbf{Resources.} The Deep Hand’s numerals, a memory of the unnumbered ages, and four thousand people who cannot be banked in the ordinary way. \textbf{Enemies.} The Court, which employs them and watches them. Anyone impatient. The age of the world, which is longer than any Vorr’s attention. \textbf{Hidden agenda.} They know the oldest repairs are not clay and have known for centuries. They will not say what they are, because the last surveyor who wrote it down was removed from the shaft in Y.H. 717.

\section*{The Dippers of Vhalen}

\textbf{Kind.} Salt priesthood and practical school of tide-lore. \textbf{Seat.} The salt pans outside Vhal, and the Council’s tar-smelling room in bad weather. \textbf{Named leaders.} \textbf{Neri Sail}, chief dipper; \textbf{Kett of the Quay}, who keeps the tide-tallies. \textbf{Goals.} To bless the nets, read the Sallow draw, and keep Vhalen’s warmth from going out with the cold tides. \textbf{Methods.} Trailing a net in the sea at dawn; contracts on salt-paper that rot in the rain, which is considered a virtue; a law that lasts too long is a law that has stopped earning. \textbf{Resources.} The pans, the boats, the glass of the Glaze Cliffs, and a people who can turn a wet document into a fortune. \textbf{Enemies.} The Court’s assessors with their lists of deductions. The salt-thing. The winter. \textbf{Hidden agenda.} The dippers know that Vhalen’s Sallow has no separate Hearth, whatever the priests say. They have measured the draw. They tell the Council only what is profitable.

\section*{II. Houses, churches and powers}

\section*{House Vail of Low Kiln}

\textbf{Kind.} Kiln-house; the closest thing Grath has to a noble family. \textbf{Seat.} The Vail kiln-house in the Clay Yard, Low Kiln, beside the Slip Brook. \textbf{Named leaders.} \textbf{Sella Vail}, keeper of the fire; \textbf{Jorra Vail}, her son; the household’s dead, whose names are kept on the roll. \textbf{Goals.} To hold the fire, keep the wheel, and see that a Vail name is written on the birth-slate of every child born under the Vail roof. \textbf{Methods.} A widow’s mark, four generations of standing, inheritances settled by who has the stronger hands, and a practical refusal to discuss anything that matters. \textbf{Resources.} One kiln, the slip pits, a house of clay-daubed rooms, and the attention of the whole district. \textbf{Enemies.} The Court, which levy one in three. Nine Pots. The cupworm. The Ash ridge that grows along the skirting board in a bad year. \textbf{Hidden agenda.} Sella took Corin in as a foundling because a slate-name with no house can be used by anyone. She has kept the boy close and has never once asked where his memory came from. She is waiting to see what he will become.

\section*{The Sunbowl houses of Sere}

\textbf{Kind.} Great slave-owning houses; the nobility of the southern empire. \textbf{Seat.} The Bowl of Sere, three hundred leagues south of Sill; provincial bowls throughout the empire. \textbf{Named leaders.} \textbf{Alessa Sunbowl}, \textbf{Rial of the Ten Bowls}, \textbf{Ilyra Gilded}. \textbf{Goals.} To empty vessels gloriously, to own beautiful returned slaves as proof of generosity, and to keep the northern measures flowing south at a price no poor household can refuse. \textbf{Methods.} The public Bowl feast, the burning of debt-slates by lot, open chattel slavery, and a law in which \emph{measure for measure} means whatever the speaker has already decided. \textbf{Resources.} Grain, wine, oil, gold crowns, the largest slave population in the world, and the warmest funerals in Sarn, which can ruin a house. \textbf{Enemies.} The Court’s deduction on their grain. The Fenmarch at the Peat Line. Any slave who remembers enough to want something. \textbf{Hidden agenda.} The houses buy poor measures to make beautiful whole returns for their own exhibits, which means a Sere household is a machine for turning a district of thin people into one decorative person. The order is intentional and is never spoken of in the counting room, where a woman’s numbers must be read by a man.

\section*{The Temple of the Return}

\textbf{Kind.} Public church of the Keeping; the faith taught to children. \textbf{Seat.} Temples in every town; the Weighing Hall in the Hearthseat is its altar. \textbf{Named leaders.} \textbf{Hesta Clay, Voice of the Return}, with the keepers of the Hearth-fire beneath her. \textbf{Goals.} To teach that the world was made whole, cracked once, and is being mended by the mercy of the Hearth; to make the Return the first thing a child learns and the last thing a dying person hears. \textbf{Methods.} Temple walls showing the single breaking; the Nine Who Kept as figures of pity and reverence; the phrase \emph{nothing is lost that we have kept}; the fire behind a grating that no priest is allowed to touch. \textbf{Resources.} Every childhood in Sarn, the Five Cold Days, and an institution that has never needed an argument because it got there first. \textbf{Enemies.} The surveyors of Keld, who have seen the layers in the rim-seam. The Deep Hand. Anyone who reads the oldest repairs as language. \textbf{Hidden agenda.} The founding story is an invoice. The temple’s own oldest slate shows the repairs laid one over another, and the oldest of all are not the work of any god that can be named. The temple keeps the slate face down.

\section*{The Marsh Mother}

\textbf{Kind.} Peat cult of the Fenmarch; a church of the mud. \textbf{Seat.} The Standing Post and the peat-dries; rites performed face down in peat. \textbf{Named leaders.} \textbf{Bogga of the Reed}, \textbf{Mudge Peat}, who sells the Ash the Court will not license. \textbf{Goals.} To keep warmth in the mud and to take it back when the household needs it; to sell cheap thin returns and ask no questions about the source. \textbf{Methods.} Lying face down in peat until the cold becomes ordinary; knot-sticks instead of writing; a child named by its mother before the collector arrives, which is the closest thing the Fenmarch has to a declaration of war. \textbf{Resources.} The peat-dries, the smugglers’ paths, cheap returns at a third of the Court’s price, and a population that can identify a collector by his boots before it can name its own children. \textbf{Enemies.} The draining of the fens. The Court’s lists. A winter in which the peat does not dry. \textbf{Hidden agenda.} The cult knows where the Ash goes, because it sells the Ash. It does not care what the Ash is, which is why it has survived where more squeamish institutions have not.

\section*{The Spending}

\textbf{Kind.} Fire church of Sere; the doctrine of glorious emptying. \textbf{Seat.} Open courts with a fire in the middle, in every bowl of the empire. \textbf{Named leaders.} \textbf{Valen Openhand, the Bowl-Priest}. \textbf{Goals.} To teach that a vessel was made to be emptied and that a life hoarded is a life wasted. \textbf{Methods.} Feasting, the emptying of a public bowl of wine to announce an act of state, and the burning of one household’s debt-slates by lot at the end of a feast, after which new ones are made. \textbf{Resources.} Wine, music, public fires, and an audience that applauds a debt carried as heartily as a debt forgiven. \textbf{Enemies.} The northern habit of calling Sere spendthrifts. The Court’s tax on funeral ash. \textbf{Hidden agenda.} The Spending teaches that emptiness is generous so that the poor will volunteer to be emptied. The priests who teach it are among the fullest vessels in the empire.

\section*{The Council of Tides}

\textbf{Kind.} Merchant republic; the foreign power of the Sallow coast. \textbf{Seat.} Vhal, in a room that smells of tar and wet rope. \textbf{Named leaders.} \textbf{Avis Wake}, \textbf{Mav Hull}, \textbf{Dolf Brine}, elected for a year at a time by ship-owners and salt-masters. \textbf{Goals.} To keep the salt and glass trades moving, to avoid being taxed into the Keeping, and to make sure no law lasts long enough to stop earning. \textbf{Methods.} Contracts written on salt-paper that rot in damp, which is considered a virtue; the pulled benches for condemned criminals; a slave who can be bought in Vhal but cannot be returned there. \textbf{Resources.} Ships, salt, glass, rope, dried fish, and a council that can turn a wet document into a fortune or the loss of one. \textbf{Enemies.} The Court’s assessors. The winter. The salt-thing. Sere’s buyers when they arrive with gold. \textbf{Hidden agenda.} The Council has never paid for a thin return and never will, which is why returned slaves are not housed in Vhalen. It has calculated that a person with no account is cheaper than a person with one, and calls this civility.

\section*{III. Criminals, rebels and secret societies}

\section*{The Ash-runners}

\textbf{Kind.} Criminal network; smugglers of warmth and quiet ash. \textbf{Seat.} The Fenmarch boardwalks and the Waiting Room at Sill, where a clerk can be paid to look away. \textbf{Named leaders.} \textbf{Slog Eel}, \textbf{Hobb Mud}, and a collector’s clerk in Slate Row whose name is not on any roll. \textbf{Goals.} To move Warm where the Court would not allow it and to sell forgetting to anyone who can pay. \textbf{Methods.} Cheap thin returns with no questions; quiet ash by the thimble; bone-hound ash from the shallow graves; a slate corrected in a second hand. \textbf{Resources.} The Fen paths, the peat-dries, a market in forgotten lives, and the complicity of people whose accounts are already damaged beyond repair. \textbf{Enemies.} The Court, which punishes the unlicensed sale of quiet ash with a greyed hand. The collectors, who are sometimes customers. \textbf{Hidden agenda.} One of their customers in Y.H. 812 paid for a whole return with the warmth of forty children of Low Kiln, and the runner who carried the slate has never been able to spell the buyer’s name. The name was Corin.

\section*{The Sere factors}

\textbf{Kind.} Slavers and buyers of measures; the empire’s advance guard in the north. \textbf{Seat.} A counting house in Greybank and a shipping quay in Vhal; the Bowl of Sere behind them. \textbf{Named leaders.} \textbf{Ilyra Gilded}, \textbf{Rial of the Ten Bowls}, with agents in Low Kiln who never give their names. \textbf{Goals.} To buy poor measures at prices no household can refuse and ship them south as beautiful whole returns for the great houses. \textbf{Methods.} Credit, the kindness of advances, the purchase of a debt before it is due, and the quiet conversion of a district into one decorated person. \textbf{Resources.} Gold crowns, grain, and the cooperation of households that have already sold next year’s warmth. \textbf{Enemies.} Grath, which hates Sere traders and sells to them anyway. The Court, which fought a thirty-one-year War of Deductions over the same trade. \textbf{Hidden agenda.} A factor does not want the poor person’s warmth for its own sake. He wants the shape of it, because a beautiful whole return must remember enough to be exhibited, and only certain lives make a good show.

\section*{The Broken Slates}

\textbf{Kind.} Rebel network in Low Kiln; descendants of the Ash Rising. \textbf{Seat.} The back rooms of the Vail kiln-house and the Fishstairs in Sill. \textbf{Named leaders.} \textbf{Ennit of Low Kiln}, whose family was taken in the levy of Y.H. 668; \textbf{Jorra Vail}, who has never said whether he is with them. \textbf{Goals.} To destroy the slates, stop the levy, and make it impossible to bank a person who does not consent. \textbf{Methods.} Broken birth-slates, a year in the clay yard for the offender, ash on the doorstep of an assessor, and a refusal that is just short of an unclosed account. \textbf{Resources.} The memory of ninety children taken in one levy, the silence of the hearth-maids, and the fact that a slate can be destroyed in a kiln in four minutes. \textbf{Enemies.} The Court, Pell Varr’s list, and the collectors who come with a slate already written. \textbf{Hidden agenda.} They want the Return abolished, which in Sarn means the end of the economy. They have not worked out what comes after. Their best argument is that nothing could be worse, and the Court’s sealed file suggests that they may be right.

\section*{The Unbanked}

\textbf{Kind.} Secret faction inside the Keeping, descended from the Ash Rising and named in a Court file of Y.H. 675 as \emph{persons of the sealed opinion}. \textbf{Seat.} No fixed seat. A locked drawer in Rusk’s room above the Fishstairs; a sealed file in the Hearthseat; the margin of a slate in Greybank signed M. \textbf{Named leaders.} \textbf{Rusk of the Weir}, who teaches a strange boy to listen to vessels; \textbf{Collector Marra}, who pays for a whole return with her own fingers; \textbf{Haldre}, whose paper \emph{The Sealed Vessel} was read by nine people in Y.H. 702 and who disappeared in Y.H. 704. \textbf{Goals.} The Final Banking: make every vessel perfectly retentive, so that nothing leaks, nothing is owed, and no one can ever give anything away. A sealed world is an unbreakable world. \textbf{Methods.} Recruitment through apprenticeship, the forgery of assessments, the purchase of whole returns with memory intact, and the long cultivation of a man who can look at a dying body and decide without prayer. \textbf{Resources.} A collector inside the Keeping, a teacher inside the Clay Yard, a cold iron tube, a locked drawer, and a hot trace that crossed between worlds. \textbf{Enemies.} The Court’s public answer is that they are mad. The Court’s private answer is in the sealed file. Their real enemy is want itself, which they mean to abolish. \textbf{Hidden agenda.} They did not make the blizzard and they did not put the minivan on the ice. They wanted a vessel that gave at the moment of breaking, and found one in a country they could not have named. They paid to have the memories left in, which is the most expensive single purchase a banker in Sarn can make. Rusk taught Corin to make him the instrument. Marra has been paying for it ever since.

\section*{The Deep Hand}

\textbf{Kind.} Secret society of scholars; a reading of the oldest slates. \textbf{Seat.} A back table in the Keld survey shaft, and one room in the Hearthseat whose key is held by an assessor. \textbf{Named leaders.} \textbf{Ludo of the Old Kiln}, a Grathic scholar who found a slate in the ash throat; \textbf{Derren the Younger}, an officer’s son with access to the sealed file. \textbf{Goals.} To read the Deep Hand and learn what the oldest repairs were made of. \textbf{Methods.} Copying slates that are not meant to leave the shaft; comparing the Keld translation \emph{Ash} with the Court’s translation \emph{language}; asking the Vorr the wrong question and recording that the numbers are correct. \textbf{Resources.} One slate from the Old Kiln, a crossed-out page from Y.H. 588, and the observation that a pot mended many times is not the pot it was. \textbf{Enemies.} The College of Assessors. The Court, which removed a surveyor from the shaft in Y.H. 717 for writing \emph{words that hold}. \textbf{Hidden agenda.} They believe the oldest repairs are made of promises that were never kept, made solid. If this is true then the world is being held together by the very thing the Court’s economy produces, and the Keeping cannot be abolished without finding out what holds the walls up.

\section*{IV. Foreign powers at the border}

\section*{Keld — the Warden of the Rim}

\textbf{Kind.} Foreign power; mountain kingdom of iron, lead, coal and warm-stone. \textbf{Seat.} Keld Keep, with the First Seam beneath it and the Cold Pass ten leagues beyond. \textbf{Named leaders.} \textbf{Karric VII, Warden of the Rim}, who shut the Cold Pass in Y.H. 741; the mine-reeves beneath him. \textbf{Goals.} To hold the passes, keep the true Hearth claim alive, and sell iron and warm-stone to whoever pays without becoming anyone’s subject. \textbf{Methods.} Mercenaries, the Descent at thirteen, a measure stated before a name, and a treaty enforced at the point of a surveyor’s slate. \textbf{Resources.} Iron, lead, coal, warm-stone, the survey shaft, and a population that can hold a hot clamp against its palm without flinching. \textbf{Enemies.} The Court, which has never accepted the Hearth claim and has never bothered to argue it. The Sweating. Grathish clay-men levied into a war they did not choose. \textbf{Hidden agenda.} The Warden knows the true Hearth is not under his mountain. He keeps the claim because it keeps the Court out of the shafts, and because a kingdom that believes it owns the source can charge more for its stone.

\section*{Sere — the Sun-Emperor}

\textbf{Kind.} Foreign power; hot empire of grain, wine, bowls and open slavery. \textbf{Seat.} The Bowl of Sere, three hundred leagues south of Sill. \textbf{Named leaders.} The \textbf{Sun-Emperor}, whose acts are announced by the emptying of a public bowl of wine; the Council of Bowls beneath him. \textbf{Goals.} To grow north by purchase rather than conquest, to buy measures cheaply, and to keep the Peat Line from becoming a border it has to defend. \textbf{Methods.} Agents with credit, the Bowl feast, a public burning of debt-slates followed by the making of new ones, and a phrase \emph{measure for measure} that means whatever the emperor has already decided. \textbf{Resources.} Gold crowns, grain, oil, wine, fine glazed ware, and the largest slave population in the world. \textbf{Enemies.} The Court’s deduction on grain. The Fenmarch. The northern habit of calling Sere spendthrifts. \textbf{Hidden agenda.} The empire does not want territory in Grath. It wants the poor. A northern district that has been bought is more useful than a northern district that has been conquered, because nobody in a conquered district signs a contract.

\section*{Vhalen — the Council of Tides (as foreign power)}

\textbf{Kind.} Foreign power and trading republic; the gate between Grath and the Sallow Sea. \textbf{Seat.} Vhal, eighty leagues north-east of Sill by the Salt Road. \textbf{Named leaders.} \textbf{Avis Wake}, \textbf{Mav Hull}, \textbf{Dolf Brine}, rotating yearly. \textbf{Goals.} To keep the Salt Road open, to tax nothing that can be avoided, and to make the Court’s assessors feel poor in a room full of salt-paper. \textbf{Methods.} Contracts that rot in damp, a welcome for any traveller who pays, and the pulled benches for those who do not. \textbf{Resources.} Ships, salt, glass, rope, dried fish, and a legal system in which a wet document is no document at all. \textbf{Enemies.} The Court’s lists. The salt-thing. A hard winter that closes the coast pass. \textbf{Hidden agenda.} Half the port’s fortunes have turned on a contract going damp at the right moment. The Council’s real doctrine is not the Sallow Hearth but the convenient disappearance of evidence, which it has elevated into an art and a foreign policy.

\section*{Glossary of Sarn}

\emph{Entries for the reader. Every coined name set down in the Compendium so far is gathered here, in alphabetical order. Where a thing is known by different names in different countries, the common Grathic name is given first. A term whose deeper meaning is will be found in its own place in the Compendium; this glossary gives only what a traveller may hear in a market or a hearth.}

\CRrule

\section*{A}

\textbf{Account-day.} The tenth, twentieth and thirtieth day of every tend, when the slate is read aloud. In Grath these are also market-days.

\textbf{The Afterdrop.} The worsening before recovery in a rekindled or rewarmed vessel. The ordinary cause is a graft settling; midwives call it the second chill.

\textbf{The Ash.} Grey silt that gathers where promises are unkept, in corners and under beds, invisible in daylight and plain by tallow-light. The poor leave it on the Last Day and burn it next morning.

\textbf{Ash-mite.} A small grey mite that lives in the Ash and drinks the thin edge of a sleeper’s measure. Invisible by day; troublesome in poor rooms.

\textbf{Ash-runners.} Criminal smugglers of the Fenmarch who move Warm and sell quiet ash without a Court licence.

\textbf{Ash Rising.} The rising in Low Kiln in Y.H. 668 after ninety children’s warmth was taken for a private account; put down by Assessor-Marshal Pell Varr.

\textbf{Ash-sickness.} The condition of dreaming in another person’s voice after long exposure to an Ash-wraith.

\textbf{Ash-wraith.} A grey shape seen by tallow-light where many have been spent; it follows the Ash ridge from room to room.

\textbf{Assessor.} An officer of the Warm Court who decides what a life was worth and how much of its measure may be kept back.

\section*{B}

\textbf{The Bowl.} The Sere coming-of-age feast at sixteen, where a young person empties a bowl of warm wine and names a debt they intend to carry.

\textbf{Bought breath.} A person sold for a term, usually a season or a year, to repay a debt. The common form of slavery in the Fenmarch.

\textbf{The Breaking.} The spring flood, when the Ilm comes up over the Fishstairs and takes the low houses; also the Silt thaw itself.

\textbf{Brine-gull.} A Sallow-coast gull that steals anything warm: a loaf, a warmed stone, a coal not yet out.

\textbf{Broken Slates.} A rebel network of Low Kiln, descended from survivors of the Ash Rising, which seeks to destroy the slates and end the levy.

\textbf{C}

\textbf{Clay-bound.} A debt-servant working off a term, commonly seven years, with interest gathering as grey Ash in the household accounts.

\textbf{Clay-name.} The name a slave is given by the household that owns them; the old name is forbidden.

\textbf{Clay Road.} The packed-clay road from the Cross at Sill to Greybank, eighteen leagues; a Court road since the treaty of Y.H. 418.

\textbf{Clay Yard.} The slip pits, wheel shed, drying racks and kiln in Low Kiln; the whole world of a clay child.

\textbf{The clinical ear.} The ability to hear a faulty vessel before touching it. Dan possesses it to an unnatural degree; the Kiln-Masters of Sill have quietly noted it.

\textbf{The Cold.} The absence of Warm; a material substance in Sarn, with a place to put what is taken.

\textbf{Cold Acre.} A barley-and-turnip hamlet two leagues north of Sill on the ridge, with one well that never freezes.

\textbf{Cold iron.} Iron that draws Warm out of a vessel and gives none back; used in the collector’s tube and in clamps.

\textbf{Cold Pass.} The mountain pass beyond Keld Keep, closed in Y.H. 741 and fixed as Grath’s northern border by the agreement of Y.H. 747.

\textbf{Cold work.} Work done in cold rooms, especially the greyed-finger labour of a collector; the Court’s phrase for a punishment that damages the hand.

\textbf{Cold-eater.} A Wasteling shape that stands close to a vessel and draws its Warm away; it takes, and does not give.

\textbf{Collector.} An officer of the Warm Court who draws the measure from the dying with a cold iron tube and records it on a slate.

\textbf{College of Assessors.} The academy in the Hearthseat that teaches Ledgerhand and the valuation of lives, and holds the only right to write a lawful deduction.

\textbf{Council of Tides.} The elected government of Vhalen, composed of ship-owners and salt-masters, whose decrees are written on salt-paper that rots in damp.

\textbf{Crown.} A gold coin worth twenty silver marks, common in Sere and rarely seen in Grath.

\textbf{Cup-bank.} A procedure for storing Warm outside the body in clay or iron, to be spent later or by another.

\textbf{Cupworm.} A blind worm that bores clay from the inside, so that a pot looks sound and breaks at first pouring.

\textbf{D}

\textbf{Deep Hand.} The unread script of the oldest slates and seam records; the Vorr recognise its numbers but will not translate it in full.

\textbf{Deduction.} The portion of a banked measure kept back for the Hearth. Taught as one part in twelve and printed on the Weighing notices.

\textbf{Descent.} The Keldish coming-of-age at thirteen: one day down the mine, after which some have their number changed.

\textbf{Dippers.} The salt priests of Vhalen, who bless nets by trailing them in the sea at dawn and read the Sallow draw.

\textbf{E}

\textbf{Ember.} The Court’s unit of booked warmth, said to be worth five marks; a newborn vessel holds twelve to fifteen embers.

\textbf{Empty Cantons.} A province sixty leagues south of Grath with roads, wells and doorways but no people after Y.H. 40.

\textbf{F}

\textbf{The Fenmarch.} The reed and peat confederacy south-west of Grath, governed by reeves chosen for standing longest in a bog.

\textbf{Fen-leech (bog-cow).} A large horned beast of the peat bogs that stands half-submerged and pushes a man down into the mud.

\textbf{The Ferr.} A cold tributary of the Ilm, about forty leagues long, carrying lead dust from the western workings.

\textbf{The Final Banking.} The Unbanked’s design for making every vessel perfectly retentive, so that nothing leaks, nothing is owed, and nothing can be given away.

\textbf{The First Breaking.} The temple’s name for the single moment of anger in which the world was cracked.

\textbf{The First Firing.} The Grathic coming-of-age at twelve: a child makes a pot, fires it, and knocks it with a knuckle. A clear note means a feast; a flat note means another year in the yard.

\textbf{The First Seam.} The deep survey shaft beneath Keld Keep, where the oldest repairs are recorded and a crossed-out entry of Y.H. 588 is kept.

\textbf{The first way.} The procedure of opening a channel in the forearm by cutting across a surface way and keeping it open while it scars.

\textbf{The Five (Five Cold Days).} The five days outside the months at the year’s end: Kindling, Weighing, the Long Night, the Remembering and the Last Day.

\textbf{The full note.} The full sound a sound vessel gives under the knuckle; a cracked vessel answers flat. The first tool of the clinical ear.

\textbf{Frost-bloom.} An ice flower that grows where Warm has recently left; a map of the leak that made it.

\textbf{G}

\textbf{Glass eel.} An eel of the Ilm and Ferr that stores a little Warm in a sac behind the head and can stop a wader’s ways in the water.

\textbf{The Glaze Cliffs.} Blue-green cliffs on the Vhaleni coast stained by old lead workings, sweet-smelling in the heat.

\textbf{Grath.} The clay country of river towns, kilns and peat in the north-west of the bowl; Corin’s birth country.

\textbf{Grathic.} The common tongue of the bowl: short vowels, hard \emph{k} and \emph{thr}, little inflection, written on slate with chalk.

\textbf{Greybank.} The Court’s granary and factor’s house eighteen leagues downriver of Sill, where the Clay Road ends and the Hearth Road begins.

\textbf{Grey fingers.} The discoloration of a collector’s fingertips after years of drawing Warm; a mark of the trade and, in extreme cases, of exhaustion.

\textbf{H}

\textbf{Hearth.} The great source of the Warm, a chamber of stone beneath the Keel, said to hold what the world had when it was made.

\textbf{The Hearth Drawing.} The first collection of the year, on Stone 12; a collector may draw from the dying and the newly born on the same day.

\textbf{Hearth-fire (Hearth-flame).} The flame kept behind a grating of cold iron in the Weighing Hall; it has never gone out and no priest may touch it.

\textbf{Hearth-maids.} The midwives, collectors’ assistants and keepers of the birth-slates in Grathic villages.

\textbf{Hearth Road.} The merchants’ road from Greybank south-east to the Hearthseat, a hundred and forty leagues.

\textbf{Hearth-tongue.} The Hearth-fire drawn up through the Keel; a procedure by which the Hearthward draw, and the name of the fire in the Weighing Hall.

\textbf{Hearth-wisp.} A small light that gathers where a banked fire has been left and draws warmth from a sleeper.

\textbf{Hearthseat.} The Court’s city on the Keel, built over the Hearth, where the Weighing Hall, the sealed files and fewer than twenty Kept vessels are kept.

\textbf{Hearthward.} The rank of a vessel that can draw from the Hearth itself and is therefore never cold.

\textbf{Held.} The rank of a vessel that can carry more than its natural measure.

\textbf{High Kiln.} The master potters’ quarter on the terrace above Low Kiln, where the glaze is mixed and the lead smell hangs.

\textbf{The Hollow.} A vessel whose head has fallen so low it can no longer draw warmth from food; also a thin-return child with almost no shape in it.

\textbf{Hot trace.} Warmth that leaves a vessel in an act of giving and stays hot, tangled with an unpaid promise, instead of cooling into the bank.

\textbf{House Vail.} The kiln-house of Low Kiln held by the Vail family for four generations; Sella Vail keeps the fire.

\textbf{I}

\textbf{Ice-mouse.} A field mouse of frozen fields whose bite leaves a spreading numb patch, frostbite by another road.

\textbf{The Ilm.} The great silty river of Grath, frozen from Stone into Ilmen, breaking in the Silt flood; it rises in the Rim and drains into the Sallow Sea.

\textbf{K}

\textbf{Keld.} The mountain kingdom of the Rim, living on iron, lead, coal and warm-stone and ruled by the Warden of the Rim.

\textbf{Keld Keep.} The Warden’s fortress city where a man states his measure before his name and the deep survey shaft begins.

\textbf{Kept.} The highest rank in Sarn: a vessel the Court has made effectively unbreakable. Fewer than twenty exist; they are spoken of with pity and no longer feel the cold. Also a term for chattel slaves in Grath.

\textbf{Kiln’s breath.} The invisible poison of a closed kiln house or cellar; it kills in sleep and is called a merciful death by those who have not identified the body.

\textbf{Kiln-hound.} A feral dog of old kilns whose hot breath carries the kiln’s own poison.

\textbf{Kiln-Masters of Sill.} The craft guild that holds the kilns, the First Firing and the knowledge of the full note in Grath.

\textbf{Kindling.} The first of the Five, when every hearth in Sarn is lit from the Hearth-flame and collectors take the year’s first measure.

\textbf{L}

\textbf{Ledgerhand.} The Court’s script: Grathic grammar with the verbs removed, in a neat upright hand, all in the passive voice.

\textbf{The Listening Stones.} Standing stones in the high Rim against which the Vorr sit for days and come away with numbers.

\textbf{The living patch.} A graft of wall taken from a donor and set over a break, held until the vessel takes.

\textbf{The Long Night.} The third of the Five; no fire after dusk, and stories of the Return are told in the dark.

\textbf{The Long Sitting.} The Vorr coming-of-age at forty: a season sitting still against a stone, listening.

\textbf{Low Kiln.} The poor quarter of Sill on the south bank below the sill, where the kilns stand and the Ash ridge lies thick.

\textbf{M}

\textbf{The Marsh Mother.} The peat cult of the Fenmarch, which keeps warmth in the mud and gives it back to whoever can reach it.

\textbf{Measure.} The amount of Warm a vessel holds, booked in embers; also the shape of that warmth, which is the self.

\textbf{Mender’s failure.} The name given in the deep accounts to a vessel recalled in an earlier age to solve the same problem, who failed in a particular way.

\textbf{Mire-fever.} A blight of the Fenmarch water that burns and then leaves the victim colder after each cycle.

\textbf{Mud End.} A peat hamlet three leagues downriver of Sill, whose winter peat decides whether anyone stays warm.

\textbf{N}

\textbf{Nine Pots.} A pottery hamlet eight leagues west of Sill with four kilns, a shared clay pit and a permanent feud with Sill over the road.

\textbf{The Nine Wells.} Hot springs in the Keel where the ground gives back more than it takes; five are sealed by the Court and the keys are kept.

\textbf{The Nine Who Kept.} The temple’s figures who came after the First Breaking, drove the first repairs and left the rite of the Return behind.

\textbf{O}

\textbf{Opened.} The rank of a vessel that has cut a way and can move Warm deliberately within the self.

\textbf{The Opening of the Slates.} Ilmen 1, when the Court opens the new year’s accounts.

\textbf{The outward way.} A procedure by which a Drawn practitioner pushes a flow of Warm out of the body at short range.

\textbf{Overfilling.} Pouring more Warm into a vessel than its walls can hold; the head rises and the vessel cooks its own contents.

\textbf{P}

\textbf{The Peat Line.} The wet frontier where Sere’s northern advance stopped in Y.H. 634 and where the two powers trade, raid and buy measures.

\textbf{Quiet ash.} The Court’s licensed medicine, sold to the rich who want their previous life to stop arriving in dreams.

\textbf{The quiet seal.} The forbidden procedure of closing a vessel so that nothing leaks in or out.

\textbf{R}

\textbf{Reed Cut.} The Fenfolk coming-of-age at twelve: cutting a bundle of reed alone in the dark and bringing it back without speaking.

\textbf{Reed-wolf.} A wolf of the lower Ilm reedbeds that hunts in packs and goes for the throat and the inside of the elbow.

\textbf{The Remembering.} The fourth of the Five, when names of the dead are spoken and whole returns announced in a clean voice.

\textbf{The Return.} The rite by which the dead are held to come back in new bodies through the mercy of the Hearth; the first thing a child learns and the last thing a dying person hears.

\textbf{The Rim.} The chain of cold mountains that rings Sarn, broken by a few passes, beyond which lies the Waste.

\textbf{Rim current.} The slow current of Warm draining toward the Waste, which makes the Keldish mines sweat.

\textbf{Rim Road.} The road from Sill west-north-west to Keld Keep, a hundred and twenty leagues of improving road and worsening weather.

\textbf{Rim-seam.} The seam at the horizon where sky and clay meet, riveted with stars; it carries many repairs laid one over another.

\textbf{S}

\textbf{The Salt Road.} The trade route from Sill through Greybank to the Vhaleni ports, eighty leagues.

\textbf{Salt Walk.} The Vhaleni coming-of-age at fourteen: walking the pans barefoot at low tide and naming a tide never seen.

\textbf{Salt-thing.} A shape of salt and cold water in the Sallow that taps the hull of a boat and draws its store away.

\textbf{The Sallow draw.} The current of Warm running seaward into the Sallow, blessed by the dippers and taxed by the Court.

\textbf{The Sallow Sea.} A cold inland sea the colour of skimmed milk whose tides run against the wind and are said to follow the Warm rather than the moon.

\textbf{Seam-blight.} A blight that travels along an old repair and widens it by a hair’s breadth each season.

\textbf{Seam-serpent.} A long thing that moves along the under-seam and draws Warm out through the stone.

\textbf{Seam-tide.} The rising and falling of the Warm in the seams of the world, read by the Vorr from the Listening Stones.

\textbf{The Second.} A person in whom a graft from a differently shaped donor has produced a want for a life that was not theirs.

\textbf{Second Want.} The Court’s struck-from-the-record name for the Warm trying to resume the shape of a former owner; the common name is the second chill.

\textbf{Sere.} The hot southern empire of grain, wine, bowls and open chattel slavery, ruled by the Sun-Emperor.

\textbf{Sere factors.} The empire’s agents in the north, who buy poor measures with credit and ship them south.

\textbf{The shape of the measure.} The pattern of a vessel’s Warm, which is the self; it is fragile and thins with every pouring.

\textbf{The Singing Sill.} The ledge of hard stone under the falls at Sill, which gives a low note when the Warm rises in Ilmen.

\textbf{Sill.} The town of just over four thousand souls where the Ilm drops four feet over a ledge; Corin’s birthplace.

\textbf{Slate-name.} The only name an officer of the Keeping has, entered on the rolls in Ledgerhand on entering the service.

\textbf{Slate Row.} The Court quarter east of the Cross at Sill, holding the collector’s table, the assessor’s house and the Waiting Room.

\textbf{The Slip Brook.} A narrow clay-coloured stream through the Clay Yard whose colour the children of Low Kiln read as weather.

\textbf{The Spending.} The Sere doctrine that a vessel was made to be emptied gloriously and that a life hoarded is a life wasted.

\textbf{The Standing Post.} The raised boardwalk in the Fenmarch where reeves settle fines and feuds and which is the only Fen place outsiders are shown.

\textbf{Star-rivet.} A rivet of the rim-seam, one of the stars; one is said to have fallen in the Ash Times.

\textbf{Steward.} The head of the Warm Court; Steward Derren holds the office in the present year.

\textbf{Stone.} The month of deep winter, river ice and counted fuel; the year begins in it.

\textbf{The Sweating.} A Keldish calamity in which Warm comes up through the stone of the deep mines in a lethal flood, heralded by the smell of hot lead.

\textbf{T}

\textbf{Tallow Works.} A rendering house for sheep fat two leagues north-east of Sill, whose smell carries into town on a north wind.

\textbf{Tempering.} The practice of repeated small strains so that a vessel’s walls learn to yield without opening.

\textbf{Temple of the Return.} The public church of the Keeping, which teaches that the world was made whole and cracked once.

\textbf{Tend.} A week of ten days; every month holds three whole tends, and the tenth day of each is Account-day.

\textbf{Thane of Clay.} Grath’s ruler, chosen by the kiln-masters of Sill and confirmed by the Court’s factor at Greybank.

\textbf{Thin.} A vessel that leaks faster than it takes in; the common condition of the poor, the spent and the returned poor. Also the bottom rank of a vessel.

\textbf{Thin return.} A return in which many measures are pooled and divided among many infants; what comes back is a stranger with a faint interest in a life it cannot name.

\textbf{Thin-child.} \emph{See} The Hollow.

\textbf{Tide-lore.} The Vhaleni discipline of reading the Sallow draw, taught by the dippers.

\textbf{Triage.} The old-life word for the surgeon’s act of sorting the wounded; Dan’s gift and his heresy in Sarn.

\textbf{U}

\textbf{The Unbanked.} The Court’s name for a secret faction of persons of the sealed opinion, descended from the Ash Rising and dedicated to the Final Banking.

\textbf{Unclosed account.} The crime of refusing a lawful collection; the offender is clip-held by the wrist until consent.

\textbf{The Uncollected.} A dead vessel whose Warm has not been drawn and which walks until it leaks into the Waste.

\textbf{Under-seam.} The seam beneath the ground, below which lies the dark where nothing is kept.

\textbf{V}

\textbf{The Vail kiln-house.} The kiln-house in the Clay Yard held by the Vail family for four generations, where Corin is taken in as a foundling.

\textbf{Vhal.} The principal Vhaleni port on the Sallow shore, seat of the Council of Tides, with salt pans and glass furnaces.

\textbf{Vhalen.} The salt republic of port towns on the Sallow, governed by the Council of Tides.

\textbf{Vorr.} A people apart, tall, long-lived, who hold their measure in the bone, keep the Listening Stones and do not undergo the Return.

\textbf{W}

\textbf{Walking thin.} People with the measures of the returned poor who keep moving because stopping is the same as cooling.

\textbf{Warm.} The living, stored heat of which Sarn is made; the poor call it \emph{the warm} and the learned call it \emph{the kept heat}. It has pressure, currents, sources and sinks.

\textbf{Warm Court.} The institution that owns the Hearth, the banks and the Returns and calls itself the Keeping.

\textbf{Warm-stone.} A Keldish stone that holds heat longer than any other substance in Sarn.

\textbf{Warm-stone grub.} A beetle larva of the deep mines that eats warm-stone from within and is a sign that a sweating may follow.

\textbf{Warden of the Rim.} The hereditary ruler of Keld; Karric VII closed the Cold Pass in Y.H. 741.

\textbf{The Waste.} The cold country beyond the cracks at the edge of all things, where everything unheld goes.

\textbf{Wastelings.} The bands beyond the Rim who take what the world loses; they have no banks, no Returns and no word for a debt.

\textbf{Weighing.} The second of the Five, when the year’s measures are read aloud and the rich learn how whole they are.

\textbf{Weighing Hall.} The hall in the Hearthseat where the measures are read at the Weighing and the Hearth-fire burns behind cold iron.

\textbf{The Weir.} The eel traps and plank walk one league upriver of Sill, where the Fish Blessing is held and Rusk walks at dusk.

\textbf{Welled.} The rank of a vessel that can draw on stores held in the world itself — wells, seams or another’s bank — without owning them.

\textbf{Well-draw.} The procedure by which a Welled practitioner draws on a store the world already holds; the level drops for everyone.

\textbf{Whole.} A vessel that holds without leaking; also the rank of such a vessel.

\textbf{Whole return.} A return in which one person’s warmth is kept intact and the person comes back as themselves, with memory.

\textbf{Widow’s mark.} The Grathic institution by which a widow holds a kiln in her own right; the only Grathic institution nobody argues with.
