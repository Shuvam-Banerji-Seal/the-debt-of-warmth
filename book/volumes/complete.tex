\section*{Sarn — the world, the lands, and the nations}

\emph{An account set down for the reader of the Compendium. What is written here is what Sarn believes about itself, together with such measurements as a traveller may check with his own feet. Where the belief and the measurement disagree, the measurement is given first.}

\CRrule

\section*{I. The shape of the world}

\section*{The Warm}

Sarn is not a world that contains heat. It is a world made of \textbf{the Warm}: a living, stored heat that runs through clay, water, bone, beast, hearth and star. The poor call it \emph{the warm}, as they call a fire \emph{the fire}. The learned call it \textbf{the kept heat}. It is not flame. Flame is what the Warm looks like when it is escaping. Fire is the world bleeding upward.

The Warm has pressure, currents, sources and sinks, the way a body has blood pressure, arteries, a heart and a wound. It can be measured, drawn off, poured, pooled, banked, spent or lost. Everything that holds it is a \textbf{vessel}. A person is a vessel. So is a beast, a tree, a river, a town, a kiln, and the world itself. A vessel has walls, channels called \textbf{ways}, a capacity, flaws, old patches and a rate of loss. A vessel that holds is called \textbf{whole}. A vessel that leaks is called \textbf{thin}. When the walls give way the Warm goes out, and where it goes is the question every religion in Sarn exists to answer.

The great source lies beneath the world: a chamber of stone called \textbf{the Hearth}, which holds what the world had when it was made. The great sink lies beyond the cracks at the edge of all things: \textbf{the Waste}, a cold country outside the world, where everything unheld goes. The whole of Sarn is the space between the two, and every vessel in it is a small, failing dam.

\section*{The common account}

Every child in Sarn is taught that the world was a vessel, fired whole and cracked in a single moment of anger. The crack is called \textbf{the First Breaking}. The sky is the lid set upon the clay body, and where lid and body meet at the horizon runs the rim-seam, riveted with stars. Beneath the ground lies the under-seam, and below that the dark where nothing is kept. The temple teaches that \textbf{the Nine Who Kept} drove the first repairs and left behind the rite of \textbf{the Return}, by which the dead come back in new bodies through the mercy of the Hearth. This is the first thing a child learns and the last thing a dying person hears.

There are surveyors in the mountain kingdom of Keld who will tell a different story, if they have drunk enough and no officer of the Court is in the room. They say the rim-seam shows many repairs laid one over another, and that the oldest of them are not the work of any god that can be named. Some are not made of stone or clay at all. In the records they look like \emph{language}. The surveyors do not say what language. They stop there, and turn the slate face down.

\section*{The ranks of vessels}

The ranks of the Warm are trades, not prizes, and a man works years at one before he is called by its name. \textbf{Whole} is a vessel that holds without leaking. \textbf{Held} is a vessel that can carry more than its natural measure. \textbf{Drawn} can shape and direct a flow. \textbf{Banked} can store Warm outside the body, in clay or iron. \textbf{Hearthward} can draw from the Hearth itself and is therefore never cold. \textbf{Kept} is a vessel the Court has made effectively unbreakable, and there are fewer than twenty in the world. The Kept are spoken of with pity. They no longer feel the cold.

No one discovers these ranks by wishing. A vessel is opened, a way is cut, the pressure is raised, and something is paid. The price is always heat taken from somewhere: a chill in the practitioner, grey fingers, a memory burned thin, a district left cold for a season.

\section*{The bowl, the rim, and the sea}

Sarn is a great shallow bowl. Its western and northern edge is \textbf{the Rim}, a chain of cold mountains, broken at a few passes by roads that never quite freeze and never quite dry. Beyond the Rim lies the Waste, and no map is honest past the last pass. The bowl’s north-eastern lowlands drain into \textbf{the Sallow Sea}, a cold inland sea the colour of skimmed milk, whose tides run against the wind and are said to follow the Warm rather than the moon. Its coasts are salt, glass and fog. The centre of the bowl is a plain of clay and old river gravel, warm by Sarnish standards and therefore crowded. Across the south-east runs \textbf{the Keel}, a low ridge of stone under which the Hearth is said to lie, and on the Keel stands the Hearthseat, the Court’s city.

Distances in this account are given in \textbf{leagues}. A Sarnish mile is one thousand paces, roughly nine-tenths of an English mile; a league is three miles, a little under three kilometres. A loaded cart makes three leagues in a day on good road, four if the horse is fresh and the driver cruel. A man on foot makes four on the Clay Road and two in the bogs. A day’s walk is the common measure of neighbourhood: \emph{three leagues downriver} means a long morning and a sore back.

\section*{II. The lands and their climates}

The Fenmarch — Damp, low, reed-choked — Peat, reeds, hemp, eels, smuggled warmth — Mire-fever and the draining ditches\par

The Waste — Cold without season — Nothing that can be counted — The cold itself, which has somewhere to put what is lost\par

The seasons are counted by the months of the Sarnish year, which are set out in `bible/calendar.md`. In Grath, ice comes on the river in Stone and breaks in Ilmen. Silt is the month of mud, when no cart can reach the high road. Seed and Tallow are the planting and lambing months. Ember is the short fierce summer in which every kiln in Grath is fired at once, so that the whole country smells of woodsmoke for thirty days. Smoke and Grain are the harvest and the second firing. Frost and Bone are the hunting months. Ash and Stone are the dark.

\section*{III. Wonders and wastes}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]

\item \textbf{The Hearth-fire.} In the Weighing Hall of the Hearthseat a flame is kept behind a grating of cold iron. It is said to be a tongue of the Hearth drawn up through the Keel. It has never gone out, and no priest has ever been allowed to touch it.

\item \textbf{The Singing Sill.} At Sill, the river Ilm crosses a ledge of hard stone before the falls. When the Warm rises in the spring the stone gives a low note under the water, audible from the Fishstairs. The people of Sill set their clocks by it and do not explain it.

\item \textbf{The Glaze Cliffs.} On the Vhaleni coast, cliffs stained blue-green by old lead workings. They smell faintly sweet in the heat. Children are warned not to lick them.

\item \textbf{The Listening Stones of the Vorr.} Standing stones in the high Rim, said to be older than the Return. The Vorr sit against them for days and come away with numbers.

\item \textbf{The Nine Wells.} Hot springs in the Keel where the ground gives back more than it takes. The Court seals four of them and keeps the keys.

\item \textbf{The Empty Cantons.} A province south of Grath with roads, wells and doorways but no people. The temple says the people were taken in the first Returns. Nobody says who paid.

\item \textbf{The Old Kiln.} A collapsed kiln one league south of Sill. The clay around it is grey and will not take a glaze. The ash in its throat is deeper than a man is tall.

\item \textbf{The Waste.} The country beyond the Rim. Travellers who go far enough come back older and thinner, and do not remember what they went for. The Court sends no collectors there.

\end{itemize}

\section*{IV. The nations and realms}

\section*{1. Grath — the clay country}

\textbf{Seat and government.} Grath is not a single state but a string of river towns under a \textbf{Thane of Clay}, who is chosen by the kiln-masters of Sill and confirmed by the Court’s factor in Greybank. Each town has a moot of householders that meets on market-day and settles small debts by shouting. The thane’s real power is the right to call a levy of clay and labour; the Court’s real power is the right to call a levy of warmth. Everyone in Grath knows which levy is the heavier.

\textbf{Coin and trade.} Grath works in copper and silver: the \textbf{bit}, twelve to the silver \textbf{mark}. Coarse pots, tile, peat and eels go downriver to Greybank; salt and iron come back up. A kiln hand earns one mark a day, a journeyman two, a child sweeping the yard six bits, and a collector’s clerk four marks. Rent on a two-room house in Low Kiln is eight marks a month, fuel four, food twelve. A man who misses three days of work is a man who has begun to owe.

\textbf{Faith.} The Return is taught by the \textbf{hearth-maids}, who are midwives, collectors’ assistants and keepers of the birth-slates in one person. A hearth-maid crosses herself at a birth-flower and says nothing about it. The poor of Grath pray to the Warm the way a man presses a coin flat: not for mercy but for a little more of it.

\textbf{Customs.} On the Last Day of the year the ash in the corners is left where it lies. It is swept up on the morning after, and burnt. A pot is broken at the door of a death-house to let the heat out. A foundling is named by whoever writes the slate, and has no house-name until a hearth claims him.

\textbf{Outsiders.} A man without a kiln-mark is a man without a debt, and Grath does not trust him. Travellers are lodged at the Cross and watched. Vhaleni salt merchants are tolerated because they pay. Sere traders are disliked because they buy measures. A Wasteling is not so much disliked as pitied in a way that closes the door.

\textbf{Slavery and law.} Grath knows \textbf{clay-bound} service: a debt paid in years of work, seven years being the common term, with interest gathering as grey Ash in the household accounts. Chattel slaves exist in the thane’s house and in the greater kilns, called \textbf{kept hands}, and are returned thin when they die. The moot punishes theft by compensation in marks; theft of warmth from another’s cup is punished by a cold iron brand on the wrist and a deduction from the thief’s own measure. Murder is not answered with death, which would waste a measure. The murderer is banked out: his warmth is drawn and divided among the victim’s household, and he is returned thin as a debt-child to work the debt off. Refusing a lawful collection is the crime called an \textbf{unclosed account}; the offender is clip-held by the wrist until he consents.

\textbf{What they fear.} The Breaking, when the Ilm comes up over the Fishstairs and takes the low houses; thin ice; the kiln’s breath, which kills a man in his sleep; the sweet sickness from bad glaze; the second chill in a rekindled child; and the grey ridge along the skirting board that grows when a debt is not paid.

\section*{2. The Warm Court — the Keeping}

\textbf{Seat and government.} The Court sits in the \textbf{Hearthseat} on the Keel and rules every vessel in Sarn in theory and most in practice. Its head is the \textbf{Steward of the Hearth}. Below him are the assessors, who decide what a life was worth; the collectors, who draw the measure with a cold iron tube; and the bankers, who press it into newborn vessels. The Court calls all of this \textbf{the Keeping}. It has no army of monsters and no dark lord. It has slates, clerks and a soft way of speaking.

\textbf{Coin and trade.} The Court does not trade in bits and marks; it books warmth in \textbf{embers}. One ember is booked at five marks, a rate no market has ever seen. The trade is in whole returns, thin returns, deductions, and \textbf{quiet ash}, a medicine sold to the rich who want their previous life to stop arriving in dreams. A whole return is booked at twelve hundred marks and forty unspent measures. A thin return costs the district thirty marks and is paid in labour. The deduction is taught as one part in twelve. In Low Kiln it is one in three.

\textbf{Faith.} The Keeping is both bank and church. Its priests say: \emph{The measure is banked. The account stands. Nothing is lost that we have kept. Your return is recorded.} They never raise their voices. Their violence is grammatical.

\textbf{Customs.} Every month’s tenth day is account-day, when the slate is read aloud. At the Weighing, in the Five Cold Days, the year’s measures are announced and the rich learn how whole they are. The Court keeps its dead on the rolls forever; an account is never closed, only carried forward.

\textbf{Outsiders.} There are no outsiders to the Keeping. A foreigner is a subject whose account has not yet been opened. An assessor can open one with a question and a slate.

\textbf{Slavery and law.} The Court owns no slaves, which it considers a mark of civility. Its \textbf{Kept} vessels belong to their accounts instead of to a master, which the Court considers kinder. Law is the slate: what is written stands; what is not written did not happen. An officer of the Keeping has no house and no kin. The account is her family.

\textbf{What they fear.} The Waste growing inward. A hot trace that will not cool. An unclosed account heavy enough to crack a slate. And above all, the day someone looks at a dying body and says, plainly, \emph{leave it}.

\section*{3. Vhalen — the salt republic}

\textbf{Seat and government.} Vhalen is a ring of port towns on the Sallow Sea governed by a \textbf{Council of Tides}, elected by ship-owners and salt-masters for a year at a time. The council meets in a room that smells of tar and wet rope. Its decrees are written on salt-paper and rot in damp weather, which is considered a virtue: a law that lasts too long is a law that has stopped earning.

\textbf{Coin and trade.} Silver marks and copper bits, with Venetian-style tallies kept by the factor. Salt, glass, rope, dried fish and ships are the staples. A sailor’s share is two marks a month and keep; a salt-worker gets one mark and a binding promise. Glass from the Glaze Cliffs is sold as far as Sere. Vhaleni shipwrights will build a boat for thirty marks and curse every hour of the work.

\textbf{Faith.} Vhaleni say the Sallow has a Hearth of its own beneath the salt, and that the Court’s Hearth is only its chimney. Their priests are called \textbf{dippers} and bless nets by trailing them in the sea at dawn.

\textbf{Customs.} Children are named for tides and winds. The dead are sealed in a salted boat and pushed out on the ebb; the boat is watched until it is out of sight, which is held to be enough. A stranger’s coin warms before his face, they say.

\textbf{Outsiders.} Welcomed if they pay, ignored if they do not. Vhalen has no word for a guest and no word for a friend, only for a man whose account is current.

\textbf{Slavery and law.} Condemned criminals are sentenced to the \textbf{pulled} benches, rowing the salt galleys for a term of seven years or until their hands give out, whichever comes first. Slaves can be bought in Vhalen but cannot be returned there: the Council will not pay for a thin return and will not house one. Law is contract; a contract written on salt-paper in wet weather is no contract at all, and half the port’s fortunes have turned on that.

\textbf{What they fear.} Cold tides that take warmth out to the Waste, being cut off from the Sallow in a hard winter, and the Court’s assessors arriving with a list of deductions on the salt works.

\section*{4. Keld — the mountain kingdom}

\textbf{Seat and government.} Keld holds the high passes of the Rim. Its ruler is the \textbf{Warden of the Rim}, a hereditary office, though a Warden who cannot hold a pass is removed by his own captains. Below him are the mine-reeves, who count every ember of iron and every life that goes down.

\textbf{Coin and trade.} Silver is scarce and iron is common: a good iron axe is worth two marks in the lowlands and half a mark in Keld. The kingdom lives on iron, lead, coal and the stone called \textbf{warm-stone}, which holds heat longer than any other substance in Sarn. Keldish mercenaries are hired by the Court and by Sere and are trusted by neither.

\textbf{Faith.} The Keldish claim the true Hearth lies beneath their mountain and that the Court’s fire is a candle lit from it. Their observatory is a deep shaft lined with Listening Stones, where surveyors record the repairs in the rim-seam. What they record is not published.

\textbf{Customs.} A Keldish person’s measure is tattooed on the forearm in numbers at thirteen. A man who returns thin has the old numbers cut out and new ones put in. Duels are fought with iron clamps, and the first man to open a way in his own forearm usually wins, because the other is busy bleeding.

\textbf{Outsiders.} Treated as tribute-bearers or spies, often both. A lowlander in Keld must state his measure before he states his name. If he lies, the mine-reeve checks him.

\textbf{Slavery and law.} The deep mines are worked by \textbf{under-vessed} slaves, condemned men and bought breath whose lives are reckoned at eleven months. The Warden’s word is law, tested by hot iron: a man who holds a clamp against his palm without flinching is presumed honest, which has produced a great many honest men with dead hands.

\textbf{What they fear.} The mountain sweating. In the deep shafts the Warm sometimes comes up through the stone in a lethal flood, and the only warning is the smell of hot lead. Keld also fears the Court’s assessors, who have never accepted the claim about the true Hearth and have never bothered to argue it.

\section*{5. Sere — the empire of bowls}

\textbf{Seat and government.} Sere is a hot southern empire of grain and great houses, ruled by the \textbf{Sun-Emperor} through a Council of Bowls. Each bowl is a province, and each bowl sends grain, slaves and warmth to the capital. The emperor’s acts are announced by the emptying of a public bowl of wine, which is read as a sign that a debt has been forgiven or a province has been ruined.

\textbf{Coin and trade.} Gold \textbf{crowns}, twenty silver marks to the crown, are the Sere currency. Grain, oil, wine, fine glazed ware and slaves go north; wool, iron and warm-stone come south. Sere buys poor measures openly and at a good price, which is why Grath hates Sere traders and sells to them anyway.

\textbf{Faith.} Sere teaches \textbf{the Spending}: a vessel was made to be emptied gloriously, and a life hoarded is a life wasted. Their temples are open courts with a fire in the middle. At the end of a feast they burn the debt-slates of one household, chosen by lot. Then they make new ones.

\textbf{Customs.} Name-days are public and generous; a Sere child may have seven names by adolescence and keep whichever the household likes. A young person comes of age at the Bowl feast: he empties a bowl of warm wine, names a debt he intends to carry, and is applauded. Inheritance goes to the eldest surviving child regardless of sex.

\textbf{Outsiders.} Welcomed as customers, enslaved as labourers, and rarely allowed to be both at once. A northerner with a thin return is a curiosity in Sere, exhibited at feasts and given small coins.

\textbf{Slavery and law.} Chattel slavery is open, extensive and legally simple: a slave’s warmth is the owner’s holding, and a slave has no measure in law. Beautiful house-slaves are given whole returns and exhibited as proof of the owner’s generosity. A slave who runs is returned thin and set to the grain mills. Justice is the emperor’s decree; the phrase \emph{measure for measure} is used to mean whatever the speaker has already decided.

\textbf{What they fear.} The Court’s deduction on their grain, the northern habit of calling them spendthrifts, and a slave rising who remembers enough to want something.

\section*{6. The Fenmarch — the reed confederacy}

\textbf{Seat and government.} A scatter of bog-villages governed by \textbf{reeves} chosen for their ability to stand in a bog longer than anyone else. The reeves settle feuds and collect fines; actual power lies with the families who control the peat-dries and the smugglers’ paths.

\textbf{Coin and trade.} Barter, copper bits, and marked sticks. Peat, reeds, hemp, eels and smuggled warmth. Fenfolk will sell a thin return for a third of what the Court charges and ask no questions about the source.

\textbf{Faith.} The \textbf{Marsh Mother} keeps warmth in the mud, they say, and gives it back to whoever can reach it. Their rites involve lying face down in peat until the cold becomes ordinary.

\textbf{Customs.} The ash in the corner is not left in the Fenmarch; it is gathered, bottled and sold. Children are taught to identify a collector by his boots before they are taught their own names.

\textbf{Outsiders.} \emph{A dry man is a dangerous man.} Outsiders are put up on the raised boardwalks and never told which boards are safe.

\textbf{Slavery and law.} \textbf{Bought breath}: a person sold for a term, usually a season or a year, to repay a peat-debt. Law is fine and feud; a fine unpaid becomes a term, and a term unpaid becomes a measure. Sexual violence exists in the Fenmarch as it does elsewhere in Sarn, and is dealt with by the reeve only when it has damaged a household’s account.

\textbf{What they fear.} The draining of the fens, the Court’s collectors arriving with a list, and a winter in which the peat does not dry.

\section*{7. The Waste — the country outside}

\textbf{Seat and government.} None. Bands of \textbf{Wastelings} move between the cold seams beyond the Rim, taking what the world loses. They have no banks, no Returns and no word for a debt. They are not Unbanked in the political sense; they are simply the spent.

\textbf{Coin and trade.} Nothing is traded that can be counted. A Wasteling may give a stone, a name or a day’s warmth, and expect nothing back.

\textbf{Faith.} None that a visitor can recognise. They say the cold is not empty; it is full of what nobody kept.

\textbf{Customs.} They leave their dead sitting upright facing the way they came. They do not cover them.

\textbf{Outsiders.} There are no outsiders, only vessels that have not yet leaked.

\textbf{Slavery and law.} No slavery, because no one in the Waste has enough warmth to own another. Law is the immediate appetite of the stronger vessel, which lasts about as long as the appetite.

\textbf{What they fear.} Nothing that survives the telling. A Wasteling asked what he fears will usually answer with a question about whether the visitor is warm.

\section*{V. Grath in detail — the birth country}

Grath lies in the north-west of the bowl, between the lower spurs of the Rim and the cold north-eastern reach of the Sallow Sea. It is a country of clay and water rather than stone: the hills are soft, the roads are rutted, and every house has a crack in it that the householder knows by name. The river \textbf{Ilm} rises in the Rim a hundred and twenty leagues upriver of Sill, runs east through the clay towns, and reaches the Sallow Sea another eighty leagues below Greybank. Its water carries fine grey silt, and in summer the shallows smell of iron and mud.

\section*{The town of Sill}

\textbf{Sill} stands where the Ilm crosses a ledge of hard stone and drops four feet. The town takes its name from that ledge. At low water the stone shows brown; when the Warm rises in Ilmen it gives a low musical note under the current, and the old people say the year has properly begun. Sill has just over four thousand souls, two bridges, three kiln-yards and a Court room.

Its districts:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]

\item \textbf{Low Kiln}, on the south bank below the sill, where the kilns stand and the smoke lies low. Houses here are two rooms, clay-daubed, with floors of trodden ash. This is where Corin is born and where the Vail kiln-house keeps its fire. The ash ridge along the skirting board is thicker here than anywhere else in Grath.

\item \textbf{High Kiln}, on the terrace above, where the master potters live and the glaze is mixed. The streets are steeper and smell of lead.

\item \textbf{the Cross}, the market square where the Clay Road and the river road meet. On market-day it holds perhaps two hundred people, eight pigs and one assessor.

\item \textbf{Slate Row}, east of the Cross: the Court’s room, the collector’s table, the assessor’s narrow house, and the Waiting Room where the collected are kept overnight before the banking.

\item \textbf{the Fishstairs}, the landing steps below the north bridge. Eels are sold here at dawn and rumours are sold here at noon.

\item \textbf{the Bone Orchard}, outside the north gate, a league from the Cross: the burial ground, the thin-return children’s graves, and the place where pots are broken for the dead.

\item \textbf{the Clay Yard}, in Low Kiln: the slip pits, the wheel shed, the drying racks and the kiln. It is the whole world of a child until he is old enough to walk to the Cross.

\end{itemize}

\section*{Roads, rivers and the cost of travel}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]

\item \textbf{the Clay Road}, Sill to Greybank, eighteen leagues, packed clay over corduroy in the wet places. A cart takes six days in Silt and five in Grain.

\item \textbf{the Salt Road}, Sill to the Vhaleni ports, eighty leagues by Greybank and the coast pass. Twenty days on foot at four leagues a day, more in a thaw.

\item \textbf{the Rim Road}, Sill north-west to Keld Keep, a hundred and twenty leagues of improving road and worsening weather. Only fools and factors travel it without a warm-stone.

\item \textbf{the Hearth Road}, Greybank south-east to the Hearthseat, a hundred and forty leagues. Merchants take it; Grath folk speak of it as if it were a fever.

\item \textbf{the Ilm}, the great river: frozen from Stone into Ilmen, broken up in the Breaking during Silt, low and silty in Ember.

\item \textbf{the Ferr}, a colder tributary from the south-west, forty leagues long, joining at Stonemouth. Its water carries lead dust and is said to make a man thin if he drinks it through a season.

\item \textbf{the Slip Brook}, a narrow clay-coloured stream through the Clay Yard into the Ilm. Children in Low Kiln learn to read the weather by its colour.

\end{itemize}

\section*{Seasons, food, smells}

The year begins in the dead of winter. \textbf{Stone} is the deep cold: the Ilm under ice, bread kept in a warmed box, every household counting its fuel. The \textbf{Five Cold Days} come at the turn of the year, and on the Last Day no ash is swept. \textbf{Ilmen} is the thaw and the first fishing; \textbf{Silt} is mud and the Breaking; \textbf{Seed} and \textbf{Tallow} are planting and lambing; \textbf{Ember} is the kiln month, short and fierce; \textbf{Grain} and \textbf{Smoke} are harvest and second firing; \textbf{Frost} and \textbf{Bone} are hunting and repair; \textbf{Ash} is the dark before the ice.

A day’s eating in Low Kiln is barley-oat bread with a brown edge, a pottage of turnip, chard and eel, salted perch in brine, dried peas in winter, sour-milk cheese when the cows are in, and small ale watered until it barely counts. At the fattening before the Five a household kills a pig and nothing is wasted, not the trotters, not the bladder. Kiln apples are baked in the embers and eaten with the outside burnt. Children are given the browned crust. This is considered kindness.

The smell of Grath is woodsmoke under wet wool, with peat smoke underneath that, and clay dust in the throat, and the sweetish edge of lead glaze from High Kiln, and tallow, and river silt when the wind comes off the water. In a Low Kiln room after dark there is one more smell: the sour, faintly mineral tang of the Ash in the corner, which everybody knows and nobody names.

\section*{Trades and prices}

Grath’s trades are clay-digging, slip-mixing, throwing, glazing, firing, peat-cutting, reed-cutting, eeling, salting, tallowing, carting, and the Court’s own trade of minding the dying. A child of eight can earn six bits a day sweeping the yard. A kiln hand earns a mark. The price of an ordinary life in Low Kiln, counted in coin rather than warmth, runs like this:

Quart of small ale — 1 bit\par

\section*{The dangers}

The Breaking takes the low houses every few years and leaves the silt waist-deep in the Fishstairs. Thin ice kills two or three children a winter. The kiln’s breath kills a man in his sleep and is called a merciful death by people who have not had to identify the body. The potter’s cough shortens breath and life. The sweet sickness from bad glaze shows first as a pale line at the gum and ends with a man who cannot hold a cup. There are wolves in the reedbeds east of Mud End. There are feuds between the kiln-houses that last longer than the men who started them. And there is the collector, who comes not with a threat but with a slate, a cold iron tube, and an account already written down.

\section*{VI. Measure, coin, and what a life books at}

\textbf{Coin.} Twelve copper \textbf{bits} make one silver \textbf{mark}. Twenty silver marks make one gold \textbf{crown}, which is rarely seen in Grath and common in Sere. The Court books warmth in \textbf{embers} and will tell you that an ember is worth five marks. No one has ever exchanged one for the other.

\textbf{The measure of a vessel.} A newborn vessel holds twelve to fifteen embers. A child holds twenty to thirty. A labourer in good case holds forty to sixty. A Held vessel holds a hundred or more. A Kept vessel holds three hundred and feels nothing at all. When a person is collected, the measure is drawn into the bank and pressed into one or many infants. A \textbf{whole return} keeps one person’s warmth intact and brings back the same person with memory. A \textbf{thin return} pools many measures and divides them; what comes back is a stranger with a faint interest in a life it cannot name.

\textbf{The deduction.} Every banked person is told that one ember in twelve is kept back for the Hearth. This is taught to children, printed on the Weighing notices, and written in the collector’s own hand. In Low Kiln the true figure is one in three, and the difference is not a tax but the mechanism by which the poor are spent.

\textbf{The Ash.} Grey silt gathers where promises are unkept: in corners, under beds, in the joints of the world. It is invisible in daylight and plain by tallow-light. The poor of Grath leave it on the Last Day. The Court bottles it and sells it to the rich as quiet ash. It is not dirt. It is what is left of a self when the Warm has been taken.

\section*{The peoples of Sarn}

\emph{This account records how Sarnish folk name themselves, how they count their ranks, and how they bury their dead. Names are set down with care, because a name in Sarn is a vessel of a kind: it holds a person, and a person returned thin may find it does not hold as well as it did.}

\CRrule

\section*{I. Peoples and races}

\section*{The Sarnish stock}

The common people of the bowl are one stock, divided by country and trade rather than by blood. A Grathic clay-hand and a Vhaleni salt-master can have children together, and the children are simply of whichever hearth raises them. What separates them is the tongue, the food, and the way they hold a slate. The six great cultures are:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]

\item \textbf{Grathic} — the clay country, river valleys, peat and kilns. Low, guttural speech, short names, long grudges.

\item \textbf{Vhaleni} — the Sallow ports. Sibilant, practical, salt-soaked; a people of contracts that rot in the rain.

\item \textbf{Keldish} — the high Rim. Clipped, numerical, suspicious of warmth they did not dig out of stone.

\item \textbf{Sere} — the southern empire. Long vowels, open courtyards, public feasting, and the largest slave population in the world.

\item \textbf{Fenfolk} — the peat and reed country. Slow nasal Cant, boardwalk villages, cheap returns.

\item \textbf{Wastelings} — the cold outside. A handful of borrowed words and no grammar worth the name.

\end{itemize}

\section*{The Vorr}

The Vorr are not Sarnish and do not claim to be. They are tall, slow, long-lived, and hold their measure in the bone rather than in the blood, which makes them difficult to collect and impossible to bank in the ordinary way. Their skin has the pale grey of old warm-stone and their fingers are doubled at the last joint. They do not undergo the Return. When a Vorr dies, the others seat the body upright facing the direction it came from and leave it. They say: \emph{We were never owed.}

The Vorr keep the Listening Stones in the high Rim and sell nothing but numbers. They claim to be able to read the oldest slates, and have never been induced to translate one in full. The Court employs them as surveyors and watches them carefully. There are perhaps four thousand Vorr in the world, most of them in Keld, and almost none in Grath.

\section*{The blent}

Where Sere experiments with grafting beast-clay into human vessels, or the Court patches a wall with material taken from a donor of another kind, the result is called \textbf{blent}. Some blent pass as people. Some do not. They are strongest in the southern empire, where they are exhibited, and commonest in the back rooms of the Hearthseat, where they are made. The law says a blent person has the measure of the vessel its donor had, which means in practice that a blent person has no measure at all.

\section*{The Kept}

The Kept are not a people but a condition. There are fewer than twenty in Sarn. Their vessels have been made perfectly retentive, so that nothing leaks in and nothing leaks out. They do not feel cold, hunger, pain or affection. They are spoken of with pity and are generally left alone in rooms with good fires, which they cannot enjoy. The Unbanked intend to extend their condition to everyone.

\section*{II. Languages and scripts}

Language — Where — Sound — Written on\par

\textbf{Grathic} — Grath, and as a common tongue everywhere — Short vowels, hard \emph{k} and \emph{thr}, little inflection — Slate with chalk; birch bark for accounts that must travel\par

\textbf{Ledgerhand} — The Warm Court — Grathic grammar with the verbs removed — Slate, in a neat upright hand; the only script all officers read\par

\textbf{Vhaleni} — Sallow ports — Sibilant, many tide-words, endings slurred — Salt-paper, which rots in damp\par

\textbf{Keldish} — The Rim — Clipped consonants, numbers before nouns — Tally cuts in bone and stone\par

\textbf{Sere} — The south — Long open vowels, elaborate politeness — Glazed tablet and paper with gold ink\par

\textbf{Fen Cant} — The Fenmarch — Nasal, slow, half-sung — Not written; marked sticks and knots\par

\textbf{Waste Cant} — Beyond the Rim — Grunts and borrowed Grathic — Nothing\par

\textbf{The Deep Hand} — Nowhere living — Unknown; the Vorr recognise the numbers — The oldest slates and the seam records\par

Ledgerhand is a deliberately cold language. It has no word for \emph{please}, no word for \emph{sorry}, and eleven separate words for the different kinds of deduction. Its sentences are all in the passive: \emph{the measure is banked}, \emph{the return is recorded}, \emph{the account stands}. Nobody in Sarn says such things at a fire. Officers say them at a table.

The Deep Hand appears on the oldest objects and on nothing else. It is taught in no school. When the Vorr look at it they say only \emph{the numbers are correct}, which is not a translation.

\section*{III. Naming conventions}

\section*{Grathic}

A Grathic name has a \textbf{given name} of one or two syllables and a \textbf{house-name} taken from a trade, a place, or a material. A person is entered in the slate as \emph{given plus house}. Foundlings receive a given name from whoever writes the birth-slate and no house-name until a hearth claims them. This is why \textbf{Corin}, written by a collector before she had seen his face, was simply Corin for the first years of his life.

Given names are often materials, tools or small domestic things: clay, ash, reed, stone, coal, a pot, a hand, a bird. They are not poetic. A Grathic child is named for what the household has plenty of.

Examples\par

Given names — Alda, Berr, Corin, Dessa, Ennit, Fenna, Garl, Hesta, Ivo, Jorra, Kess, Ludo, Mira, Nils, Orra, Rusk, Sella, Tarn, Ulda\par

House-names — Vail (kiln), Reed, Clay, Silt, Tallow, Marsh, Ash, Sill, Weir\par

Full names — Sella Vail, Jorra Vail, Alda Reed, Mira Silt, Tarn Vail, Hesta Clay, Rusk of the Weir\par

Foundlings — Corin, Ennit, Ulda — one name only, no house\par

The Vail house of Low Kiln is a kiln-house, and has been Vail for four generations. The hearth-maid who attended Corin’s birth was \textbf{Alda Reed}, who crossed herself at the birth-flower and told nobody what she had seen.

\section*{Vhaleni}

Vhaleni given names are short, open and usually taken from tides, winds, birds or salt. A house-name follows, taken from a boat, a quay or a trade. A Vhaleni who has no house-name is assumed to be a guest, which in Vhalen is a precarious condition.

Examples\par

Given names — Avis, Cella, Dolf, Eda, Gull, Havi, Jeva, Kett, Lona, Mav, Neri, Orro, Pia, Ravi, Tavi, Ulla, Veno\par

House-names — Wake, Hull, Quay, Brine, Sail, Salt, Net\par

Full names — Avis Wake, Kett of the Quay, Mav Hull, Dolf Brine, Neri Sail\par

\section*{Keldish}

Keldish names are blunt and frequently begin with a hard consonant. The number of a man’s measure is tattooed on his forearm and is considered part of his name; a Keldish person asked who he is will often give the number first. There are no house-names in the lowland sense. A person is known by the mine or the pass.

Examples\par

Given names — Arl, Bret, Caul, Dorn, Ekk, Farr, Gred, Hald, Ivar, Jarn, Karr, Lod, Marn, Orv, Pike, Ragn, Siv, Tord, Ulf, Vek\par

Designations — of the First Seam, of the Cold Pass, Iron, Rime, Vein\par

Full names — Siv of the First Seam, Tord Rime, Karric of the Cold Pass, Hald Iron\par

\section*{Sere}

A Sere child may accumulate seven names by adolescence and keep whichever the household prefers. Names are long, vowel-heavy and often taken from heat, light or generous acts. House-names are grand and frequently false.

Examples\par

Given names — Alessa, Bastian, Dalia, Feria, Halia, Ilyra, Joren, Liria, Maris, Oriana, Rial, Solene, Talia, Valen, Zeri\par

House-names — Sunbowl, Ember, Gilded, of the Ten Bowls, Openhand\par

Full names — Alessa Sunbowl, Rial of the Ten Bowls, Ilyra Gilded, Valen Openhand\par

\section*{Fenfolk}

Fen names are short, thick and usually nouns from the bog. A Fen person is known by a given name and a reed-name; the two are often the same word said twice. They dislike written names and will not sit at a table where a slate is open.

Examples\par

Given names — Bogga, Dib, Eech, Gorr, Hobb, Jow, Keg, Lull, Mudge, Nib, Ork, Pog, Quill, Rube, Slog, Tuss, Uck, Vobb\par

Reed-names — Peat, Reed, Eel, Mud, Rope\par

Full names — Mudge Peat, Bogga of the Reed, Slog Eel, Hobb Mud\par

\section*{Wastelings}

Wastelings take a single word as a name and change it when it stops fitting. Nobody has a surname in the Waste. A Wasteling asked for a second name will usually point at the cold.

Examples\par

Names — Ash, Bone, Cold, Dregs, Grey, Husk, Knuckle, Loam, Mote, Nub, Pith, Rind, Skag, Thistle, Vole\par

Forms — Grey of the Waste, Husk, Knuckle Who Counts\par

\section*{The Vorr}

Vorr names are long-vowelled and given one at a time, many years apart. A Vorr is known by the stone he keeps rather than by a family.

Examples\par

Names — Ao, Eru, Iyn, Oun, Uro, Aen, Oev, Itha, Elo, Unn\par

Forms — Ao of the Third Seam, Eru Who Counts, Iyn of the Listening Stone\par

\section*{The Court and the naming of officers}

An officer of the Keeping has no house and no kin. On entering the service she is given a \textbf{slate-name}, which is entered on the rolls in Ledgerhand and is the only name she has. Some are one word, some two, and the second word is usually an office gone stiff: Varr, Rennick, Haldre, Marra.

Examples\par

Slate-names — Marra, Derren, Haldre, Rennick, Varr, Wessen\par

Titles set before — Steward Derren, Assessor Pell Varr, Collector Marra, Keeper Haldre\par

Rule — The account is her family; a question about her parents is a question about nothing\par

\section*{Slaves, foundlings and the returned}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]

\item \textbf{Slaves} are given a clay-name by the household that owns them and their old name is forbidden. In Sere the forbidden name is sometimes written on the inside of a bowl, which the slave is never allowed to look into. Grathish kept hands are usually named for the house that holds them: \emph{the Vail’s Tarn}.

\item \textbf{Foundlings} receive a given name from the writer of the birth-slate. In Grath this is the hearth-maid or the collector, whichever arrives first. A foundling without a house-name cannot inherit a kiln, a boat or a debt, which is usually the point.

\item \textbf{The returned} keep their name if the return was whole. If it was thin, the district’s name-pot supplies a new one at birth and the old name is entered in a separate column of the slate that the family is not shown. A person banked five times may have five names and remember the wrong one.

\end{itemize}

\section*{IV. Class structure}

Rank — Who they are — How they are spoken of\par

\textbf{The Kept} — Fewer than twenty; vessels made unbreakable by the Court — With pity, and from a distance\par

\textbf{Court officers} — Steward, assessors, collectors, bankers, keepers — \emph{Clean hands}. They do not carry fuel\par

\textbf{Held} — Guild masters, factors, ship-owners, successful mercenaries — \emph{A vessel that carries more than its share}\par

\textbf{Whole free} — Craftsmen, farmers, potters, would-be scholars — \emph{A whole man}, meaning one who holds\par

\textbf{Thin} — Labourers, the returned poor, the walking thin — \emph{Thin}, never to their face in Grath\par

\textbf{Clay-bound} — Debt-servants working off a term, commonly seven years — \emph{Owed out}; their children are born to the debt\par

\textbf{Bought breath} — Chattel slaves; the owner holds their measure in law — \emph{A kept hand} in Grath, \emph{a bowl} in Sere\par

\textbf{The uncollected} — Those who die without being banked — Nobody. Their warmth goes into the Waste\par

\textbf{The Unbanked} — A secret faction inside the Keeping, not the same as the uncollected — Not spoken of outside sealed rooms\par

A person may be high in one column and low in another. A Held merchant in Vhalen may be thin in the Court’s books and clay-bound to a Sere factor. Sarn’s classes are not a ladder but a set of overlapping accounts, and the accounts are never reconciled.

\section*{V. Gender customs}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]

\item \textbf{Grath.} Both men and women throw clay and fire kilns. A widow can hold a kiln in her own right, which is called \emph{a widow’s mark} and is the only Grathish institution nobody argues with. The house-hearth passes to the eldest child regardless of sex; the slip-pits pass to whoever has the stronger hands. A woman who is banked thin may still keep the house but not the wheel.

\item \textbf{Vhalen.} Boat shares pass through mothers. A Vhaleni man who marries into a boat-owning family is described as \emph{come aboard}, and his opinion on the boat is counted at half. Sailors of either sex are common; captains are mostly women, because the sea does not care and the Council has learned not to either.

\item \textbf{Keld.} Only men go down the deep mines, because a woman’s measure is said to be \emph{closer to the surface} and would draw the sweat out of the stone. Women do the counting above ground and hold the mine-reeve’s keys in some families. A man who returns thin cannot descend, and is given the keys instead. This is considered a kindness and is not one.

\item \textbf{Sere.} Inheritance goes to the eldest surviving child regardless of sex. Concubinage with returned slaves is open and is a public measure of a household’s standing. A woman of a great house may own bowls, slaves and a husband’s debt; she may not enter the counting room, where her numbers are read by a man.

\item \textbf{Fenfolk.} Reeves are chosen by who can stand longest in a bog, and about half are women. A Fen mother names her children before the collectors do, which is the closest thing the Fenmarch has to a declaration of war.

\item \textbf{The Vorr.} The Vorr do not divide work or inheritance by sex. They divide it by whether one has sat against a Listening Stone long enough to hear it, which takes most of a life and is not permitted to the impatient.

\end{itemize}

Across Sarn, a person who has been banked more than three times is considered an unsafe marriage partner, regardless of sex, because the Second Want may arrive in a child’s body wearing the wrong face.

\section*{VI. Coming-of-age}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]

\item \textbf{Grath, the First Firing, twelve years.} A child makes a pot, fires it, and knocks it with a knuckle. A clear note means the household holds a feast; a flat note means the child serves a year in the yard and tries again. Corin, being a foundling with no kiln of his own, will be judged on a pot made in the Vail yard.

\item \textbf{Vhalen, the Salt Walk, fourteen.} The young walk the pans barefoot at low tide and name a tide they have never seen. Salt is considered a sufficient teacher.

\item \textbf{Keld, the Descent, thirteen.} The young go down the mine for one day. Some come back with their number changed. A few do not come back, and the family is paid in iron.

\item \textbf{Sere, the Bowl, sixteen.} A public feast. The young person empties a bowl of warm wine and names a debt he intends to carry. The applause is for the debt, not the drinker.

\item \textbf{Fenfolk, the Reed Cut, twelve.} The child cuts a bundle of reed alone, in the dark, and brings it back without speaking. If the bundle is wet, the child is not yet of the bog.

\item \textbf{The Vorr, the Long Sitting, forty.} The Vorr sit still against a stone for a season and listen. What they hear is not recorded, but their numbers change afterwards.

\end{itemize}

\section*{VII. Death rites}

\textbf{The common rite.} The collector arrives with a cold iron tube and a slate. The measure is drawn, booked, and deducted. The body is washed and laid with its feet toward the nearest water. A pot is broken at the door to let the heat out. A year later, the return is announced at the Weighing. The rich receive themselves. The poor receive a child who is faintly interested in a life it cannot name.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]

\item \textbf{Grath.} Feet to the river. A broken pot at the door. The ash in the corner is left on the Last Day and burnt the next morning. A household that cannot pay the deduction keeps the slate and pays in labour, usually by a child sent to the kiln.

\item \textbf{Vhalen.} The dead are sealed in a salted boat and pushed out on the ebb. The boat is watched until it is out of sight. A Vhaleni who dies at sea is considered already returned.

\item \textbf{Keld.} The dead are walled into the mine, facing the deep seam. The mountain keeps its own, they say, and the surveyors add numbers to a slate that nobody reads aloud.

\item \textbf{Sere.} Cremation in a public bowl, with wine and music. The warmest send-off in the world. The Court taxes the ash, and the family buys it back at the price of quiet ash, which is how a Sere funeral can ruin a house.

\item \textbf{Fenfolk.} The body is laid on a peat raft and pushed into the reedbed. The mud keeps what it wants. Fenfolk do not object to thin returns; they expect them.

\item \textbf{The Vorr.} They stop. The others seat the body upright facing the direction it came from and leave it uncovered. There is no slate, no deduction, no return. When asked why, a Vorr will say: \emph{We were never owed.}

\end{itemize}

\section*{VIII. Crime and punishment}

Crime — Common penalty — Who profits\par

Theft of goods — Compensation in marks, plus a day’s labour for the moot — The wronged household\par

Theft of warmth from another’s cup — Cold iron brand on the wrist; deduction from the thief’s own measure — The Court\par

Refusing a lawful collection — \emph{Unclosed account}: clip-held by the wrist until consent — The Court\par

Assault — Compensation for the damage to a vessel; a cut hand paid in marks — The injured person\par

Murder — The murderer is banked out; warmth divided to the victim’s house; the killer returned thin as a debt-child — The victim’s household and the Court\par

Bearing false slate — In Ledgerhand, \emph{a false measure}: the hand is struck from the rolls and the fingers greyed by cold work — The Court\par

Speaking the Waste (denying the Keeping) — Fine, and a gag of ash worn for a market-day — The Court\par

Running from bought breath — Returned thin and set to the mills; in Sere, publicly exhibited first — The owner\par

Breaking a birth-slate — In Grath, a year in the clay yard and the household’s name entered in a second column — The hearth-maids\par

Sexual violence exists in Sarn as a fact of power, most openly in the southern empire and the Keldish mines, and most quietly in households where the victim has no account worth damaging. It is punished when it damages a household’s standing, and usually not otherwise. It is not a subject for decoration. It is entered here because a reader should know what the law is worth before he is shown a court.

The cruellest penalty in Sarn is the one that sounds kindest: banking out. A murderer is not killed, because a life wasted is warmth wasted. He is emptied, returned thin to the household he wronged, and made to work the debt down. He may remember nothing of the crime. The debt remains. This is what the Court calls \emph{nothing lost that we have kept}.

\section*{The reckoning of days in Sarn}

\emph{The Court counts everything. It counts embers, deductions, returns and the years of a debt. It does not count the days a man is warm, which is the one count that matters to him. This file is the rule by which the days in the books are named.}

\CRrule

\section*{I. The year}

A Sarnish year has \textbf{365 days}: twelve months of thirty days, followed by five days outside the months called \textbf{the Five Cold Days}, or simply \textbf{the Five}. No day is ever added to make the sky agree with the slate. The Court teaches that the Warm holds the seasons in their places, and that the thaw has come in Ilmen for eight hundred years. The Keldish surveyors, who have counted both, do not say so in front of an assessor.

The months, in order, with the Grathic glosses that are used everywhere:

No. — Month — Gloss — Days of the year\par

1 — \textbf{Ilmen} — the thaw, the river rising — 1–30\par

2 — \textbf{Silt} — mud; the Breaking — 31–60\par

3 — \textbf{Seed} — planting — 61–90\par

4 — \textbf{Reed} — reed-cutting — 91–120\par

5 — \textbf{Tallow} — lambing — 121–150\par

6 — \textbf{Ember} — the kiln month, short fierce summer — 151–180\par

7 — \textbf{Grain} — harvest — 181–210\par

8 — \textbf{Smoke} — the second firing — 211–240\par

9 — \textbf{Frost} — first frosts — 241–270\par

10 — \textbf{Bone} — hunting and repair — 271–300\par

11 — \textbf{Ash} — the dark — 301–330\par

12 — \textbf{Stone} — deep winter, river ice — 331–360\par

The Five follow Stone and belong to no month:

Ordinal — Name — Observance\par

361 — \textbf{Kindling} — Every hearth in Sarn is lit from the Hearth-flame. Collectors take the year’s first measure.\par

362 — \textbf{Weighing} — The year’s measures are read aloud. The rich learn how whole they are; Low Kiln learns its deduction.\par

363 — \textbf{the Long Night} — No fire after dusk. People sit in the dark and tell the stories of the Return.\par

364 — \textbf{the Remembering} — Names of the dead are spoken. Whole returns are announced in a clean voice.\par

365 — \textbf{the Last Day} — Bread is baked. The grey Ash in the corners is \textbf{not} swept away. It is burnt the next morning.\par

Other named days:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]

\item \textbf{Account-day}, the tenth, twentieth and thirtieth of every month. In Grath these are also market-days; the slate is read at the Cross before the pigs are weighed.

\item \textbf{the Opening of the Slates}, Ilmen 1. The Court opens the new year’s accounts. The first thin returns of the year are announced at the Weighing, not now.

\item \textbf{the Hearth Drawing}, Stone 12. The first collection of the year; a collector may draw from the dying and from the newly born on the same day.

\item \textbf{the Fish Blessing}, Reed 15. The hearth-maids bless the weirs and the eel traps. In Sill this is done at the Weir, one league upriver.

\end{itemize}

\section*{Weeks}

A week is a \textbf{tend}, ten days long. Every month contains three whole tends: days 1–10, 11–20 and 21–30. The tenth day of each tend is Account-day. The tend-days are Firstday, Secondday, Thirdday, Fourthday, Fifthday, Sixthday, Seventhday, Eighthday, Ninthday and Tenthday.

\textbf{The Five are outside the tend.} They have names, not tend-days, and no market is held during them. On Ilmen 1 the count begins again at Firstday. This is why Account-days in the first weeks of a year fall on Ilmen 10, 20 and 30, and why there are three Account-days in the last ten days of Stone before the Five interrupt them.

\section*{Eras}

Years are numbered from the lighting of the Hearth: \textbf{Y.H. 1} onward. Earlier years are \textbf{B.H.}, before the Hearth; the temple calls them the Broken Years and the Keldish call them the Long Want. Scholars use informal names for stretches of the past — the Quiet Centuries (Y.H. 120–300), the Glaze Ages (300–420), the Bowl Years (420–560), the Ash Times (650–700) and the Settlement (after 747). The present year, \textbf{Y.H. 812}, is called the Thin Year in Low Kiln, after the Long Cold.

\begin{quote}\itshape \end{quote}

\begin{quote}\itshape \emph{Y} = 812 + floor(\emph{t} / 365)  — the year of the Hearth.\end{quote}

Then:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]

\item If \emph{y} ≤ 360: the month is floor((\emph{y} − 1) / 30) + 1, and the day of the month is ((\emph{y} − 1) mod 30) + 1. The tend-day is ((day of month − 1) mod 10) + 1.

\item If \emph{y} is 361 to 365: the date is Kindling, Weighing, the Long Night, the Remembering or the Last Day, in that order, in year \emph{Y}. There is no tend-day.

\end{itemize}

\textbf{Worked examples.} Day 24: \emph{t} = 359, \emph{y} = 360, \emph{Y} = 812 → Stone 30, Tenthday, Y.H. 812. Day 30: \emph{t} = 365, \emph{y} = 1, \emph{Y} = 813 → Ilmen 1, Firstday, Y.H. 813. Day 100: \emph{t} = 435, \emph{y} = 70, \emph{Y} = 813 → Seed 10, Tenthday, Y.H. 813. Day 365: \emph{t} = 700, \emph{y} = 336, \emph{Y} = 813 → Stone 6, Sixthday, Y.H. 813, the day before the first birthday.

For any day not in a month, write the name of the holy day and the year; do not invent a date for the Five. For all other days, write the month, the day of the month and the year, in that order, as \emph{14 Ilmen, Y.H. 813}.
