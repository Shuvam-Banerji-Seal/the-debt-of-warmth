\section*{Cindermere: The World and Its Realms}

\emph{Compiled from the common reckoning. Where the priesthood’s account is the only one left standing, it is given as the priesthood tells it. A margin note here and there records what the old surveyors found and chose not to explain.}

\CRrule

\section*{The common account of the world}

The world is a vessel that was fired and cracked and is still being repaired. This is not a metaphor to the people of Cindermere; it is the first thing a child is taught, and the last thing a dying person hears. The earth is the clay body. The sky is the lid set upon it. Where lid and body meet, at the horizon, runs the \textbf{Great Seam}, riveted with the stars — every star a rivet-head, old and bright with handling. Beneath the ground lies the \textbf{Under-Seam}, and below that the dark where nothing is kept.

According to the templars of Ond, the vessel was made whole in the beginning and broke in a single moment of anger. The break was called the \textbf{First Crack}. The \textbf{Farriers of the First Light} are said to have driven the first staples and left the rite of the \textbf{Mending} behind so that mortal hands might continue the work. That is the teaching. The older surveyors left a different note: the Great Seam shows many repairs, laid one over another, and the oldest of them are not the work of any god that can be named. Some are not even made of seam-metal. They are made of something that looks, in the record, like \emph{language}.

Three substances are spoken of in every market and every court.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]

\item \textbf{The quick.} The silvery fluid in living blood. It is what a body has that a corpse does not. In the Mending it is drawn off and set into a new vessel.

\item \textbf{The Owed.} A grey silt that gathers where promises are unkept — in neglected corners of houses, under beds, in the seams of the spent, and in the joints of the world itself. It is invisible in daylight and plain by tallow-light. Every household knows the small grey ridge along the skirting and knowns not to sweep it away on the last day of the year.

\item \textbf{Seam-metal.} Any brass-like alloy used for staples. The common sort is a dull yellow and is mined in the Rift Marches. The fine sort, used by the House of Seams, is said to be worked with lead and silver and is the colour of old button brass.

\end{itemize}

The poor know a fourth thing, though the schools do not name it: \textbf{the salt-sickness}. Drink of the \textbf{Pale Water} and you lose the self but keep the memories. The dry cantons sell it by the thimble to people who want to forget. The House of Seams sells it by the barrel and calls it a medicine.

\section*{Shape of the land}

The known world is one great landmass, saucer-shaped, tipped slightly to the south-east. At its centre lies the \textbf{Pale Water}, a shallow salt lake some sixty leagues from north to south and thirty across, the colour of a bruise that has been rubbed. Its shores are grey shelves of silt that shift with the wind; at low water the shelves show shapes the boatmen will not describe and the priests call temptations. Around the lake lie the inhabited rings. Beyond them are the wastes.

\textbf{Leagues and smaller measures.} A hand is about the width of a man’s palm. A foot is three hands. A pace is two and a half feet. A chain is forty paces, a furlong ten chains, a mile five furlongs (two thousand paces). A \textbf{league} is ten miles — roughly a long day’s walk, or two days with a cart. A man walking steadily covers two leagues between sunrise and sunset. Distances in this book are given in leagues, miles and chains.

\textbf{The ring of lands, going clockwise from the north-west:}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]

\item \textbf{The Clay March} — low, wet, ochre ground west and north-west of the lake. The protagonist’s homeland. River Gyle, river Vey, clay pits, kiln-yards, flax, washing.

\item \textbf{The Wold} (the realm of \textbf{Harrow}) — cold highland forest to the north, all iron and pine and frost.

\item \textbf{The Gloam Weald} — ancient forest to the west, older than the survey, full of dim light and older laws.

\item \textbf{The Salt Marches} — the flats around the Pale Water, south and west, given over to salt clans and rumour.

\item \textbf{The Rift Marches} — broken high country east of Ond, scarred by the old wars; scrap, ruins, free companies.

\item \textbf{The Coast of Nine Harbours} — the warm south-eastern seaboard on the \textbf{Cinder Sea}, a league of merchant cities.

\item \textbf{The Ashen Waste} — the empty south, salt and cinder, where nothing is surveyed and little returns.

\end{itemize}

Two seas bound the world: the \textbf{Cinder Sea} to the south-east, warm and bright with shipping, and the \textbf{Gloamwater} to the far west, cold and seldom crossed. The strait between the Cinder Sea and the Pale basin is called the \textbf{Ashen Strait}; the old charts mark it \emph{passable at slack water only, and not then without paying}.

\section*{Wonders}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]

\item \textbf{The Pale Water.} The lake itself. It never freezes, even in the hardest Wold winter, and it hums at night — a low note that the boatmen say is the sound of the Great Seam working. At low water it gives up grey silt in long ropes. The silt is gathered and sold.

\item \textbf{The Singing Staple.} In the Salt Marches, twenty-two leagues south-west of Ond, a brass staple as tall as a cathedral stands driven into the flat ground. No one knows who set it. At dawn it gives out one note, pure and small, and every Mending in the region is timed to it. The House of Seams has built a chapel around its foot and forbids drilling it.

\item \textbf{The Glasswold.} In the Rift Marches, a stand of trees turned to green glass by some ancient firing, each trunk still holding the shape of bark. Scrap-pickers take slivers from it; the slivers cut and do not heal cleanly.

\item \textbf{The Bone Orchard.} A grove in the Gloam Weald where pale shapes hang from the boughs like fruit. The Weald-born say these are Names waiting to be called. They do not let strangers count them.

\item \textbf{The Star Seam.} The horizon at dawn. Travellers in the Salt Marches insist that on the longest night the rivets along the edge of the sky can be heard cooling, one by one.

\end{itemize}

\section*{Wastes}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]

\item \textbf{The Salt Waste} — the grey shelf-lands of the Pale Water. Barren, alkali, salt-crusted.

\item \textbf{The Ash Waste} — south of the Rift Marches, deep in volcanic cinder. Nothing grows; scrap-iron is found there, and sometimes coins of a mint no realm admits.

\item \textbf{The Silt Basin} — south-east of the lake, a bog of grey ooze into which whole carts vanish. It is the source of much of the Owed used in Ond.

\item \textbf{The Frost Weald} — the northern reach of the Wold, where the trees stand frozen in winter and do not rot when they fall.

\end{itemize}

\section*{The realms}

\section*{1. The House of Seams (seat: Ond)}

\textbf{Government.} A theocratic ledger-state. The \textbf{Seam-Master} is both high priest and chief accountant, chosen by the \textbf{Riveter Synod}, a council of twelve who hold the great seals. Below them the \textbf{Factors} keep the Ledger — the register of every soul Mended, every debt entered, every name struck through. A person’s standing is his account. The city is governed as a workshop is governed: by foremen, with a bell.

\textbf{Economy.} The Mending is the industry. Riveters draw the quick and set staples; seam-metal is mined in the Rift Marches and worked in the Cinder Quays; salt is scraped from the Pale Water; the Owed is harvested as silt and sold as raw material. Ond exports second lives, fine staples, salt, and the \emph{habit} of Mending. A cheap Mending costs one florin; a fine one, twenty florins and upward; a Grand Seam (an unbroken series of fine Mendings) is priced by the Synod and never advertised. An unskilled labourer in the Cinder Quays earns six mites a day. A Riveter of the Synod earns a seal a year and is housed.

\textbf{Religion.} The cult of the Seam. Its saying, carved over the House door in the sharp script of Ond and repeated at every Mending, is: \emph{What is broken is owed.} The great feast is the \textbf{Sealing}, at the year’s end. The faithful believe that every Mending strengthens the world’s great repair; the more often one is mended, the more one is held. The poor are Mended as often as they can be afforded, in bulk, in the \textbf{Cull Warrens}.

\textbf{Customs.} Every citizen is expected to be Mended at least once, at sixteen. The seam-mark — a silver-white line across the palm — is a brand among the poor and a badge among the great, who have it removed by a knife and wear the removal as a sign of having been mended often enough to afford it. Names are entered in the Ledger at birth and struck through at death. No one in Ond says the word \emph{dead} in a room where a debt is being discussed.

\textbf{Outsiders.} Welcomed politely and entered immediately as debtors-in-waiting. An un-Mended foreigner is called \emph{raw} and is not allowed to hold property for more than a year and a day. Foreign craftsmen are recruited, tested, and sometimes never seen again. The House is especially interested in anyone who can hear whether a repair is true.

\textbf{Slavery and law.} Slavery is ordinary and legal. Debtors who cannot pay are worked in the salt quays until the account clears; most never clear it. \textbf{Culls} are the over-mended — souls stapled so often and beaten down so far that only fragments remain — and they are the House’s domestic labour. The Ledger is the law: an offence is not a crime but an arrears, entered in a clean hand and collected. The worst punishment is not death but \textbf{un-Mending}: the refusal to repair a soul, so that it is left to the silt.

\textbf{Fears.} That the Great Seam will split and the world will fall into the Under-Seam; that the Pale Water will rise and wash the names out; that an un-Mended soul will gather enough Owed to crack a vessel; and — most of all, though it is never written — that a repair could be done \emph{truly}, in a way that leaves the memory whole and the account unsettled. Such a repair cannot be hollowed. The Synod keeps a file for it.

\section*{2. The Free Villages of the Clay March (no capital; largest village: Kilnwick)}

\textbf{Government.} Each village chooses a \textbf{clay-reeve} for a year by show of hands at the spring threshing. Reeves meet at Veyford four times a year. There is no standing army and no tax in coin; each household owes three days of road-work on the River Road. The March cannot resist Ond and does not try; it survives by being poor and awkward to govern.

\textbf{Economy.} Clay-digging, lead-glazed pottery, washing, flax, eel-fishing, salt from the flats, and barley. A clay-digger earns two groats a day, a washerwoman one, a potter three. A two-room cottage rents for four groats a week; a loaf of rye bread costs two mites; a pig, thirty groats; a barrel of eels, six groats. A traveller’s Riveter comes to Kilnwick every nine weeks and Mends the village’s dead in the chapel yard for one florin a head — the cheap rite, done quickly, in a line.

\textbf{Religion.} The \textbf{Hearthrite}: household gods of the fire, the pot, the threshold and the washing-line. The Mending is accepted as a fact of life, the way weather is, but not loved. A clay-marcher will say \emph{the pot holds, the pot holds} and leave it there. The old women keep the quarter-day customs that the priests of Ond call superstition.

\textbf{Customs.} Proverbs and half-sentences. Address the pot, the ground, the fire, and only then each other. At twelve a child is given a \textbf{clay-mark} at the wrist and counted a worker. The dead are laid under a cairn of kiln-wasters with a pot set upside down over the face. A visitor is given bread and asked no questions until he has eaten.

\textbf{Outsiders.} Tolerated, watched, fed, and not trusted. An Ond man in Kilnwick is called a \emph{ledger-man} and is expected to be pleasant and dangerous. A stranger who can mend something is welcomed at once and never quite forgiven for being useful.

\textbf{Slavery and law.} No chattel slavery. Debt-service is common and short — a season, a winter, rarely longer — and a debtServant keeps his name. Law is restitution: pay for what you break, in kind if you cannot in coin. A man who breaks a pot mends it or pays for the pot; the village cares more about the honesty of the repair than the loss.

\textbf{Fears.} The Gyle coming up in the thaw; the Riveter’s cart; the blue line at a potter’s gum; the salt-sickness; the winter fever; being taken for a Cull and filed; the grey silt gathering in a house that nobody has swept.

\section*{3. The Salt Cantons (the Dry Marches, south and west of the Pale Water)}

\textbf{Government.} Nine clans of salt-harvesters, each ruled by a \textbf{Cryer} who speaks for the clan and can be shouted down at the flats. No written law. Decisions are made standing in salt up to the ankle, which keeps them short.

\textbf{Economy.} Salt, grey silt, fish, and the sale of forgetting. A thimble of Pale Water fetches a groat; a flask, six. The cantons also buy and sell Names — not souls, only the spoken forms — and will keep a name safe for a price.

\textbf{Religion.} Ancestor-names. The cantons say that a name spoken aloud is a staple in the world, and that the House has been striking names out of the Ledger for centuries to hollow the world quietly. Their dead are not Mended. They are named once, at low water, and left to the lake.

\textbf{Customs.} A person is known by the name of the flat he was born on. Guests drink salt water and say nothing. Children are taught to count the shapes in the silt and never to count them twice.

\textbf{Outsiders.} Traded with, never hosted. A canton will sell a stranger forgetting and will not tell him what he has forgotten. They have a particular hatred of Riveters and a particular curiosity about anyone bearing a seam-mark.

\textbf{Slavery and law.} No slaves; blood-feud instead. A wrong is answered by taking a name — the offender’s given name falls out of use until the wrong is paid. This is considered worse than death and usually is.

\textbf{Fears.} The House’s Riveters coming up the salt road; the Pale Water rising; being hollowed; having one’s name entered in a Ledger; the shapes at low water.

\section*{4. The Kingdom of Harrow (the Wold)}

\textbf{Government.} A hereditary crown, held by the \textbf{Harrow} line at Harrowstead, under a council of thanes. The king cannot tax without the thanes and cannot make war without the frost. It is a poor crown and a stubborn one.

\textbf{Economy.} Iron, timber, wool, fur, and strong horses. Iron from the Wold is the best in the world for tools that must not fail. The Wold trades little with Ond and refuses to trade in Mending at all.

\textbf{Religion.} The old gods of frost, bone and the iron take — \textbf{Hearth-Bone}, \textbf{the Rime Mother}, and \textbf{the Smith Under the Hill}. The Wold alone among the realms rejects the Mending outright, calling it soul-theft practised by southern clerk-men. A Wold man who has been Mended is not executed but is made to wear a wooden ring and is not spoken to in company.

\textbf{Customs.} Clan and honour. Hospitality is given for a night and a day and then ends. The \textbf{Iron Take} at sixteen: a young person is given a tool and told to make something that will outlast him. Women fight, rule, and smith in the Wold as a matter of course; the southerners find this obscene and say so only in Ond.

\textbf{Outsiders.} Examined closely, hosted formally, distrusted by instinct. A guest who is rude to a horse will not leave the Wold alive, and this is not considered a crime.

\textbf{Slavery and law.} Thralls exist but are few and are treated as members of the household, badly. Law is the \textbf{weregild} — a price set on every bone and every insult. Weregild is paid in iron, never in coin.

\textbf{Fears.} The thaw; the southern House of Seams; the hollowing of the young; the rumour that one of the Harrow line was Mended in secret and now sits in Ond wearing a wooden ring.

\section*{5. The Gloam Weald (the old forest, west)}

\textbf{Government.} A council of \textbf{Gloam-mothers} at Wealdhollow, chosen by long life and a good memory. It governs by refusing things. No tax, no army, no roads.

\textbf{Economy.} Dye, bark, mushrooms, resin, and secrets. The Weald-born practise \textbf{gloamcraft} — the shaping of dim light and half-remembered things — which the Ond schools dismiss as peasant fancy and the Salt Cantons fear. They sell knowledge of Names and do the work of burying the un-Mended.

\textbf{Religion.} No temple. The Weald-born say the world is full of unfinished sentences and that a name spoken at the wrong moment can let something in. The Bone Orchard is their dead.

\textbf{Customs.} A person is given a secret name at birth that is never spoken aloud, and a common name for use with strangers. At coming of age the common name is put aside and a new one is taken from something the person has done. Iron is left at the boundary; a guest who brings iron in is asked to leave.

\textbf{Outsiders.} Tested with riddles and fed mushrooms. A stranger who answers patiently may be helped; one who answers cleverly is usually lost.

\textbf{Slavery and law.} No slavery. Wrongs are settled by the taking and returning of names. The Weald-born keep a debt against the House of Seams that is older than any living Gloam-mother and is not explained to visitors.

\textbf{Fears.} Iron, the axe, the Ledger, and the day someone learns the true names of the Weald.

\section*{6. The Coast of Nine Harbours (the Cinder Sea)}

\textbf{Government.} A league of nine merchant cities, each ruled by a \textbf{First Factor} and a council of houses. Once a year the Factors meet at \textbf{Verran} to set weights, peace, and the price of passage. They are rich, loud, and impossible to besiege all at once.

\textbf{Economy.} Shipping, glass, bronze, dye, grain, and credit. The Nine Harbours mint the \textbf{seal} in gold and will lend it at a rate. They are the only realm that trades openly with both Ond and the Wold and grows rich on the difference.

\textbf{Religion.} The \textbf{Ledger of Tides}, a commercial faith that treats debts as sacred and saints as successful merchants. Every harbour has a chapel where accounts are balanced aloud before a voyage.

\textbf{Customs.} A person’s word is a bond; a bond is entered in a book. Coming of age is at eighteen, when a young person is given a line of credit and a set of weights. Marriage is a merger. The dead are given a RECEIPT.

\textbf{Outsiders.} Welcomed if rich, tolerated if useful, ejected if neither. There is no prejudice in the Nine Harbours against the Mended; there is a strong prejudice against the poor.

\textbf{Slavery and law.} Galley and plantation slavery on a large scale, defended by contract. Law is the bond: anything written and sealed is binding, including bondage. The Ond monopoly on fine Mending is the great grievance of the coast, and smuggled seam-metal moves through Verran in wine casks.

\textbf{Fears.} The House raising the price of Mending; the Pale Water’s salt reaching their grain; a debt that cannot becalled in; the coast cities being Mended into Ond one by one.

\section*{7. The Rift Marches (the east)}

\textbf{Government.} Free companies, garrison-lords, and what is left of the old border administration. A man holds what he can hold. There are eleven forts and no borders that last a season.

\textbf{Economy.} Scrap-metal, seam-metal ore, mercenary contracts, and the digging of old ruins. The \textbf{Glasswold} supplies slivers that the Ond buyers pay well for. Coin is scarce; payment is often in staples.

\textbf{Religion.} A stew of broken gods. Every company carries a household saint it has half-invented, and every fort has a shrine to whatever saved it last.

\textbf{Customs.} Nicknames replace surnames. A person is known by the worst thing he has survived. Coming of age is not celebrated; it is simply noted that the person is now worth hiring.

\textbf{Outsiders.} Anyone with a weapon is welcome. Anyone without one is a target. Ond Factors travel here with guards and write things down from a distance.

\textbf{Slavery and law.} War captives are slaves by right of taking. Law is the contract between company and employer, enforced by the company. Punishment is short and final.

\textbf{Fears.} Another war; being conscripted for the Grand Seam works; the old ruins waking; the glass cutting deeper than it should.

\section*{The Clay March in detail — the protagonist’s birth region}

Everything below is measured from \textbf{Kilnwick}, which sits on the west bank of the \textbf{Gyle} where the river bends around a low clay bluff. Kilnwick is fourteen leagues upriver from \textbf{Veyford}, where the Gyle joins the \textbf{Vey}; from Veyford it is twenty-six leagues down the Vey to \textbf{Ond}. A boat going downriver with the current makes Kilnwick to Ond in six or seven days. Upstream, with hauling, it takes eighteen. This river road is the March’s one great artery, and the reason Ond bothers to govern the place at all.

\section*{The villages of the Gyle}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]

\item \textbf{Kilnwick} — population about three hundred and forty. Clay pits to the west, the kiln-yard and its three cone kilns to the south, the wash-house on the bank, the Hearthrite chapel under the ash tree at the centre, and the reeve’s house with a leaded window that the village is quietly proud of. Two lanes: \textbf{Clay Lane} and \textbf{the Wash Bank}. No inn; travellers are put up in the wash-house.

\item \textbf{Clayholt} — three leagues upriver. Smaller than Kilnwick, all potters, with a better clay seam and worse water. It supplies the bricks for Kilnwick’s kilns.

\item \textbf{Mire End} — six leagues downriver, in the wet flats. Eels, rushes, and a bridge that washes away twice a decade. The people are said to be slow and to hold their breath when crossing.

\item \textbf{Barrow Wick} — two leagues east over the rise, built around an old burial mound. The mound is dug into every winter and nothing is ever found in it, which is considered a good sign.

\item \textbf{Eelhook} — four leagues downriver on a bend, a fishing hamlet of eleven households. Its eels are salted and sold as far as Veyford.

\item \textbf{Veyford} — fourteen leagues downriver, at the joining of the Gyle and the Vey. A town of about nine hundred, a toll bridge, the district Riveter’s post, and the only market where a clay-marcher can sell a pot for coin. Every March grievance begins or ends in Veyford.

\end{itemize}

\section*{Roads and tracks}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]

\item \textbf{The River Road} — the hauling path along the Gyle and then the Vey to Ond. Mud half the year, dust for six weeks in high summer. Every household owes three days a year keeping it passable.

\item \textbf{The Old Kiln Way} — an overland track from Kilnwick through Clayholt and Barrow Wick to Veyford, eighteen leagues, used when the river is high or the toll is hated.

\item \textbf{The Salt Track} — nine leagues south from Kilnwick to \textbf{Saltham} on the first flats. Salt comes back on it in sacks; the occasional stranger goes down it and does not come back up.

\end{itemize}

\section*{Rivers, seasons and weather}

The \textbf{Gyle} is a slow brown river, thirty paces wide at Kilnwick and deep enough to drown a man where it turns under the bluff. It rises in the thaw (Brook and early Silt) and again after the long rains of Rot, and it is at its lowest in Kiln. The \textbf{Vey} is broader, silt-heavy, and carries the whole basin’s clay to Ond; its flood is feared more than the Gyle’s because it takes the bridge at Veyford with it.

The year turns as follows in the March:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]

\item \textbf{Fasten and Brook} — winter loosening. Frost in the morning, mud by noon. The Gyle comes up. Nothing is planted. People mend what they did not get mended in the autumn.

\item \textbf{Silt} — planting. The ground is brown and sticky; every smith is shoeing oxen; every child is sent to scare birds off the new barley. Wren’s birth month.

\item \textbf{Grow and Hay} — the good months. Peas, barley, oats, turnips; the washing-line work; the first eel traps. Bees in the clay hedges.

\item \textbf{Kiln} — the firing month. Three kilns at Kilnwick burn for nine days and the whole village tastes of hot clay and smoke. Lead-glazed ware goes out; blue lines appear at the gums of those who work the glaze.

\item \textbf{Glean, Rook and Rot} — reaping, then the flocks, then the rot. Bread, pottage, salt fish, the last of the ale. The grey silt in the houses grows thicker as the days shorten.

\item \textbf{Fell, Tallow and Wold} — slaughter, tallow, deepest winter. Pigs killed at Fell; candles made at Tallow; at Wold the village sits close to the fire and says the names of its dead. Winter fever takes the old and the thin.

\end{itemize}

\section*{Food, smells and ordinary dangers}

A clay-marcher eats rye-and-barley bread, pea pottage, turnips, salted eel, whey, and on good days a little pork. Ale is weak and small; the good beer goes to Veyford. There is no tea. There is mallow tea for a cough and a bitter infusion of bog-myrtle for the ague. A child’s first solid food is bread dipped in pot liquor.

The smells of Kilnwick, in order of the day: woodsmoke from the bank fires; wet wool drying on a body; clay dust from the pits; brine and eel from the wash-house; tallow and hot iron from the chapel windows; baking bread from the reeve’s oven; and, under all of it when the river is low, the sour vegetable reek the Gyle carries from the upper marshes. People there do not mention that last smell. They have learned not to.

Dangers are ordinary and close. The Gyle takes a person most years, usually a child or a drunk. The kilns burn; the glaze poisons slowly and without drama; the winter fever comes with the first hard frost. Boats going down to Veyford sometimes come back with a Riveter aboard, and a household with an unpaid account can lose a member to the Cinder Quays for a season. In the Gloam edge beyond Clayholt, wolves take a sheep now and then and once took a child, and the old women say the wolf was a man who had forgotten his name. Grey silt gathers in the corner behind the wash-house, and no one sweeps it out after dark.

\section*{Note on money (the whole realm’s reckoning)}

Four coins are in common use: the copper \textbf{mite}, the brass \textbf{groat} (twelve mites), the silver \textbf{florin} (twenty groats), and the gold \textbf{seal} (twelve florins). The Nine Harbours mint the seal; Ond mints the florin and the groat; the March uses mites and groats and very little else. Prices given above are the common prices of the common years. In a bad winter bread doubles and wages do not.

The coin \textbf{Wren’s mother kept in a board by the bed} — a silver florin of Ond, its script sharp and unlike any March writing — is worth, in the March, more than a year’s wages. That is why no one in Kilnwick looked at it twice: poor people do not spend coins they cannot read.
