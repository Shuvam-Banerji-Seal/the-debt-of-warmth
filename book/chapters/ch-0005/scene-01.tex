\dropcap{T}{he coals} had held under their ash through the night, and first light came into the \place{Vail kiln-house} at the door's finger's width of failure, thin as a pulled thread, going along the floor to the corner where the grey lay. It was the same light that had come yesterday and the day before, and the boy did not spend anything on surprise. \person{Corin} lay under the fold with a thread of drool at the corner of his mouth that nobody had wiped in the night and would not be wiped now until somebody's hand happened to pass that way. Seven days old. He had the floorboards already: four on the near board, four on the next, and a knot the width of a thumb standing in for the fifth nail. The knot had not moved. The cold had come in at cheek, then temple, then wrist, in the order he knew and could not argue with, and the house had gone on breathing around him the way a vessel breathes when it is held and not yet cracked.

\person{Sella Vail} came between the bed and the fire and put her hand into the fold without once looking at his face. The little-door burn across the back of her hand --- two straight sides, a lintel and a threshold, the skin inside it puckered and pale --- closed first on nothing, then on the wool, and she set the fold right at his ear and under his chin the way a potter straightens a rim by touch in the dark. She did not wipe the drool. That was not a cruelty and she would not have called it one; there was a dish and a spoon and a hundred things the house required before a corner of wool at a child's mouth. The boy had learned the order of her hands. He had learned that what she did not do was as exact as what she did.

She went to the coals, and before she lifted the kiln lid her hand went to \lore{the widow's mark} at her throat, the thumb-length of lead-glazed clay on its cord with the Vail kiln scratched into it. She held it a moment longer than the touch required. Then she let it go and gave the instruction to the fire.

“There,” she said to the coals. “That'll take. Don't set that near the door. You'll pay for it in the thaw.”

She said it to the pot, and then to the fire, and never once to the basket. The boy lay under the fold with the rule of the mark already in him as he had the rule of the kiln's breath: touched before the opening, at no other time, not at the bank last night, not to answer a man who spoke her name in a market and called the answering a kindness. He did not know what the rule was for. He knew that it held, the way a seam holds while the heat is right, and that the household kept its fire inside the rule and would not step outside it to explain anything. A man who had spent his life making people hold still while he counted could respect a rule he could not read. It was the only kind of respect he had left in this house, and it was useless to him.

The lid came up and the kiln took its breath. Four, and four, and the breath it missed. The boy kept the count without deciding to, the way he kept the count of everything that had a rhythm and a gap in it. \person{Garl Vail}'s proverb went to the fire in a voice improved by twenty years of use: feed the fire before you feed yourself, for the fire would still be there in the morning and a man would not. Nobody said it to the boy. Nobody said it to a person at all. It went into the ash and stopped there, and the house straightened up around it the way a room straightens around a thing it has agreed not to mention.

The gruel came from the house's own dish. Barley thinned with milk and warmed at the fire, and the spoon wiped on her sleeve before it came near him. She lifted his head with two fingers behind the ear because he could not hold it up himself, and the warmth went in through his mouth and belly and stayed. She said \emph{the house} where another woman would have said \emph{I}. The house fed him. The house put the fold right. The house kept the fire and let the boy lie where the fire could reach him. It was not a lie; it was the grammar she had chosen for a thing that would become enterable the moment she said it in the first person. He had heard that grammar before in a white room where a chart said one thing and a room had done another, and had recognised it then as the sound of a man making himself clean by making himself absent. Here the woman was kinder and the grammar was the same.

Between one spoonful and the next his eye went where it wanted, which was not where his head wanted. The bed was under the roof. The loft was above the \place[the Vail wheel-shed]{wheel-shed}, and there was a ladder up to it that a debt climbed and a foundling did not. The hollow seat at the wheel kept a man's shape on its left side and nobody filled it. Three arrangements, and a fourth in the corner where the grey lay. He read them with the part of him that had once sorted twenty injured people in ninety seconds into those who would live, those who would live only if he chose them, and those who would die whatever he did. He had done it hundreds of times and had never once found a way to make it clean. Now he lay in a basket under a fold and could not lift a hand to his own chin, and the part of him that had done the sorting went on doing it with nothing to sort and no standing to act on what it saw. The household did not know that it was sorting. It knew only that the bed was under the roof and the loft was above the shed and the dish was the house's dish.

“That's the house's boy,” she said to the dish, not to him. “The house will keep him.”

The spoon went back to the fire. The little-door burn closed on nothing and the hand went to the coals again, and the feeding was entered nowhere --- not in the slate at \place{the Cross}, not in the private roll she kept in a careful hand, not in the district's figures, not even in the arithmetic of the household, where a loaf was a loaf and a bed was a bed and neither had a name written on it. She had paid for him twice and had taken a measure out of the district yesterday and had not said so, and she went on keeping him the way she kept the fire: by doing the thing every day and leaving no entry where an officer might look for one. The warmth was real. The warmth was not his to refuse. He had learned in seven days that a kindness which cannot be answered cannot be refused either, and that this was not an argument he could win even if he had the words.

\scenebreak

The pot waited on the bench in the shed. \person{Jorra Vail} had one hand under its belly and was bringing it down, slowly, the way a man sets down a thing he has just made and does not want to hear criticized. There was bone-coloured slip dried up both forearms to the creases of the wrist, and the little finger of his left hand bent where a splinter had taken it at eleven. He did not look toward the basket. He never looked toward the basket.

The boy turned his head before the pot touched the bench.

It was not a hearing. He had not heard anything. The pot had not given its sound yet, and his head went where it went without asking him, toward the bench and the soft shape of the thing above it, because in the seven days of his life a pot set down on a bench in this house had given a small flat knock and he had learned to turn toward it. That was all. It was the expectation of the sound, not the sound. He had no more choice in the turning than he had in the drool at his mouth, and no word in \lore{Grathic} for the thing he expected, and no hand to lift toward the bench two paces off even if the turning had meant something he could do.

Jorra set it down softly. He set it down the way one sets down a thing that must not be startled, and it gave nothing. Not the flat knock. Not the little wooden tick of clay meeting boards. Nothing at all. The interval where the note should have been stayed open and the boy kept it.

He did not add it to the count. Five hearings from four sources, and the fifth from a person, and that was where the count stood and where it would stand. A thing that did not happen could not be a hearing; he knew that as well as he knew that a vessel which gives no sound under the knuckle has not answered you at all. He kept the interval beside the other facts he kept and put no name to it. It would have been a decision to call it anything, and he had no standing to decide. He had learned that a thing could be kept without happening, which was a new fact and a useless one, like most of what he knew.

Outside, \person{Tarn} came up the yard with his broom.

He had swept before light and he would sweep again; a yard swept twice is a yard no man can say was left untidy. Grey \lore[the Ash]{Ash} sat in the creases of his knuckles where no scrubbing lifted it, and the pale ring showed at his left wrist where the iron clip had sat six years ago. The small bench knife was already back on the sill where it could be checked. He went through the motion of checking it with his eyes --- a glance, a small nodal inclination of the head, nothing that could be called looking at the boy --- and then lowered his head over the broom.

The bristles went over the trodden ash. Twice. On the second stroke, at the soft set-down of the pot in the shed, the broom stopped for half a stroke and did not go on. Tarn's head stayed down. He did not turn it toward the shed. He listened for something that did not come, and the yard gave him only the wind at the door and the sound of his own bristles going nowhere, and after the half stroke he swept on as if nothing had interrupted him.

He had seen the boy turn his head before the pot was set down. That was the thing. He had been coming up the yard with the dawn at his back and had seen the bundle under the fold move its head toward the bench before Jorra's hand let go, and he had known what the turning was for, because he had done the same thing himself for six years and had never once said what it was. A clay-bound hand who can hear a pot before anyone taps it is worth more than his debt, and a debt who says so is a debt the house can price. So Tarn swept and said nothing, and the boy under the fold lay with his mouth working slightly and said nothing either.

Two ears under one roof, each kept out of the other's reach, and no count of either anywhere in the house. Nobody named the sound. Nobody named the stopping of the broom. The boy had spent seven days being the only person in a room who noticed, and now someone else under the roof had noticed him noticing and had chosen silence. He could not enter that silence and could not hand it back. It was not safety. It was not yet a threat. It was an equality he had no word for, and he lay with it against him under the wool the way another child might lie against a warm stone.

\scenebreak

Jorra went back to the wheel and set his weight into the hollow on the left, which he had spent three years failing to fill. The wheel took under him. He drew a pot up out of the clay and spoke to it before he spoke to anyone, in the flat voice he kept for pots and tools and the damper.

“You'll do,” he said. “The shoulder's thick. I'll thin it.”

Then, with his eyes on the damper and not on Sella and not on the basket, “I'll shift that. It's sticking.”

Sella had not finished telling him to shift it. He shifted it before she had finished and shifted it again a moment later, and she let him, and the kiln took the change in its breath without either of them saying whether it was right. He was her son and he obeyed her so quickly that there was nowhere for an argument to go, and he never looked at her while he did it, and the house had learned to read the lowering of his voice the way it read the weather.

There was no slate dust on his forearms today. That was the fact the boy kept turning over, because yesterday there had been dust grey and fine worked into the creases past the bend of the little finger, and today there was only slip the colour of old bone. Slate dust had meant the back room, and the low talk, and the men whose names were not spoken, and a door kept closed. Its absence meant either that the back room had been cold in a different way or that there had been no back room at all, or that Jorra had washed. The boy could not ask which it meant. If he could have asked, Jorra would have answered the wheel. So the difference went into the file beside the other differences and stayed a difference, which in this house was a thing that could last for years.

The household went on being ordinary around the two of them. Sella counted spoons. Tarn swept. Jorra spoke to the clay and shifted the damper and did not take up the silence about the room he had come out of yesterday. \person{Garl Vail}'s coat kept its salt-stiff shape on its peg behind the door and his seat kept its hollow on the left and his proverb kept its place in the house's mouth, and the ridge along the skirting board turned the corner without a break. Nobody said what had been taken out of the district two doors down yesterday. Nobody said where it went. \place{Low Kiln} would say three days to \lore{the Five} when it said anything at all and would not say what it counted, and the morning went on being a morning in which a woman fed a child from her own dish and a man swept a yard and a boy at a wheel told a pot it would do.

The boy lay under the fold with the drool frozen at the corner of his mouth and the warmth of the gruel going out of him by degrees into the cold. He had the rule of the mark, the rule of the fold, the rule of the dish, and now a fourth rule he had not known existed until a man with a broom chose to keep it with him. Four rules and a gap, he thought, and then corrected himself, because the gap was not a rule and the counting of it was not the same as the keeping of it. He had been the only adult in a room full of people who could not know he was there. Now there were two of them, and the other one had a broom and six years of a seven-year term and the small knife back on the sill where it could be checked, and neither of them had said one word to the other.

His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the bench and came down.

The pot on the bench gave nothing. Tarn's head stayed lowered over the broom. The coals took under their ash, and the door stood at a finger's width, and the wind went in along the floor to the corner where the grey lay, and nobody in the house shut it.
