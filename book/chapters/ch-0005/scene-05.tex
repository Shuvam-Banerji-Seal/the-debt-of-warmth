\daymark{9}{Stone 15, Y.H. 812 (Fifthday)}

There was a skin of frost on the \place{the Clay Yard} on the morning of Stone 15, and it broke under the basket the way a crust breaks under a thumb, fine and without giving. \person{Sella Vail} carried him down with the wool over his ears and the little-door burn shut on the handle, and said what she said to the wind and not to him. “Keep it off his ears. It's the river wind.” She had said it every morning of his nine days and had never once said it to his face, and he had never once been able to tell her that the wind off the \place{the Ilm} was not the only cold in the district. Nobody under the roof was free to mind him, so the house carried him, which was the reason stated nowhere and acted on every morning; a reason spoken aloud becomes a thing that wants an answer, and she had arranged her whole life around not wanting one. The gulls were already over \place{the north bridge} and \place{the Fishstairs}, crying as they had cried the day before, and the smell of salt and wet rope came up the steps to meet them before the fish did.

She set the basket down at the edge of the fish-trade where the shade of the bridge fell across the boards and stopped, in a patch of ground no slate had ever reached. It was the same care she had used at the Drawing: a place where he could see and not be seen into. The boy understood the shape of it without having a word for the shape, which was \lore{Grathic} and which he had absorbed through the ear and had never been given the key to. He was nine days old and carried everywhere and entered nowhere. The shade smelled of river silt and cold rope, and the sun had not yet done anything about either.

It was another room of the same architecture he had been finding since the first day: a bench or a board, a draught that came under it from somewhere, a place where a thing was missing and nobody mentioned the missing. The kiln-house had a ridge along the skirting board; the death-house had the same ridge thicker; the Vail bench had a pot that gave nothing when it was set down; the Fishstairs had a landing where a step should have been. Here there was a girl with green at her mouth and a woman with a count in her thumb, and a patch of shade where a slate did not lie open. He set the room beside the others and did not join it, and the gulls went over and the shadow of the bridge moved half a hand across the boards.

\person{Dessa Silt} stood at the end of her nineteen steps with her shoulder against nothing.

She was twenty-four and had been worked older by a life spent in other people's cold: thin, with slip-grey nails and wet clay dried the colour of old bone at her hem, and a pale scar the width of a sieve-wire running up the inside of her left forearm. The wind came off the steps and went into her, and she coughed from \lore{the slip pits} in a low short way, as if the cough belonged to the weather and not to her; when it had passed she put her face back into the smile she kept for saying nothing further. The boy had seen that smile once before and had no name for it. It was a door shut quietly. The door had been shut for three years.

He read what he could read without guessing and without a diagnosis, which was not much and was all he had ever been allowed. The vessels under the skin were going thin; the cough came up from somewhere deeper than the chest and brought nothing with it; the thumb went to the pale scar whenever a truth came near enough to be almost said; the shoulder leaned on a wall that was not there. He set the observations down in the order they arrived, the way he had once set down a trauma note, with nothing added and nothing joined. The habit had been the whole of his trade. Here it was the whole of his nine days, and it bought him nothing he could spend.

Nobody in the market said anything else about the cough. A woman with a board of perch called something to a man with a slate and the man called something back, and the cough went into the general noise of the market and came out of it again, unentered. He had known rooms like this in the old life: a thing everybody could hear and nobody would name, because naming it would have made it somebody's responsibility. The surgeon in him knew what the cough was worth and the infant in him wanted the breast, and the two went on arguing in the cold without either of them getting a hearing.

“Nineteen,” she said to the drying rack, or to the cold, or to somebody who had asked her nothing. “Nineteen out and nineteen back. I counted them when the pits were water and I counted them after they froze. Four loaves on the seventh of Stone in eight hundred and twelve, three to \lore{the levy} and one eaten with my feet up, and a bit and a bit and a bit to the woman who comes with a basket.” She smoothed the scar with her thumb, the way another woman might smooth a cuff. “Weather,” she said, because the cough had come up again and wanted a name put on it before somebody put one there.

The date was a handle, and he knew a handle when he saw one: specific enough to hold, ordinary enough to be taken for nothing. She had put a year and a day and a number of loaves around the thing too large to say, and the thing stayed where it was, and the door stayed shut. The count itself had gone out of her feet into her thumb six years ago and had run there ever since without any legs to carry it. The boy lay at the edge of it and did not know he was inside it. He was put among the bits, and did not know he had been put there, and could not have corrected the entry if he had; he had been entered in two accounts already without being asked, and this was a third, and it was the quietest of the three because nobody thought it was an account at all.

He had counted the kiln's breaths and the floorboards and the bell and the death-house bench, four, four and a gap in each, and had put them side by side and joined none of them. Nineteen was a new number and did not fit the shape, and he did not try to make it. To make a number fit was a kind of deciding, and he had no standing to decide, and the thumb went on counting because counting kept a larger thing out of the room.

The cold came in at cheek and temple and wrist, in that order, and he knew the order as he knew the back of his own hand, and could no more argue with it than he could have argued with the fact of having no teeth. The thread of drool at the corner of his mouth had frozen on the walk down and nobody wiped it, because a basket at the edge of a fish board is a pot set down and left; it is not a person who needs wiping. He had once been the man who decided whether a patient was worth warming. He was now a thing the shade was kept off, and the keeping was exact and unentered and he could not refuse it.

Sella said something short to the board about eels, and Dessa answered about the weather, and the two women did not look at each other in the way women look at each other when there is nothing between them. Dessa's eyes went over the basket and past it and came back to the drying rack. She did not look into it. He had learned to read that kind of not-looking in nine days: the man on the block had priced him, this woman kept him, and neither had thought to ask whether he would consent to either. There was no word in the district for the difference and he had stopped expecting one.

Somewhere back toward \place{the Cross} the market bell went, and he counted it the way he counted everything: four, four, and a place where a stroke should have been. \lore{the bell} was a Court instrument doing a temple's work and nobody crossed himself at it, and the gap was the part he kept. The nineteen went on running in the thumb without legs, and the door stayed shut, and the morning went on being an ordinary morning in a district that had counted what it had and did not care to say it.

\scenebreak

Across the market a girl stood with green at the corner of her mouth.

She was a silhouette against the pots and the light, with the pale staining of lead glaze at the corner of her mouth where she had been tasting her work and had not wiped it away. The boy took in the green and the stillness of her and the fact that her attention was on a row of pots three stalls away and not on the basket at his side. She said nothing. She did not look across at him once. He did not know her name and would not know it for years, and the green meant nothing to him except that it was green and belonged to her face the way the slime belonged to the woman at the eel board; he filed it without knowing he filed it, and went on looking at the pots because she was looking at the pots.

\person{Mira Silt} was at the eel board with slime dried pale on the sleeves of her coat and the top of her left ear gone. She held her head a little to the right against the cold where the ear had been, and she flicked an \lore[eel-bones]{eel-bone} against her thumb while she decided whether to lie, and put it away when she had decided. The boy had watched her do it the day before and had learned nothing from the watching except that the bones moved when the answer was being chosen, which was a piece of information he could not use and could not forget.

A man asked her the price of eels. “You want a price or you want the pair?” she said. “The pair's two marks and two short, and I've counted them twice, and I'll count them again after I've eaten.” The man went away with a price he had not asked for and thanked her for it, and she went on with the knife as if no account had passed between them. He had heard the trick all his life in corridors outside operating rooms, where a nurse told a family everything and nothing by answering the question the family had not put into words. He watched her mouth instead of her hands and learned nothing she had not decided he should learn.

Somebody said a figure --- two marks, or two pair, or the days to the Five given out by a voice that had counted what it had --- and Mira's left hand went in under the apron to a cord at her waist, and her thumb found a knot, and the hand came away and went back to the knife. The boy saw the movement and not the knots. He had seen the hand go there three times in two mornings and had tried to make the counting and the cord agree, and had never managed it, because the counting belonged to the stall and the cord belonged to something the stall did not hold. A thing done often enough becomes invisible to the people who do it, and she had done this for years; he understood that much, having spent a life watching men do small things with their hands until the small things became the whole man.

The cord was grey wool the colour of a wet morning, and it lay at her waist where an apron string might have lain and could have passed for one. Under it, twisted twice around the belt so that it lay close and did not swing, was a second cord. That one had never gone up the river to \place{Nine Pots}. In all the years she had walked that road she had come back with it still dry and still in its place, and she had never once lifted it at a door. The boy could see the two of them where they lay, and could see that the thumb went always to the first and never to the second. He had no word for what the keeping was and no word for the difference between them, and the wool went on holding whatever it held while the eels went over the board.

Her not-looking was not the same as the other woman's. Dessa's was a door shut with somebody outside it; Mira's was a door held on the latch, which could be opened to let a thing out and shut again before anyone saw the movement. He could not have said what either kept, but he could hear the difference in the way the sentences were built: one woman said the number of loaves, the other asked a question and answered it herself so that nothing in the sentence wanted a reply. One kept by counting; one kept by leaving no seam for anybody to enter. She did not look into the basket either, and he put that not-looking beside the other and kept the two apart, as he kept all his objects apart.

He watched a woman keep accounts in a material no Court could confiscate. He had spent forty years among institutions that could take a man's name off a wall and put it in a book and send him to a room where nobody leaned in to ask whether the name was true, and he had learned early that the only record worth keeping was the one the institution did not know existed. Here it was: grey wool, at a fish board, in the hands of a woman who answered a question with another question so that nothing in the sentence would want an answer. He had no words in Grathic for the knowing. He had nine days of life and a hand that went up an inch and came down, and the knowing went in and stayed, and there was no one in the market he could have told it to and no reason he could have given if he had tried.

Attention was the only thing he had ever been able to keep, and it had never once, in forty years or in nine days, given him a hand to act with. He counted because counting was what he did when there was nothing to do: four, four and a gap in the bell, nineteen and nineteen in the thumb, four to the landing and a landing where a step should have been on the stairs. Every number was useless and none of them could be taxed. He kept them in a file no collector would think to look for and that he could not open himself, and the file grew, and the market went on with its ordinary arithmetic.

\scenebreak

Mira gave him the crust before anybody could make a speech about it.

It came across the edge of the board in two brisk movements, the same movements she used for moving a bone or shoving a loose board out of a barrow man's path, and it went into the fold at his side, not into his hand and not toward his mouth, because she knew as well as he did that there was no hand on the end of his arm that could close on anything. “It came from a warm house, that's all,” she said; and then, because the sentence was turning into a speech, she shut it before it could: “There's barley in it and a little smoke and it's a crust, not a sermon.” She did not look into the basket. She had not looked into it all morning and she went on not looking now, and went back to the eels.

He wanted the breast. He wanted the crust. He could have wanted both at once and did, and the two wants argued in him while the women went on with their arithmetic, and neither want got a hand to act with. The adult in him knew that the crust had been given by a woman who had paid for it with something she would not name; the infant in him knew only that it smelled of smoke and barley and lay at his side out of reach. Both were true and the second was louder, which was the whole humiliation of nine days.

It was brown at the edge where the smoke had taken it. The barley smell came through the wool in small waves as the basket shifted, and under the barley was the smoke of a house he knew, the Vail smoke that had been in the wool since the first morning and was entered in no account, not even the private one the house kept and did not show. The warmth lay against his side through the fold: a small flat warmth with no weight in it that he could neither lift nor push away. He smelled it. He could not eat it. His mouth worked under the wool and made the small wet sound, and the thread of drool at the corner froze where Sella had left it, and the crust lay where the woman had put it, whole and warm and entered nowhere.

The barley had come from a house with a fire in it, and the fire had been fed by somebody who got up before light to feed it, and the smoke had gone into the crust in that house before the crust came across a market board in a woman's hand. There was a payer for every warmth in this district and a recipient who could not refuse it, and the payer and the recipient lay at the edge of the same shade without either of them saying whose fire it had been.

He had once reduced a shoulder in the dark and packed a cavity with both hands and known, in the ninety seconds before anybody else knew, which of twenty people would live and which would die whatever he did. He could not lift a crust an inch into his own mouth. The hand outside the wool went up toward it and stopped at the height of a button and came down again, and the warmth went on lying at his side. The fact was so plain and so funny and so awful that there was no room in nine days of life to be angry at it. He had been given a warmth by a woman he could not thank, in a market that would not have believed he understood the gift, and the gift was entered in nothing, not even in the file he kept for things nobody else would enter.

Sella had not seen the crust go in, or had seen it and made nothing of it, which was the same thing where she was concerned. She stood a half-pace off from the board and half a pace from nothing, with the little-door burn shut on nothing, and she said to the wool what she said when the draught came under the door at home: “Keep it off his ears. It's the river wind.” She did not thank the woman at the eels and did not refuse her, because a refusal would have been an answer, and a thank-you would have been an entry, and both would have turned the crust into a thing with a name on it. The house took what was given and kept the giving out of the account, and the boy lay in the fold with the warmth of another house against him and no word in Grathic for either house.

He had been carried to the edge of the market and set down to watch a woman count, and found himself counted by her without her knowing it, and the two things were the same thing seen from different sides. She had put him among the bits because a woman with no house counts what she can count and leaves the rest in the dark; he had watched her count because counting was the only thing in the district he had ever been given leave to do. Neither of them could have said what the count was for. The surgeon in him wanted to name the thing and the infant in him wanted the breast, and between them the morning went on, and the crust went on being warm.

He set the new objects beside the old ones in the file he could not open: the grey cord and the second cord kept apart, the eel-bones moving hand to hand, nineteen in a thumb, the brown edge of a crust, the green at a girl's mouth, the bell's gap back toward the Cross. Beside them lay the two \lore{the Ash} ridges, the charred slate in the kiln ash, the barrow of grey going out toward the north gate, the death-house bench's four and four and a knot, the interval where a note had not come. He joined none of them. Joining would have been a deciding, and he had no standing to decide, and the file went on growing without anybody's permission and without a single entry another person could read.

Dessa had not watched the giving. She stood at the end of her nineteen steps with the door shut on her face and counted the nineteenth into her thumb again, the way a woman counts a thing already counted because the counting is the point and the thing counted is not. The cough came up once more and she called it weather and went on with the count. She did not look into the basket. She had not said the number in three years and had not said the name in three years, and the number lay in her thumb and the name lay under the pale scar, and neither went on any slate in \place{Sill}.

The boy lay at the edge of the shade with the crust warm in the fold at his side. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward it and came down. Across the market Mira's thumb came away from the grey cord and went back to the knife; the girl with the green at her mouth went on looking at the pots; and Dessa's smile shut the door on nothing at all. The bell went four, four and a gap somewhere back toward the Cross. Nobody had said the number and nobody had said the name and nobody had looked into the basket. The morning went on being an ordinary morning, and the warmth lay in the fold, entered nowhere.
