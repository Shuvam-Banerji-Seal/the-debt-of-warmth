By the time the market had opened in earnest the rumours had acquired prices. They had been in the air since before light, going stall to stall ahead of the fish, and now they were cried and weighed and knocked down like perch, each with a figure fastened to it that a man could repeat and a woman could refuse. Which boats on the \place{the Ilm} carried something other than fish; which collectors took their ale before a collection and which after; which households in \place{Low Kiln} were warm and which were cooling. The boy lay at the edge of the steps with a thread of drool freezing at the corner of his mouth and heard the figures go past him in a language he had absorbed through the ear and had never been given the key to. He could count what he heard. He could not spend any of it. It was an account read aloud in a tongue he did not have, and he was carried through it at the level of a hip, his mouth working under the wool, and there was nobody in the whole market who would have thought to hand him a slate.

He had sat at the head of tables where figures were read out by men who had already decided what the figures meant, and had watched other men lean in as though leaning could alter the number. The leaning was the same here. A woman with a board of perch leaned toward a man with a slate and said a figure; the man said another; the woman laughed and slapped the board with her hand, and the two went on to the next customer, and the figure lay where they had left it, unclaimed and unchanged. The boy, who had once been paid to know the difference between a variable and a constant, had eight days of life and no words in \lore{Grathic} with which to ask which of the two was the patient.

The man with the slate at the perch board said something about a collector who took his ale before and a collector who took it after, and the woman named one and then the other, and neither lowered his voice, because there was nothing in the sentence an officer would have called a secret. The boy heard the order of it and set it down the way he had once set down the order of a script: who went first, who followed, where the break came, and what the break was for. A man who drinks before is steadying a hand. A man who drinks after is washing a taste out of his mouth. Both arrive at the bed with a clean slate and a reason, and the reason is the part that gets entered, not the ale. He had known a dozen men who could tell you exactly what they had done and exactly why it had been necessary, and had learned to listen for the one small thing they left out, which was never the necessary part.

\person{Mira Silt} knew which of these rumours were true because she had made some of them herself, and the boy knew that she knew from the order in which she repeated them. A boat going out with something other than fish in its hold was a boat whose owner had paid \lore{the levy} in something other than coin, or so the stall said, and the stall said it in the same breath as it said the price of eels, so that a listener could take whichever part he could afford. He had learned years ago that a person who prices several things in one sentence is usually hiding which of them he is selling. He watched her mouth instead of her hands and learned nothing she had not decided he should learn.

She sold eels. She sold them the way she did everything, quick and without waste, the knife going down the length of a fish and coming up clean, the bones into the bowl at her right and out again when she wanted to think. A woman at the perch tub bought six and was given five and did not count them, which was a thing he had learned to see in the first year he worked and had never learned to unsee. Mira named a price and then named it lower for the same woman, and the woman thanked her for nothing in particular, and both went on with the morning as if no account had passed between them. She answered a question about the weather with a question about the ice, and answered that one herself, and the man who had asked went away satisfied that he had been told something, which was a trick he had known nurses to perform on families in a corridor outside an operating room.

She said a figure under her breath, low enough that it belonged to the eels and not to the stall, and one of the \lore{eel-bones} went from her left hand to her right. Another figure; another bone moved. The boy watched the counting and then watched the hand go in under the apron to the cord at her waist, and tried to make the two agree, and could not. The counting belonged to the stall. The cord belonged to something the stall did not hold, and the thumb came away and the hand went back to the knife, and there was no seam between the two movements that a man with a slate could have entered.

On the walk in he had seen the hand go to the cord once, when a number was given out from the top of the steps. He had seen it again now, when the number was said under the breath. Twice in one morning, and no more visible the second time than the first. A thing done often enough becomes invisible to the people who do it, and she had done this for years; he understood that much, having spent a life watching men do small things with their hands until the small things became the whole man. He did not know what the cord held. He did not know that there were knots on it to count. He knew only that a figure had been spoken and a hand had gone to a place on her person where no collector's thumb would think to look, and that she had gone on talking about eels the whole time.

Attention was the whole of what he had. It was the one possession he had ever been able to keep, and it had never once, in forty years or in eight days, given him a hand to act with. He could read the shape of a woman's not-looking and the order of a man's reaching and the gap in a flight of steps. He could not lift a bone out of a bowl two paces from his basket. His head went where it wanted toward the light on the water and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and nobody at the stall was looking, so nobody put it back. The cold came in at ear and temple and wrist. He knew its order. He had known the order of a great many things and had never been given a use for any of them.

The collector crossed the \place{the north bridge} a little before noon. The boy did not see his face, only the cuff, turned back twice at the wrist and softened by washing, coming along the shadow of the bridge with a flat satchel held against a dry coat. The hand went to the satchel's clasp before the eyes went to the stalls, or so it seemed from a basket at the foot of the steps; the same order he had watched in a room two doors down the yard, where a man had settled his slate before he looked at the person lying on the board. It was an ordinary hand. It had done this ten thousand times and had never once been thanked for it, and the boy had no way to address it, no standing to speak, and no wish that was not a wound.

The light in the cafeteria is wrong and it is four in the morning and it hums. He has eaten the crumpled paper wrapper to its last crumb because waste offends him, and his hand is going into a pocket where a phone lies lit with a name he will not look at.

The river took it back. The gulls went over the ice, crying, and the boy lay under the fold at \place{the Fishstairs} with his cheek against cold wool and the cuff gone into the crowd at the top of the steps. He had been at home for two sentences in a room he could not have named and would never see again, and the homesickness of it was not for the room. It was for the appetite: the small clean satisfaction of having finished a thing that was put in front of him, crumb by crumb, because leaving it would have been an insult to something he could not explain. He had taken gruel from a dish that morning and bread the day before and would take eels tonight by somebody else's fire, and the hunger of the infant body and the thrift of the grown man turned out to be the same appetite wearing two faces. He had always known that about himself and had never known where to put it.

Nobody on the steps turned to look at the man with the cuff. He was an ordinary man going about an ordinary morning, and when his work was done he would go home to a room with a fire in it and sit down, while the boy who had watched the order of his hand lay in a basket at the foot of the stairs with a thread of ice at his mouth. There was no cruelty in it. That was the part neither life had ever got used to: that men who did this work went home to be warm, and were kind to their children, and would have been offended by the suggestion that they had taken anything.

He counted the steps down to the water going out and he counted them again coming back. Four to the first landing, four to the second, and then a landing where a step should have been with no step in it, only wet stone and the shadow of the bridge thrown across the place a foot would have gone. Four, four and a gap. He put the gap beside the kiln's missing fifth breath and the bell's missing stroke under the Vail roof, and beside the fifth mark on the floorboard that was a knot and not a nail, and made nothing of the setting. To make anything of it would have been a deciding, and he had no standing to decide, and the count went on happening without anybody asking him to keep it.

In \lore{the Breaking} the river would come up over these steps and take them, and put them back when the water went down with the landing a hand's breadth out of line. The district read what the water did as weather and went on using the steps. The boy kept the place where the step was missing and let the river have the rest.

His hand went outside the wool to the place where a pocket should have been. There had been a coat once with a small cold rectangle of light in it, and he had reached for the place twice in the death-house two doors down the yard and once more in a room where the light hummed, and the name under the light had not been looked at then and could not be looked at now. The hand found wool. It closed on wool. He had promised a boat nine times to a woman who had organised her life around not waiting, and had never bought the boat, and the promise came back to him now the way the phone came back: as an absence in a pocket that did not exist, carried everywhere, heavier for being weightless. He had been a man who could reduce a shoulder in the dark. He could not lift an eel-bone out of a bowl, and the bowl sat at the woman's right hand within reach of her thumb, and she moved a bone without looking and went on with her counting.

\person{Sella Vail} bought the eels. She did not ask about the boats or the collectors or which households were cooling, and she did not haggle; she laid down her marks and took up the basket the girl at the stall pushed toward her, and the little-door burn shut on the handle and held. “Two pair,” she said to the board, and the girl put four eels in the bucket, and Sella counted them with her eyes without touching them, which was a way of buying that trusted the count and refused the intimacy of checking it. To the wool she said what she said to the pot when the draught came under the door: “Keep it off his ears. It's the river wind.” She did not say that a cuff had crossed the bridge, or that the woman at the stall had put her hand to a cord when a number was spoken, or where they had been that morning or where they were going. The boy had been carried through three markets under this roof's rule and knew the shape of it: a kindness with a wall built into it, and a silence nobody would enter. He had noticed the cuff. He had watched the cord. He would carry both out of the market and enter neither anywhere, and she would not ask him what he had seen, which was the other half of the same arrangement.

She lifted him against her hip, took the eels by the handle of a bucket, and went up the steps with the bucket swinging at her knee. River silt went up with them, and salt, and the cold off the water, and over these, as the \place{the Clay Yard} came into view between the roofs, the smell of woodsmoke from the Vail chimney, which had been on the wool since the first morning and was entered in no account, not even the private one the house kept and did not show. He lay with his cheek against the fold and counted the way up the way he had counted the way down: four, four and a place where a step should have been. The bucket knocked her knee in time. The gulls went over the ice behind them, crying. He had been given the whole morning. He had taken in the figures and the bones and the cuff and the cord and the landing that was not a step, and he could carry none of it out where anybody could read it, and that was the end of the morning, a bucket at a woman's knee and a smell of silt going up the yard.

At the \place{the Vail kiln-house} she put the fold right at his ear without once looking at his face, which was the whole of her attention and the whole of her refusal in one movement, and went in. The door did not quite shut behind them, as it had not shut on any night since he came under the roof, and the cold came in at a finger's width and went along the floor to the corner where \lore{the Ash} lay. The boy lay under the fold with the river on the wool and his hand outside it closing on nothing. Four, four and a gap. The coals held under their ash.
