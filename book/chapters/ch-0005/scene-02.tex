By mid-morning the wheel had taken the house into its rhythm and the day had a shape, the way a pot has a shape before it is fired. The wheel turned wet and steady in the \place[the Vail wheel-shed]{shed}, and \person{Jorra Vail} drew clay up out of the basket under his hands and set each pot on the rack with the same small tilt of the wrist, and the pots stood in a row with their bellies out, waiting for the fire. Slip had dried to the colour of old bone up both his forearms. He spoke to them as they came. “You'll do,” he said to the third. “The shoulder's thick. I'll thin it.” He did not look toward the basket where the boy lay. \person{Corin} had spent seven days learning the order of this house and understood perfectly that he was not being addressed, and understood nothing about why that should be a relief.

To the wheel he said a thing he would not have said to a person in the room. “It doesn't care whose hands,” he said, settling his weight into the hollow on the left. “It cares if they're steady.” The wheel took under him and the pot came up wet and round, and the boy, who in the life before this one had worked under men who said the same sort of thing about instruments --- that a tool has no interest in who holds it, only in whether the hand is steady --- took the sentence in without letting anyone see he had taken it. Jorra never said it to Sella. He never said it to Tarn. He said it to the clay, which could not report him.

The kiln breathed behind the wall. Four, and four, and the breath it missed. He kept the count because there was nothing to stop him keeping it, and because the part of him that had once counted a pulse at the head of a bed while a machine wrote the number on a screen went on counting now with nothing but a kiln to count. He had the floorboards as well: four on the near board, four on the next, and a knot the width of a thumb standing in for the fifth nail. The knot had not moved since first light. That was the kind of fact a man could build a day on if he had no other work.

\person{Sella Vail} moved between the fire and the rack with her hands never still. When they had nothing to hold they counted: loaves on the shelf, bits in the jar by the door, the days. She did not say the days aloud. A man had come up from the iced river before light with water on his legs and a look in his face like a door standing open in a cold house, and somebody in the yard had asked how long it was to \lore{Kindling}, and he had said, “Not so far as a man would want,” and gone into his own house. That was the whole of the answer the yard had. The number went around in the shape of a fist closed on something and came through the door as an absence, and the boy took the absence in with the rest of the morning. He could feel the shape of it. He could not have said what was inside, and it did not occur to him to be angry about that. A withheld number was a thing he had met before, in a room where a chart said one thing and the room had done another.

\person{Tarn} came in from the \place{Clay Yard} with \lore[the Ash]{Ash} in the creases of his knuckles and the pale ring at his left wrist where the iron clip had sat six years ago. He had six bits to a term and one year left of seven, and he had counted every day of the six and would not count the seventh, and the not-counting was the one entry in this house that the house could not check. “Yes, house,” he said to nobody. “I'll sweep it. I swept it this morning. I'll sweep it again.” He agreed with everything so completely that an argument had nowhere to put its foot.

The bits were not in a jar and were not on a slate. They were in his head, laid out in the order he had earned them, and the seventh was not there because he would not let it be there. The boy knew a man who had kept a set of instruments the same way --- every clamp in its place, one empty place in the tray where the thing he was afraid of using would have gone --- and he had never once asked the man why the place was empty. He did not ask Tarn either. It was not a question a boy in a basket could ask, and it was not one a clay-bound hand could answer without being priced for it.

Sella passed him with the coal-shovel --- it was the back of a broken pot, blackened along one edge --- and said, “You've done well, Tarn.” It was not a large sentence and she said it to the floor the way she said most things. Tarn stopped altogether. The broom stood still with its bristles on the trodden ash, and the hand on it went still, and for two breaths the whole room held the position it had been in when the sentence arrived. Then he said “Yes, house” and began again.

The boy had seen that stop before, in a white room with a senior at the head of the table. A resident, asked a question he could not answer, had stopped just so --- not the hesitation of a man thinking, but the stopping of the whole animal, the eyes going to a place on the wall where there was nothing to see --- and he had known without being told that the man was agreeing with something he had no standing to disagree with. Here the thing agreed to was that a clay-bound hand had done well, which was a kindness and therefore not enterable. He read the stop exactly. It bought him nothing. He had a great talent for reading stops and no way to put a hand on one.

Jorra went quieter when \lore{the levy} came into the room, which was not often and never by name. A woman at the drying rack said, “There's the house's share to think of,” and left the sentence lying where the loaves lay, and Sella answered her with the price of slip, which was not an answer and did not need to be one. She said, “That'll go the house's way,” to a pot, and the yard straightened up around the sentence the way a shelf straightens when a heavy jar is set upon it. Jorra's hands went on turning the wheel, but his voice dropped out lower than hers had gone. He did not say the thing he had said three days ago and had never taken back. He did not say it now. The boy had been under this roof five days and the sentence was as sealed as it had been on the hour it was spoken, and the not-saying of it had become a part of the house like the hollow in the seat.

There was no slate dust on the forearms today. There was only slip, bone-pale, dried in the creases past the bend of the little finger. \place{the back room} was either cold in a different way or had not been opened, and the boy kept the difference the way he kept all his differences, side by side and unjoined. If he had been able to ask, Jorra would have answered the wheel.

Under the fold he made his inventory. It was not a record and it was not a decision; it was what his hands used to do at the end of a shift, laying what they had found in a row on a table so that the shape of it could be seen before anybody wrote it down. He had the two ridges of Ash: one under the Vail skirting board, near two thumbs wide, turning the corner without a break; one in the death-house two doors down, thicker by a finger's width, turning its corner without a break as well. He had the charred edge of a slate in the kiln ash, black on one side and eaten into the shape of a strip of bark, with the writing gone off the end. He had the barrow of grey that had gone out through \place{the north gate} toward \place{the Bone Orchard}, heaped a little above the sides. He had the bench from the death-house with four nail-heads on the near edge and four on the far and a knot the width of a thumb standing in for what was missing. And he had the interval where the pot on the bench had not given its \lore{the flat note} --- an empty place with the shape of a sound in it, which he kept as carefully as if something had actually happened. He had known \lore{the full note} from its flat shadow for seven days and had never once been thanked for knowing it. He set them all side by side and joined none of them. He had no word in \lore{Grathic} for any of it and no standing to make one.

The same arrangement kept returning and he could not have said why. A bench by a door. A draught under it. A pot on the bench. Four, four, and a space where the fifth should be. It was the architecture of a room in which something had been done and nobody had entered it, and his eye, which had found the same architecture in a hundred rooms of the old life and made a living out of finding it, went on finding it here and made nothing of the finding. To join the rooms would have been to decide what had happened in them, and he had no standing to decide. He had learned that in his five days under this roof and had spent every day since proving that he had learned it.

The body went on with its humiliations without asking his opinion. The thread of drool at the corner of his mouth had frozen during the morning and stayed frozen, and no hand had happened to pass that way again. His head went where it wanted, which was toward the light at the door, and when it went too far a small wet sound came out of him that was not a word, and would have embarrassed him once, when he had been a man who could be embarrassed by such things. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the bench two paces off and came down. Two paces. He had once reduced a shoulder in a room the size of this one, with three people holding the light and nobody saying what to do. He lay now with his own chin beyond the reach of his own hand. There was a joke in it and it cost him something and he did not laugh.

Dusk came into the \place{Vail kiln-house} the way it came into it every day of the world --- by degrees, through the door that would not shut, along the floor to the corner where the grey lay. Sella banked the coals with the back of a broken pot and pushed ash over them with the edge of her hand, and the little-door burn closed first on the broken edge and then on nothing. “Lie you there,” she said to the coals. She did not touch \lore{the widow's mark} at her throat; that was for the hour before an opening, and there was no opening now. The days to Kindling were still a closed fist somewhere in the yard. She had gone around them all afternoon without once putting a figure to them, passing the question from the fire to the rack and from the rack to the pot, and the boy had followed the passing the way he had once followed a case conference in which everyone agreed to call a thing by a less exact name so that nobody would have to decide what it was. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without once looking at his face, and the hand did not stay to wipe the drool, and none of it went into the arithmetic of the house, where a loaf was a loaf and a bed was a bed and neither had a name written on it.

\person{Garl Vail}'s coat kept its salt-stiff shape on its peg behind the door and his seat in the shed kept its hollow on the left, and his proverb went to a pot in the voice twenty years had made better than the man had ever said it: “Leave the crack showing. People trust a thing they can see has been handled.” Nobody said it to the boy. Nobody said it to a person at all. The debt against the tiles, which had never been called, lay where \person{Berr Clay} had left it, spoken around all day and never named, and the house went on being short of coin and warm.

Five days under this roof had taught him something he would have preferred not to learn. The whole house was an arrangement of things nobody would enter: a bed under the roof, \place{the Debt Loft} above the shed with its ladder, a seventh year a man would not count, a number a man had gone around, a ridge nobody would sweep, a slate with its writing gone off the end, a debt that could not be paid because it could not be settled and could not be done with. Everything was kept exactly. Nothing was entered. He had kept his file through five days without help and without a single thing in it that anybody could have taken from him, which was the whole of his wealth and not enough to buy a crust. His competence was useless here; he had the eye of a man who could read a room in ninety seconds and the hands of a creature that could not lift a crust, and the only possession anybody had failed to tax was the attention he could not put down. It was not despair. It was a colder attention than he had brought under this roof on the third day, and it went on taking the measure of the room after the light had gone.

Tarn swept the yard once more at dusk and put the small knife back on the sill where it could be checked, announcing it to nobody in the tone of a man stating the weather. Jorra came in with slip on his forearms and spoke to a pot and shifted the damper, and did not take up the silence about the room he had come out of yesterday. Nobody said what had been taken out of a house two doors down on Stone 12. Nobody said where it had gone. Supper was eaten over the fire; the pot was set on the bench and gave nothing; the yard went quiet by degrees. The household went on keeping its accounts in the only way it knew, which was by doing tomorrow what it had done today.

The boy lay on his bed under the roof with the fold at his chin. The coals held under their ash. The grey ridge ran to the corner and turned it without a break. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the bench and came down. Four, four and a gap. \place{Low Kiln} said three days to \lore{the Five} without saying what it counted, and the wind went in at the door's finger's width, and nobody shut it.
