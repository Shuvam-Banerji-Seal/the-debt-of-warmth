\daymark{244}{Smoke 5, Fifthday, Y.H. 813}

The hand lay against the fold where it had come down, and \person[Corin]{the boy} did not spend the small hours examining the place it had made. A man lays a tool on a bench at the end of work and leaves it where the next hand will reach for it; he does not turn it over to admire the work. He was two hundred and forty-four days old, six months and sixty-four days, and neither number was spoken in \place{Sarn}, \place{Sill} or \place{Grath}, over his head or in his hearing. The count had gone forward out of the dark without a voice and without a hand to enter it, exact, complete, unspent. He had long since stopped expecting to be told, which is not the same as not wanting it; the wanting went on under the not-expecting the way a coal goes on under ash. Smoke had come on without a boundary and went on without one, neither of the district's two names for the months set right or left wrong in the telling. No bell rang on a Fifthday, no market stood at \place[the Cross]{Cross}, and Smoke 10 --- the first Account-day and market of the second firing --- stood five mornings ahead and had not been brought into a day that had not earned it.

Under \place{the Vail kiln-house} roof in \place{Low Kiln} the smells came in their old order: hot clay under tallow, wet wool under that, and under all of it the sour \lore{Ash} breath of the unswept corner the house called \lore{the thaw}. The day's first sound was the kiln drawing in four low pulls and stopping where the fifth should have stood, not a bell and not a voice. Under the floor \lore{the carried fire} answered four low pulls with that same silence, its payer without a name for two hundred and forty-four days. Through the finger's-width door the river kept its note of \lore{water over water}, a fifth low thing never counted among the hearings. Overhead \place{the boards} kept four nails on the near board, four on the next, and a thumb-width knot where the fifth nail should have stood. Three counts in three places: fire under the floor, river through the door, nails overhead. A man who wanted them to agree would have to put a payer under every one, and no payer stood to any of them, so the boy left them on three trays and the trays lay where they lay. Instruments that will not read against one another are still instruments; an instrument is a thing a hand uses, not a thing a man believes.

One stiff leathery brown-edged barley crust lay two inches from the ear. The empty place beside it kept its name to itself and no third came to lie down and make the row come out even, because a kindness with a figure on it is the beginning of an account and this house had learned to give without entering. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the dry heat and was unwiped, and would stay unwiped, because nobody under any roof had ever been given an instruction about the thread and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. Cold had stopped at cheek, temple and the bare joint of the wrist where the wool rode back, as though it had come to the end of a line written out for it and had neither an order to go further nor an order to go away. The afterdrop waited beyond all three, past a warming nobody in this house would perform. He knew the afterdrop when he saw it: knew the face severe cold wears is a quiet face, knew a cold patient cannot clot, knew a man must be warmed slowly and from the outside. He knew all of it and wanted the crust. Both were true, and the second was louder, because a child's hunger keeps no hours at all while the register goes on being exact inside a body that goes on being hungry. The surgeon took the inventory and entered it nowhere.

The fire's keeper had gone to her own bed before the dark settled and left the coals to keep themselves; there was no breathing number anywhere in the house and no fire-sitter standing over them. The boy lay on \place{the bed} at the low angle of \place{the loaf shelf} in the room that does not touch the room of the fire, and borrowed no rhythm from the other side of the wall. \lore{the widow's mark} hung where it hung on its cord at the keeper's throat, a thumb-length of clay under \lore{lead glaze} with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and it was not touched in the small hours, because the hand goes to the mark before the kiln and at no other hour. The one opening before the kiln on Smoke 1 lay behind the household and was not looked at again, not as a sign, not as a lesson, not as a mending performed and not as a payment; no slate had been entered and no payer named. A shelf, a well, a rack and a basket were not openings. He did not need to be told this and had never been told it; the house kept the hour the way a vessel keeps a seam, without discussion.

\scenebreak

Sella Vail went to the fire before she went to the basket. She told it the thaw, turned the coals with the back of a broken pot, fed them and said \emph{The house will keep it} where another woman would have said \emph{I}. Then she stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand and counted under her breath toward no opening --- small intake, hold, letting go --- a count she kept toward nothing and had never offered to any ear under this roof. She pushed the coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the burn so the grey streak showed pale off the coals, and the burn across the back of her hand, two straight sides and a lintel and a threshold, closed on nothing and opened again to the work. She did not look toward the bed. The widow's mark hung where it hung at a hearth and a morning, because neither was an opening.

The dressed grain came out from under its wool for the damp. She spread it thin on the flat stone near \place{the hearth}, turned it with the back of her hand, gathered it back by the handful and set the fold flat again. It changed state and gained no figure. She counted it in glances, in handfuls and by the weight of the carrying arm, which knows weight and not cost; there was no slate near the stone and nothing was entered on any of the three days of this firing. It was not laid across the crust in the fold, the two gaps on the shelf, the round ring where the shallow clay pan had stood, the empty place in the returning line or the place where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out. The figure read over the dressed grain on Grain 30 was still not the figure paid; the true subtraction stayed locked in \person{Berr Clay}'s box, going up and down \place{the clay road} and answering nothing for the man carried out on Day 188. A figure read is a figure read and a figure paid is a figure paid, and no signature stood between them for a boy who could not have read the signature if it had been put in front of him.

At \place{the loaf shelf} she stood with her back to the basket and moved her lips along the row, the way a woman reads a row of loaves without taking a figure off it. The row stood with two gaps in it and she gave nobody a figure; her lips went over the gaps and did not stop at them, and no number came out of her mouth. No third gap came either. She glanced once at the clay jar on the wall shelf, cold under its glaze, and shut her hand half over nothing, and opened it again to the work. Her finger stayed on top of the shelf's front edge, not beneath it, so the charcoal tally that lay under the board stayed unread and uncounted; this was not an Account-day and no slate came near it. She set the pot rim out and turned its handle two inches beyond the reaching hand, letting her hand rest a half-beat longer at the setting than the setting required, and did not look toward the bed. The widow's mark hung where it hung at a shelf, because a shelf is not an opening.

The yard went around its walked-around places as she carried him out. It went around the clean dark line where the \lore{Ash} ridge turned and the finer grey called the thaw beside it, around the space beneath \place[the drying racks]{the lowest rack} where the broken birth-slates lay, around the round empty ring, around the bare dark \place{the trough} beside \place{the yard well} with its water called weather, around the field's empty place out on the worked strips, and around the returning-line place with a look and a wide step and no number. Seven in a row were still seven; they were not an account until a payer stood under them, and none stood under any of them. The boy was carried at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, and no instruction went to the basket, because the basket had not been given an instruction and had no need of one.

A neighbour at the threshold hitched a pot against her hip and asked what the storing would take now the grain was under wool. Sella sent the question to the pot and the weather: \emph{Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there. The house has said it}. Nothing was priced, refused or entered; the woman carried the question away level, the way one carries a full pot, with nothing slopped and nothing given. She did not look into the basket, and the boy did not take the pot she had set on the sill for an answer to anything. A man come down the clay road with the dried pale scurf of mud on his coat skirt brought a number up out of his fist toward the well, looked at it, and put it back into his sleeve. The boy read the closed fist as he had once read a chart turned face-down on a table: the shape of the paper is not the writing under it, and a number put into a sleeve had not been put there for him. Both readings were exact and neither bought him the crust. The figure arrived nowhere. It was not a missing figure, because a figure kept in a sleeve is a figure kept in a sleeve and a sleeve is not a slate; a thing not priced is not therefore free, it is a thing whose payer has not been named. No collector came up the clay road that morning and none went down it. The neighbour and the man went off about their work with their questions still on them, and the yard gave neither of them a figure.

\scenebreak

At the well the water went over water under the lip and the trough stood bare and dark beside it, holding nothing and called weather by those who did not taste it. The boy was set down at the edge of the work where no slate lay, with the fold at his ear and the crust two inches off, and the wool riding back at the bare joint of his wrist. \person{Ilva} stood at the well with a pail against her knee, a woman of the firing work with clay dried pale in the crescents of her nails and the grey dust of the racks worked into the seam of one thumb; she was of the yard and not of the house, and nobody in the yard turned to look at her twice. A woman with a pot under her arm asked her how many firings the month had taken.

The order of her reaching was this: her hand went into her sleeve and brought out a short strip of birch bark notched along one edge; the nail of her thumb passed across the notches, pale where the wood had been rubbed and dark in the uncut grain between; only then did her eyes go to the woman who had asked; only after that did her mouth open to answer. The hand went to the record before it went to the person, and to the pot not at all. The boy did not read the notches. He had no more right to them than to lift a lid or turn a slate, and wanting the crust had made him honest about the distance between an inch and two inches. What he took was the order, and the order he had seen before.

Eight record-first hands stood already on their places: a midwife laying slates before she turned to a fold, a thane passing a finger over a figure before he looked at the crowd, a keeper reaching a finger beneath a board before her eyes went to the row, a runner settling a strap and turning a wrist before her eyes took the yard, a clerk entering a buyer's number before he looked up to find the man who had said it --- eight shapes kept apart, a finger between every pair, because a row of likenesses will call itself an account if a man leaves it standing long enough to want an answer. Now he put the ninth on a place of its own beside the eighth, a finger between them and fingers between them and the charcoal under the shelf, the reed-cutter's cord, the knots in a woman's apron, the pages turned down toward a floorboard, the tins with their pale folds, the missing hand and the pale line where something had been worn too long. Nine record-first hands were nine. They must not stand up and call themselves a rule, for a rule is an account that has found a payer and no payer stood to any of them. The recognition was the danger: he had known the shape before he had a word for it, and knowing a shape is the first step toward wanting it to mean something. He held this one apart.

The notches held a count of firings, plain as a firing is plain. They were kept in wood because a slate would want a figure put on them and the yard had no wish to give the slate one. Nothing on the strip waited to be read; no figure came off it that a clerk could enter and carry up the road, and she gave no name to the thing and nobody asked her for one. She was not the grey-coated collector, not the slate-runner with the edge-on slate, not the soft-cuffed clerk set below the bridge for a measure nobody called and not an officer with a reason; she was a woman who kept a count in wood and drew water in a yard, and she was joined to none of them. Her thumb-nail came away from the notches. \emph{Five to this morning}, she said, and put the strip back inside her sleeve with the flat of her hand, and went on with her water. The woman with the pot went off with her answer level, and nothing was sold, entered or explained.

He put the question to his hand the third way of the chapter, in the ordinary morning with the well going on behind him. The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the low note through the finger's-width door. There was no count in the house to hold it against --- no breathing number, no fire-sitter, no instruction given to the basket --- so he kept it to the river's own low interval, the small intake and the little silence after it, and brought it down on the next note. It was down before the note had finished and after the interval had stood where it stood. It was not a longer reach, not a miss entered as a finding, not a hearing of \lore{the full note}, not a seventh, not a tenth stopping, not a payment, not one of the held changes, not a key, not a power and not a rule. Sixty-three answers stood on the sixth place now, exact, complete and worth nothing at two inches of air. Once he had taken a bleeding vessel out of a man's abdomen with two fingers and a clamp and now he could not close two inches on a pot handle; exactness and humiliation were one motion in the small hand and neither stood up to say what the other was. He filed the answer with no payer named and went back to the crust.

The thumb-nail had come away from the birch bark and the woman's mouth had answered; the strip was back in the sleeve where nobody would take a figure off it, and the ninth hand-shape lay on a tray of its own with a finger between it and the eighth. The hand came down on the river's next low note and the file lay open on the towel, exact, complete and worth nothing. Two hundred and forty-four stood exact, complete, unspent and spoken by nobody in \place{Sarn}, \place{Sill} and \place{Grath}; six months and sixty-four days were told by nobody; the fire's fifth was unpaid the same number of days, its payer without a name; the widow's mark had not been touched at a shelf, a well, a rack or a basket, because the hand goes to the mark before the kiln and at no other hour; the seventh year stood at its very end and had still not given. Smoke went on without a boundary drawn and Smoke 10 stood five mornings ahead, and the count went forward into a day that had not yet earned its heading.
