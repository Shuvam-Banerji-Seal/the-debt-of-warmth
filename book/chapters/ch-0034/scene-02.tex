\person{Sella Vail} turned in off the yard and came under \place{the Vail kiln-house} roof with the basket held level against her hip, the way a woman carries a pot filled to the brim and will not have it slopped. The light changed at the threshold. Out in the yard it had lain on the reeds in long grey ranks and on the skin of ice at the trough; under the roof it came down through the oiled paper in a soft brown sheet that reached the floor and went no farther, so that the far corner kept its own darker air to itself. Woodsmoke went before her and clung in the beams, and under the smoke lay the dry warm smell of \place[the loaf shelf]{the shelf} where the loaves had stood all morning, a smell of crust and ash and old baking that was almost a warmth in itself. Crossed through it, for one breath, came the sharper smell of charcoal from beneath the board --- clean, bitter, the smell of the inside of a banked kiln --- and then it folded back under the shelf as though it had never been let out.

She set the basket low near the bench leg. There was an angle to the place and she found it without measuring, as a woman finds the lip of a cup in the dark: a person standing saw the top of the board and the row of loaves upon it, but a person lying on the floor saw the underside of that same board become a roof over his face, with the iron bracket that held it standing cold at his cheek. \person[Corin]{The boy} lay at that angle. He had lain at it on other afternoons and knew the grain of the wood above him better than he knew the faces that stood over him. The cold iron of the bracket touched the edge of his ear and did not go away.

“Keep it off him,” Sella said to the wool, tucking the fold with her thumb. “It's come all the way down and it'll go all the way back up.” To the pot she said, “Don't set that on the floor. You'll pay for it in \lore{the thaw}.” To the broom, upright in its corner with the bristles up, “Round it. Go around.” And to the fire, with her back already turned toward the basket and her mind gone somewhere else, “It was the thaw. That's all it was.” She gave no instruction to the basket. A basket set down was a pot set down and left, and the thing in it was nearly four months old and could be trusted to keep its own heat, or not, without anybody naming which.

\place[the hearth]{The hearth} breathed behind her. It had given four low pulls through the morning and kept the silence where the fifth should have stood, and the boy lay with that silence coming up through the floor boards under him and the river's note of water over water coming through the finger's-width door to join it from another direction. Two low things in two places. He had long ago stopped making one of them. A man who had learned to tell one sound from another in a room where the difference mattered knew the difference between two noises that happened together and one noise with two halves; these were two. The river's note came through a door that kept a finger's width of open, and the kiln's gap came up through the floor under blind ash, and neither had been counted under this roof for a hundred and forty-one days, and neither wanted to be counted. He kept them on two places and went on breathing.

Above him the shelf kept its two spaces. One was the old space at the row's end, which the planting had brought in and which nobody called a number; the other was a palm's breadth of bare board that had come in on the night of Seed 6, between a dusk and a light, with no scrape left on the wood, no crumb, no print, no word. A loaf's worth had stood there and then had not stood there, and the row had gone on with one less in it as a row of loaves will. The shelf kept the two gaps in the room above, and the fold at his ear kept one stiff brown-edged crust and the empty place beside it in the room below, and the two rooms did not touch. He had held them apart on purpose for a hundred and forty-one days. A saying of the loaf-shelves of \place{Low Kiln} held that two gaps stay two gaps until a finger lays them together, and an account then wants a name, and no finger in the district had laid these together. He had never understood why the saying was needed at all, until he had understood it too well: a thing left where it is stays a thing left where it is, and the whole district could go on reaching past bare boards and never once put a hand to the space between them.

A neighbour came in out of the yard with a pot in one hand and the dry mud of the strips still worked into the creases of her wrists. She had come up from \place{the worked strips} east of the houses where the planting had gone in and the hands had gone in after it, and she carried the weather of that ground on her sleeves and a strip of dry reed caught in the crease of her thumb. It was the same mud she had had on her wrists when the seed went in, and the same question about the hands that had gone down the yard with it then had gone down the yard again with her now, unanswered. She went to the shelf for a loaf. Her hand went past the bare board toward the loaf beyond it --- past the palm's breadth first, because that was where her hand had learned to go, and past the old space at the row's end as well, without either place stopping her --- and her hand was at the bread before her eyes came down to the basket. In between, for the half-beat it takes to find what is not there and go on, she opened on nothing. The opening was so small that her own face did not change. She closed on the hard heel of the loaf beyond.

“That came in with the planting,” she said, meaning the space. She had the loaf in her hand and the pot balanced on her hip, and her glance went down at last and found the boy in the basket, the fold at his ear, the small wet sound coming and going as his head wandered. “And that one there---” She nodded at the basket, at the place where the wool rode back by his ear. “That's a child's appetite under an appetite. One hunger at the board and one at the ear.”

She said it in one breath, the way a woman says two ordinary things while her hands are full, and went off with the loaf and the pot level, nothing slopped. She never saw that she had laid the two bare boards under one cause, or that the hunger she had put at his ear was the same hunger she had just reached past on the shelf. She had not laid a finger across the two places. She had not named anything. She had made two rooms into one absence and was already gone with her pot.

The boy lay where she had left the sentence hanging in the air above him and took it in as a man takes in a second wrong reading of the same chart, without correcting either. He had kept the first telling on a place of its own since the planting, and the watcher's turn a half-beat late behind a pot's note on another, and the neighbour's one-breath saying now went on the same place beside them, a further shaping of the same held absence, advanced and not paid. He did not let it lie across the shelf's two gaps, or across the crust in the fold, or across the space in the returning line where a hand had gone in and had not come out. He did not make a third gap to match the two. He laid no finger of his own across anything, because he had no finger that could reach, and if he had had one he would not have used it. A likeness is not a cause, and an absence in a loaf row is an absence in a loaf row until a hand not his comes along and calls the two of them one account. He filed the saying where the others lay and left it there, exact, complete and worth nothing to anybody.

Sella stood at the row with the neighbour's question still in the air and moved her lips along the loaves without making a figure. She counted them the way the house counted --- in a breath here, a glance there, a space at the end nobody said aloud --- and her glance went once to \place{the clay jar} on the wall shelf, cold under \lore{lead glaze} even in the warm of the afternoon, and came away. She gave nobody a number.

“Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there,” she said to the doorway the woman had gone out of. “The house has said it.” The woman carried the unanswered question away with her as level as she had carried the pot, and the question went down the clay yard and arrived nowhere, as such questions had arrived nowhere since the reeds first came in. A cost asked in the form of a direction is answered with a direction, and the answer leaves no entry for anybody to read.

Sella's lips finished the row. She stood a moment with her hand at her side and then, still with her back to the basket, reached a finger beneath the front edge of the board where the charcoal tally lay. The little-door burn on the back of her hand closed on the wood's lip and opened again as the finger went under. She did not look at what her finger was doing. She held it a breath out and a breath back, the way a woman feels along the underside of a shelf for a thing she has put there and knows is there, and brought the hand away. She did not go back to the shelf. She did not wipe the finger or say what it had touched or why it had gone under. The dry smell of the loaves closed over the place and the sharper smell of charcoal went back into the grain.

From the basket's low angle the boy had seen the hand go under and come back. He had seen the burn close on the lip of the wood and open on nothing, and the finger come away with nothing showing on it that he could read. He saw the motion and he saw nothing of what the marks said. He did not count the strokes, though the charcoal lay in little clusters a finger's breadth apart with the ones at the row's end faded nearly into the grain. He did not press a thumb where her thumb had been. He did not lay the underside of the board across the two gaps above it, or across the one stiff crust two inches from his ear in the other room, or across the free place in the returning line the yard had gone around that morning. He made no cause of the habit. Such a hand, going under a board without looking, either knows the board by heart or has been told not to look, and the reason stood between the two, and he had no standing to step up to either one. He had seen the same order of reaching in a yard shoulder turning before a yard gaze and in a clerk's hand going to a slate before his eyes went to a stall, and he never made the two into one rule; this hand under this board would stay where it had been put.

The \lore{widow's mark} hung where it hung at her throat, a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and she did not touch it. A shelf was not an opening. An afternoon was not an opening. The mark was touched before the opening of a kiln and at no other hour, and there had been no opening here, only a row of loaves gone through with the lips and a finger under a board and come away without a word. She turned from the shelf and told the fire nothing further, and the fire gave its four low pulls and kept the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for a hundred and forty-one days.

The boy lay with the cold bracket at his ear and the brown light going off the boards. The shelf above him kept its two gaps and its charcoal marks beneath, and the hand had come away without a word, and in the other room the crust lay stiff in the fold with its empty place beside it still unnamed. The two rooms of the house did not touch. His small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water behind the wall and came down against the fold, and one hundred and forty-one went on with nobody's thumb upon it.
