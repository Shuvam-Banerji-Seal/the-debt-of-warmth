The last of the light came down the clay bank thin as slip poured back into its jar, and the yard stood with its bundles still in the ruts. They had been cut in the beds and carried up and set down and left, grey where the frost had taken the tips pale as old bone, green where the knife had bitten the broken ends, and nobody counted them where they stood. A row of cups on a shelf when the household has gone to bed stands so: each in its place, each full of what it holds, not one of them entered. The bundles stood with the same dumb exactness, full of a weight no slate had ever heard named. \person{Sella Vail} carried \person[Corin]{the boy} at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, woodsmoke going before her and the river wind coming up to meet it, and gave the instruction to the wool and to the wind and never to the basket --- keep it off his ears, it had come all the way down with them and would go all the way back up --- and then gave nothing to the wind at all, having said it once, because a wind takes an instruction and carries it away and there is no more to be done with it.

\place{Low Kiln} and its \place{the Clay Yard} went about their last work without declaring it last. The reed month had gone into its final week and would not say the word itself; there was only the shorter bundle in every arm coming down, and the green at the cut ends, and the frost taking the tips. Six days off stood the third Account-day, the thirtieth of Reed, and nobody in the yard counted toward it aloud, though every arm in the yard was keeping a kind of count in its own muscle and would have denied it if asked. A cutter came up out of \place{the reed beds} with a bundle slung across his shoulder and set it on the boards with his knee against it while he found the end of his cord. A girl with a creel stood waiting. He told her to lift her bundle and let her arm tell her what went into it.

“That's the whole of what it knows,” he said.

“I can ask my arm,” she said, “but it won't tell me what they'll give for it.”

“An arm doesn't know what they'll give. Ask it how heavy it is. It'll tell you that fast enough.”

He tied the cord low with his teeth and a pull of the thumb and went on stacking, and the girl lifted her bundle and let her arm tell her, and the arm told her exactly what it carried and nothing about what it cost. It was a record in muscle, complete and closed, and worth nothing anyone could pay; it was the same record the cutter's arm kept and the same record every arm in the yard kept, and not one of them could be entered on a slate. The boy lay with his cheek to the fold and read the lifting from the low angle: the hand to the bundle first, the eyes to the girl after, the cost nowhere at all. He had learned that order before he could hold up his own head and had never once been given standing to enter it anywhere.

A woman come up from the beds with dry mud worked into the creases of both wrists asked what the cutters' hands would be paid when the tend was out. A man at the racks, not looking up from his stacking, told her the weather had been kind to the cutting and the river had lain lower than it had any call to lie, and that was the whole of the account.

“That isn't a figure,” she said.

“It's what there is. You can ask the river for the rest. It's nearer than I am.”

She stood a moment with the question in her mouth and then shut two fingers out of a fist as she turned away, the way a woman closes a thing she has been carrying and will not set down where anyone can see it. Her hand went to the fist before her eyes went to the gate, and the boy set the reaching on a place of its own beside the others and did not make a row of them and call it a rule. Cost asked in the form of a direction had been answered with a direction, and nothing had been entered. The mud in her wrist creases was the colour of the clean dark line at the Vail corner, and he did not join those two either.

Around the yard they went as the yard had gone all month. There was the clean dark line at the corner where \lore{Ash} ridge had turned and been dealt with and left lying, and beside it the new finer hairline of grey that had gathered since, thinner than the old and called \lore{the thaw}; there was the space beneath the lowest of \place[the drying racks]{the racks} where the broken birth-slates lay, gone around as wide as a pail of hot water set down where a child might knock it; and there was the empty place in the returning line where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out. Sella's step widened at that place. Her eyes went to it and went off it, and she carried the basket past with a look and a wide step and no number, as she had carried it past every morning of the month. Nothing had come up out of the strip in all the days since, and the yard had settled into going around the place the way water goes around a stone set in it: not over, not through, and never once calling it a thing that had been put there. A slate would be found and a figure made if a hand came up in a month or a year. No hand had come up. The boy did not ask which hand, or whose, or what the figure would have been. He had no standing to ask and no word in \lore{Grathic} that would have carried the question, which was perhaps the same thing.

The last bundles had gone downriver in the morning and come back on shoulders in the afternoon, and the afternoon had set them down in the ruts and left them there while the light went out of the bank. Dry mud had taken the print of the boot that set them and kept the print after the boot had gone, so that the yard held a record of the carrying in the only place it ever held one: the ground. The boy read the prints from the basket as a man reads a bone that has been set and is healing, not what it had cost but what had been done and in what order, and set them beside the arms. No thumb would ever be laid on either.

His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water that came from beyond the racks and came down against the fold. Nothing within two paces could be reached: not the bundles in the ruts, not the green ends, not the space under the rack, not the place in the returning line. The count he kept went on beside the bundles, exact and unspent, the only figure in the yard that nobody had asked for and nobody could have entered. It went on without anybody's thumb upon it.

Under \place{the Vail kiln-house} roof Sella set the basket low near the bench leg, at the angle at which the underside of a board becomes a roof to a man lying on the floor while a man standing sees only the top of it. The cold iron of the bench bracket was at his cheek. She gave the instruction to the wool, the pot, the broom and the fire and never to the basket --- the wool first, then the pot, then the broom, then the fire, and the basket last if at all, which was never --- and told the fire it was \lore{the thaw}, which it was, and would go when it would go. The fire gave its four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, and she did not count the pulls aloud and did not ask anybody to hear the silence. Above the basket \place{the loaf shelf} kept its two unnamed gaps in the room the fold did not touch: the old space at the row's end, which the planting had brought in and which had stood there through all the days of his life without anybody deciding what it was, and the palm's breadth that had come in on the night of Seed 6 with no scrape, no crumb, no print and no word, and had kept its place beside it ever since. Two gaps. A third would have been somebody's doing, and nobody had done it.

A neighbouring woman came up from the strips with a wet pot in both hands. Her hand went past the bare board toward the shelf before her eyes went to the basket, the reaching arriving a half-beat ahead of the looking; then she stopped, with the pot tilted in her hands and a little water gone over her wrist, and asked what the row would want before the tend was out.

“Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there,” Sella said. “The house has said it.”

“I asked the house.”

“Then you've asked it twice. The pot's more use.”

The woman set the pot down where the pot was meant to go and carried the question away level, so that nothing slopped, and gave nobody a figure. Her going did not join the shelf's old gap to the empty place at the boy's ear, though she had stood with the one in front of her eyes and the other two inches from her hem; she heard two sayings and made of them one absence, and went off with the pot, and the absence went with her and not into the basket. The boy laid the reaching on the tray beside the other reachings and did not lay it across anything. Sella's lips went on moving along the row. She glanced once at the clay jar standing cold under its glaze at the shelf's far end and gave nobody a figure either, because a jar cold under its glaze is a jar cold under its glaze and not an answer to a woman with a wet pot. The dry warm smell of the shelf crossed for one breath with the sharper smell of charcoal from under the board, and the smell went the way the smell went, and neither of them was entered.

The shelf had stood over every morning of his life and would stand over more, and its underside kept a record that the top of it gave no sign of. She was standing with her back to the basket when she reached a finger beneath the front edge of the shelf where the charcoal tally lay. She had been moving her lips along the row and she did not stop moving them. The little-door burn closed on the wood's lip and opened again. The finger went under, stayed out a breath, and came back, and she brought it away without looking at it and without a word, and did not go back to the shelf.

From the low angle the boy saw the hand go under and come back and saw nothing of what the marks said. He knew the tally was there: short grouped strokes of different darkness pressed hard into the underside, the ones at the row's end faded almost into the grain, a record kept where nobody thought to look for one and handled by a hand that taught itself not to look. Such a hand either knows a board by heart or has been told not to look, and the reason stood between the two and would not come down on either side. He made no cause of the habit. He did not count the strokes, did not press a thumb where her thumb had been, did not lay the charcoal under the shelf across the two gaps above it or across the one stiff brown-edged crust lying two inches from his ear in the fold in the other room. A keeping put where nobody expects to be read tells more by the care of its hiding than the writing would, and he had learned that without being taught and could not spend it. The two rooms did not touch. No third gap came into either of them. He had the appetite to set the two rooms out on a towel, each object in its own hollow, the crust in one and the marks in the other and nothing resting across, but an inventory is not a key, and a man who cannot lift a thing cannot use what he has catalogued. He kept them apart by hand, which was the only hand available, and it was a child's hand. He had seen the hand go under and could not enter the seeing anywhere, because there was no slate in the district that would take it and no standing in the room to put it on one.

\lore{the widow's mark} hung where it hung at her throat, a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and she did not touch it. She touched it before she opened the kiln and at no other hour, and a shelf and an afternoon are not openings. The clean dark line at the corner and the finer grey beside it stayed where they lay, unswept and called \lore{the thaw}, and the fire was told the thaw and did not ask to be told twice. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down against the fold, exact, complete, unspent and spoken by nobody. Outside, the reed month went on in its last week without drawing a boundary anywhere, and the last bundles stood grey in the ruts with nobody counting them. The finger had come away from beneath the board without a word. The two rooms of the house did not touch.
