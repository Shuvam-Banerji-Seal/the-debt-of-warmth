\daymark{143}{Reed 24, Y.H. 813 (Fourthday)}

The reed month went on in its last week without drawing a boundary anywhere, the way a river goes on under its skin of ice and gives no sign at the place where one reach leaves another. There was only the shorter bundle in every arm coming down, and the green showing where the knife had bitten the broken ends, and the frost taking the tips pale as old bone. Nobody in \place{Low Kiln} said the month had turned a corner; the yard had never been one for announcing its own arithmetic. \person{Sella Vail} carried \person[Corin]{the boy} down the river road at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, woodsmoke going before her and the river wind coming up to meet it, and gave the instruction to the wool and to the wind and never to the basket --- keep it off his ears, it had come all the way down with them and would go all the way back up --- and then gave nothing at all to the wind, because a wind has no instruction to receive. He lay with his cheek to the fold and the small wet sound coming and going as his head wandered and was not set right. The wool had ridden back from the small bare joint of his wrist, and the cold had got in there in its known order, wrist before temple, temple before cheek. One hundred and forty-three days old. Four months and twenty-three days. Neither number was spoken under any roof that morning, and if somebody in the district had said one aloud nobody there would have known what it counted.

\place[the Weir]{The Weir} stood a league upriver with its traps set in the gaps and their mouths turned upriver, so that an eel went in and could not find the way out. The \place{plank walk} ran along the north bank on posts, and the posts kept their rings of salt where the flood had touched them and a darker line above where it had not. Sella set the basket at the very edge of the work where no slate lay, a half-pace from every board a clerk might have written on, and stood with the little-door burn shut on nothing. “Keep it off his ears. It's the river wind,” she told the wool, as she had told it coming down and would tell it going up, and told the running water nothing, because running water takes an instruction and carries it away. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down against the fold. Nothing within two paces could be reached: not the bundle, not the post, not the cord inside the coat.

The reed-cutter came along the boards with a bundle on his shoulder and the short length of grey wool or reed-fibre cord looped inside his coat. The cord had gone grey with the oil of a working thumb, the grey a stitch-hole takes after a thread has been drawn through it a dozen times. He set the bundle on \place[the blessing stone]{the flat blessing stone}, where the hearth-maids had blessed the traps in the spring and the stone still kept a pale scatter of salt in its grain, straightened the cord on his thumb, and turned it once so the knots stood in the small light. He tied one low with his teeth and a pull of the thumb, bit the end off, and put the cord back inside his coat, drawing the coat closed over it with the flat of his hand. There was no figure in the air over the stone, and no knot was counted where anyone could hear it counted. He went to lift a trap.

A weir woman with salt dried white at the ends of her sleeves stood where the boards ran level and asked what the cord held.

“You can ask the cord,” the reed-cutter said.

“I can't read a cord.”

“No more can the Court.”

He said it without anger and without a smile, the way a man says the weather, and lifted the trap with the question going down into the water after it. The eels in the basket below the stone knocked once and went quiet. The boy kept the answer and the question and the going-down of it on a place of their own. A cord in a coat is a record in wool that tells what was carried and not what it cost; it is exact, it is complete, and it is worth nothing anyone could pay. The man's hand had gone to the cord before his eyes went to the woman, the order he had learned before he could hold up his own head, and he set that order beside the others without making a row of them and calling it a rule. He did not lay the cord in the coat beside the charcoal under the shelf in \place[the Vail kiln-house]{the Vail roof}, or beside the empty place in the returning line where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and not come out, or beside Mira's grey cords, or beside Orra's knot-string. Private records stood across the district and he had no standing to join any two of them. He did not touch the bundle, the post, or the coat. His hand came down against the fold.

\scenebreak

At \place{the lower boards} \person{Orra Weir} stood with her board on its side, turning it with the wrist that had turned it ten hundred times. Her hands were like old rope and the pale eel scars lay uncovered across both forearms, arcs of white where a hook or a rim had taken skin years ago and left it thin; salt stood white at her wrists where the water had dried and dried again. The grey streak showed first through the black hair when she moved her head, and the nasal half-song of the Fen came into her voice where she was tired, a note held back in the nose that the Cross had never quite bred out of her. A grey cord lay at her waist. She pulled one knot down with her thumb and set it among the others with a small flat knock, the sound of a wet board tapped by a knuckle, and did not look into the basket.

A woman come down from the rails with a creel against her hip asked what the levy would take now the hands were out in the reeds and the month nearly spent.

“The ninth of Ilmen,” Orra said. “The collector's boat came round the bend on the flood and had six oars. One man stood at the stern and never touched them.”

“That isn't the levy.”

“It's a date. The river takes what the Court doesn't. You can shout at either one. I've tried.”

She went back to the knots. Her thumb went to the cord again at the end of the sentence, and the boy could not tell whether that was a lie or the habit of counting, and made no cause of it. The last knot on the cord showed its double turn, tail tucked, tied left over right with the small extra weight of a thumb pressed in. It was legible to him without its name. The word that would have named it stayed where it had stayed since the year of the flower, unspoken in a morning that had a great many figures in it and not one he could enter.

A date given where a figure was asked is an answer with the question taken out of it: a chart closed over a note with the neat margin left in and the finding left off. He set it where it belonged, on its own place, beside the told bell and the twice-wiped thumb and not across either. The man who stood at the stern and kept his hands off the oars was the man whose work is the counting of other men's pulling; he went on a place of his own, apart from the midwife's reaching hand and the thane's finger passed over a figure and the woman at the shelf whose hand went beneath the board before her eyes went to the row. No cause was written across them. Nothing in the date was joined to the hand that had not come back out of the strip, and nothing in the strip was joined to the boat. The boy set the boat where it stood and the stern where it stood and asked no third thing of either.

\person{Mira Silt} worked the eels at the boards below, with pale slime dried in long grey strokes on her sleeves and silt dried pale in the hollow of her neck, her head held a little to the right where the top of her left ear had been, her small smile kept on her face the way a door is kept on the latch. She never sat down where a slate lay open, and there was no slate on these boards, only fish and water and the two women's keepings side by side. A weir woman with a pail asked whether the run would be thin now the hands were in the reeds and the tend nearly out.

“Thin?” Mira said. “Who's to say thin? It comes when it comes. Ask the river.”

She flicked an eel-bone against her thumb while she decided whether to lie, moved two from hand to hand, and put one away. The eels were small and cold and went through her fingers with a slap that caught the light off the boards, and she said something low about a boat that had come up on the flood and nothing about the hand that had not come out of the strips. The boy could not hear the low part and did not try to make it out. Her not-looking was a keeping set on a place of its own, the opposite of the pricing face turned the same way across a market, and he had no word in \lore{Grathic} for either.

A buyer in a coat with the river in its hem named a figure for two pair.

“Two marks the pair and two short,” Mira said. “You've said it twice and I've counted it twice. There's nothing in the sentence that wants an answer.”

Her thumb went under her apron to the grey wool cord and stayed longer than the figure required, while she decided what the figure meant to her and not what it meant to the buyer. Shifting a creel showed the second cord twisted twice beneath the grey, the one that had never been carried upriver to \place{Nine Pots} and never lifted at a door; the knots on neither were counted by any thumb but her own, and the knot for the thin child upriver was untouched. She set the creel down level. The buyer took the eels and went off along the boards, and her thumb came out from under her apron with an eel-bone between finger and thumb, which she put away.

So three private records stood within a dozen paces of the basket: a cord in a coat, a knot-string on a board, a second twist beneath an apron. Every one was visible and none was enterable. Mira's board lay beside Orra's and did not touch it, and the reed-cutter had gone up the boards with his knots still inside his coat. The boy kept the three side by side and not across, the way a man lays instruments on a towel with each in its own hollow and nothing resting on anything else. He had the appetite to arrange them --- the old habit of a hand that wants a field set out before the instrument comes down --- and no standing to enter any of them anywhere. A doctrine is an account that has found a payer, and there was no payer named to any of these three, so he would not let them stand up together and call themselves one. He held them apart by hand. It was the only honest work available to him, and there was no slate in the district that would enter it.

\scenebreak

Below \place[the Singing Sill]{the sill}, at \place{the fish-trade shade}, a woman wound a child's foot in three turns of wool and drew the wool tight and left it. The foot stayed dark inside the wrapping. She had the look of a woman who has done a necessary thing quickly so nobody can make a speech about it, and a neighbour with a pail said the water was weather and would go when it would go, and that a person could not carry on with a river. The woman did not answer her. The child's foot kept its dark, the dark kept its place inside the wool, and the weather went on going when it would go.

The boy read the foot the way he had once read a field before the dressing went on. The extremity had gone before the core, which was the order of it; the small almost-smile on the child's face was not peace but the slackening of a mouth that had stopped asking, and the warming nobody would perform was still ahead, with the afterdrop waiting past it to take the core down after the shell had come up. He knew the sequence well enough to have named the hour it would break. It bought him nothing. He could not lift the foot, could not put a hand on the core, could not say the one word in Grathic that would have made a woman turn the wrapping looser, and there was no word in Sarn for what he knew, so he set the whole reading down as an object worth nothing, joined to no cure and no cause. He did not write a silt-leach across it, did not make a recognition of it, did not lay it beside any other dark in the district and call the two the same. No cause came into the shade and no cure went out of it. The foot stayed the cold to the woman who had wrapped it and weather to the yard.

Below the north bridge at \place{the out-of-line landing}, the soft-cuffed clerk was still set for a measure nobody called. His cuff was grey from washing and grey again by midday; the slate lay against his forearm with the writing turned inward where no goer-by could read it, and his hand went to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls, every time, whether or not there was a figure to enter. The market sold its reeds and its eels over the top of him as water goes over a stone that lies where a stone lies. Nobody stepped around him, nobody asked what he was set there for, and nobody had taken him off since the month turned, or if somebody had, somebody had put him back. The boy kept the set measure as a shape and did not make a rule of it; the judge of it was not in the basket and had no standing in the landing.

At the going of the light Sella carried him home at the half-pace, with the instruction given to the wool and to the running water and never to the basket, going around every board a slate might have lain on. The buyer went off with the eels and Mira's thumb came out from under her apron and the reed-cutter's cord was back inside his coat with its knots unread. Orra's knot-string went back upriver to the Eel House where no clerk could spell it, and the child's foot stayed dark in three turns of wool. One hundred and forty-three days old and four months and twenty-three days, exact, complete and unspent, told by nobody who would have known what the number counted. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down against the fold. The count went on into the last days of Reed, forward, not closed, waiting on a hand that had not come.
