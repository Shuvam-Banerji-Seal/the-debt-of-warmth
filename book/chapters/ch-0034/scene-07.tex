The light went out of the clay bank without anybody marking the hour, and \person{Sella Vail} took the basket up again from under the roof and carried it onto \place{the clay road}, going up at the half-pace she kept from every open slate. Woodsmoke went before her and the river wind came up to meet it, the two passing in the dark between the houses the way two people pass on a stair without speaking. She gave the instruction to the wool and to the wind and never to the basket --- keep it off his ears, it had come down with them in the morning and would go back up before the night was out --- and then gave nothing more to the wind, having said it once, because a wind takes an instruction and carries it away and there is an end of it. The boy lay with his cheek to the fold and listened to the two smells go over him: smoke going up from the low houses, river coming up off the sill with the cold in it, neither belonging to anybody and both older than the district. One smelled of a fire that had been kept, the other of water that had not, and between them he was carried at a pace that was not his own choosing and had never once been hurried.

Halfway up she went around a pan set out to dry, tilting her shoulder and the basket with it so that the pan stayed where the pan stood and nothing knocked against it; and at the well she went around the trough with its skin of ice, thin as a skim of fat set aside in a dish, and did not break the skin to get at the water underneath. The well had its own little roof and the ice had gathered under it where the wind could not come at it, and she carried him past at the half-pace and gave the instruction to the bucket, which stood where a bucket stands and wanted nothing from her. Weather, the neighbours called it, and it would go when it would go. He had learned before he could hold up his head that a thing called weather is a thing nobody has agreed to count, and that a man who wants a figure from weather is a man who will be given a direction instead.

At the yard she went wide around the space beneath the lowest of \place[the drying racks]{the racks}, where the broken birth-slates lay. She went as wide around it as a woman goes around a pail of hot water set down where a child might knock it --- a step taken for the thing's sake and not for her own, and not explained to anybody, least of all to the child in the basket, who could not have knocked over a cup if he had been set beside one. The \lore{Ash} in the corner lay where it had lain, the clean dark line where the ridge had turned and turned again, and beside it the new finer hairline of grey that had gathered since. Both had been called \lore{the thaw} all week and neither had been swept, and the name had not made either one go. Beyond the ruts the last bundles stood grey in the dark where the afternoon had left them, green at the broken ends and pale where the frost had bitten the tips, and nobody had counted them since the light went. The mud kept the print of the boot that had set them down and gave it back slowly, and the boy read the print from the low angle as a man reads a bone that has been set and is healing: what was done, and in what order, and never what it cost.

\scenebreak

Under \place{the Vail kiln-house} roof she lifted him out of the basket without once looking into it, the way a woman lifts a pot by the handle and does not look inside because looking will not change what is in it. She set him on \place{the bed} and not in \place{the Debt Loft}, setting him down as one sets a pot down where it will not be slopped --- the base first, the weight settled, the hand kept where it is until the thing is sure of its place. Then she put the fold right at his ear and under his chin, and did not once look at his face. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the cold and she left it frozen and unwiped, because nobody under any roof in the district had been given an instruction about the thread, and a pot set down is a pot set down and left. Her hand rested a half-beat longer than the setting needed before it went away. She had learned that half-beat in the winter her own child cooled while everybody was kind to it and had never told anybody what she had learned, and the house had straightened around the silence every time since. Then the hand went away and she did not go back to it.

She told the fire it was \lore{the thaw}, which it was, and would go when it would go. She turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and fed them and said “The house will keep it” where another woman would have said \emph{I}. The fire gave its four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, and she did not count the pulls aloud and did not ask anybody to hear the silence, and its payer had been unnamed now for a hundred and forty-three days. She stood a moment with the back of the broken pot in her hand, counting something under her breath toward no opening. She pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn, the grey streak at her temple showing pale off the coals, and set the pot where it could be found in the dark, rim out and the handle toward the reaching hand --- the handle standing two inches beyond the hand that would have reached for it and would not close on it --- and did not look toward the bed.

\place{the loaf shelf} stood above the low angle and kept its two unnamed gaps in the room the fold did not touch: the old space at the row's end and the palm's breadth that had come in on the night of Seed 6 with no scrape, no crumb, no print and no word. Two gaps. A third would have been somebody's doing and nobody had done it. Under the board the charcoal lay in its short grouped strokes, and she had reached a finger beneath it twice that day and brought it away without looking and without a word, and did not go back to the shelf now. \lore{the widow's mark} hung where it hung at her throat, a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and she did not touch it. She touched it before she opened the kiln and at no other hour, and a dusk is not an opening. She went to her own bed without explaining the count and without looking toward the bed where the boy lay, and the two rooms of the house did not touch.

In the small hours he turned out his file the way a man lays instruments on a towel before a case begins, each thing going into its own hollow and nothing resting across anything else. He had kept it through a hundred and forty-three days in a body that could not have lifted a single object from the towel, and the keeping of the things apart was the only work in the room that no slate would enter.

Six hearings of \lore{the full note} lay beside one another and never among them: a cup across a birth room, a pot under the wheel-shed wall, the district's slate twice at the Cross, a man giving at the Drawing, and the small flat knock out of a woman as the men lifted, that last from a person and filed beside the five and never counted with them. No seventh had come in three days. The bell had been a told bell and a heard bell, kept in two places; the wheel and the pots under the roof were notes he had left where they sounded; the clerk's slate was a figure he could not read. He had not invented one to fill the empty place. An empty place kept honestly is a record of a kind, and a man who fills one to make a row come out even is a man with a reason, and he had learned two lives ago to be afraid of the shape of a reason in a man with clean hands.

Three near-full answers stood on their own tray, advanced and not paid: a cup across a birth room, a pot under the wheel-shed wall, a rack pot lifted a hand's breadth too high and set down again. Each had wanted a following stroke that nobody had made, and he had not made it for any of them; the wheel-keeper's knowledge of a pot's note had gone to the same tray and not to the hearings, because a thing withheld is not a thing said and a knowing is not a hearing.

The older intervals lay where they had always lain: the half-beat behind a standing finger, the stroke that had gone on over the flagstones without the check, his own held breath from the market, the unchecking stroke come up beside the half-beat with no cause written across them. And on the same tray, in the place he had made for them the night before, lay seven stoppings of the near-saying --- Reed 5, Reed 8, Reed 11, Reed 14, Reed 17, Reed 20, and now Reed 23 --- seven objects and not a rule; seven intervals and not seven payments. He held them apart by hand because he had no other hand to hold them with. Seven likenesses standing in a row will stand up and call themselves a doctrine if nobody keeps a finger between them, and a doctrine is an account that has found a payer; no payer had been named to any of the seven. The sentence about an ear under this roof being worth more than the debt that holds it had come all the way to the word that would call the loan and had stopped there, with no figure and no name, and the breath that should have gone in at the top of the reach had stayed where it was. The seventh year stood one tend from its end and had not given. The loan stood uncalled.

Beside these lay the pieces that had no tray of their own and would not be put on one: \lore{the damper} as one iron with two bites, the bite that holds a vessel whole and the bite that can take the hand off a man, filed joined to no kiln; the two tellings of the counting room shut in the war, side by side with no third, and nobody under this roof having gone into a shut room to make one; the told bell of \lore{the Long Night} beside the heard bell out of the yard, two sounds of the same shape and not the same sound. He laid them out and looked at them and did not let the look become a joining. An inventory is not a key. A man who cannot lift a thing cannot use what he has catalogued, and the towel at the end of it held nothing he could carry to any table.

In the other room \place{the loaf shelf} kept its two unnamed gaps, and the charcoal under the board stayed on a place of its own, unread by him and unread by anybody who had gone past it that day; a hand had gone beneath the board and come back, and what the marks said had stayed under the board. One brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold two inches from his ear, with its empty place beside it still unnamed and no third coming into either room. The word forty stayed unspoken in the district while the forty-first double turn stood legible on a knot-string beside a board that did not touch another board; it was a shape and not a name, and a name was not going to be made of it in the dark. The fifth green across the square was a colour still, joined to no ear, and he had not made it a face or a cause and had kept it on a place of its own, a colour and nothing more.

The reed-cutter's cord was back inside the coat with its knots unread; \person[Mira Silt]'s second twist lay twice beneath an apron and had not been carried upriver to \place{Nine Pots} or lifted at a door; \person{Orra Weir}'s knot-string kept its place on its own board. \person{Alda Reed}'s six pages lay turned toward a floorboard three doors down in \place{House Silt}, and her little tin stayed shut in her bodice, and the round pewter tin above the abandoned bed kept its pale fold and thumb-bright lid closed. The soft-cuffed clerk was still set at \place{the out-of-line landing} below \place{the north bridge} for a measure nobody had called, cuff grey from washing and grey again by midday, slate to his forearm with the writing inward, hand to slate before eyes to stalls; nobody had taken him off and nobody would. A child's foot below the sill stayed the cold to the woman who had wrapped it in three turns of wool and weather to the yard, and the reading of it --- the extremity going before the core, the almost-smile that is not peace, the afterdrop waiting past a warming nobody would perform --- worked as perfectly as it had on the day he learned it and bought him nothing he could spend. He kept every one of these where it lay and laid none of them across another.

Through the finger's-width door came the river's note of water over water, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, a fifth low thing and never counted among the hearings; it was not a hearing and he did not make it one. Through the floor under blind ash the carried fire gave four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for a hundred and forty-three days. Overhead the boards kept four nails, four, and a fifth that was a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts in three places, and none of them became one: a note that was not counted, a fire that missed a pull, a fifth that was a knot and not a nail. He had learned early that three likenesses in a row want to stand up as a rule, and that the only honest work was to keep a hand between them; his hand was outside the wool and went up an inch and came down against the fold, exact, complete, unspent, and spoken by nobody.

Cold came in its known order: cheek, temple, wrist, the wool riding back from the small bare joint. One hundred and forty-three days old and four months and twenty-three days, told by nobody, and if somebody under that roof had said the number nobody would have known what it counted. He wanted the crust two inches from his ear, and he wanted the number spoken by somebody who would have known what a number was, and he got neither and could not ask for either. The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down against the fold. The note went on over water through the gap in the door, the fire missed its fifth under the floor, and the charcoal waited under the shelf for a hand that was not his and a day that had not come. His hand went up an inch toward the note and came down. One hundred and forty-three, exact and unspent in the dark, and the reed month had gone into its last days with the loan uncalled and the seventh year standing one tend from its end.
