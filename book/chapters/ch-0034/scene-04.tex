The afternoon leaned over \place{the Cross} without any boundary drawn at its coming, the light going flat off the boards and finding the dust in the hollows of the market tables, so that every cup set out there looked like a cup set down by somebody who meant to come back for it and had not yet come. The last reeds of the month went off in armfuls judged by the arm that carried them, and the eel tubs kept their skin of cold water at the rim where the morning's ice had turned. \person{Sella Vail} carried \person[Corin]{the boy} a little way along \place{the market stalls} and set the basket where the boards ran level and no slate lay, and stood a half-pace off with the little-door burn shut on nothing. “Keep it off his ears. It's the river wind,” she told the wool, as she had told it coming down and would tell it going up, and told the wind nothing at all, because a wind has no instruction to receive. His cheek was to the fold and the small wet sound came and went as his head wandered and was not set right; the wool had ridden back from the small bare joint of his wrist and the cold had got in there in its known order, wrist before temple, temple before cheek. One hundred and forty-two days old and four months and twenty-two days, and nobody under any roof in the district had said the number or would have known what it counted.

\person{Berr Clay} came up short off \place{the clay road} a little after the middle of the day, with the red wool cord of his office folded square under his coat instead of worn across it and the green of tasted \lore{lead glaze} at one corner of his mouth. Hot clay went in ahead of him and the smell of nothing at all came behind, the most expensive smell in \place{Grath}. His hands were large and clean. He wiped the thumb twice on his apron before he touched nothing, as a man wipes an instrument twice before he lays it on a towel, and held a narrow slate unopened against his forearm with the writing turned inward where the afternoon could not read it. He passed a finger over the figure and let his eyes rest on the writing a breath longer than the writing required, the way a man checks the bite of a clamp before he trusts it with a vessel. Then he read one in twelve, as \lore{the notice} says, low into the goers-by, called the \lore{levy} and denied it: “It is not called by me.” To a woman who brought a number up out of a fist and asked what the last of the reeds would want before the tend was out, he said “\emph{Your house},” and left it there. Never \emph{I}. He took himself out of his own sentence as neatly as a hand goes out of a field before the dressing goes on, and the sentence stood afterward with nobody inside it, which is the way this district likes its arithmetic to stand.

The figure went into the air and not into any house. The true subtraction stayed in the locked box in his house up the clay road toward \place{High Kiln}, where it had sat through other readings and would sit through this one, and a figure read aloud was not the figure paid. The one went over the top of the basket into the noise of the stalls; the other went up the road under a coat with the cord folded square under it, and the two did not travel together and did not arrive at the same door.

At the eel tubs he took two pair and laid two marks on the stall-keeper's outstretched palm. The woman put the marks in her palm without counting them where anyone could hear, the way a woman puts a hot coal into a pocket she means to empty later, and shut her hand on them; the eels went into a basket with a small wet slap and a flick of a tail that caught the light off the boards. Two pair. Two marks short. There was no figure in the air over the tub, only the slap and the flick and the closing of a hand, and the boy set the whole transaction on a place of its own as a record that told exactly what had been carried and not what it had cost.

“A number goes into a slate, not a bundle,” Berr said. “If your house wants it again, there'll be a slate at the watch.” \emph{Your house}. Never \emph{I}. To the stall boards, in a voice lower than the one he had used at the block, he let fall that the levy was called and was not called by him, one in twelve as the notice says; nobody leaned in at that hour, so the words were spent on the boards and went nowhere any ear could follow.

The stall-keeper went on wiping the tub rim with a rag gone grey with slime and did not count the marks, and did not seem to mind the two eels that had not come with them. A kindness performed in the form of a subtraction is still a subtraction, and a man can believe he has done good with the very hand that carries the shortfall into a slate where nobody will look at it. The boy had learned to read the order in which a hand goes to a record before it goes to a person, and he read it now in the large clean hands going back to the eels. There was nothing cruel in the hands. That was the difficult part of them, and the reason he kept them on a tray of their own, apart from the midwife reaching for her slate before the child, the clerk going to his slate before his eyes went to the stalls, and the woman at the shelf whose hand went under the board before her eyes went to the row above it. He did not join any of them. A habit is a habit, and he had no standing to enter any hand anywhere.

Berr went off toward High Kiln with the cord folded square under his coat, the narrow slate under his arm and the green at his mouth where it had been, and up the clay road to the house whose door had never been opened in the boy's sight. He believed he had done the district a kindness, and the belief was a clean thing in him, like the slate he wiped before he touched it. The boy kept the belief where he kept the twice-wiped thumb --- on its own place, joined to no figure and no payer. The levy went on standing as read, and the district went on with its eels.

Below \place{the north bridge}, at \place{the out-of-line landing}, the steps kept four, four and a landing a hand's breadth out of line where a step should have been, and the market went up and down over the gap carefully without making a fall of it. A girl with a creel took it sideways, one hand out to the rail, and went on selling without looking at her feet, and nobody called it an accident. The soft-cuffed clerk was still set there for a measure nobody called. His cuff was grey from washing and grey again by midday, and the slate lay against his forearm with the writing turned inward, so that no goer-by could read what he had entered. His hand went to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls, every time, whether or not there was a figure to enter, and the market sold its reeds and its eels over the top of him as water goes over a stone that lies where a stone lies. Nobody stepped around him, nobody asked what he was set there for, and nobody had taken him off since the month turned, or if somebody had, somebody had put him back.

The boy kept the order of reaching on a place of its own. He had learned it before he could hold up his own head: the hand to the record before the hand to the person, whether the record was a slate, a cord, the underside of a shelf or the edge of a door held a finger's width open. He set the clerk's order beside the midwife's, the thane's twice-wiped thumb and the woman's hand at the bare shelf, and joined none of them. The clerk was not Marra and not the grey-fingered collector at the Drawing and not the soft-handed man who held a slate at the edge where nobody else could see the figure. No sixth sighting of the neat dark hand had come to the market's edge, and he did not invent one to keep the row tidy. A measure set down before anybody called it is a measure set down ahead of something; he kept that as a shape and did not make a rule of it. The judge of it was not in the basket and had no standing in the landing.

At the going of the light Sella carried him away with the instruction given to the wool and the running water and never to the basket, and the clerk stayed set where he had been set, with the writing turned inward against his forearm, waiting on a figure nobody had called.

Under \place{the Vail kiln-house} roof the going of the light came in through the finger's-width door as a note of water over water, low and fifth and never counted among \lore{the full note}. Sella carried him up at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, woodsmoke going before her out of the clay road and the river wind coming up to meet it, and gave the instruction to the wool and to the wind and never to the basket. She went around a pan set out to dry and the trough by the well with its skin of ice, and went wide around the space beneath the lowest rack where the broken birth-slates lay as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. Under the roof she set him on his bed and not in \place{the Debt Loft}, lifting him out of the basket without looking into it and setting him down as one sets a pot down where it will not be slopped. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without once looking at his face, left the thread at his mouth frozen and unwiped, and let her hand rest a half-beat longer than the setting needed before it went away, because nobody under any roof has been given an instruction about the thread.

The coals were banked under blind \lore{Ash} with the back of a broken pot, and she told the fire it was \lore{the thaw}. She turned them, fed them, said “The house will keep it” where another woman would have said \emph{I}, and stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand counting under her breath toward no opening. She pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn, the grey streak pale in the dying light, set the pot rim out with the handle standing two inches beyond the reaching hand, and neither looked toward the bed nor touched \lore{the widow's mark}, because a dusk is not an opening. The fire answered with four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, and had done so for a hundred and forty-two days with nobody naming the payer.

The small knife lay on its sill at the very edge where it could be checked, with six standing in a worn thumb-mark where the house could check and the seventh where it could not --- the one entry in this house the house could not see. The unforged collector sentence lay beside it, unentered, as it had lain for six years. \person{Tarn} took the broom only after it had been given its instruction, and measured the kindling with his eyes as a debt is measured: four, four, and a place where the fifth should have stood. “Six counted. The next I don't count,” he told the wood, and did not fill the place. He set the knife back where the house could check it. His chest had been tight since the dark, and the breath he had decided not to spend on Seed 16 stood behind the small interval at the top of each reach, a gap in the breathing shaped like a held thing. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line, and the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back.

Then the sentence came.

It came in the shape it had worn for six years, a small plain sentence about an ear under this roof and what it was worth against the debt that held the man who owned it. It had come on Reed 5 and Reed 8 and Reed 11 and Reed 14 and Reed 17 and Reed 20, and now on Reed 23 it came a seventh time. It went all the way to the word that would call the loan. The word stood with a figure behind it and a name behind the figure, and Tarn stopped there. He did not say the word. He did not say the figure. He did not say the name. He drew the breath that should have gone in at the top of the reach and did not spend it, said “Yes, house” to the fire, and went on with the broom.

Sella, within hearing, gave the end of her instruction to the pot and asked nothing, as she had given it to the pot on six nights already and would give it again. \person{Jorra Vail}, within hearing, did not ask about the ear; Tarn did not ask about the room off the shed whose door kept its finger's width of open. The two silences lay side by side under the one roof and were not joined, and nobody in the room made one of them the other. Jorra came through from the shed with the dust of the day still ground small as meal into both forearms past the bent little finger and told a pot it would do, and did not look toward the bed. The household straightened around his silence the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted, and went on.

The boy laid the day's objects out in the order they had arrived, the way a man lays his tools back on a towel, each in its own hollow and nothing resting across anything else. He took the seventh stopping out of the air where Tarn had left it and set it on the interval tray beside the six. It lay there as an object among objects: Reed 5, Reed 8, Reed 11, Reed 14, Reed 17, Reed 20 and now Reed 23, seven stoppings of the near-saying, each brought all the way to the word and stopped there with no figure and no name.

It was a seventh interval and not a seventh payment. It was not a hearing and not a seventh hearing. The six hearings of the full note stayed six, five from four sources and the sixth from a person, beside and never among, and he did not let the stopping lie down with them. He would not let seven likenesses stand up and call themselves a doctrine, because a doctrine is an account that has found a payer, and no payer had been named to any of the seven. A thing held seven times at the teeth is a held word still. Seven times owed is not seven times paid, and a row of held things does not become a rule because it has reached a number a man can count on both hands. The tray held seven objects and no account.

Under the floor the carried fire kept its four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, and nobody had named a payer for it in a hundred and forty-two days. The loan that can hear stayed uncalled. The seventh year stood one tend from its end and had still not given; something must give at the end of it, but that end had not come under this roof tonight. Tarn's stroke went on over \place{the flagstones} and past the shallow bright patch the stones kept, without the half-check, toward the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector, and he did not turn his head along it.

The seventh stopping lay on the tray among six others and none of the seven had a payer. The fire gave four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been. The boy's hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down against the fold; one hundred and forty-two days old and four months and twenty-two days, exact, complete and unspent, with nobody under the roof to say what the number counted. He could not call the loan, could not spend the interval, could not lift the tray to any table in \place{Low Kiln}. The river's note came through the finger's-width door and the fire's four low pulls came through the floor, and the count went on under the roof, forward, not closed, waiting on a hand that had not come.
