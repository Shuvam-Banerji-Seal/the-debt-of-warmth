\daymark{53}{Ilmen 24, Y.H. 813}

The eels came up out of \place{the Ilm} still moving, and the slime they left on \person{Mira Silt}'s sleeves dried pale before the light got down to the landing. She worked at the outer edge of \place{the Fishstairs}, below \place{the north bridge}, where the stalls thinned and the shadow lay long across the wet boards. River silt sat in the hollow of her neck and dried there in the cold. She held her head a little to the right against the place where the top of her left ear had been, and kept the small smile on her face the way another woman keeps a door on the latch: it could be shut without anybody seeing the hand that moved it. Salt, cold fish and tallow smoke went ahead of her. An eel went into the basket, came out, and went in again; her hands knew which end to hold without the help of her eyes, and her eyes were on the buyer and the steps and the river, in that order.

\person{Sella Vail} set the basket down before the fires were fairly up, in the lee of a stack of eel creels where no slate had ever reached, and gave the instruction to the wool. “Keep it off his ears. It's the river wind.” She had carried \person{Corin} down the way a woman carries a pot filled that must not be slopped, and she went around a slate lying open on a crate as wide as she would have gone around a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it over. He was fifty-three days old. The fold was turned right at his chin and one crust lay in it, with the empty place beside it holding its edges as exactly as a wound holds its edges after the knife comes out. She did not look into the basket. She never gave an instruction to the basket.

A rack-woman came along the row with her sleeves wet to the elbow and asked whether the run would come now the ice had begun to go soft. Mira did not answer at once. She took three eel-bones from her pocket and moved them hand to hand, one against her thumb, then another, her face still turned toward the basin as if the question had been asked of the eels. The bones went faster while she decided. When the deciding was done she put one away and answered the question and her own answer both. “Does it come now? It comes when it comes. Ask the river.” The rack-woman said something about the weather and went off to her tubs, and the smile did not move on Mira's face.

The boy watched the bones more closely than he watched the woman's mouth. He had learned to read the order in which a hand reaches: the midwife to the slate before the child, the clerk to the paper before the face, the man at the block to his thumb before he touched nothing. This was the same knowledge in a different material. A woman choosing which answer to let a listener have moved her fingers before she spoke, and the lie, if there was one, had already been weighed and put away by the time the sentence came out. He had no word in the district's tongue for the small traffic of those bones and no standing to ask what they counted. The surgeon in him named the movement as precisely as he would have named a hand going past a turned note on a chart, and the child in him wanted the crust in the fold and could not reach it. Both were true, and the second was louder, and neither bought him anything he could lift a hand to.

\scenebreak

The buyer said a figure out of the side of his mouth, four marks the pair and not a half-mark more, and set it on the boards between them as if figures were eels to be counted by the pair. Mira's thumb went under her apron to the grey wool cord at her waist and stayed there while she decided. The boy knew the gesture the way he knew the cold arrive at cheek, then temple, then wrist: it came before the answer and lasted longer than the figure deserved. Her hand went to the record before her hand went to the fish. He laid that beside the other reachings on a tray of its own and made no cause of it, because three likenesses laid in a row had once taught him in a better-lit room what a man can do with an answer he wants too much.

“Four, then,” she said, brisk, because a bargain in her house was closed quickly, before anyone could make a speech about it. She took two eels from the basket and slapped them down on the board, and the thumb came out from under the apron with an eel-bone between finger and thumb, the deciding finished and nothing said. The buyer counted what lay on the board, found nothing worth a sentence in it, and went off with the pair, because a man who buys eels at the Fishstairs learns early where not to look. Nobody tapped a slate. The figure stood where the buyer had put it until the wind took it.

When Mira shifted the basket's weight to her other hip, the second cord showed twisted twice beneath the grey. It had never been carried upriver to \place{Nine Pots}. It had never been lifted at a door. The knots on neither cord were counted by any thumb but her own, and her thumb went always to the first. The boy saw the double turn of the twist and the way the lower cord sat under the upper one like a thing held down, and set the two cords on a board of his own, side by side and unjoined. He had no word in \lore{Grathic} for the second cord, and no hand to lift toward either knot even if he had found one. The grey cord and the one beneath it stayed where he put them, two sounds in a language spoken by one woman, and the shape of each was plainer to him than the name of either would have been.

Mira's board lay beside the board \person{Orra Weir} kept, and did not touch it. Orra was not there. The rope-hands and the cold fish and the tallow smoke had gone back upriver before the light, and the grey wool \lore{knot-string} hung where no clerk could spell it and no boy could read it. Only the board remained, and the shape of the last knot was legible to him without its name: a double turn, the tail tucked under, tied left over right, with the small extra weight of a thumb setting it. He had read the shape the week before and had no word to spend on it now. Orra had said the word once, low, into a basket that could not answer, and had not said it again; the boy kept the word and the shape on separate places and would not let them touch, because a shape was not a name and a name was not a thing a woman could hand to a child in a basket. Nothing was said of the child upriver, thin and returned, living in a house whose door Mira had walked to twice and never knocked on. No door was knocked this morning. The boy filed the not-knocking as an interval with the outline of a sound that had not come and gave it no cause.

Six hearings of \lore{the full note} stayed six --- five from four sources and one from a person, beside the five and never among them --- and no seventh came into the market with the eels. The nearly full answer that had come into the yard the day before lay on its own tray with the three intervals from Stone thirteen, Stone seventeen and Stone twenty-five, advanced and not paid; it did not join the hearings or the cords or the word. The two women's cords were records in wool that no Court's thumb would think to look at, and the Court's own figure had stood for a breath on the boards and gone off with the wind. He kept them all where they were: six and not seven, nearly and not full, the shape and not the name, the hand that reached for the record and not the hand that reached for the child. The count went on in him exactly as the morning went on around him and bought him nothing but the knowledge of where each thing lay.

\scenebreak

At the turn of the wind Sella came for him. She lifted the basket level and went up the road at a half-pace from every open slate, and behind her \person{Tarn}'s broom went over the trodden clay of \place{the Clay Yard}, going and coming, going and coming, without once turning along the road a man takes when a house wants a collector. Six years of the seven were counted and the seventh held back where he always held it; \lore{the Ash} was worked into the creases of his knuckles that no scrubbing lifted. He did not look at the basket and did not say what he had or had not heard. Past \place{the drying racks}, where wet pots stood in the thin light, Sella went around the space under the lowest rack where slate should have stood and did not go into it. She carried him under \place{the Vail kiln-house} roof, not into \place{the Debt Loft}, and set him on his bed and put the fold right without looking at his face. She gave the instruction to the fire and the wool. She said nothing about the figure the buyer had spoken, or about the second cord under the apron, or about where they had been.

\person{Garl Vail} was absent and kept by objects, which in this house is a way of keeping a man no Court can enter. His salt-stiff coat held a broader man's shape on the peg behind the door and the hollow seat kept its twenty-year lean and nobody filled it. The clean dark line where the Ash ridge had turned its corner was not swept, because a line left where it is is still a line kept, and his proverbs went to the pot and the fire and never to the basket. Behind the boy the carried fire lay under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees and missing one breath in every four. Four, four and a gap.

One crust lay in the fold and the empty place beside it was carried unchanged out of the morning, eaten by nobody, given to nobody, taken by nobody anyone would name. Nobody had said the smoky crust was gone or who had taken it, and no third crust had come. The boy set Sella's hand, \person{Alda Reed}'s hand and Mira's hand on three places on a tray weeks ago and would not let the tray become a cause; the tray had not become one all week and did not become one now. Mira's hand stood on the tray with the other two, and the hand that had gone to the cord when the figure was spoken went on standing there, kept, not entered. He was the only adult in this room and the least use in it, and he could not have lifted the crust from his own ear to his mouth if the doing of it would have saved the whole district a levy.

\scenebreak

Under the roof he lay with one crust in the fold and the empty place beside it, fifty-three days old. On the board of his own the grey cord lay beside the second cord twisted twice beneath it and the two did not join; beside that board lay the other woman's board, and the two boards did not touch. The knots were uncounted. The word she had said low into a basket was unspoken and had no shape in any tongue he could use, though the shape of the last knot was plain to him and had been plain for days. Six hearings stayed six, five from four sources and one from a person; the nearly full answer lay on its tray advanced and not paid; the word and the knot's shape and the weight of a thumb were apart; two cords and a knot-string stood side by side and did not touch. The fire behind him missed one breath in every four. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth and came down.
