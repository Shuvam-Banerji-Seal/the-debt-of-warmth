\daymark{49}{Ilmen 20, Y.H. 813}

The \place{Vail kiln-house} was still dark when \person{Sella Vail} lifted the basket off the bed and took the house down with her in the smell of wet wool and cold ash. She carried him the way a pot is carried when it is filled to the lip and the slip has not set: both hands under, elbows in, the little-door burn shut on the handle, her feet going where they had already decided to go and nothing slopping over. \person{Corin} rode level in a fold turned right under his chin, forty-nine days old and awake to every jar of the carrying. The instruction went to the wool. “Keep it off his ears,” she said. “It's the river wind.” She did not say the boy and she did not look down.

They went down \place{the Clay Yard} at the half-pace she kept from every open slate --- not the slow of a woman carrying and not the haste of one with a market to reach, but a pace measured off a thing she would not step between: a hand and the writing it was going to. Where a slate stood open propped at a doorway she went around it as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it over; if a woman had a slate under her arm and a finger already on its edge, Sella waited for the finger to finish before she passed. He kept the distance she kept. He had once known a man go past a chart with his hands in his pockets and his eyes on the door, and the distance had been the same distance, held for the same reason and given to a different god. She gave this one to the house and not to him, and there was no word for it in \lore{Grathic} and no cause written across it.

By the road the sky had come up the colour of skimmed milk over \place{the Ilm}, and the frost that had written on the well-lip the day before was water in the ruts with nothing in it a person could read. \lore{Account-day} had the yard by the throat already. Men went down before them with pigs on strings and slates under their arms, and the pig went where the string took it and the slate where the hand took it, and neither stopped for a basket. A draught came up off the water with silt in it and the thin smell of cold iron. The boy's drool froze at his mouth and nobody wiped it, because a basket carried is a pot carried and a pot's rim is not wiped in the street. His head went where it wanted and came back with the small wet sound, and cold arrived in the known order, cheek, then temple, then wrist. The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the fold and came down. Reducing shoulders had been ordinary work once; keeping a wool fold under his own chin was not, and the joke had not gone stale and had never been funny enough to say aloud. He was the only adult on this road and the least use in it.

At \place{the Cross} the market had settled itself around the block the way a pot settles into the hollow of a shelf: pigs at the weighing board, turnips in a heap with figures left standing on them side by side and never made to agree, eels and hoop-strips, the Court's table set at the \place{Slate Row} fringe with its top slate laid ready. \place{Sill} had come down in its thousands, or so it sounded, and \place{Low Kiln} had come with it and brought its cold in the folds of its coats. A pig went over the board and made a sound like a slate dragged over stone, and the weigher called a figure nobody wrote down and went on with the next pig.

Sella set the basket in the \place{moot-post}'s finger's width of shade out of the draught and turned the fold right at his chin without once looking at his face. The post was cold iron and his cheek took it first. She did not put her hand to \lore{the widow's mark} at her throat, because a reading is not an opening; the mark stayed where it lay under her collar while the market went on around it. “Keep the wind off him,” she told the wool, and to the basket she said nothing at all.

The printed notice hung from a nail in the post above his head, a square of paper soft at one corner, with a row of marks the wind kept turning over and back. He could see the row and could not read it. In a little while the row would be spoken aloud over the top of the basket, and he would lie inside the number it made and could not correct it, as a body lies under a chart nobody has read to it. A neighbour with a slate under her arm stopped a hand's breadth short of the basket and asked what house had sent it.

“The house,” Sella said, and gave the rest of the sentence to the wind: “It'll do till the pigs are through.” She did not explain the reading. The neighbour went off with her slate still under her arm and no more of an answer than she had come with.

\scenebreak

\person{Berr Clay} came to the block a little before the bell, with the red wool cord of his office folded under his coat instead of worn across it and the green of tasted glaze at one corner of his mouth. He smelled of hot clay and, under it, of nothing at all, which is the most expensive smell in a district that sells its warmth by the measure. His hands were large and clean. He wiped his thumb twice on his apron before he touched the slate, and after the second wipe he did not touch it with the thumb at all; it went back to his side and the slate stayed clean. The crowd leaned in because he spoke low. That was how the figure got heard --- not by shouting but by two hundred people bending their backs toward a man who would not raise his voice.

“One in twelve, as the notice says,” he said into the top of the leaning crowd. He let it stand a moment, then added the part that made it theirs: “If your household has heard otherwise, your household has heard wrong.” A finger went over the figure on the slate and came back, making sure it was still where he had put it. “The levy is called for the thaw. It is not called by me.”

The true subtraction went over the top of the basket and was spoken by nobody. It went the way a number goes in this district: a fist shut on a door-latch, a mouth that thought better of speaking, a shoulder lifted at a man who had done neither. It arrived nowhere. The boy lay under the notice with one crust and an empty place in the fold and the printed figure over him, and he could not spend the reading. The institution's most ordinary sentence had been said in a market voice and accepted, and the acceptance had gone around the pigs like a passing dish.

A neighbour who had a figure of his own in his mouth began to ask it. “If the ice goes soft before ---”

“A number goes into a slate, not into a well,” Berr said. “If your house wants it, there'll be a slate at the watch.” He said the house and the slate and never I, and took himself out of his own sentence as neatly as a hand out of a field before the dressing goes on. The neighbour shut his mouth on the rest of the question. The crowd went back to the pigs, which was what crowds do when a man has given them a sentence they can carry home without owning it.

The bell went four, four and a gap over the market, longer than any church bell in Sill, and nobody crossed himself. It was a Court instrument doing a temple's work with a Court's hand, and the weighing went on under it as if a bell were only a bell. The boy kept the told bell of the Long Night on its own tray beside the bell he was hearing now and did not let the two touch: one had been spoken in the dark by warm people telling a story, and one was iron in open air over a weighing board, and both kept the missing stroke in the same place. He wrote no cause across the likeness. He had learned in a better-lit room what likenesses will do to a man who lays three of them in a row.

\scenebreak

Six hearings of \lore{the full note} stayed six --- five from four sources and the sixth from a person --- and no seventh came into the market with the pigs. The five pauses stayed five objects on their own tray, unjoined; the three intervals from Stone 13, Stone 17 and Stone 25 stayed on another; and the nearly full answer of Ilmen 6 lay on its own still, advanced and not paid, neither a hearing nor the third thing. There was no word in the district's tongue for nearly and none for the flat answer, and he had no hand to tap a slate and ask it to say the thing again. The neat dark hand had not shown itself a sixth time in all the morning, and the collector at the drawing with his ordinary hand and no pause in it stayed in the place the boy had put him, neither joined to the five nor called as a sixth.

Word came up from the north bridge with a neighbour who had been down for eels: the clerk was still set at \place{the Fishstairs} with his cuff grey from washing and his slate held to his forearm, and nobody had taken him off. It was the eleventh morning. The boy had not been carried past the steps this day and had not seen the man, but he knew the order of the thing as surely as if he had: the hand went to the slate before the eyes went to the stalls, and the slate was for a measure nobody had called. He filed the order on a tray of its own, with Berr's twice-wiped thumb beside it and the hand at the well that had gone to a slate before a child, and did not make one hand of the three. He filed the absence of a draw for \person{Dessa Silt}, eleven mornings gone and uncollected, as an interval with the outline of a sound that did not come. The clerk did not speak. He was not the woman at the flare light and not the soft-cuffed man at the Court's table; the boy had filed both elsewhere and did not bring them here. There was no sixth sighting to add and no name to give the man, and he was left a fact on his own tray for a measure nobody called.

Berr read the figure a second time for the households that came late, and the boy could not spend either reading. He had lain inside it twice and it had bought him nothing he could lift a hand to: no word, no slate, no warmth entered in any account that would own him. At home the arithmetic of the house would be waiting in loaves moved along a shelf and a glance at the clay jar on the wall shelf, with a space where a loaf should stand and no figure spoken; he would keep the count exactly beside it, one crust and an empty place in the fold. \person{Tarn} at the yard and \person{Jorra Vail} at the wheel had silences of their own to keep under one roof and were not here to hear a number read over a basket, and the boy did not need them to. A loan that can hear is a loan that can be called, but there was nobody in the market to call either of them and no sentence said under any roof about it.

At midday Sella lifted the basket off the post. She did not look into it and said nothing about where they had been or what had been read over the top of him; the instruction went to the wool again, and the house got the sentence another woman would have spoken to the boy. The pigs were still at the board behind them and the bell was still doing the temple's work in the open air, four, four and a gap, dying by degrees as the yard took them back. Up the Clay Yard the ruts had dried to the colour of old bone and the draught had the silt out of it now. The fold was at his chin and one crust lay in it with an empty place beside it, carried through another day without a name; no third had come and nobody had said the one that went was gone. The figure he could not spend went up the yard inside him, and the house's arithmetic waited at the end of it in loaves and glances with no number spoken. Nothing had been joined or paid or explained. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth and came down.
