\daymark{118}{Seed 29, Ninthday, Y.H. 813}

The well had been muddied by the going out of the hands. All morning the buckets had come up brown and been set on the stone lip to settle before anybody used them, and the rope wore a skin of mud that squeezed out under \person{Sella Vail}'s little-door burn when she drew, leaving the print of two straight sides and a lintel in the wet wood. She gave the water its instruction before she gave it to anybody: stand, settle, be clear, you have come a long way up out of the dark and you will be finer in a moment. She said it twice, the way a woman tells a pot a thing and then tells it again because the pot has not moved. \person[Corin]{the boy} sat on the flat stone at the outer edge of \place{the yard well}, where the buckets did not swing and no slate lay, the basket held level on her hip and the wool put right at his ear without a look at his face. The instruction went to the wool and to the running water and never to the basket: keep it off his ears, it is the river wind; keep it dry, it is running water. To a woman who asked whose boy it was she said \emph{the house}, and the answer went where answers given to a house go and did not come back.

The hands were out of the strips now and the yard had gone back to its buckets, and the one place in the returning line was still gone around with a look and no figure, as it had been since the going of the light on Seed 27. Mud under the fingernail. Water on the stone lip going brown and then clearer and then not quite clear. The boy took them in the order he had taken everything in for a hundred and eighteen days: the sound first, the sense of it after, and the thing itself never. Cold arrived in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist. The wool had ridden back from the bare joint in the night and the wind under the boards found the place it had come for. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and nobody put it right, because the instruction had gone to the wool and not to the wandering. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody under this roof had been given an instruction about the thread. He had once clarified a sample by letting the grit fall out of it before he read it, and here he could not keep a fold over two inches of his own chest. He wanted the stiff crust two inches from his ear and the breast and the bucket's lip and the number of his days said by somebody who would have known what a number of days counted when they said it. He got none of them and could ask for none of them. Both facts were true, and the second was the one that was loud.

A woman came up from the strips with the worked brown ground on her skirt and chaff worked under the nail of one thumb. She had been bending since first grey and her back kept the shape of the bending even when she stood straight, and she held the emptiness of her fist in front of her as though it were a thing carried carefully. She had the look of a woman who had spent the morning deciding what to ask and had decided at last to ask the ground, because the ground did not answer and could not be blamed for it. She looked at the bucket on the lip, and at Sella, and at the boy, and went back to the bucket, which was the order people used when they wanted an answer to come out of a thing and not out of a person.

“What's the planting cost, now the hands are out of it?” she said.

Sella did not answer the woman. Her eyes went for a moment toward the kiln-house behind her, where the clay jar stood cold under its glaze on the wall shelf, and she moved her lips a little as though counting something that had not been given her to count, and then gave the answer to the pot that sat by the well with its mouth open.

“Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there,” she said. “The house has said it.”

The woman took the answer as a woman takes a pot handed to her, by the rim with the other hand ready for the weight, and carried it off up the yard without looking into it. It went with her the way a figure goes from a fist to a mouth to a shoulder and arrives nowhere. The house had said it, and no mouth had said it, and no slate in the district carried it, and still it was handed over as change. Nobody under this roof had named a figure for the planting when the hands went in and nobody named one now that they had come out of it. The question had gone to a pot and the pot had given back mud, and the woman went off with an unanswered thing held level in her hands, which in Low Kiln was the same as an answer and the same as carrying water in a sieve. The boy set the exchange on a place of its own. A cost with no figure was still a cost, and everywhere in \place{Low Kiln} the cost had a payer whose name nobody entered. He knew the shape of that perfectly and could not spend a word of it.

The woman went up the clay road with her pot and her question and the chaff still under her nail, and Sella watched her go the way one watches a pot carried badly, not to correct the carrying but to note where the spill would be. Then she lifted the boy, the little-door burn closing first on nothing and then on his weight, and carried him up the yard at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, going around the space beneath \place[the drying racks]{the lowest rack} as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and came under \place{the Vail kiln-house} roof.

\scenebreak

The light came in through the finger's-width gap at the door and lay on the floor in a strip that moved a little when the wind moved. \place{the loaf shelf} stood in the next room on boards that had kept the house's days badly for a hundred and eighteen days: loaves pushed along, a glance at the clay jar, a space at the row's end nobody called a number, and a newer palm's breadth beside it nobody called anything at all. Sella set the basket on the flat board at the shelf's outer end, where no slate lay and no passing hip would knock it, and gave the instruction to the pot and the broom and the fire and the wool and never to the basket --- keep it off his ears, it is the river wind; don't set that near the door, you'll pay for it in the thaw; round it, go around, it cost enough to come in and it'll cost more to take out. She moved her lips along the loaf row without a figure, the way a woman reads a row of loaves by their edges and not by their number, and stood a half-pace from every open slate between the shelf and the door.

A second woman came in with a pot on her hip. She had come up from the strips or from the rack end, and there was chaff in the creases of her fist and a fine grey dust at one wrist that was not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel. She went to the shelf the way everybody in this district went to a record, hand first and eyes after. Her hand went past the palm's breadth of bare board that had come in the night of Seed 6, the fingers opening for the half-beat it takes to find what is not there, opening a little wider than the loaf required, and closed on the hard dry heel of the loaf beyond. Only then did her eyes find the boy.

The half-beat stood in the room after her hand had gone on. The boy had kept that half-beat on an object of its own for a hundred and eighteen days: the space where a thing should have been and was not, exact, complete and worth nothing, a chart closed with a neat line over a note nobody had opened. He read her hand the way he had once read the flat note of a cracked vessel under a knuckle, small and exact and the one thing in the room he could not put a finger into. His own hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the shelf and came down. There was nothing within two paces that it could close on.

“This end place came in with the planting,” the woman said, her hand going on with the loaf and her mouth already opening on the next part of the same breath, “and that there by his ear's a child's appetite. A growing child. You'll be wanting to feed him.”

She said it all in one breath, the old gap at the row's end and the empty place at his ear laid under one cause before she had looked at either, and went off with her pot and her loaf without once seeing that she had done it. Two bare boards stood where she had left them. The old space and the newer palm's breadth were still two absences in a room that did not touch the fold where the boy lay, and she had gone out through the gap of the door carrying them together as though they had always been one thing. That was the whole of how a cause got made in this district: a reach, a half-beat, a mouth already open on the second part before the first had been looked at, and a woman warm in her own kitchen with nothing to own.

The boy set her saying on a place of its own. On Seed 24 another mouth had laid the row's old gap and the empty place at his ear under the same cause in a single breath, and before that, on Seed 12 and Seed 18 and Seed 21, other mouths had named one bare board or the other and gone off satisfied, and on Seed 19 a small foundling's head had turned a half-beat late toward a pot and stopped in the middle of the turn. He set the new saying beside the Seed 24 saying and the earlier tellings and the watcher's late turn, each on its own place, two tellings on two places and the rest lying where they had always lain. He did not make her joining his own. He did not let a fourth likeness grow out of the three, and he did not let a third gap come, and he laid no finger across any of the places. A likeness laid on a likeness grows a cause as surely as silt gathers behind a weir if no hand holds them apart, and a line drawn through the places would be a cause with a clean hand at the foot of it, and the clean hand was the one object he had spent two lives declining to become. The account stood advanced and not paid, toward a far-off day when it might want a name. Two mouths had now made the same joining without either of them seeing that it was a joining; the hearing of it was not the making of it, and the making of it was not in his hands and never had been. His exactness was the only arithmetic in the district, and the more exact it grew the less of it anybody could take up.

Sella moved her lips along the row standing with two gaps and gave nobody a figure. She glanced once at the clay jar cold under its glaze on the wall shelf and went on with the loaves. She did not touch \lore{the widow's mark} at her throat, because a shelf is not an opening and a loaf row is not a door and no opening had come under this roof for a hundred and eighteen days. Her hand, when it had nothing to do, counted the loaves by their edges and came back to the same place twice, keeping the shape of the days as a shape no collector could take up as a number. The grey streak at her temple showed pale in the strip of light at the door, and she pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carried the little-door burn, the burn closing on nothing as the hand came away. She did not look toward the bed, and the fold lay where she had put it with the thread at his mouth frozen and unwiped, because nobody under this roof had been given an instruction about the thread. The carried fire answered four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, warming the boards by degrees under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, its payer unnamed for a hundred and eighteen days. Every warmth in this house had a payer somewhere, and no slate in the district carried the name.

The woman went out with her pot and her loaf and the answer she had carried off unanswered, and the two bare boards stood apart in her wake as they had stood before she came in. Neither was entered. Neither was named. The old gap at the row's end and the newer palm's breadth kept their two rooms in a house that did not touch the fold where the boy lay with one stiff crust and its own empty place. The fire's four, four and a gap came up through the floor and the river's note of water over water came through the finger's-width door, and the two counts in two places did not meet, and the three counts did not become one. His small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note and came down. One hundred and eighteen stood where one hundred and seventeen had stood in the small hours, exact, unspent and spoken by nobody who would have known what a number of days counted when they said it. Nothing was joined, named, paid or ended; the place in the returning line went on being gone around with a look and no figure, and the two bare boards went on being two.
