\daymark{117}{Seed 28, Eighthday, Y.H. 813}

Before first grey the men came up \place{the Clay Yard} with their collars high, and \person[Corin]{the boy} was carried out to the edge of the light at \place{the Vail wheel-shed} and set down where no slate lay, the way a woman sets a pot at the edge of a table and not in its middle. They went in low-voiced to \place[the back room]{the room off the wheel-shed}, which stood a finger's width open and did not shut, and the boy did not go in and no name was taken down. \person{Jorra Vail} came out through that finger's width of door ahead of them and gave the house no name for them and did not say the room was cold. He had learned to carry an undeclared thing the way a potter carries a cracked vessel: level, and with the hand under it. The low voices went on behind him and came to nothing in the yard, and the yard went on without them, the way a floor goes on under a spilled bucket that somebody will wipe on the morrow. Collars held high are a way of giving a face to nobody, and a face given to nobody is a face no slate can take; the boy had known men who did this in a better-lit room, and he knew the shape of it here without needing the words for it in \lore{Grathic}.

\person{Sella Vail} had given the instruction to the wool before she gave it to anybody: keep it off his ears, it is the river wind. The wind came under the eaves off \place{the Ilm} with the sour edge of mud in it, went under the fold at his ear and found the place it had come for. Cold arrived in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist. The wool had ridden back from the bare joint in the night and the cold found that place too. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and nobody put it right, because the instruction had gone to the wool and not to the wandering. He counted the boots going past the basket without meaning to, the way he had once counted a pulse before putting a hand into a wound; four pairs, and a pause, and another. The count arrived nowhere, because there was nowhere in this district to put a count of men. The men did not look at the basket. A thing that does not look at a basket has already decided what a basket is worth, and the boy had spent a hundred and seventeen days being worth what the district decided, which was nothing that could be entered.

Jorra came through with fine grey slate dust ground small as meal into both forearms past the bent little finger --- not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel, which dried to the colour of old bone and stayed on the outside of a man. This had been worked inward, into the creases, the way grit works into a knee or a splinter into the flesh beside a nail. The boy kept the dusty forearms and the clean ones of the other mornings on one tray, two states of one man's morning, and asked neither which slate had been broken nor whether it was a birth-slate. Those were the boy's two questions and he could not ask either, so they stayed where questions are allowed to last for years, which in this house was anywhere and forever. The broken birth-slates lay under the lowest board of \place[the drying racks]{the racks}, gone around as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, entered by nobody, and the space above them went around the basket as the basket went around the space. A district can go around a thing for three years and call the going-around care; the boy had learned to go around it too, and to call nothing at all.

He spoke to the wheel before he spoke to anyone. “It doesn't care whose hands,” he said to it, in the flat voice he kept for the tools and the pots and never for the basket. “It cares if they're steady.” The wheel gave back its low note, the note of a thing that has turned long enough not to mind whose hands turn it. Garl's seat kept its twenty-year lean to the left and Jorra put his weight into it the way a man puts weight into a hollow he has never managed to fill. To a pot coming up under his hands he said “You'll do,” in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either, and he knew \lore{the full note} of a Vail pot before it left the wheel --- knew it the way a man knows a vessel by the feel of it under his hand before he puts iron to it --- and would not say so before a kiln-master come up from \place{High Kiln}, who would call it early and make something of it. A man who knows a thing early and says it early spends the knowing; a man who keeps it keeps the one piece of the house nobody can take off him. The boy knew that he knew and kept it on a place of its own and did not lay it beside the dust, because a likeness laid on a likeness grows a cause unless a hand holds them apart.

The kiln-master went along the row of finished pots with his hands behind his back and said nothing that a slate could carry away, and Jorra let him, and turned the wheel a half-revolution and said to the pot under his breath that it would do. Under the shed roof the air held damp clay and tallow and the sweetish edge of lead glaze carried up from High Kiln on a bad wind, and under that, faintly, the sour mineral tang that came up out of the corner of the house where \lore{Ash} had lain two years and would lie until somebody burned it and called it dealt with. The boy lay at the edge of the light with the small wet sound coming and going at his mouth and wanted the pot's note, and wanted the breast, and wanted the number of his days said aloud by somebody who would have known what a number of days counted when they said it. He got the note. The other two he had learned not to expect, which is not the same thing as stopping wanting them.

Sella had not finished telling Jorra the fire was low. “It's low,” she said, and the rest of the instruction was still in her mouth --- the part about feeding it before anybody fed themselves, Garl's proverb that had gone to a pot with no crack long ago and cost the more now that there was nobody to say it in his own voice. Jorra's bent finger went into the cold iron of \lore{the damper} ahead of her sentence. The iron took the warmth out of the fingertip and kept it, giving a little back as it warmed; it is the nature of iron to take first and return what it has no use for. He moved the tongue of iron a hair's breadth and left it standing there. The end of her instruction arrived at the pot instead of at him, and the household straightened around his silence the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted: Sella's hand finished its half-circle with the broken pot, Tarn's broom found its line, the wheel went on turning, and the low voices in the room off the shed went lower. Nobody had ordered the straightening and nobody had observed it. Obedience that arrives ahead of the instruction is obedience and a correction at once, and both of them went past the basket without a word.

The boy read the iron once more as he had read it every time the finger had gone into it, and kept the reading longer this time because the thing had been done before he was told it was being done. It was the bite that holds a vessel whole and it was the bite that can take the hand off a man. One piece of iron, two mercies, and no word in Grathic that held both. He had learned in a better-lit room what a clamp does when it is left too long on a limb, and what happens when it is taken off too soon, and here the two were the same object with the same name, and nobody under the roof would have known what he was looking at. He filed the reading as an object worth nothing, joined to no kiln, and set it beside the other object on the same tray without drawing a line between them. The two tellings of the shut counting room lay on their own tray and had not been brought across; nothing under this roof was going to bring them across today either. The truest thing in the room was the least spendable thing in it, and it lay where no Court thumb would ever read it.

Tarn took the broom only after Sella had given it its instruction. She gave it to the broom first --- go around, it cost enough to come in, it will cost more to take out --- and only then let the broom be taken, and \person{Tarn} took it up as a man takes up a tool already named, saying “Yes, house” to an early thaw and a late one in the same breath, agreeing so entirely that an argument had nowhere to put its foot, keeping the late because a late thaw puts off the paying though the paying had stood in the ruts since the water went. His seventh year was pressed to the very edge of the sill and still held. The small bench knife lay at the edge with six years standing in a mark his thumb had worn almost flat and the seventh nearly gone, the one entry in this house the house could not check, and to the kindling he said “Six counted. The next I don't count.” The unforged collector sentence stayed where he kept the seventh year, and neither was entered. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line and no scrubbing lifted it; the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back.

His chest had been tight since the dark and the breath he had decided not to spend on Seed 16 was still unspent. There was a small interval at the top of each reach where the breath should have gone in: the broom went up, the interval opened, the breath did not come, the broom came down. He had been sent once for a collector in the Long Cold and come back at the frozen ford and told the house it had been done, and he kept that sentence where he kept the year and never looked along the road that went down toward the Fishstairs. A loan that can hear is a loan that can be called, and the sentence that would call it had not been said under this roof in six years and would not be said today.

On Seed 22 his stroke had checked half a beat behind Jorra's standing finger and then gone on. Today it did not check. The bristles went on over the flagstones without the half-beat, past the place where the stroke had stopped that day, and the stones kept the place as a thing nobody would enter --- a shallow bright patch where a broom had stood still a moment longer than a broom needs to stand still, worn into the stone the way a small omission is worn into a life, kept without being named. Neither man named the other's silence. Jorra did not turn from the damper; Tarn did not turn toward it; the dust on the forearms and the Ash in the knuckles went on being carried past each other in one room without either man's eyes going across. The boy set the unchecking stroke beside the earlier half-beat on the interval tray, two objects and not a rule, and laid no cause across them and no finger across the place on the stones.

Jorra did not look toward the bed or into the basket. \emph{He's not Vail} stayed sealed as tightly as the hour it had been spoken three years ago, and the promise of the foundling's name to the men who burn slates was neither given nor withdrawn; it went past the basket with the low voices and came to nothing in the yard. Two silences stood in one room with no hand joining them, the wheel-keeper's and the debt's, and the equality under the roof held at the place it had moved to. Outside, the men went down the yard with their collars high and no name entered anywhere, and the kiln-master went up toward High Kiln with his hands behind his back and nothing said about the pot that would do. The wheel kept its low note and the low note was not a hearing and did not join the six hearings that stood beside and never among.

Jorra's bent finger stood in the cold damper ahead of Sella's unfinished sentence, the iron taking what it takes and giving back what it has no use for, while Tarn's bristles went on over the flagstones without the check and the stones still kept the place where the stroke had stopped, and neither man named the other's silence. The boy's small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the wheel's low note and came down. One hundred and seventeen stood where one hundred and sixteen had stood in the small hours, exact, unspent and spoken by nobody in the district, while the fire waited for the word that had gone to the pot, and the count in one place and the count under the floor in another did not meet.
