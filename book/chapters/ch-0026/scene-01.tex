\daymark{116}{Seed 27, Y.H. 813}

\dropcap{T}{he going} of the light came down over \place{the worked strips} east of \place{Low Kiln} the way chaff comes down in a still room, slowly, and settling on whatever has been left standing. The seed stood up in the rows and the turned brown ground kept the shape of every back that had bent over it since Seed 15, the heel-marks along the drills, the little dark pits where a fist had let seed go, the pale dust of the broken clods. The last hands were coming out of the strips now, shaking the dirt from their fists the way a man shakes water from a rag and gets most of it off, and the yard took them in the way it had taken everything this month: in glances and handfuls and the space where a man's arm should have been. No figure went with them. A woman at the head of the second strip lifted two fingers with chaff worked deep into the creases and held them up a moment for the woman beside her to see, and did not say what they stood for, and shut them again. A man with a barrow came up behind her with the wooden rim of it against his hip, opened his mouth on the number of stooped backs the row had held, shaped the word with his teeth, and thought better of it. He shut his mouth the way the woman had shut her fist. The barrow went on up the yard with nothing in it that a slate could have read.

“Seed's in,” the woman with the chaff said. She said it to the ground, because the ground was what had been done to and the ground was what would answer in the end. “The rest is weather.” She rubbed the two fingers together and the chaff came away in little dry flakes and went down onto the turned earth, and she closed the fingers into a fist again and went to the next hole as though nothing had been held in them at all. Her sleeve was rolled above the elbow and the skin there was the colour of the dry slip in the yard, with a pale line across the wrist where a rope had once been and a scab the size of a pea on the knuckle. The woman beside her looked at the fist, looked at the row, and went on with her own holes. Nobody asked what the two fingers had been. In this district a thing was counted when it was in the pot and not before, and a woman holding up two fingers in a field was a woman counting nothing any clerk would want, and the chaff went on falling out of her crease into the brown ground as she worked.

The man with the barrow stopped where the strip broke into the yard and set it down on its legs. The rim had been rubbed smooth by hands that had pushed it through four plantings and the wood had gone the colour of old bone where the hips went; a crack ran along the under-edge and had been stuffed once with clay and gone dry again. He stood a moment with his mouth still half open on the number, looking along the row as though the row might supply the word if he waited long enough. It did not. The seed stood up in its small green and gave back nothing that would have served. He shut his mouth, spat once into the dirt for the having of it, and took the barrow up again by the handles. A girl at the rack end called to somebody about a pot and got no answer, and he did not turn, because the question had been given to the pot and not to him, which was the way questions were given in this yard. The barrow's legs lifted out of the soft ground with a small sucking sound and he went off up the yard with his back to the strips.

That was the whole of the counting: two fingers, a half-shaped word, a glance along a row. The district had kept its figure for the planting the way a woman keeps a coal under ash, by covering it and going on with the day. It had wanted hands in the month of Seed and hands had gone in, and the strips had been worked and the seed was up, and the figure for what it had cost had gone from the strip to a man's teeth and back again and arrived nowhere. Weather would have the last word, the yard said, and the weather had not spoken yet, and a body got used to going on without the word because there was nothing else to do. \person{Sella Vail} had carried \person[Corin]{the boy} to the very edge of the work and stood a half-pace from every open slate, the basket held level the way a woman carries a bowl filled to the lip and will not let it slop. She did not look into the basket. Beyond the fold the rows went on with their small green under the going light, and the river wind came off \place{the Ilm} with the sour edge of mud in it, and she gave the instruction to the wool and to the wind and never to the basket.

The hands came out of the last strip and the line of them shortened toward the yard gate, and there was one place in the line that the yard went around. It went around it the way it went around other places: the clean dark line where \lore{Ash} had turned under the corner of \place{the Vail kiln-house}, the space beneath \place[the drying racks]{the lowest rack} that a pail of hot water might have stood under if anybody had set a pail of hot water there. A woman with a pot came up the strip's edge and stepped wide of the space without looking at what she was stepping wide of, and a man following her stepped wide of it too because the woman had, and a girl with a broom went around it with the bristles lifted and set them down again beyond it. Nobody said what the space was. Nobody called it an accident or sent for anybody or put a figure against it. One of the hands that had gone in on Seed 15 had not come out with the rest. Nobody said so. It had been a place in the line in the morning and it was a place in the line at dusk and the going of the light found it still gone around, exact and unentered, held the way a coal is held under ash.

“Mind there,” the woman with the pot said to the girl with the broom, and the girl minded, stepping the wider way and setting her bristles down beyond it. The words were ordinary words about a place in the ground and did not say which place or why, and the woman went off with her pot under her arm and did not look back at what she had gone around. A man come up from the bends stopped with his foot already set toward the space, took it back and set it down on the other side of it, and went on, and the two men beside him went on too without asking him why he had changed his foot. If a hand were lifted out of the ground in a month or a year, a slate would be found for it and a figure would be made to stand at the foot of it; if no hand came up, the space would go on being gone around and the month would go on standing short without a figure. The yard kept the place the way it kept the clean dark line and the space under the rack, by going wide and entering nothing and calling it nothing.

The boy read the going-around the way he had read the shut fist at the well three days before: a chart closed with a neat line drawn over a note nobody had opened. Exact, complete, worth nothing. He had once stood at the foot of a bed and drawn such a line over a man already beyond the room and gone on to the next, and the neatness of the line had been the whole of the harm; here the line was drawn by feet stepping wide and the note beneath it was left where it lay. He set the field's empty place on a place of its own. He did not lay it beside the shelf's two gaps in the next room or the one stiff crust in the fold or the empty place at his ear, because a likeness laid on a likeness in this district grew a cause as surely as silt gathers behind a weir, and a fourth would have made the cause with a clean hand at the foot of it. The clean hand was the one object in this world he had spent two lives declining to become. He kept his own hand out of the field.

“Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. “It's the river wind.” She said it twice, the way a woman tells a pot a thing and then tells it again because the pot has not moved. To the wind she gave the rest of it, and to the basket she gave nothing at all. When the woman with the pot asked whose boy it was, Sella said “The house's,” and went on with the fold, so that the answer stood where an answer given to a pot would stand and went no further. Her little-door burn shut on nothing as her hand passed the basket's rim and opened on the handle when she took the weight again. The grey streak at her temple showed pale in the dying light off the river, a single pale thread in a head of coal-black that she said had come in the winter Garl died and would not discuss further. She kept her half-pace from every open slate and did not look into the basket toward the strips.

Cold arrived in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist. The wool rode back from the bare joint so the cold found the place it had come for, and the river wind went under the fold at his ear and found the other place. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and nobody put it right, because the instruction had gone to the wool and not to the wandering. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours under the roof and had not been wiped, because nobody under that roof had been given an instruction about the thread and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. He wanted the stiff brown-edged crust two inches from his ear in the fold, and the breast, and the bucket's lip two paces off where a bucket stood with a cold iron handle on it, and the number of his days said aloud by somebody who would have known what a number of days counted when they said it. He got none of them and could ask for none of them. He was a man who had once held pressure in a cavity with both hands while a student counted the pulse out loud, and he could not close two inches on a fold that smelled of wet wool and nothing else. Both facts were true, and the second was the one that was loud here, as it had been every day since the stone at his belly went cold.

He watched the order of the yard as he had watched it in a better-lit room: the record before the person, the instruction before the hand, the going-around before the look. The woman's two fingers had gone up before her face had; the man's mouth had shaped the figure before his eyes had gone to the barrow; the girl's broom had gone around the space before anybody had said there was a space to go around. It was the same order he had kept on the other side of his life, where a chart was laid open before a family was told and a name was written before a body was carried out. Here nobody was being carried out. The order went on all the same, exact and worthless, the way instruments laid out on a towel are exact and worthless until a hand takes one up, and no hand in Low Kiln was going to take this one up today.

His small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the worked strips and came down. One hundred and sixteen stood where one hundred and fifteen had stood under the roof in the small hours, exact, unspent and spoken by nobody in the district, while the seed stood up in the last tend and the line came out of it with one place gone around. He had long ago stopped expecting to be told and had not stopped wanting it, and the two went on together the way a coal goes on under blind ash, giving back nothing a man could see and keeping its heat all the same.

The yard turned away from the strips into the dusk. The man with the barrow went off up the road with his mouth shut and the rubbed rim against his hip; the woman with the chaff went home with the two fingers closed in a fist nobody would ask about; the girl's broom went around the space and left it where it was. Sella turned the basket back toward the kiln-house with the level held and the fold at his ear, going around the space as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and did not look into the basket toward the strips. Behind them the empty place in the line stood where the yard had left it, gone around and entered by nobody, and the going light went off the worked ground and left the rows standing on their own small green. Nothing was named, nothing was counted, nothing was paid, and the place went on being kept.
