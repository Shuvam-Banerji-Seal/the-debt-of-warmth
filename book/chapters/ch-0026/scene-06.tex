Dawn came up \place{the Ilm} without taking the cold off the water. Under \place{the north bridge} \place{the Fishstairs} kept the dark a moment longer than the town above it, and the outer boards of the shade held a wet shine that was neither sun nor frost but the two things standing in one another's place. \person{Sella Vail} carried \person[Corin]{the boy} down and set him at the very end of the shade where no slate had ever been laid and where the running water went past close enough to be heard but not close enough to reach a basket. The instruction went to the wool. “Keep it off his ears. It's the river wind.” It went into the wool and into the running water and never into the basket, and she stood her half-pace from every open slate with the little-door burn closing first on the handle and then on nothing. Cold came off the water in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, the wool riding back from the bare joint so the cold found the place it had come for. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and nobody under the bridge had been given an instruction about that sound. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had wiped it. He was a man who could have told you the temperature of a body by the feel of the back of his hand on its brow, and he could not close two inches on the rim of the basket that held him. Both things were true. The second was the one that was loud.

\person{Mira Silt} worked the eels at the outer boards with pale slime dried in long grey strokes on her sleeves and silt dried pale in the hollow of her neck, her head held a little to the right where the top of her left ear had been, her small smile kept on her face the way a door is kept on the latch. She had a short knife in one hand and a string of eel-bones in the pocket of her apron, and she moved the bones against her thumb while she decided things, a small dry clicking that went on under the talk the way a second pulse goes on under a fever. The boy took the order of it first, as he took the order of everything from the height of a basket: the hand to the bones, the hand to the knife, the thumb to the cord afterward when a figure came near. The sense of it arrived after, and the thing itself never. From where he lay the eel boards stood higher than a man's table and the whole trade went on over the top of him, and nobody in it had any reason to turn and look down at what lay at the end of the shade.

A woman come down from the weir stood at the boards with salt dried white at her wrists. She had the look of a woman who had been up since the dark and had come to ask a question she had already decided the answer to, so that she could carry the answer away and be blamed for nothing.

“Will the run be thin, now the hands are out of the strips?” she said.

Mira did not stop working. She answered the question and then answered it herself, which left the woman nothing in the sentence that wanted a reply.

“Thin?” she said. “Who's to say thin? It comes when it comes. Ask the river.”

The eel-bones moved against her thumb, three of them going from hand to hand, and one went back into the pocket when the deciding was done. She said something low about a boat that had come up on the flood and did not say a word about the hands that had gone into \place[the worked strips]{the strips} and come out of them one short. The woman took the answer away as she had meant to, holding it level, and went back up the steps. The boy set the low saying on a place of its own. A direction with no figure at the foot of it was still a direction, and a woman who would not say a number in the bright part of the market said something else in the dark part under the bridge, and the two sayings stood edge to edge and did not touch. He had spent a hundred and eighteen days learning how near two things could lie without one of them becoming the other's cause. It was the only craft he had left, and it had no use here.

A buyer in a coat with the river in its hem named a figure for two pair. Mira's thumb went under her apron to the grey wool cord and stayed there longer than the figure required, longer by several breaths than a price asked for eels had any call to hold a thumb. When she shifted a creel to set a pair down, the second cord showed twisted twice beneath the grey. It had never been carried upriver to \place{Nine Pots} and had never been lifted at a door. Neither knot was counted by any thumb but her own, and the knot for the thin child upriver was not touched. The boy had the word forty on a tray of its own with no voice to spend on it, and the readable shape of the double turn lay beside it, and the two things sat side by side without his laying a finger across. A cord kept out of love gathers the same interest as any other debt, and this one went on gathering it on a board no Court thumb had ever looked at.

Her board lay beside \person{Orra Weir}'s knot-string and did not touch it. Orra was upriver and present only as the board, the cord lying where a cord lies when a woman has gone away from it and left it where it was; the boy kept the two boards from touching as carefully as Sella kept her half-pace from an open slate. Nobody lifted either cord. Nobody counted either knot. Nobody under the bridge said what either board was for.

Mira brought no crust. She did not look into the basket. Her not-looking was a keeping set on a place of its own and joined to no pricing, and the man who had priced the district in the strips had looked away from the same basket for a different reason, and there was no word in \lore{Grathic} for the difference between the two faces turned the same way. Two faces turned the same way meant opposite things, and the boy kept them on two places and wrote no cause across the space. Sella lifted him when the light came up over the rail, the little-door burn closing on the handle and opening on nothing, and carried him out of the shade at the half-pace without once looking in.

Midday found the same care three doors down. Sella set the basket just inside the finger's-width-open door of \place{House Silt}, at the edge of the light and away from every place a slate might lie, and gave the instruction to the wool and never to the basket. She opened no slate. The errand that had carried her three doors stayed an errand to a room and did not turn into a walk to \place{Slate Row}, though most of what this room held would have read better there. She stood a half-pace off with her burn shut on nothing. In the back room a neighbour scrubbed at a board with lye water and did not look up at the writing, the tin or the fold pressed too long; a low song came up through the floor with its words flattened out of sense by work. Above the abandoned board-bed the round pewter tin kept its pale fold at the seam and its thumb-bright rub on the lid, unopened.

\person{Alda Reed} came short, as a woman comes who has been called out of a room where somebody else has just been fed: soap-and-milk gone cold under river silt, lye sour under the soap, her nails scrubbed to the quick with clay still under them. She had her birth-slates under her arm and a narrow slate of her own in the fold of her grey shawl. She crossed to the bench and laid the writing-down flat before she turned toward the fold. It was the order of a hand that reaches for the record before the person, and it did not vary; he had watched it from a basket at a market's edge and from a bed and had never once seen her reverse it. He had watched a senior man lay his hand on a chart before he looked at the patient under it, and learned there what the order meant: the record was the thing that would still be there when the person was carried out. There was no word in Grathic for that order and he had no voice to spend on it, and she performed it again now in a room where the person was not going to be carried out at all.

She pressed the wool flat at his shoulder a breath longer than a fold requires. The red edge of \lore{the birth-flower} showed where the wool had ridden back at his wrist, a branch of it running from the third rib down the forearm and stopping short of the hand, and she crossed two fingers at the corner of it before she knew she had done it, the sign made in front of a basket and covered before it could be read. She covered it with the date she had given the woman: \emph{Stone seven. The account went up on the eighth, before the paper went out.} It stood now over a woman more mornings dead than any living person keeps count of, uncollected, and no living person could take it off the sign.

“Warm and breathing,” Alda said. “That's all I've set down.”

It was exact. It was not true. He had no way to tell her so and no standing if he had, and she did not wait for an answer; she went on with dates after the question had stopped, because dates are a handle on a thing too large to carry: a cooper's seventh in \place{Mud End}, a potter's third child in \place{Stonemouth}, the last high water, the exact day of a birth in a house nobody in this room would ever enter. The dates came faster than dates had any business coming, and the room arranged itself around them the way a market arranges itself around a reading it has not understood.

Her hand went once into her bodice to the little tin on its cord and came away without opening it. The grey line of the cord showed for a moment at the edge of her shawl, a small weight at the end of it, and was gone. On her own narrow slate she wrote what she did not leave lying about, in the second hand no Court physician had ever seen, and turned it writing-down into her shawl toward a floorboard in a house the boy could not name: six pages in that hand, all unread, the sixth written weeks ago and no seventh written today, and the account not paid again. Below the pewter tin the fold of grey wool held only the hollow where the small pale river stone had once lain, and nobody in the house said either thing was gone.

Two private records in two materials stood apart in one room and were carried to no door: the tin above the bed with its pale fold and thumb-bright rub, and the turned slate going toward the floorboard. Mira's cord and her second twist lay on another board in another part of the town, and Orra's knots lay beside it without touching, and the three records stood in three places that did not touch. The boy set them where they stood and wrote no cause across the space between them. He could not read Grathic, could not ask which house held the floorboard, could not hand the gesture back to the woman who made it. A date laid over a sign is a cloth laid over a thing nobody wants looked at, and a cloth is not a cure and not an answer and not an opening.

Alda went out as she had come in, short, with the birth-slates under her arm, and Sella asked nothing about the writing or the tin or the fold pressed too long. A half-pace and a turned slate stood between the two women for the whole of the visit, which was the arrangement, and neither had put a word into it in three years. Sella lifted the basket level and carried him back out at the half-pace from every open slate, going around the space under the lowest rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and did not look into the basket on the way.

Under \place{the Vail kiln-house} roof at the going of the light he turned out the file as a man lays instruments on a towel and found every object where he had left it. Six hearings of \lore{the full note} stood beside and never among, the sixth out of a person and not a seventh; three near-full answers stood advanced and not paid; three intervals kept their places with the half-beat and the unchecking stroke lying beside them as two objects and not a rule; the told bell stayed beside the heard bell in two places that did not touch; the word forty stayed unspoken with the readable double turn beside it and the weight of a thumb on wicker apart from both; four low things kept four places with the river's note a fifth; the two tellings of the shut room stayed joined to no kiln and the damper's double bite stayed an object filed and spent on nothing; the held breath in a man's chest was still unspent and the loan that could hear was still uncalled. He added what the day had given: the place in the returning line gone around without a figure, the second mouth making the same joining without seeing it, Mira's not-looking on a place of its own, Alda's date laid over a sign nobody could take it off, two private records in two materials carried to no door. He laid no finger across any of them. A likeness laid on a likeness grows a cause the way silt gathers behind a weir if no hand holds them apart, and the clean hand at the foot of a cause was the one object he had spent two lives declining to become. The account stood advanced and not paid, toward a far-off day when it might want a name, and no name came today.

Below the sill a child's foot stayed dark inside three turns of wool. It was the cold to the woman who had wrapped it and weather to the yard, and the two facts went past each other as they had gone past each other every morning of his life and never met. The extremity went before the core; the face put on its almost-smile that was not peace; the afterdrop waited on the far side of a warming nobody would perform. He knew the whole of it the way he had once known it under the lamps of a trauma bay, and the reading worked perfectly and bought nothing: no silt-leach, no cause, no cure, no word for it in the language of the house, no name to carry to any door, and no way to close two inches of his own hand on a fold. It was the most exact thing in the room and the least spendable. He had heard tell, from other mouths and never from hers, that the woman under this roof had once sat three nights with a child of her own who cooled while everybody was kind; he had not laid that story beside the dark foot, and he did not lay it there now. A story from a yard and a foot below a sill were two things in two places, and he had spent a hundred and eighteen days learning the cost of drawing the line.

Sella told the fire it was \lore{the thaw}, turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and said the house would keep it, standing a moment with the back of the pot in her hand counting something under her breath toward no opening. She pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carried the little-door burn; the grey streak at her temple showed pale in the dying light. She set the pot where it could be found in the dark and did not look toward the bed. The fold lay where she had put it, one stiff brown-edged crust in it and the empty place beside it still without a name, and the thread at his mouth had frozen again in the going light and nobody under the roof had been given an instruction about the thread. The carried fire answered four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for a hundred and eighteen days. Every warmth in this house had a payer somewhere and no slate in the district carried the name.

The fire's four, four and a gap came up through the floor and the river's note of water over water came through the finger's-width door, and the two counts in two places did not meet, and the three counts did not become one. His small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note and came down. One hundred and eighteen stood where one hundred and seventeen had stood in the small hours, exact, unspent and spoken by nobody who would have known what a number of days counted when they said it. Nothing was joined, named, paid or ended; the two records went on standing apart in the room three doors down, the two boards went on not touching under the bridge, the place in the returning line went on being gone around with a look and no figure, and the morrow would bring the last market of Seed and a reading over the worked ground where the hands had been.
