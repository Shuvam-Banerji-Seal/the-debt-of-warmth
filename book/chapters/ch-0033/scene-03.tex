\daymark{139}{Reed 20, Tenthday, Y.H. 813}

The second Account-day of Reed came up over \place{the Cross} the way a pot comes to the boil with its lid not quite on --- a small sound, a smell, a leaning of everybody toward the same boards --- and nobody in \place{Low Kiln} said the date aloud, though the thin finger of shade under \place{the moot-post} had fallen across the same ground on the tenth of Ilmen, the tenth of Silt and the tenth of Reed already, and would fall again on the thirtieth with nine days between. \person[Corin]{The boy} was one hundred and thirty-nine days old, four months and nineteen days, and a woman saying the number over the pig rail would have been answered with a look, because a day is a small vessel and no house in the district counts what a child has lived through the way a Court counts embers. \person{Sella Vail} carried him down at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, woodsmoke going before her out of \place{the clay road} and the river wind coming up to meet it, and gave the instruction to the wool and to the wind and never to the basket. “Keep it off his ears. It's the river wind.” It had come down with them and it would go back up with them, and that was the whole of what she said about the morning.

Under the wool his head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far. The crowd leaned over \place{the reading block} the way the pot leans with its lid not quite on: shoulders forward, chins down, the backs of coats making one long brown roof with a gap in it where the slate would be held. Eight pigs went over the wet rail behind them with a smell of warm mud and bristle, and an assessor from \place{Sill} wrote them into a slate held out of a rain that was not falling, his hand going to the writing before his eyes went to the rail. The boy set that order on a place of its own and did not ask it to stand for anything beyond itself. In a hundred and thirty-nine days he had learned that the hand is sent to the record before the eyes are sent to the person, and that the habit is neither honesty nor deceit but a way of keeping the figure where the figure belongs, which is under a thumb and not in the mouth of a room.

She set the basket in the moot-post's shade where no slate lay, a hand's breadth in from the edge of the boards, and stood a half-pace off with the little-door burn shut on nothing. The cold iron of the post's bracket took the cold out of his cheek and kept it. The thread at his mouth had frozen on the walk down and nobody under any roof had been given an instruction about the thread, so it stayed where it had frozen. Across the square \place{the pot board} kept its shallow shelf with dust in the hollows, the light going along the rims, and beyond it \place{the market stalls} stood with their reed bundles and their eel tubs and the smell of wet wool and river silt laid over all of it, the last reeds of the month going off in armfuls judged by the arm that carried them.

\person{Berr Clay} came up short off the clay road a little before the top of the morning, with the red wool cord of his office folded square under his coat instead of worn across it and the green of tasted glaze at one corner of his mouth. Hot clay went in ahead of him, and over it the smell of nothing at all, which is the most expensive smell in \place{Grath} and the one this district has learned not to question. His hands were large and clean; he wiped his thumb twice on his apron before he touched nothing, as a man wipes an instrument before setting it down, and held a narrow slate unopened against his forearm with the writing turned toward his own ribs. He passed a finger over the figure and let his eyes rest on the writing a breath longer than the writing required. Then he read.

“One in twelve, as \lore{the notice} says,” he said low into the goers-by, and the crowd leaned in, because a low voice makes a crowd lean, and a man who does not raise his voice in a moot is more frightening than a man who shouts. “The \lore{levy} is called. It is not called by me. If your house wants it again, there'll be a slate at the watch.” \emph{Your house}. Never \emph{I}. He took himself out of his own sentence as neatly as a surgeon takes his hand out of a field before the dressing goes on, and the sentence stood afterward with nobody inside it, which is the way this district likes its arithmetic to stand.

The figure went into the air over the pig rail and not into any house. The true subtraction sat in a locked box up \place{the clay road} in a house set toward \place{High Kiln}; no thumb at the Cross had been near the box this morning, and no voice had given the difference between what was read and what was paid. A figure read aloud is not the figure paid. The boy set the one on the boards under the moot-post and the other in a room up the road whose door he had never seen opened, and drew no line between them. He had the use of neither a thumb nor a slate, and the keeping of two figures apart was the only honest work a child in a basket could do.

Berr let the slate lie a moment against his forearm after the reading, the finger still where it had passed over the figure, and looked neither into the basket nor toward the woman standing across the square. He closed his clean hand on the slate's edge and went off toward the stalls with the cord folded square under his coat, the narrow slate under his arm and the green at his mouth where it had been. The boy watched the large clean hands go and set the twice-wiped thumb on the tray where the other record-first hands stood, and did not let it take its place at the head of them. The square gave a performance of arithmetic: a figure carried from a slate to a mouth to the air above the pigs, and the true subtraction carried nowhere any ear could follow. He could read the performance and could not enter it. That was the whole of the morning's difference and nobody in it knew there was a difference to be found.

\scenebreak

Across the square a woman stood at the pot board looking at the pots. She said nothing and nobody spoke to her; she stood the way a pot stands where it has been set, with her weight settled and her attention elsewhere. Clay stood under her nails in the small crescents, and a pale line went across the back of one hand where something had once been worn tight and long enough to leave its mark behind when it was taken off. The sweetish green of \lore{lead glaze} lay in the crease beside her lip, not painted on but tasted there, the way Berr's green sat at his own mouth corner. She turned a cup a quarter with the fingers of one hand, not lifting it, so the light went along the rim and came off it; she held it there a breath; then she set it back exactly where it had stood, the handle pointing the way it had pointed before she came.

The boy took the green into his file without taking the woman with it. He had no name for her and did not make one, and no voice in him supplied a face for a colour to belong to. She was a mouth across a square and a hand with a pale line on the back of it and a cup put down where it had been found, three observations lying side by side and not laid across one another. The colour was green. That was all it was. He had filed the green at Silt 30, the green at Seed 28, the green at Reed 7 and the fourth at Reed 15, each on a place of its own, and Berr's own glaze sat beside them on another place because a man's mouth is not the same object as a woman's, even when the colour in the crease of one is the colour at the corner of the other. This was the fifth green at a mouth and the sixth shape of the colour. Five mouths, six shapes, no name, no ear, no cord-mark, no pots carried up the road and no box opened up the clay road. A colour is not a face and a likeness is not a cause; the fact that a pale line crossed the back of the woman's hand and that Berr's thumb went twice over a figure did not make a joining, because the district was full of hands and only a payer makes an account of them.

He did not look at her twice, and she went on standing with the cup where she had set it and the light coming off the rim into the dust of the board. The green stayed in the crease beside her lip until she turned her head, and then it was a green seen from the other side, and then it was gone among the coats going past. He kept both appearances on the same place and did not make two colours of them, because a colour turning with a head is still one colour, and the mouth that held it was not a name spoken under any roof.

The bell struck through the morning from the Cross, longer than any church bell, four strokes, four strokes, and a gap where the fifth should have stood. Nobody crossed himself. The goers-by went on leaning and the pigs went over the rail and the assessor's slate took them down without a pause. The boy kept the heard bell on the place where it sounded and the told bell of \lore{the Long Night} on the place where it had been spoken of in the dark, two objects in two rooms of the file that neither agreed nor disagreed. He did not add either to the six hearings of \lore{the full note}: a bell is a bell and a note is a note, and five of the six had come from four sources and the sixth from a woman being lifted out of water, and none of them had been the bell. The heard bell stood beside the told and the gap stood where the gap stood, and no crossing of anybody's hand made the three into one count.

Below \place{the north bridge} at \place{the out-of-line landing} the soft-cuffed clerk sat where he had sat since the month turned, his cuff grey from washing and grey again by midday with the dust of the stalls. His slate lay to his forearm with the writing turned inward where nobody else could read it, and his hand went to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls, every time, whether or not there was a figure to enter. The steps kept four, four and a landing a hand's breadth out of line; a girl with a creel took it sideways with one hand out to the rail and went on selling without looking at her feet, and nobody made a fall of it. The market sold its reeds and its eels over the top of him as water goes over a stone that lies where a stone lies. Nobody stepped around him, nobody asked what he was set there for, and nobody took him off.

A measure set down before anybody calls it is a measure set down ahead of something. The boy kept the clerk's order of reaching on a tray of its own beside the midwife reaching for the slate before the child, Berr wiping his thumb before he touched nothing, the man who priced him, Jorra speaking to the wheel before the pots, Sella giving the instruction to the wool before anybody, and the woman at the bare board taking her loaf. A row of likenesses will stand up and call themselves a doctrine if no hand holds them apart, and a doctrine is an account that has found a payer. He held them apart. The clerk was not the thane, the thane was not the midwife, and the woman at the pot board was not any of them; no payer had stood up in the square this morning and said a name, and so the row remained a row of hands and not an account.

\scenebreak

Sella stood a half-pace from every open slate and did not turn toward the reading. When a draught came off the water and pushed the fold back from his ear she gave the instruction to the wool again and to the river wind, and never to the basket, and did not repeat the figure. A neighbour with a reed bundle on her shoulder came near enough to hear the reading and said something to the boards about one in twelve; Sella answered the pot. “Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there. The house has said it.” The woman shifted her bundle and went on with it, and the question went with her and left no figure in the shade. Sella's hand shut on nothing and opened; the grey streak at her temple showed pale in the flat light off the boards, and she did not look down into the basket or up toward the pot board, where the green was going off with the light.

The boy lay with the cold iron at his cheek and watched the square perform its arithmetic. The figure went from a slate to a mouth to the air above the pigs; the true subtraction stayed in a box up the clay road; a cup was turned a quarter on the pot board and set back where it stood; the woman with the pale line went off without a word; the clerk's hand went to his slate before his eyes went to the stalls; the bell gave four, four and a gap and nobody crossed himself. Six acts, six places, no sum. He had once laid instruments on a towel and known each by the wear of its handle; now he could not close two inches of his hand on the rim of the basket and could not lift a finger to the green at a woman's mouth two hundred paces off. The surgeon's register noted the order of the morning and the child's body noted the cold at the cheek and the small wet sound of its own head, and both were true and the second was louder, and he had stopped expecting the first to make the second any smaller.

The reed month stood in its last tend and the third Account-day was nine days off at Reed 30. The levy had been called and denied by the man who called it, and the number that went into the air did not know the hand that had gone into the strips on Seed 15 and had not come back, and did not know the child under the post's shade whose days were counted by nobody. One hundred and thirty-nine days old, four months and nineteen days, he kept the number exact and unspent and spoken by nobody, the way a man keeps an instrument clean without ever being asked to use it. The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water coming up under the boards and came down against the fold, exact, complete and worth nothing anybody in this square would pay for.

Below the bridge the clerk's hand went to his slate before his eyes went to the stalls, and above him the market sold its reeds over the stone where he sat; across the square the green went off with the light, still a colour.
