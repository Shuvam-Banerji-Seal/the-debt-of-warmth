## Chronicle

The reed month stood in its last tend. On the nineteenth day Sella Vail carried the boy down, giving the instruction to the wool and the river wind and never to the basket, while the last bundles came in armfuls judged by the arm that carried them. A cutter told a girl with a creel to lift her bundle and let her arm say what went into it; a woman at the racks who asked what the hands would be paid was answered with the weather and the river. The yard went around the clean dark line, the finer grey called the thaw, and the empty place in the returning line where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out. Beneath the loaf shelf Sella reached a finger under the board where the charcoal tally lay, without looking and without a word, and brought it away.

On the twentieth day, the second Account-day of Reed, Berr Clay came up short under the moot-post with his office cord folded under his coat. He wiped his thumb twice, passed a finger over a narrow slate and read one in twelve, calling the levy and denying it with your house, never I. The figure went into the air; the true subtraction stayed in a locked box up the clay road. Across the square a silent woman turned a cup a quarter and set it back where it stood, the fifth green at a mouth, a colour and not a name. At the stalls Berr paid two marks short for eels and went up the road believing he had done a kindness. That night Tarn brought the ear-sentence a sixth time to the word that would call the loan and stopped with no figure and no name, drawing the breath he did not spend; the boy filed it beside the first five, six intervals and not six payments.

On the twenty-first day three or four men went low-voiced into the room off the wheel-shed whose door kept its finger's width of open, and Sella carried the boy past without a name for them. Jorra came through with slate dust into both forearms, spoke to the wheel before anyone, and put that finger into the cold damper ahead of her unfinished sentence. At the Weir a reed-cutter turned his grey cord so its knots stood and told a weir woman no more could the Court; Orra Weir gave a date where a figure was asked; Mira Silt kept her thumb under her apron to a second cord twisted twice beneath it. In the small hours the boy turned out his file, nothing laid across anything else. One hundred and forty days old, he wanted the crust two inches from his ear and got neither it nor the number of his days spoken by somebody who would know what it counted. His hand went up an inch toward the note of water and came down.
