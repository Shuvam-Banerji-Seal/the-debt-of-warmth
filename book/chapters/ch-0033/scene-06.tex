The half-pace had carried them down the river road while the morning was still young, out of the smell of wet clay and woodsmoke and into the smell of cold fish and tallow smoke that came up off the water before \place{the Weir} showed itself between the banks. \person{Sella Vail} gave the instruction to the wool and to the running water and never to the basket --- keep it off his ears, it had come all the way down with them and would go all the way back up --- and set \person[Corin]{the boy} at the edge of the work where no slate lay and the salt stood white on \place{the plank walk} where the flood had touched the posts and gone away. The reeds stood in their last grey ranks beyond the bank, broken ends dark under the frost and the tips pale as old bone, and the cutters went down at first light and came back against the going of the sun with armfuls judged by the arm beneath them and by nothing set on a slate. A bundle standing in the mud was worth what the arm under it said it was worth, which was a weight and not a figure. The boy watched the shoulders go down and come back and noted the order of them without deciding to: the hand went to the load before the eyes went to the road, and the road got no more look than the load got a name.

He had learned what a record kept in muscle could tell and what it could not, and he did not need to learn it again. An arm that has carried the same load all morning can say how often it lifted and never what the lifting cost; it is exact, complete, and worth nothing to a man with a slate. The surgeon's register in him noted the order of reaching and gave him no standing to enter it anywhere. His small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down against the fold. Nothing within two paces could be reached --- not the bundle on the stone, not the cord going into the coat, not even the fold he was wrapped in, which he could not have drawn back an inch from his own ear. He kept the count where no \lore{the Court} thumb would read it, exact and unspent, and went on watching.

The reed-cutter came along the boards with a bundle on his shoulder, walking them as a man walks a floor he laid himself and knows where it gives. From inside his coat he took a short length of wool or reed-fibre gone grey with the oil of a working thumb, the grey a stitch-hole takes after a thread has been drawn through it a dozen times; it was the colour of the stone beside it and might have been mistaken for a piece of that stone but for the knots. He set the bundle down on \place[the blessing stone]{the flat blessing stone} with its broken ends squared to the edge, straightened the cord on his thumb, drawing it through his lightly closed fingers the way a man draws a measuring line and not the way a man opens a book, and turned it once so the knots stood up in the small light off the water. He tied one low with his teeth and a pull of the thumb, bit the end off level, put the cord back inside his coat and drew the coat closed over it with the flat of his hand. He did not show it to anyone and he did not count it where anyone could hear him count.

A weir woman come up from the traps with salt at her wrists asked what the cord held.

“You can ask the cord,” he said.

“I can't read a cord.”

“No more can the Court.” He lifted the bundle off the stone and went on along the boards toward the traps, and the question went down into the water after him with no figure on it and no name.

The boy took the shape of the hand in as a man takes the measure of an instrument laid on a towel before anyone names what it is for. He had seen a knot tied low with teeth and a thumb before, and had not once counted what the cord held; a tie, a turn, a thumb-pulled tail, a record in a material no Court thumb would spell, exact and complete and worth nothing to anyone who wanted a figure. The greyness was itself a record of a hand that had been there long before any figure was wanted and would be there after the figure was forgotten, the way the worn thumb-mark on a tool tells which hand held it and tells nothing of the work the tool was bought to do. He read the shape and did not supply the want that would make it an account. He did not lay it beside the charcoal under the shelf two roofs up the yard, or beside the empty place in the returning line where a hand went in on Seed 15 and did not come out; the grey cord went back into the coat with its knots unread and stayed there, carried and not paid. Sella stood a half-pace off with the little-door burn shut on nothing and gave the instruction to the wool again, and did not look into the basket to see what he had been watching.

At the lower boards \person{Orra Weir} stood with her board on its side, turning it with the wrist that had turned it ten thousand times. Her hands were like old rope, the pale eel scars lay uncovered across both forearms, salt stood white at her wrists, and the grey streak showed first through the black hair when she moved her head. The smell of river silt, cold fish and tallow smoke went ahead of her, and the nasal half-song of the Fen came into her voice where she was tired, as it had come at the blessing and every morning since, and she had never entirely lost it. A cord of grey wool hung at her waist with its knots set close along it, the thing people noticed last. She pulled one knot down with her thumb and set it among the others, and did not look into the basket. The traps stood in the gaps of the weir with their mouths upriver, and the board she turned had the shape of ten thousand eels worn into it, and neither the board nor the cord had ever been laid where a slate could be opened beside them.

A woman at the rails asked what the levy would take now the hands were out in the reeds.

“The ninth of Ilmen,” Orra said. “The collector's boat came round the bend on the flood and had six oars. One man stood at the stern and never touched them.” The half-song flattened the words and let them out slow, one after another the way a trap is lifted and set down.

“That isn't the levy.”

“It's a date. The river takes what the Court doesn't. You can shout at either one. I've tried.” Her thumb went to the cord again at the end of the sentence, and the boy could not tell whether that was a lie or only the habit of counting, and made no cause of it. The half-song went quiet in her after the last word the way a man's breath goes quiet when he has lifted something heavy and set it where it belongs.

A date given where a figure was asked is an answer with the question taken out of it --- a chart closed over a note whose finding nobody had read, the neat margin left standing --- and he set it where it belonged, on the place where the told bell stood beside the heard bell, and the man who keeps his hands off the oars stood apart from the midwife's reaching hand and the thane's twice-wiped thumb. He had no \lore{Grathic} word for the shape of it and did not supply one. The date went on its own place and did not touch the cord in the reed-cutter's coat, though both had passed along the same boards in the same hour; and the woman who asked went off with her question still in her fist, carried away level so nothing slopped. Two accounts with no figure in them are still two accounts, and he had long ago given up making them agree.

The last knot on Orra's cord showed its double turn. The tail was tucked, it was tied left over right, and the small extra weight of a thumb had been pressed into it, the shape legible in the small light and the word that would have named it staying where it had stayed since the year of the flower. The boy read the shape without the name. He did not count it --- not the last knot, not the ones before it --- and he did not say the word that was not spoken anywhere on these boards, because a word spoken by a child who could not speak would have been a thing entered in an account he had no standing to open. He watched her watching the basket out of the corner of an eye that never quite arrived, and the watching got no answer from a bundle that could not lift a hand. The knot kept its double turn and kept its name, and that was the whole of what he was allowed to carry away.

Behind Orra, on the next board down, a second board lay that did not touch hers. \person{Mira Silt} worked the eels over it with pale slime dried in long grey strokes on her sleeves and river silt dried pale in the hollow of her neck, her head held a little to the right where the top of her left ear had been, her small smile kept on her face the way a door is kept on the latch. The two boards lay side by side the whole morning and no edge of one crossed the edge of the other. She worked fast and without a beginning, the way she did everything, her knife going in under the jaw and the eel-bones coming out onto the board in a small wet heap while the smile stayed where it was.

A weir woman come down to the outer boards asked whether the run would be thin now the hands were in the reeds. Mira flicked a string of eel-bones against her thumb while she decided whether to lie, moved two from hand to hand, put one away, and answered the question and then her own. “Thin? Who's to say thin? It comes when it comes. Ask the river.” She said something low about a boat that had come up on the flood and nothing about the hand that had not come out of the strips. The bones went back into her pocket and the smile did not move on its latch. The boy set the order of it on the tray where the other record-first hands lay: the bones moved before the answer was chosen, the hand to the cord before the figure was priced; and he did not join her hand to the midwife's or the thane's or any other hand that had gone to a record before it went to a person.

A buyer in a coat with the river still in its hem named a figure for two pair. Mira's thumb went under her apron to the grey wool cord and stayed there longer than the figure required, while she decided what the figure was worth to the woman who had said it. “Two marks the pair and two short,” she said. “You've said it twice and I've counted it twice. There's nothing in the sentence that wants an answer.” The buyer took the eels and went. Shifting a creel showed the second cord twisted twice beneath the grey --- the one never carried upriver to \place{Nine Pots}, never lifted at a door --- and then the apron came down over it. Neither knot was counted by any thumb but her own, and the knot for the thin child upriver was not touched. She said nothing about the child and did not look into the basket; her not-looking was a keeping on a place of its own, the opposite of the pricing face a buyer turns across a market, and she had no word in Grathic for either one, and the two stood on separate trays and did not touch.

Three private records stood on the boards in wool and teeth and thumb, all within a dozen paces of the basket and every one visible and none enterable. The reed-cutter's tally lay in the coat drawn close over it, gone grey where a working thumb had oiled it; Orra's knot-string hung at her waist with its last double turn legible and its name withheld; Mira's second twist lay twice beneath the apron, never taken to the door it belonged to. Each was kept by a hand that would not let a figure be written where another hand could read it, and each hand had passed over its record in the same hour. The boy laid them side by side and not across, the way a man lays instruments on a towel, knowing each by the wear on its handle and refusing to mistake three handles for one tool; for three likenesses in a row will stand up and call themselves a doctrine if no hand holds them apart, and a doctrine is an account that has found a payer, and no payer had been named to any of these. He entered none of them, and he did not carry the reed-cutter's cord upriver to any door or lift Orra's knot-string from her waist or draw Mira's second twist out into the light, because a thing set down where nobody expects to be read is still a thing set down and not an invitation.

Below the sill at \place{the fish-trade shade} the morning went on at its own pace. A woman wound a child's foot in three turns of wool and called the cold weather, and a neighbour with a pail said the water would go when it would go, and the foot stayed dark inside the wool with the cold in it that no wrap had taken out. The boy read it exactly --- the extremity going before the core, the small almost-smile that is not peace, the afterdrop waiting on the far side of a warming nobody would perform --- and the reading worked as perfectly as it had under another roof and bought nothing. No silt-leach, no cause, no cure, no word written across it; and he did not lay it beside the green that had gone off with the light across the square the day before, because a colour is not a foot and an absence is not an absence of the same thing, and likeness is not cause. One cold thing stood on its own place and kept it, the cold to the woman who had wrapped it and weather to the neighbour with the pail, and the surgeon's register noted the order of it and had nowhere to put the note.

Sella set the basket at the very edge of the work where no slate lay, gave the instruction to the wool and to the running water and never to the basket, and stood a half-pace from every open board. The reed-cutter went up the planks with the cord inside his coat and the knots unread; Orra's last knot kept its double turn, tail tucked, tied left over right with the small weight of the thumb pressed in, and kept its name unspoken; Mira's thumb came out from under her apron when the buyer had gone and her board lay beside Orra's without touching it. River wind came up to meet the smell of cold fish and tallow, and the note of water went over water and under the boards where no slate lay.

At the going of the light she carried him home at the half-pace, up the river road with the woodsmoke of \place{Low Kiln} going before her and the river wind coming up to meet it, and gave the instruction to the wool and never to the basket. One hundred and forty days old, four months and twenty days, and nobody on these boards or under any roof had said so. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down against the fold, exact, complete and spoken by nobody; the three keepings went on being kept where no Court thumb would read them, and his count went on going.
