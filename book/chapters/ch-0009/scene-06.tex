\daymark{36}{Ilmen 7, Y.H. 813 (Seventhday)}

The hairline had come up out of the thaw with everything else that had been lying under the ice all winter, and the yard had already given it a name that let a person go home. \person{Corin} was thirty-six days old and carried in the basket under the fold to \place{the yard well} before the light had cleared the poles, and the first thing he heard that morning was not the bucket or the bristles but a man's boot coming across the boards with cold on it, and the cold came off the boot in a small dry smell the yard read as weather. Someone had been down to \place{the Ilm} again. Someone was always down to the Ilm now, and came back with wet on their legs and a look in their face they put away before they reached the well.

A man at the well held a sliver of ice up against the tallow under the little roof and turned it so a dark line showed. The line ran in from one edge and stopped a thumb's breadth short of the other. He turned the sliver again, as though a second looking would make the line go all the way across, and it did not.

He had seen that shape before, a dark hair in a shard held to a tallow light on Stone 19, when the river was still frozen solid and the district had called the water different without saying what had made it different. He had kept the shard's shape on its own tray since that morning --- a line that ran in and stopped short, an absence with the shape of a presence --- and now the whole river had written the same shape into its skin, longer than a man could lift and older than any of the hands standing around the well. He set the new hairline beside the missing step at \place{the Fishstairs}, four down and four and a landing where a step should have been, and beside the gap in the bell that had been told in the dark, and kept the three in three places. A likeness was not a cause. He had put three things into one sentence once in a room with better light, and the rest of a life had been the cost of it. Here the three lay apart on boards nobody could see, and the day went on being weather.

The man who had been down to look at the crack had a figure shut in his fist with the thumb tucked under, the way a man holds a coal he means to keep. “It's come a hair further,” he said, and set the fist on the well's stone lip, and looked at it, and thought better of the rest. Whatever the number was, it went no further than the fist. \person{Sella Vail} drew the bucket up over the lip with the little-door burn closing first on the rope and then on nothing, and said to the fire before she said it to the man, “It's a cold. That's all it is. Feed the fire.” She did not ask what was in the fist. The figure went around \place{the Clay Yard} that morning as a shape and not a number --- passed from a fist to a mouth to a shoulder-shrug, kept whole by nobody giving it out, the way a pot is passed from hand to hand in a dark room and never set down.

The bucket came up cold and sour and tasted different to everyone at the well, and nobody said \lore[the Breaking]{the word} for it. Sella poured into the house pail and set the basket out of the draught with her knee, and put the fold right at his ear without looking at the face under it. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. “It's the river wind.” The little-door burn shut on nothing and opened on the pail handle. The boy's hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the swinging bucket and came down. He could hear the draw of it --- head, rope, the give of the full weight --- as he had heard it five days back at this same well, and the hearing was the only part of him that had grown in all that time.

\person{Berr Clay} came back from the river with the red wool cord of his office folded under his coat instead of worn across it, and green of tasted glaze at one corner of his mouth. He had the look of a man who had been doing a piece of work nobody had asked him to do and had decided it was a kindness. He wiped his thumb twice on his apron before he touched nothing, and passed a finger across the figure on a narrow slate he held in his other hand, a thing the boy could not have read if it had been laid to his eye. He had a number. He did not care to give it. A neighbour at the well started to ask, and Berr said, without looking up from the slate, “A number goes into a slate, not into a well. If your house wants it, there'll be a slate at the watch.” He said \emph{your house}, not \emph{my account}, and the neighbour went away with the shape of a figure and no figure. He never said \emph{I}; he said the slate and the watch and the house, and took himself out of his own sentence as neatly as a man takes a hand out of a field before the dressing goes on. The boy had learned to know a man by what he left out of what he said. Berr went off toward \place{High Kiln} with the box in his house still locked, and he looked nowhere near the basket. The boy watched the green at the corner of his mouth and the thumb wiped twice before the touching of nothing, and put the gesture on the tray where the other record-first hands lay, and did not call it the same hand as any of them.

\place{the Singing Sill} gave its low note under the water a little earlier than it should, and the note came up through the boards of the well-platform and into the cold in a way the boy felt at his cheek before he heard it. It was not the flat answer of a vessel and not the round nearly-full of the pot at the racks the day before; it was a note with the whole river behind it, older than the yard. The yard called it weather. A woman come up from High Kiln with the sweetish lead on her breath said the thaw would come early, and half the neighbours wanted it early and half wanted it late, and the ones who wanted it late said so most: a late thaw put off the levy, and a late thaw gave a body its weeks. The woman said the Warm was rising under the stone and that was the whole of it. Somebody else said the ice was as sound as it had been in Stone. An old man at the tubs said the levy would come whether the ice held or broke, and that a late thaw gave a man two more weeks of his own fire, which was the only argument for cold weather this yard had ever needed. A girl with her sleeves rolled past the elbow said her mother wanted it early so the pits could be worked, and was told to hold her tongue about the pits. Both sides ended in the same word, the one that let a person go home without owning a day of it, and the argument went on being an argument after everybody had stopped caring which side they had been on. It had the shape of the well-roof above their heads --- four rafters, four, and a stone in the wall that did not ring when a finger was set against it --- and the boy lay under the fold and counted the parts of it that a room refuses to enter.

\place{Slate Row} was spoken of from the well as a direction and not a place anybody meant to go that morning. One in twelve stood printed where it had stood the year before, and nobody challenged it; the true subtraction that would have put a figure on what a house had actually lost was spoken by nobody and went past the basket shut and unopenable. The boy could not read the printed figure, could not spend it, could not correct it, could not hand it back. He had been carried past a hundred figures in thirty-six days and not one of them had been offered to him as a thing he might hold, and yet every one of them had been taken as though from his pocket. That was the arithmetic of this district: the number was always read over the top of him, and the absence was always left in his fold with the two crusts.

\person{Tarn} agreed with the woman from High Kiln so thoroughly that there was nowhere for an argument to put its foot, and he agreed with the neighbours who wanted it late with the same face and the same small nod. “Yes,” he said to the early thaw. “Yes,” he said to the late. And then, to the boards and not to anybody: “Late's mine. It's a longer week before the paying.” He had set the small knife on its sill before the light and told the empty yard where it was, and it stayed there while he swept, within reach of the hand that would check it. He had six years of the seven counted and the seventh held back, the one entry in this house the house could not check, and the ice going against him now did not make him count it. He had wanted the thaw late for a year and the thaw was coming on in a hairline and a low note and a taste in the water, and he went on sweeping as though the length of a man's week were a thing a broom could arrange. The boy kept the seventh year on its own tray with the other gaps, and kept the ear Tarn had and the thing Tarn had once heard under the roof in their old places. Two silences had kept their equality for thirty-six days and neither man had a word for it.

\scenebreak

They set the basket down at the edge of the well while Sella worked, a pot set down where a pot would not be slopped, and the boy lay with the fold at his chin and the cold coming in its known order --- cheek, temple, wrist. He had kept the count exactly all seven days since the year turned while the household had kept its number badly in fists and mouths and weather. Four, four and a gap in the kiln; four, four and a landing at the Fishstairs; four, four and a space at the kindling; four, four and a gap in the told bell; and now a line that ran in and stopped short of the edge, which was not a count at all but had the same shape as the part nobody entered. The two crusts lay in the fold at his side, one gone past smelling of anything and one that gave a thin breath of barley smoke when the wind turned, and neither was lifted or named; the wanting of them was the loudest thing in the body that wanted them. He knew the anatomy of a vessel going thin --- the slow failure, the enormous capacity held behind a skin of ice --- the way he knew the back of his own hand, and he had no word in \lore{Grathic} for the failure and no hand to point at it. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the water he could not reach and came down. He had reduced a thousand shoulders and could not lift an inch of wool off his own chin; the joke was as dry as the boards and he had learned to find it funny without laughing, which kept the same blade in it and cost less.

At dusk Sella carried him back up the Clay Yard under the \place{Vail kiln-house} roof. The clean dark line where \lore{the Ash} had turned the corner for a year and two days held nothing and was not swept; \person{Garl Vail}'s coat kept its salt shape on its peg and the hollow seat kept the leftward lean of twenty years, and the proverb went to the pot and the fire and not to the basket. She turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and did not touch \lore{the widow's mark}, because a bank was not an opening. The carried fire that had come up the yard on Kindling was still in the house, under blind ash and the broken pot, warming the boards by degrees and missing one breath in every four. The boy lay on his bed under the roof and not in \place{the Debt Loft}, with the two crusts where they had been put and no third in the fold, and filed the hairline in three separate places where nobody could take it up. Outside, the river went on breaking in plain sight and the district went on calling it weather. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth behind him and came down.
