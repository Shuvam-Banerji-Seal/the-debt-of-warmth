\daymark{37}{Ilmen 8, Y.H. 813 (Eighthday)}

\person{Sella Vail} carried him three doors down \place{the Clay Yard} in the grey before the smoke had cleared from the morning fires, with the basket at her hip and the fold tucked at his chin, and set him in the shade of the doorway at the end of the nineteen steps below \place{House Silt}. It was a pot's place, level and out of the draught, chosen with her knee and not her eye; she had set him in a hundred such places in thirty-seven days and had never once asked him where he would rather be. \person{Corin} was thirty-seven days old and saw the doorway from the low angle of a thing that cannot stand, which is the angle at which doorways are largest. The oiled paper at the Vail window had lifted less hard every morning of this thaw, and the cold came at him in its known order --- cheek, temple, wrist --- and the little-door burn on the back of her hand closed on the handle and shut on nothing as she set him down.

“Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. “It's the river wind.” She did not say it to the basket and she did not look into the shade.

The nineteen steps ran from the slip pits up to the rack, and the yard went up and down them all morning with wet slip on its boots and no figure in its mouth. A girl came past with a barrow of grey and did not look at the basket; a woman at the tubs straightened and went back to her work without appearing to have seen it. This was the courtesy of the district, not to see what had been carried past, and the boy had learned to read it as a form of attendance: a whole yard standing aside so that one house could keep one child without a speech. Thirty-seven days of it had built a wall no slate could enter and no collector could take up.

At the end of the nineteen steps \person{Dessa Silt} stood with her shoulder against the jamb, as though the jamb were the only thing holding her up. Wet clay had dried at her hem in the shape of a hanging petticoat, and her nails were the colour of slip mixed too thin. The cough came up out of the pits in a low short way, shorter than a cough should be and lower, and she put her hand over her mouth and kept it there while the sound finished with nothing after it. When it passed she took the hand away and said to the ground, “It's the pits. Comes up in the cold. Weather.”

It was worse than it had been on Stone 28, and she gave it the same name. That was the whole talent of this district: to put an old word on a new thing and go home warm.

Because a truth had come near, she got specific. “Nineteen out and nineteen back,” she said to the ground. “Four loaves on the seventh of Stone in eight hundred and twelve. Three to the levy and one eaten with my feet up. Bits to the woman who came with a basket.” She laid the figures out the way a woman lays nails on a bench, small and separate, so that nothing larger can be fitted onto the bench. Her thumb went once over the pale sieve-wire scar that ran up the inside of her left forearm, and the door-shut smile came down over her face and shut. “A body can be warm and still be gone,” she said. “You learn that in the pits.”

He had been carried past this doorway often enough to know the shape of her standing, and he read it now with the part of him that had once stood on the far side of a room and decided which bodies were worth opening. The hand over the mouth had gone down too late. The shoulder kept more of her weight than one shoulder should have to keep. Under the smile, at the throat, something beat faster than the rest of her admitted to. Her hands were small and quick and never clean, and the grey had gone into the nails the way the years had gone into the door-shut smile. Severe cold put a look on a face that passed for composure; he had seen it in a bay with the lights too bright and had learned not to trust it. He had once watched a man come off a cold street into a warm room and grow worse in the ten minutes after while everybody said the man was settling. He had learned to distrust the word settling. It was what people said when they wanted the room to go on being warm. He knew how a vessel went thin --- not by breaking, but by handing its work to the next thing and the next, until the skin held more than it had ever been meant to hold and the face went on arranging itself in the ordinary way. In the language he had lost there were words for it, and a row of them would have gone into a chart in a room with better light, and there was no chart here and no hand to write one. He had no word in \lore{Grathic}. He could not name what he saw in her face. He kept the looking, which was the only part of it he could keep.

The nineteen steps went out of her feet and into her thumb. She counted the nineteenth into the thumb again as though counting a thing already counted, because the count kept a larger thing out of the room, and the larger thing was the reason she had come to the doorway and could not say why she had come. He had a count of his own that kept objects on separate boards --- four, four and a gap in the kiln, four, four and a landing at \place{the Fishstairs}, five hearings of \lore{the full note} from four sources and no sixth --- and he knew without being told that the two counts were doing the same work: a row of small exact things laid across the mouth of something too large to look at. He did not let the knowledge turn into a likeness with a cause in it. He had put three things into one sentence once in a room with better light, and the rest of a life had been the cost.

She did not look into the basket going past her shade. She believed him spent among children she had never seen, and the belief had the weight of a fact because she had laid it down so often; a thing laid down every morning became the floor. She did not say the number and she did not say the name. The number lay in her thumb and the name under the scar, and neither came up, and the boy was carried through the shade with the drool freezing at his mouth and nobody wiping it, because a basket set down was a pot set down and left.

\scenebreak

\person{Mira Silt} came up from the eels while the basket was still in the shade, with pale slime dried on her sleeves and silt in her neck, holding her head a little to the right against the cold where the top of her left ear had been. She kept the small smile on her face the way another woman keeps a door on the latch: it could be shut without anybody seeing the movement. She passed her sister without looking at her. Passing the basket she made room for it with her hip, brisk, and when Sella said nothing about whose it was, Mira answered the silence. “Whose do you think? A basket's a basket. It came from a warm house and it's going back to one.” She flicked an eel-bone against her thumb and put it away. To a woman at the tubs who called after her about the cough she said, “Don't ask a Fishstairs woman what she saw. She'll sell it to you and then it's yours and you'll have to carry it,” and went on down with her head held to the right.

When Dessa said the figure of the loaves, Mira's thumb went under her apron to the grey cord at her waist and stayed there while she decided; shifting her weight showed the second cord twisted twice beneath it, the one that had never gone upriver to \place{Nine Pots} and had never been lifted at a door. Neither was counted. She brought no crust and put nothing into the fold, and no third came into it, and she went back down the yard toward the eels without once looking in.

Dessa did not look at Mira either. The two not-lookings had the same shape and were opposite things --- one woman keeping a door shut from the inside, the other standing outside a door that had never been opened to her --- and the boy set them on separate boards and would not let them touch. He did not know what either woman kept. He knew only that the shapes matched, and that matching shapes were where a mistake started if a man had a hand free enough to follow them. There was no fool here with such a hand; his own lay outside the wool and went up an inch and came down.

Behind \place{the drying racks} the \place{Slip Brook} ran a little higher than it should and clay-heavy, under a thin film that no longer held the wind the way it had held it in Stone. It came up past the racks with a small continuance that had sound in it, the sound of water going over more water, and a woman at the racks said the pits had been worked early and that was the colour of it, and another said weather, and both went on with their hands in the slip. Nobody said the word for what the water was doing; a child who went near the bank was told to come back by the middle of the yard, which was as near as the district came to a warning before it went back to calling the colour of the water the weather. The boy set the brook beside the honeyed ice under the mud at \place{the Ilm} and beside the hairline that had come up out of the thaw --- a dark line running in from one edge and stopping a thumb's breadth short of the other --- and kept the three objects on three places. The count of flat hearings stayed five from four sources; the nearly-full answer of the pot at the racks was filed as an answer to an interval and was not added to them. A likeness was not a cause, and three likenesses laid in a row were not an argument either. Outside, the argument about the thaw went on ending in the word that let a person go home without owning the day.

\scenebreak

When Sella lifted the basket past the doorway, Dessa's hand came off the jamb and went to the wicker. The thumb lay along the edge of it; the grip was small and quick, the grip of a woman who had carried clay up nineteen steps and children down them, and it stayed for the space of one breath and then let go. Neither woman looked at the other. Sella shifted the weight up against her hip and gave the instruction to the wool --- “Keep it off his ears; it's the river wind” --- and the little-door burn closed first on the wicker and then on nothing.

The shape of the grip went somewhere before it reached his face. A hand in a grey sleeve went into a coat pocket for a small cold rectangle of light with a name on it. A sentence about a boat on cold water, nine promises old, waited in the pocket and was not said. The name under the light was not looked at. The hand came back with wool. Nothing crossed into the basket that a baby's hand could carry.

He did not know that the flat note would be heard a sixth time tomorrow, or that the sixth would come from a person, or that the weight of this thumb on the wicker was the detail he would be given to keep and never to spend. He kept the shape where he kept the other things that had no hour yet, and the keeping cost him nothing he could name and everything he had. Tonight the woman was alive, the cough was weather, and the two crusts lay in the fold at his side --- one gone past smelling of anything, one giving a thin breath of barley smoke when the wind turned --- neither eaten, lifted or named. He wanted the second one. He could not reach it, and the wanting of it was louder than the knowing.

At dusk Sella carried him up the Clay Yard under \place{the Vail kiln-house} roof. \person{Garl Vail}'s coat kept its salt shape on its peg and the hollow seat its leftward lean of twenty years, and the proverb went to the pot and the fire and not to the basket: leave the crack showing; people trust a thing they can see has been handled. The clean dark line where \lore{the Ash} had turned the corner for a year and two days held nothing and was not swept. She turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and did not touch \lore{the widow's mark}, because a bank was not an opening. The carried fire that had come up the yard on \lore{Kindling} was still under blind ash and the broken pot, warming the boards by degrees and missing one breath in every four.

He lay on his bed under the roof and not in \place{the Debt Loft}, thirty-seven days old, with the two crusts where they had been put and no third. He had kept the count exactly for seven days while the household had kept its number badly in fists and mouths and weather, and the keeping had not bought him a hand or a word. It had bought him the shape of a thumb on wicker, which he could not spend, and the knowledge of a woman going thin, which he could not treat. He was the only adult in the room and the least use in it. Behind him the Slip Brook went on running clay-heavy a little higher than it should. The district had entered a year on its slates and had not entered him; his count went on, four, four and a gap, into the part nobody had thought to enter, and his hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth behind him and came down.
