\daymark{31}{Ilmen 2, Y.H. 813 (Secondday)}

\dropcap{T}{he bucket} went down on the end of the rope before there was light enough to see the water take it. The woman at \place{the yard well} let it down as a woman lets down a thing she has let down ten thousand times, arm over arm, the rope running through her palm with her eyes on the dark under the roof, and the water took the bucket with a small hollow suck. Frost lay on the stone lip in a rim as white as the glaze inside a cracked cup. Above them the little roof of the well kept the last of the dark under it, and cold came off the stones in a breath that had weight and the taste of iron in it. It was the hour when the yard had not yet decided to be awake: a door going somewhere at the far end, a cough, the small sounds of a woman reaching for what she had set down the night before.

A man stood a little apart with his boots still wet from the bank below the sill. He had come up from \place{the Ilm} and had not gone home to change, and the river was in the creases of his face and in the way he kept his hands to himself, as though he had carried something all night and was not yet rid of the feel of it. He watched the bucket go down.

“Tastes different,” the woman said.

She said it to the water, or to the ground, or to nobody; it was not a sentence that wanted an answer. She drew the bucket up hand over hand and set it on the lip, and a little of the water slopped over and ran dark down the stone, and she tasted a finger and made a small sound with her mouth that was not a word.

“It's the ice,” the man said. “Honeyed under the mud. That's what it is.”

He said it the way a man says a thing he has already said twice that morning and expects to say again before the day is out. The woman did not ask him what he meant. Nobody at the well asked him what he meant. They took the word \emph{honeyed} and passed it around the way a cup is passed, and went on with the drawing and the carrying and the waiting of turns. A girl ahead of them shifted a full bucket from one hand to the other and set it down again and said nothing at all.

\person{Corin} lay in the basket at \person{Sella Vail}'s hip with the fold tucked under his chin and listened to a thing the whole of \place{Low Kiln} could taste and not one person in it would name. He was thirty-one days old. He had no word for the change either, and no way to put a word down if he had one, and so he kept the shape of it instead: the small sour note spoken over the bucket, the man's \emph{honeyed}, the way the sentence went around the well and came back without anybody claiming it. In \lore{Grathic} there were words for weather and words for water and a whole district's stock of words for the cold, and none of them had been laid on this. A thing everybody could hear in the water had no name anybody would give it. He had known rooms where a thing nobody would name was given a number and written at the top of a page in a clean hand, and the room went on afterward as though the writing had settled it. His head went where it wanted and made its small wet sound and came back; the thread at his mouth froze a little in the small hours and nobody wiped it. Cold arrived in its known order, cheek and temple and wrist. He kept the shape of the unnamed thing because keeping a shape was the only work available to him.

\scenebreak

The small signs had come in a row all week and the yard had put a name on none of them. At \place{the Vail kiln-house} the oiled paper over the little window had lifted hard through the deep cold, snapping back when the draught took it, and this morning it lifted less hard, sagging in the frame as though the hand that had held it stiff had opened a little in the night. \person{Tarn} noticed it before he noticed the light. He had been out before first light, as he was out every morning, with \lore{Ash} worked into the creases of his knuckles that no scrubbing lifted, and his broom went over the boards in the direction of the slip pits as though sweeping toward a thing would put it off. Six years of the seven stood counted; the seventh he held back, the one entry in this house the house could not check, and he said nothing about it to the paper or to the boards or to anybody.

Behind \place{the drying racks} \place{the Slip Brook} had lost its stiff skin. It moved now under a thin grey film that broke and closed again around the stones, and the sound of it had changed with the skin: less like a thing tapped, more like a thing breathing through a clog. He had heard the brook from his bed under the roof for thirty-one days and had filed its stiff note with the other things the house kept without entering. This morning it had gone and something quieter had come in its place, and nobody had said so. The grey film went along under the bank and did not hold, and the clay of the pits came down in it now, a thread of the same colour as the forearms of the boy at the wheel.

Down \place{the Clay Yard} a woman said it was a warm spell. A man said it was the weather. By the time the bucket went around a second time the warm spell had become the weather in everybody's mouth, the word that lets a person go home without owning a day of it. The boy had learned the shape of that word in a house where it was used to close a shelf and name a cough and set a ridge on fire. Where the house could not measure a thing it called it weather or mercy, and the accounts came out even and everybody went on with their work. He set the taste at the well and the sagging paper and the moving film each on its own place and did not make a cause of them, because a likeness laid on a likeness will grow a cause whether or not anybody puts one there, and he had made that mistake once in a room with better light and carried it for the rest of a life.

\person{Jorra Vail} came up from \place{the Vail wheel-shed} with slip dried the colour of old bone on both forearms and the bent little finger of his left hand leading the way to the damper, which he moved before Sella had finished telling him the fire was low. To a pot going past on a board he said, “You'll do,” in the flat voice he kept for pots, and did not look toward the basket. He agreed with nobody about the thaw and contradicted nobody; his silence went around the yard as its own kind of weather signal, and the yard straightened around it without an order. Tarn agreed with everything so thoroughly that there was nowhere for an argument to go --- with the fire, with the ground, with the neighbour who said the thaw would be early and the one who said it would be late. He agreed with both and swept on, and wanted the late one because a late thaw puts off the paying, and had not once that morning said the word for what was coming.

\scenebreak

Sella drew her own bucket when her turn came. She did not hand the basket to anybody or set it down where the rope might swing over it; she kept it at her hip with the fold at his chin and drew with her free hand, the little-door burn on the back of her hand closing on the rope and then on nothing as she changed her grip. The burn had the shape of a door, two straight sides and a lintel and a threshold, and it shut when the hand had nothing to hold. He had watched it close that way a hundred times and it still told him nothing except that the hand had let go.

“That'll do,” she said to the bucket, not to the woman who had let hers down. And to the wool at his ear, “Keep it off him. It's the river wind.” She said \emph{the house} where another woman would have said \emph{I}, and did not say where the warmth in the household dish had come from or who had paid for it. The instruction went to the fire and the wool and the bucket and never to the basket, and the basket was where he was, and so the keeping went on without an entry, as it had for thirty-one days. She did not wipe his mouth. She did not look into the fold.

When she carried the full pail back toward the kiln-house the boy saw, past her shoulder, the peg behind the door where \person{Garl Vail}'s coat kept its salt shape, and the hollow in the wheel-seat worn by twenty years of a man's leaning, and the place under the skirting board where the grey ridge had turned its corner for a year and two days and was now a clean dark line. The ridge had been burned in the morning of the day before and called dealt with by a neighbour with a pail of water by her knee, and Sella had stood over it with a broom and said nothing. The proverb went to the pot as she passed: leave the crack showing; people trust a thing they can see has been handled. The crack had been showing all year in a grey line along the wall, and now the line was gone and the proverb went on being said to the pot, as though a thing left in plain sight could not be made to disappear by a woman with a pail of water. The seat stayed hollow. Nobody filled it. The coat kept the shape of a broader man, and the debt against the firing of tiles lay where it had lain through another turning of the year, neither paid nor settled nor done with.

\scenebreak

\person{Mira Silt} came up from the eels halfway through the morning with pale slime dried on her sleeves and river silt in the hollow of her neck, her head held a little to the right against the cold where the top of her left ear had been. The smile was on her face in the way she kept it, on the latch, shut without anybody seeing the movement. She had come up the yard in a hurry and with nowhere to be, which was how she came up the yard, and the slime on her sleeves had dried pale in the cold before she reached the well. A woman at the well asked her whether the eels ran early this year, and Mira answered with a question.

“Do they run early? They run when they run. Ask the river. It'll tell you the same as it tells me.”

She flicked an eel-bone against her thumb and put it away. The woman at the well said a figure for something --- the levy, or the feast, or the price of eels by the pair --- and the boy felt rather than saw Mira's thumb go under her apron to the grey wool cord at her waist and stay there while she decided which answer the woman would get. She shifted the basket's weight from one hip to the other as she passed, and under the grey cord the second cord showed twisted twice beneath it, a short length of wool with knots in it that had never been taken upriver to \place{Nine Pots} and never lifted at a door. The thumb went always to the first cord. The knots on neither were counted, and she did not look into the basket.

“Early or late,” she said, “it's weather. I've eels to sell.”

The boy kept the two cords on the tray where he kept the things no slate could enter, and kept the figure that had gone past on its own, and did not let them touch. He had learned that a question answered with a question was a door with a latch on it, and that the hand under the apron was the part of the sentence the woman at the well would never hear, and that the second cord was a record no Court's thumb would think to take up. He had no word in the district's tongue for any of it and no hand to lift a finger toward the cord. She went back down the yard the way she had come, brisk, with the smile still on the latch and nothing said about knowing him.

\scenebreak

He was thirty-one days old and a month old tomorrow. Nothing in the yard marked the day, and tomorrow would not be marked either; the district had entered a year on its slates that did not contain him, and one in twelve stood printed where it had stood last year and nobody challenged it. The month turned under a fold he could not lift. In the fold at his chin the two crusts lay where they had been put. One had been stiff since Stone 15 and gone past smelling of anything; the other still gave a thin breath of barley smoke when the wind turned off the yard, though the wind had turned a hundred times since the smoke left it. Neither had been eaten, lifted or named, and no third had come in six days. Bread there was in the house, and an oven that had been hot five days ago, and the crusts lay where they lay.

The bucket cleared the lip and the full weight came onto the woman's arm. He watched the whole of the draw the way he had once watched the give of a body under his hands in a room with better light --- the set of the shoulder, the angle of the forearm, the rope shortening hand over hand, and then the small dip and recovery of the full bucket as it came over the stone and the water slopped down it. It was a clean piece of mechanics, head and rope and the give of the full thing, and he could have read it in the dark and said what it would do next. The sour smell of the water came to him with the iron in it, and the cold off the stone went up his sleeve.

He could hear it changing. That was the exact and useless thing: the ear that had held \lore{the full note} five times from four sources and kept the count without a sixth was taking in a change in the water that nobody in the yard would put into words, and he had no word either, and his hand was the size of a fold. The surgeon in him read the draw and the weight and the give. The infant in him wanted the breast and wanted the crust even cold and got neither, and the blanket would not stay on, and the wet thread at his mouth was not wiped. He was the only adult at the well and the least use in it.

The bucket came up cold over the well's stone lip, the water tasting different to everybody who watched it come and to nobody who would say the word for it, and the boy's hand outside the wool went up an inch toward it and came down.
