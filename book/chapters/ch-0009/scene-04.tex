\daymark{34}{Ilmen 5, Y.H. 813 (Fifthday)}

Soap came into the room before \person{Alda Reed} did, and warm milk under it, and the faint mineral tang she carried from a house where somebody was always being born. \person{Corin} was thirty-four days old. He had been carried in from \place{the drying racks} before the mid-morning light had properly crossed the bench, set down where a pot might be set down without slopping, the fold at his ear and the two crusts where they had been put. Under the \place[the Vail kiln-house]{Vail roof} the day smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and of something washed that morning. A woman's hands arrive ahead of the woman the way weather arrives ahead of rain; he had learned to read a room by what came into it first, as he had once learned to read a bay by the scrub and the alcohol on the air before anybody opened a door. Then the midwife herself came in with the grey shawl at her shoulders and the birth-slates under her arm, and the room arranged itself around her the way a room arranges itself around a woman who has done this four hundred times and will do it again before supper.

She set the slates writing-down on the bench before she turned to the fold. That was the order. It was always the order. The hand went to the record before the hand went to the child, without haste and without apology, the way a man lays out his instruments before he touches the field; he had watched that order in a midwife bending over a bed, in a neighbour at a well, in a clerk behind a Court table, in a man who priced him and a woman who kept him, and each time the speaker went out of the sentence and the room accepted what was left behind. Alda Reed reached for the slate. The child lay under the wool and waited. He could not have lifted the fold, could not have put a knuckle on the slate, could not have made the smallest correction to the order of a room he had been in for thirty-four days and understood better than anybody in it. The surgeon in him noted the order. The infant in him wanted the breast.

Nobody had asked her anything. The question was in the room all the same --- the question that was always in a room when a midwife came under a roof where a child had been born three doors down and was now kept under another --- and she answered the space where it stood as another woman might have crossed herself. “Stone seven,” she said to the bench. “The account went up on the eighth, before the paper went out.”

It was a date where a wonder should have stood. He had learned the shape of it in the dark on \lore{the Long Night}, when the house sat without fire and told \lore{the Return} and the stories arrived with dates in them where the answer should have been: a child come back thin, a man returned with somebody else's hands, a bell going four, four and a gap over \place{the Cross} with nobody crossing himself. Dates were a handle on a thing too large to carry. That was the whole of it, and the whole of it was kindness, and the kindness had a payer who was never named.

Then she turned from the bench to the fold and pressed it flat at his shoulder with two fingers that stayed longer than the cloth required. Her hands had come from washing and were red at the knuckles, scrubbed to the quick, with clay still under the nails; they were the steady hands a mother grabs by the wrist when the pain comes, and they were doing a small unnecessary thing to a fold of wool as though the fold were a dressing that might come loose in the night. The surgeon in him could tell a necessary touch from a touch that did penance. He had once been able to tell it in a room with better light, and the knowing had done nobody any good there either.

Inside her bodice her hand went once to the little tin on its cord and came away. It did not open. He had seen the gesture often enough to know that it would not open, and had learned to file the not-opening as carefully as other people file a thing done. A tin that stays shut is still a record. A hand that goes to a record and turns away has reported something, though nothing has been said and no figure has been given. The half-pace of the woman at the fire held exactly where it had always held. The little-door burn shut on nothing and said nothing.

\scenebreak

The wool at his wrist had ridden back. The red edge of \lore{the birth-flower} showed --- the branching thing that ran from the third rib down the forearm and stopped short of the wrist, the colour of a line drawn with a hot wire through wet clay. Alda looked at it. Her two fingers went across it in the sign he knew and could not put into any word she would have understood, and she did not know she had done it in front of him. She had done it in front of a market full of people and in a dark room telling stories and once beside a bed where another woman's child had come back wrong; she never knew she had done it, and the sign came first every time and the covering came after.

“Stone seven,” she said. “The account went up on the eighth, before the paper went out.”

It was the same date. She laid it over the sign as a woman lays a board over a crack and calls the floor mended. Then, because the question had stopped and there was no longer any question in the room to answer, she went on with dates. “There was a cooper's seventh in \place{Mud End}. A potter's third child in \place{Stonemouth}, in eight hundred and six, the fourth of Stone; he's a carter now.” They came faster than dates had any business arriving, the exactness piling up, one upon another, until the room was full of other people's children and their birth-days and not one of them was the child under the wool. When she was lying she became unusually exact, as if the exactness itself were a kind of honesty that would stand in for the thing left out. He had learned that too in a better-lit room. A chart could be correct in every figure and false in the one line that mattered, and the correctness was what made the false line hold.

He kept the sign and the date laid over it on the tray where he kept such things, beside the slate turned writing-down and the told bell kept beside but never on the heard bell in two places, and did not let any of them touch. He could not read \lore{Grathic} in speech or on a slate. He could not ask which house held the floorboard, or what the tin kept that the tin would not say, or why a woman who had come to look at a child reached for the writing first. He could not take his hand out of the wool and hand the gesture back. He had once watched a hand go past a note turned face-down on a chart and go on to a signature, and he had known exactly what the hand had not done; knowing had never once given him a hand. Here the knowing was the same and the body was a month and four days old. So he filed the likeness and wrote no cause beside it. A likeness was not a cause. He had made that mistake once and had carried it for the rest of a life, and the room had better light than this one and still had not forgiven him for it.

\scenebreak

Alda took up a narrow slate from under her shawl. On it she wrote what she did not leave lying about, in the second hand no physician of \lore{the Court} had ever seen, the small close writing of a woman who had kept a record for thirty years and knew exactly which part of it could be shown and which part would cost her the room if it were. The hand did not hesitate; the caution came after the writing, in the glance toward the bench, the turn of the wrist, the slate going face-down into the shawl as a thing goes into a pocket before anybody can ask to read it. A fourth note went with the others toward a floorboard in a house the boy could not name, into a tin that stayed shut under a floor he had never stood on. Still unread. Still a kindness with a payer nobody had entered, and a record kept badly, in secret, at a cost to the woman who had lied to a mother three doors down.

Sella stood a half-pace off with the little-door burn shut on nothing. She did not ask what had been written. She did not ask why the slate went face-down or where it would go when the midwife went home. The arrangement between the two women held without a word passing through it --- an arrangement with a door in it that neither opened, the door quieter than the writing and more solid than the wall --- and it had held since the child was three days old. The boy lay between them at the edge of the bench and read the half-pace the way he read the space where a fifth breath should be: an absence with the shape of a presence, kept exactly, not to be entered. Two careful hands were entering the same child in two different accounts and neither account had been shown to him. He had no standing to ask for either.

“He lies quiet,” Sella said to the fire.

“Warm and breathing,” Alda said. “That's all I've set down.”

It was exact. It was not true. A body can be warm and still be going; a hearth-maid of four hundred births knows the small evidence as well as any physician, and knows which of them a mother can bear to hear. Alda had told the lie three years ago in the grammar of a warning, and had gone on telling it every time a woman's face came up in the street, answering with the weather and hating the sound of her own voice. Three doors down a cough went up out of \place{the slip pits} and somebody in the yard called it weather --- the word that lets a person go home without owning a day of it --- and the lie told for mercy had gone into a woman's face and stayed there, a thumb-print in soft clay, with no slate in \place{Slate Row} ever asked to correct it. The boy heard the cough through the wall as a sound among sounds and gave it no more leave to enter his file than the fire's missing breath or the crack nobody would put a figure on. He did not know who coughed. He did not know that a face had been made to hold a lie for three years. He knew only that the room had gone quieter at the sound and that both women had found something else to do with their hands.

Under this roof \person{Garl Vail}'s name was not spoken. Alda laid a date where a name should stand, and the house went around the word as Sella had gone around \lore{the Ash} in the corner before a neighbour burned it and called it gone, arriving at the edge of it and turning without ever putting a foot in. The boy set the silence where the name should have stood with the other gaps and not with the hearings. Five hearings of the flat note stood at five from four sources and nothing this morning had added a sixth; a silence was not a hearing even when it was kept as carefully as one, and a thing that did not happen left a shape that was not a sound. He kept the trays apart as he had kept them all his thirty-four days, and the keeping had bought him nothing he could lift a hand to.

Alda went back to the birth-slates and set them in a row, writing-down, and pressed the fold once more at his shoulder without looking at the face under it, and went out with the smell of soap and warm milk going ahead of her down the yard. Sella put her hand to the fire and told it something about the draught. The two crusts lay in the fold where they had been put, one gone past smelling of anything and one still giving a thin breath of barley smoke when the wind turned, neither eaten, neither lifted, neither named, and no third come into the fold. The boy lay under the Vail roof with a date laid where a name should stand, the tin shut inside a woman's bodice and a fourth note turned face-down toward a floorboard he could not name, the fire behind him taking its breath in fours and missing one. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth and came down. Three doors down the cough was weather. The district had already entered tomorrow on its slates and would not enter him, and the count went on.
