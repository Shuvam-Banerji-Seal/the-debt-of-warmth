\place{Ash} kept its twelfth day without a boundary drawn. The older women of the district named the months by the weather and the slatemen by the number of Account-days that had gone into them, and neither habit was set right or left wrong in so many words under the Vail roof; the month carried both names the way a pot carries two handles and is none the worse for it. There was no market, no Cross and no bell on the twelfth, and the going of the light would come as an ordinary going of the light, measured in loaves and glances and the space where a loaf should have stood. The boy took the afternoon as a surgeon takes a field before the light, by the small sounds first, because the small sounds do not lie and the large ones are mostly weather: the dry rasp of the river under its skin of ice, the rattle of a pail against a stone lip, the small knock of a pot set down somewhere out of sight, the scrape of a broom going over ground the broom had already gone over. Each stood separate, the way instruments lie separate on a towel, and none of them was an answer to anything.

The crust lay two inches from his ear in the dry cold, stiff and leathery, its corner standing a hair off the wool and the empty place beside it keeping its name to itself; no third had come since the morning to make the row come out even. The thread at his mouth had frozen and would stay frozen, because nobody under any roof had ever been given an instruction about the thread. Cold had come to cheek and temple and the small bare joint of his wrist and had stopped there as at the end of a line written out for it; \lore{the Afterdrop} waited past the stopping for a warming nobody in this house would perform. He knew the composed face that is not composure and the ease that is no ease, and he wanted the crust lifted the two inches to his mouth, the thread wiped away and a hand that would close on the pot handle brought within reach. Both were true and the second was louder and neither won; the argument went on under the wool without a verdict, and the surgeon's register took the afternoon in its old order while the infant underneath went on being cold.

She went around the yard as the yard went around itself, with a look and a wide step and no number. The hunts and the repairs of the afternoon stayed directions judged by the carrying arm and the weather, never figures. Another with a cracked jar in a sling of rope asked what the month's mending would want and was told to ask the pot, and the pot, being a pot, said nothing. A woman with a basket of kindling asked what the seventh year would want when it came, and two men mending a strip of fence said the thaw would be early and late. \person{Tarn} said “Yes, house” to each of them and kept the late, because a late thaw puts off the paying. He agreed so thoroughly that there was nowhere for an argument to find a foot, and the woman went away with the late and the men went on with the fence and nobody weighed the seventh year or opened a slate over it. The boy read each answer as a chart left blank where the number should stand and entered none of the blanks. A cost question answered with the weather was a direction arrived nowhere, and the arriving was the whole of it.

At the going of the light the twice-mended pot came up off \place{the pot board} with Sella's thumb first where the wire crossed the crack, as it had come up on every morning of three years. The hairline of the original break stood dark between the old-brass staple and the tighter wire, and she left it showing. “If it's worth mending it's worth mending twice. People trust a thing they can see has been handled.” The sayings went to a pot and a fire and never to a person; they had improved in the keeping and were very likely not as good as the man who had first said them, which did not stop them being true. Repair stayed apart from restoration: a restored thing hides the hand that handled it, a mended thing leaves that hand showing, and here the hand was left showing in the dark hairline between brass and wire. He read the mending as a wound closed in a place the hand would not have chosen, by what the hand had done and by what it had left undone, and kept the two readings apart, for a seam that holds is not a seam that never broke.
