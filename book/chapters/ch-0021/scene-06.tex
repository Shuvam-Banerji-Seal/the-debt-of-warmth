Nothing in the house had been given a figure for the day, and the day had gone anyway, the way water goes out of a bowl nobody has tilted. \person{Corin} lay on his bed under the \place{Vail kiln-house} roof with the fold put right at his ear by a hand that had not once looked at his face, and one brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold beside the empty place that had kept its name from him for a hundred and three days. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had been told to wipe it, because a pot set down is a pot set down and left, and a basket is the same thing under another name. Cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, and the wool rode back from the wrist so the cold found the bare place; his head wandered and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and the hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the river and came down. He had once stood over a table and known, before the first cut, where a vessel would be thin. Here he could not lift that hand to his own chin.

In the next room \person{Sella Vail} stood over the fire with the little-door burn on the back of her hand closing on nothing. “It was the thaw,” she told the fire, the way a woman tells a pot what it is to hold. “You'll have wetting enough before the week's out.” She turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and fed them, and the fire took the coal and gave back four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been. The payer of the warmth had gone unnamed for a hundred and three days and the house had gone on warm all the same, the way a debt goes on standing when nobody says who owes it. The hollow wheel-seat kept its leftward lean behind her and the salt-stiff coat kept \person[Garl Vail]{Garl}'s broader shape on its peg, and neither was spoken of. She gave the instruction to the fire and the wool and the pot and never to the basket, and she did not look through the finger's-width-open door toward the bed, or say what she had seen in the room three doors down, or whose writing lay face-down under a floorboard she could not have named.

A neighbour's voice came up from below the sill with something about the water and the weather, and Sella answered it without turning. “Ask the pot,” she said. “The house has said it.” The voice went off with the answer and the yard took no note of it either way. She shifted a pot a finger's breadth out of the draught and told it not to be set near the door, because a wet thing set near a door is a thing a house will pay for come the thaw; the pot got the instruction and the basket got none, and the boy got none, and this was the whole of the joke such as it was, a kindness that cost the pot nothing and left the person standing outside it. “Feed the fire before you feed yourself,” she said, which was Garl's proverb and not hers, and fed the fire.

\scenebreak

He checked the day the way a man checks instruments laid out on a towel before he puts his hands into a body: not to find anything new, but to make sure nothing had been moved while his back was turned. Three doors down in \place{House Silt} the room had held its bench, its pot, the draught going under the door along the floor to the ankles before it reached the face, and the grey ridge along the skirting. Those four things were the furniture of the birth room where he had been born, of this kiln-house where he slept under the roof, of \place{the death-house} two doors down the yard where a woman had lain and been laid out, and of \place{the Court's room} that had reached him only as a voice through a doorway, with a place in the figures where a line should have stood and did not. Five rooms, five places, the same four objects and the same gap in each. He had not joined them in the room and he did not join them lying here; a likeness laid on a likeness grows a cause unless a hand is there to hold it apart, and he had no hand long enough to reach any of them. He set them on five places and went no further, the way a man will lay five instruments on a towel and leave the sixth instrument, which is his own judgment, in its place.

\person{Alda Reed} had gone on with her dates after the question had stopped, because dates are a handle on a thing too large to carry. A cooper's seventh in Mud End, a potter's third child in Stonemouth, the last high water before the flood, each laid down flat and level beside the others and none of them in the order in which they had happened; she had laid the writing-down on the bench before she turned to the fold, in the order of a hand that reaches for the record before the person, and she had pressed the wool flat at his shoulder a breath longer than a fold requires, being careful in the only way she knew how, which was a way that never asked his face anything. Then she had crossed two fingers at the red edge of \lore{the birth-flower} where it ran down toward his wrist, before she knew she had done it, and covered the sign with the date she had given a woman more mornings dead than any living person keeps count of --- the seventh of Stone, the account up on the eighth. She had touched the little tin on its cord inside her bodice and come away without opening it. The sixth narrow slate lay face-down toward a floorboard in a house he could not name, six pages in a second hand that no Court physician had seen and no living person had read, and no seventh page had been written. Above the empty bed the round pewter tin kept its pale fold at the seam and its thumb-bright rub on the lid, shut, and below it the fold of grey wool held only the hollow where the small pale river stone had once lain, which nobody said was gone. Two private records in two materials that no Court could confiscate stood apart in one room and were carried to no door. The mercy-lie had curdled all the way through to a fact in a dead woman's face: a date laid over a sign that nobody alive could take off, and nobody alive who knew there had ever been a sign beneath it. He could read the keeping in neither record. He could read the shape of it --- a woman who would not show a note, a woman who would not count a knot --- and the reading was exact and worth nothing.

Sella had set the basket just inside the finger's-width-open door at the edge of the light, away from every place a slate might lie, and stood a half-pace off with her burn shut on nothing, asking nothing about the writing or the tin or the fold pressed a breath too long at his shoulder. The arrangement between the two women held without a word passing between them, a wall with a door in it that neither would open; he had lain in the edge of the light and watched the wall hold, and had no name for it in any word he knew and no cause to write across it. Both of them had reached for something before they reached for him --- one for a slate, one for the shape of a room's silence --- and the two reaches did not touch.

\scenebreak

The three places had come back up the \place{Clay Yard} with him and lay where they had been laid. On \place{the loaf shelf} in the next room the row kept two unnamed gaps: the old space at the end that nobody called a number, and the new palm's breadth within the rank that had come in the night of Seed 6 with no scrape, crumb, print or word to mark it. A woman's hand had passed over the new gap and opened for the half-beat it takes to find what is not there and closed on the loaf beyond and gone on with her pot, and no figure had passed. In the fold at his ear one brown-edged crust lay stiff with the empty place beside it still unnamed and no third beside it. He set the two gaps and the one gap on three places that did not touch and let them be three absences in rooms that did not join, because the neighbour's single breath had laid a shelf's old space and a child's appetite together in one sentence without once seeing that they were one absence, and he had filed that breath as two sayings on two places --- a planting and an appetite --- and written no cause across the space between them. He had learned in a better-lit room what three likenesses in a row will do to a man who wants an answer. Here were three likenesses and a man who wanted an answer, and he kept his finger off the shelf. It bought him nothing, and the nothing was the whole of what it bought. The two gaps on the shelf and the one gap in the fold went on in rooms that did not join, and the neighbour's one-breath naming of them went on as two tellings on two places, waiting for some far-off hand to lay a finger on the shelf and call the two places an account that wanted a name. He kept his own finger where it was. No third gap came into either room and no hand laid the rooms together. He was the only adult in the room and the least use in it.

\scenebreak

Below the sill the yard went on with its own two facts. A woman wound wool round a child's foot that stayed dark inside three turns of it and called it the cold; a neighbour at the tubs called the water weather, and the two facts went past each other in the yard and never met. He knew the order of it as he knew the order of his own cold. The extremity goes before the core. The face puts on an almost-smile that is not peace, which is the thing a kind room will misread when the vessel is turning, and the afterdrop waits on the far side of a warming nobody will perform, so that a body begins to fail again at the very hour everybody has agreed it is saved. There was no word in \lore{Grathic} for the one fact and no hand two paces long for the other, and the reading worked perfectly and bought nothing, the way a diagnosis works perfectly on a man you cannot reach. The woman wound the wool a third time and the foot was neither cured nor caused nor named nor entered, and he did not lay it beside the crust or the shelf or the shut pages, because a likeness laid on a likeness grows a cause unless a hand holds them apart, and the only hand here was his, and it could not have reached the wool. The cold below the sill stayed the cold to the one woman and weather to the yard, and the two went past each other as the river went past the door.

\scenebreak

The door stood a finger's width open. Through the gap came the river's low muttered note from \place{the Ilm}, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, and through the floor came the fire's four, four and a gap under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, the two sounds in two separate places as they had come for a hundred and three days. \lore{The widow's mark} hung where it hung at Sella's throat and was touched at no hour, because a door standing a finger's width open is not an opening and a shelf, a strip, a basket and a bed are not openings either; she had lost a husband to that danger and had never once described it that way herself. In the corner the clean dark line lay where it had always lain and the new hairline of grey, finer than the old, lay beside it unswept and called the thaw that would go when it went. Every warmth in the house had a payer and none was entered, not the coal, not the milk, not the bread, not the hands that had carried him up the yard and set him here and not in \place{the Debt Loft}. He lay in the half-light with the cold arriving cheek, temple, wrist, the wool riding back, his hand outside the wool going up an inch toward the note of water going over water and coming down. Nothing was joined, nothing was paid, nothing was named. The count stood at one hundred and three, unspent, and the dark under the roof went on keeping it without a figure.
