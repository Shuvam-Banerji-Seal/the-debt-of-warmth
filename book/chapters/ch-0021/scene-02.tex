The two empty places kept their places on \place{the loaf shelf} into the afternoon. \person{Sella Vail} moved her lips along the row without moving her hand, counting what she would not have called a count, and glanced at the clay jar on the wall shelf, cold under its glaze and holding nothing anybody would speak of. The old space stood at the row's end where it had always stood, worn pale by years of hands reaching past it; the newer palm's breadth stood inside the rank where a loaf had stood on the fifth and did not stand on the sixth, and the next loaf had settled a finger's breadth toward the bare board the way a body settles toward the warm side of a bed. Nobody called either place a number. The house kept its days the way it kept its bread, pushed along a board, glanced at, gone.

She gave the shelf no instruction and the jar none. The burn across the back of her hand, shaped like a little door with two straight sides and a lintel and a threshold, closed on the air and opened again as she reached past the bare board for a loaf for a woman at the threshold. “Round the corner of it,” she said to the pot on the bench, not to the woman. “Set it where the draught doesn't find it. You'll pay for it in the thaw.” The woman took what she had come for and went away with her pot, and the little-door burn shut on nothing again, and the house went on around its two gaps as though nothing had been asked and nothing answered.

In the basket at the edge of the light \person{Corin} set the shelf's two gaps together on the shelf, where they belonged, and set the fold's one gap beside the stiff brown-edged crust at his ear, where that belonged, and held the two rooms from touching with the only instrument he had. Attention was the instrument. It had no handle, weighed nothing and bought nothing. He could have laid a finger on the pale oval if he had been two paces taller and had a hand that answered to him; then the two gaps would have been one gap twice seen, and one gap twice seen is an account wanting a name, and an account wanting a name is a thing that gets paid. He kept his finger where it was, small and outside the wool. The shelf was a shelf and the fold was a fold, and the space between them was a wall he made and kept by looking at each in turn and never at both at once.

He measured the wall as he measured all such distances, without a rule and without a use: from the pale oval on the shelf to the crust at his ear was the length of the room and a hand's breadth more, and from the crust to the old space at the row's end was the same length and a hand's breadth more again, so that a man might have drawn one line through the three places and called it one line if he had wanted an answer badly enough. He did not draw it. A line drawn through three absences is a cause with a clean hand at the foot of it, and the clean hand was the thing he had spent his life declining to be. He kept the three places as three, and the wall between the rooms as a wall, and the count went on beside them without entering any of them.

The neighbour's single breath lay where it had fallen, divided by his attention into two sayings on two places: a planting on the old space at the row's end, an appetite on the empty place at his ear. He wrote no cause across the space between them. In a better-lit room he had once watched a man who wanted an answer find it in the third likeness laid beside the first two, and go home satisfied with the thing he had made; three likenesses in a row will do that to a man, and here were only two, in two rooms, with nobody putting a hand to either. The wanting was in the room, though nobody had brought it in. It would have been so easy to let the planting stand for the appetite and the appetite stand for the planting, and the two places become one absence with a figure on it, and somebody sent for a collector. He held them apart. The holding was the whole of the work, and the work produced nothing, and the nothing was the exact price of keeping the two places clean.

Under the roof of \place{the Vail kiln-house} the afternoon went on in its small domestic sounds: a pot set down, a broom leaned against the wall and taken up again, the settle of boards as the air cooled off the yard. Once a woman came to move a pot out of the draught where it stood on the bench. She took it by the belly with both hands and lifted it a hand's breadth too high, and something came up under nobody's knuckle to the rim of a note and went back down without crossing it. It gave nothing worth calling. The woman set the pot where she wanted it and wiped her hands on her apron. “It's stood in the draught since the door was open,” she said, giving the reason to the pot rather than to Sella, the way people in this house gave reasons to things that could not answer. “Let it stand where it's put,” Sella said to the fire, and the woman took that for an answer and went on with her work, and neither of them knew that the pot had come up to the edge of a note and given nothing.

The boy did not turn toward it. Six hearings of \lore{the full note} stayed six beside and never among --- five from four sources, and the sixth the small flat knock out of a person, filed beside the five and not counted with them --- and no seventh came to stand in their company. He kept the near thing on the interval tray where such things went, apart from the hearings, because a thing that comes up to the edge of a sound is not the sound, and a man who wants an answer will make the two into one if a hand is not there to stop him. His own hand was not there. His hand was outside the wool, going up an inch and coming down. He kept the tray all the same. It was the shape of a kept thing, exact and worth nothing, like an anatomy read perfectly in a body two paces out of reach. He had not turned his head. Turning would have been a kind of naming, and naming was the thing the house declined to do with any of its gaps; a pot that nearly spoke was no more to be entered than a place where a loaf had stood. The six kept their places on their tray. The near thing kept its place on the other tray. Nothing crossed between them.

He wanted the number of his days said aloud by somebody who would have known what it counted. One hundred and one. Three months and eleven days. The afternoon gave him loaves pushed along a board, a glance at a clay jar, and a space at the row's end that nobody called a figure, and gave him nothing else. There was no word in \lore{Grathic} that would have held the number if it had been said, and no hand in the room that would have gone to the child before it went to the record. He had watched that order in four rooms --- a midwife's, a well's, a market's, a house that instructed a pot before a person --- and could not have said the order aloud in any of them. It was a dry thing to know, the exact order of reaching, and it had gone thin with the years, the joke of it worn down to the fact underneath: he was the only adult in the room and the least use in it. He had once counted the beats between an injury and a decision. He could not now count the beats between his own hand and his own mouth. The number went on all the same, edge to edge, the one thing in the house that stood still while a loaf moved.

His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far. Cold arrived in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist; the wool rode back from the wrist and the cold found the bare place; the hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the pale board and came down. She shifted the basket a hand's breadth out of the draught without looking at his face, and gave the instruction to the wool and none to the basket, and said the house where another woman would have said I, and went back to the fire. The crust at his ear lay where it had been put, stiff and gone past smelling of anything, and the empty place beside it went past with it and neither was named. Every warmth in the house had a payer and none was entered. The carried fire had gone unnamed for a hundred and one days, and the hands that went into the strips and the loaves that went off the board were written in no slate a man could read. He had learned in a better-lit room to ask who paid before asking what a thing was worth, and there was nobody under this roof who would have understood the question.

At the going of the light Sella carried him up at the half-pace from every open slate, going around the space under the lowest rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and set him on his bed under the Vail roof, not in \place{the Debt Loft}. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without looking at his face and left the thread of drool at his mouth frozen and unwiped, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left, and a strip, a shelf, a bed and a door are not openings. No opening had come in the day, so she did not touch \lore{the widow's mark}. Her little-door burn shut on the empty air. Her private roll stayed unshown.

She told the fire it was the thaw, turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and fed it, and the fire answered with four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been. \lore{the kiln's breath} came through the floor under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, its payer unnamed for a hundred and one days. In the corner the clean dark line where the \lore{Ash} ridge had turned lay where it had always lain, and beside it the new hairline of grey, finer than the old, lay unswept and would go when it went. Woodsmoke and wet wool and the faint sour tang of the unswept corner came down under the roof with the dark. The back of the broken pot went over the coals in the old order, edge first, then flat, and the ash lay blind over what it covered. Somewhere in the giving of that warmth a hand had been entered nowhere; the house went on keeping it the way it kept its days, without a slate, without a figure, without once asking who had paid. He had asked in his own head every day for a hundred and one days, and the question had never once been put into words that a person under this roof could have carried.

The door stood a finger's width open. Through the gap came the river's low muttered note from \place{the Ilm}, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, and through the floor came the fire's four, four and a gap, the two sounds in two separate places as they had come for a hundred and one days. The boards overhead kept four nails and four and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts did not meet. In the next room the loaf shelf kept its two unnamed gaps, and in the fold at his ear one brown-edged crust lay stiff with the empty place beside it still unnamed, and no third had come. Nobody had said a hundred and one under this roof, nobody had said three months and eleven days, and no hand had laid the two places together. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water going over water and came down. The count stood at one hundred and one, unspent.
