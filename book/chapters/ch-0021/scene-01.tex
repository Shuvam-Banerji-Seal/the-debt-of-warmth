\daymark{101}{Seed 12, Y.H. 813}

\dropcap{T}{he second of Seed} came up over \place{the Clay Yard} the colour of wet slate, with the mud the flood had left set into a brown skin that held the print of a boot and gave it back slowly as the light crossed it. Out east of \place{Low Kiln} the seed stood in \place{the barley fields} where the fists had let it out a week ago, not green yet, only worked ground with the earth drawn over it in a line that stopped where the next strip began. The women went around the strips with their brooms as though a line drawn in dirt were a thing a man might step over and spoil. Nothing in the ground said a figure. A fist had opened and a fist had closed, and the strip had taken what it was given without a mark to show whose hand had paid it. The morning went on in the small sounds of bristles and boots, and a man at the edge of the strips said the work would want hands before the week was out, and a woman at the racks said the weather would have the last word, and the two sayings passed one another in the yard without either putting a number to the hands or the strips, the way two carts pass on a road and neither leaves a mark of how far the other has gone.

\person{Sella Vail} set \person{Corin} at the edge of the work with the wool at his ears and gave the broom its instruction before she gave it to anybody. “Go around,” she said to the bristles. “It cost enough to come in. It'll cost more to take out.” The women took it up as though it had been said to them and went on with their sweeping; the basket got no instruction at all. The little-door burn across the back of her hand closed on the wicker's edge and then on nothing. He lay with the cold coming in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, with the wool riding back from the wrist so the cold found the bare place, and his head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had been told to wipe it. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the light and came down. Nothing within two paces could be reached.

Under \place[the drying racks]{the racks} the clean dark line where the \lore{Ash} ridge had turned lay where it had always lain, and beside it the new hairline of grey, finer than the old, lay unswept and was called the thaw that would go when it went. Sella stood over both with a broom in her hand and swept neither, and went around the space under the lowest rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. The broken birth-slates lay beneath that space and were gone around by every foot in the yard and entered by none. He kept the boot-print, the clean line, the finer grey and the empty space on four places that did not touch, because a likeness laid on a likeness grows a cause unless a hand holds them apart. The boards overhead kept four nails and four and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb, and that count met none of the others either.

Mid-morning she carried him in and set the basket just inside the door of \place{the Vail kiln-house}, away from every open slate, and went to \place{the loaf shelf}. The row stood with two empty places where one used to stand: the old space at the end that nobody called a number, and a palm's breadth of bare board within the rank where a loaf had stood on the fifth and did not stand on the sixth, with the next loaf settled a finger's breadth toward it. A neighbour came in for a loaf with her pot in her hand. She reached past the bare board with her fingers, opened for the half-beat it takes to find what was not there, closed on the loaf beyond, and said in one breath, “That came in with the planting, that space at the end there, and that at his ear's a child's appetite, a growing thing; it'll want filling come Reed.” She took the loaf she had come for and went on with her pot, and did not look again at the place her fingers had crossed. Her hand had gone to the board first and to the boy's ear afterwards, and she had not looked at the boy at all while she said what she said about him. He marked the order of that too, the way he marked every hand in every room, and put it on a place of its own. Two tellings in one breath, a planting and an appetite, and she had not once seen them for the same absence. There was a worn pale oval on the board where a loaf had stood longer than the others stood, and her fingers had gone over it without stopping, the way a hand goes over a dent in a table it has eaten at for years.

Sella said the house where another woman would have said I. To the pot she said, “Don't set that near the door. You'll pay for it in the thaw.” To the fire she gave the thaw and its instruction, and to the broom she had already given hers out in the yard, and to the basket she gave none. She moved her lips along the row and glanced at the clay jar on the wall, cold under its glaze, and left the space at the row's end with no figure. She did not touch \lore{the widow's mark} at any hour, because a shelf, a strip, a basket and a bed are not openings. She had lost a husband to that danger and had never once described it that way herself. When the neighbour's breath had gone out of the room the house went on around its two gaps as though nothing had been said, and the gaps went on being two.

He was one hundred and one days old. Three months and eleven days. The count had gone edge to edge past the round place where a man might have set a mark and shut a column, if there had been a column, or a slate, or anybody who knew what the column counted. A round number is a handsome thing at the foot of an account; it asks to be entered and asks it in a clean hand, and there was no clean hand within reach of this basket. The neighbour's single breath lay on his attention as two sayings on two places: a planting on the shelf's old space, an appetite on the fold's empty place, and he wrote no cause across the space between them. He had learned in a better-lit room what three likenesses in a row will do to a man who wants an answer, and here a single breath had laid two absences together with nobody putting a name to either, and he kept his finger off both. A likeness laid on a likeness grows a cause unless a hand holds them apart, and the only hand he had was small and outside the wool and could not have reached the shelf if it had tried. The count had no object but its own number. He wanted it said aloud by somebody who would have known what it counted, and no word in \lore{Grathic} would have held it if it had been said.

\person{Tarn} took the broom only after Sella had given it its instruction, and went around the two lines and the space under the rack, and then took it over the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector, over and over, without once turning to look along it. The small knife lay on its sill with six years standing in a mark his thumb had worn almost flat and the seventh nearly gone and still held, the one entry in this house the house could not check. To the kindling he said, “Six counted. The next I don't count.” Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line and no scrubbing lifted it; the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back. No collector came down the road, and he went on sweeping it, and the seventh year stood where it stood.

One brown-edged crust lay stiff at his ear with its empty place beside it unnamed, and no third had come. The body humiliated him in its known order. The drool froze and was not wiped. The head went where it wanted with the small wet sound. Cold arrived cheek, temple, wrist, and the wool rode back from the wrist so the cold found the bare place, and the hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the low note of water and came down. He was the only adult in the room and the least use in it: a surgeon who could read the order of a failing body and could not keep the wool on his own wrist, a man of forty who had reduced a thousand shoulders and could not lift his hand to his own chin. He wanted the crust two inches from his ear and the bucket's lip and the chipped cup, and could reach none of them. He wanted the number of his days said aloud, and got a loaf's worth of glances and a space at the row's end that nobody called a figure.

Before the light turned, Sella fed him from the house's own dish with two fingers behind his ear, the spoon wiped on her sleeve and his mouth not wiped, and said the house where another woman would have said my boy. The crust at his ear lay where it had been put and the spoon went past it without touching it, and the empty place beside the crust went past too, and neither was mentioned. She gave the instruction to the wool and to the pot and none to the basket, and when she had done she set the dish down and went to the fire. He wanted the crust, and the wanting was louder than the counting, and the counting went on all the same. Every warmth in the house had a payer and none was entered; the carried fire had gone unnamed for a hundred and one days, and the hands that went into the strips and the loaves that went off the board were written in no slate a man could read. He had learned in a better-lit room to ask who paid before asking what a thing was worth, and there was nobody under this roof who would have understood the question.

\scenebreak

At the going of the light Sella carried him up at the half-pace from every open slate, went around the space under the lowest rack, and set him on his bed under the Vail kiln-house roof, not in \place{the Debt Loft}. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without looking at his face and left the thread at his mouth frozen and unwiped, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left, and a strip, a shelf, a bed and a door are not openings. No opening had come in the day, and she did not touch the mark. Her private roll stayed unshown.

She told the fire it was the thaw, turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and fed it, and the fire answered with four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been. \lore{the kiln's breath} came through the floor under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, its payer unnamed for a hundred and one days. In the corner the clean dark line lay where it had always lain and the new hairline of grey, finer than the old, lay beside it unswept and would go when it went. Tallow smoke and wet wool and the faint sour tang of the unswept corner came down under the roof with the dark.

The door stood a finger's width open. Through the gap came the river's low muttered note from \place{the Ilm}, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, and through the floor came the fire's four, four and a gap, the two sounds in two separate places as they had come for a hundred and one days. The boards overhead kept four nails and four and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts did not meet. On the loaf shelf in the next room the row kept its two unnamed gaps, and in the fold at his ear one brown-edged crust lay stiff with the empty place beside it still unnamed and no third. Nobody had said a hundred and one in the yard or under the roof; nobody had said three months and eleven days; nobody had set a mark at the foot of anything that would have known what it counted. He kept one hundred and one exactly against a house that kept its days badly, and he was the only adult in every room of this day and the least use in all of them. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water going over water and came down. The count stood at one hundred and one, unspent.
