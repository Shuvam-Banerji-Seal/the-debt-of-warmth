\daymark{103}{Seed 14, Y.H. 813}

The fourth of Seed came up cold off the water, and \person{Sella Vail} carried \person{Corin} down to \place{the Fishstairs} with the wool at his ears and the little-door burn shut on the rim of her hand. At the end of the fish-trade shade where the boards ran out and no Court slate had ever reached, she set the basket down and gave the instruction to the wool and the water and never to the basket. “Keep him,” she said to the wool. “You'll have wetting enough before the week's out.” The basket got nothing. A basket set down is a pot set down and left, and she had carried it all the way from the clay road at a half-pace from every open slate, going around the reading block at \place{the Cross} without once turning her eyes toward it, and she did not begin now.

Below the shade the market was already spending its morning inside an account it would not address. Eels went over the boards in a wet, muscular ripple; slime had dried pale on the sleeves of the women who handled them and silt had dried pale in the hollows of their necks, so that they looked half made of the river itself. Salt stood white at the weir woman's wrists where the water had gone off and left its mineral. A man with a creel called a figure and a woman called another figure back, and the two figures stood side by side on the boards without either becoming the price of anything. The boy had learned in a better-lit room what three likenesses in a row will do to a man who wants an answer, and here were two figures and a morning full of hands, and nobody laying them together.

His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had been told to wipe it, because a pot set down is a pot set down and left and a basket is the same thing under another name. Cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, and the wool rode back from the wrist so the cold found the bare place. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the low note of water going over water and came down. He was the only adult in that shade and the least use in it; he had once stood over a table and known, before the first cut, where a vessel would be thin, and here he could not turn his own head without a sound.

\person{Mira Silt} worked the eels at the outer boards with pale slime dried on her sleeves and river silt dried pale in the hollow of her neck, her head held a little to the right where the top of her left ear had been, her small smile kept on her face the way another woman keeps a door on the latch. It could be shut without anybody noticing the movement. She did not look into the basket. A weir woman come down the steps with salt white at her wrists asked whether the run would be thin now that the water had gone down.

“Thin?” Mira said. “Who's to say thin? It comes when it comes. Ask the river.”

She moved eel-bones against her thumb while she decided whether to lie, and put one away when the deciding was done. The bones made a small dry sound, like seeds shaken in a closed fist, and the weir woman took the answer away with her and went on up the steps with it, satisfied or not satisfied, which was Mira's business and nobody else's.

A buyer in a coat with the river in its hem named a figure over the eels. Mira's thumb went under her apron to the grey wool cord at her waist and stayed there while she decided, and shifting a creel to reach a pair of eels showed the second cord twisted twice beneath the grey, never carried upriver to \place{Nine Pots}, never lifted at a door, the knots on neither counted by any thumb but her own and the knot for the thin child upriver untouched. Her board lay beside \person{Orra Weir}'s knot-string and did not touch it. Orra watched from where she watched and got no answer, and had got no answer for a year and would go on getting none from the basket on the boards; the boy set Mira's not-looking on a place of its own and did not join it to the figure or to the pricing, because two faces turned the same way can mean opposite things and there was no word in \lore{Grathic} that held them both.

The double turn of the second cord beneath the grey was a shape he could read without its name, the way he could read the knot a woman had tied over a story from the year of the flower without anybody saying the number that went with it. That word was not spoken in the market or anywhere within the sound of it. A man who wants an answer will lay three likenesses in a row and go home satisfied with the thing he has made; here were a cord twisted twice, a string with a count nobody would call aloud, and a woman who answered her own question, and no hand among them that would lay two turns together and call the total. He kept the shape beside the unspoken word and beside the board that did not touch, and did not let any of them become the same thing, because he had learned what a likeness laid on a likeness will grow if a hand is not there to hold it apart.

Below the shade the steps came back four, four and a landing a hand's breadth out of line where the third step should have stood, and a whole market walked over them carefully without a fall, the way a household walks around a thing it will not enter. At the landing the soft-cuffed clerk still sat with his cuff gone grey from washing and his slate held to his forearm for a measure nobody had called. His hand went to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls, and the boy kept that order on a tray of its own, filed apart from \person{Marra} with her neat dark hand, from the Drawing collector with his grey fingertips, and from the clerk behind the Court's table at \place{the Cross}. Four hands that reached for the record before they reached for the person, four places, and no sixth sighting of the neat dark hand. No two of them touched.

\scenebreak

At midday \person{Sella Vail} carried the basket three doors down to \place{House Silt} and set it just inside the finger's-width-open door at the edge of the light, away from every place a slate might lie. She opened no slate. She turned no errand into a walk to \place{Slate Row}, and stood a half-pace off from the bench with her burn shut on the rim of her hand, asking nothing and waiting for nothing that anybody would have to answer. A neighbour scrubbed at a board in the back room with lye water and did not look up. In the room the draught went under the door along the floor and reached the ankles before it reached the face, and the grey ridge along the skirting lay where it had always lain. The bench, the pot, the draught going under the door and the ridge along the skirting were the same furniture a boy could have found in the birth room where he was born, in \place{the death-house} two doors down the yard where a woman had lain and been laid out, in the kiln-house where he slept under the roof, and in \place{the Court's room} that had reached him only as a voice through a doorway, with a place in the figures where a line should have stood and did not. Five rooms, five places, the same four objects and the same gap in each, and he kept them apart the way a man keeps instruments laid out on a towel, each in its own space, writing no cause across the space between them.

\person{Alda Reed} came short into the room with soap-and-milk gone cold under river silt, lye sour under the soap, and clay still under her nails scrubbed to the quick. She laid her birth-slates writing-down on the bench before she turned to the fold. The order was the order of a hand that goes to the record before it goes to the person, and the boy watched it from the low angle of the basket as he had watched it in a midwife, at a well, behind a Court table, over a reading block and in a house that instructed a pot before anybody. She pressed the wool flat at his shoulder a breath longer than a fold requires, being careful with him in the only way she knew how, which was a way that never asked his face anything.

Then she crossed two fingers at the red edge of \lore{the birth-flower} where it ran down toward his wrist, before she knew she had done it, and covered the sign with the date she had given a woman more mornings dead than any living person keeps count of --- the seventh of Stone, the account up on the eighth. The date had stood over that death for so long now that it had stopped being a mercy and become a fact, and there was nobody alive who could take it off the sign, and nobody alive who knew there had ever been a sign underneath it.

“He lies quiet,” Sella said, to the pot or to the room or to nobody; it was not a question and had not wanted one.

“Warm and breathing, that's all I've set down,” Alda said. It was exact and it was not true, and it had been exact and not true for three years, and the woman it had been told for mercy was past the reach of any correction a living voice could make.

Alda went on with dates after the question had stopped, because dates are a handle on a thing too large to carry: a cooper's seventh in Mud End, a potter's third child in Stonemouth, the last high water before the flood, each one laid down flat and level beside the others on the bench, none of them touching the birth-slates and none of them in the order in which they had happened. Her hand went once to the little tin on its cord inside her bodice and came away without opening it. There was a sixth narrow slate turned face-down toward a floorboard in a house the boy could not name, six pages in a second hand that no Court physician had seen and that no living person had read, and no seventh page was written in this room and the record stood where it stood. Above the abandoned board-bed the round pewter tin kept its pale fold at the seam and its thumb-bright rub on the lid, shut, and the fold of grey wool below held only the hollow where the small pale river stone had once lain and that nobody said was gone. Two private records in two materials that no Court could confiscate stood apart in one room and were carried to no door. The boy could read the keeping in neither. He could read the shape of it, which was something and was worth nothing: a woman who would not show a note, a woman who would not count a knot, both of them holding warmth in a material a slate could not take hold of, and both of them standing a half-pace from a basket with their faces turned away.

\scenebreak

Sella lifted the basket at the going of the light and carried him back up \place{the Clay Yard} at the half-pace from every open slate, going around the space under the lowest rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. She said nothing about the writing on the bench, or the tin, or the fold pressed a breath too long under his shoulder. She set him on his bed under the \place{Vail kiln-house} roof, not in \place{the Debt Loft}, put the fold right at his ear without looking at his face and left the thread of drool at his mouth frozen and unwiped. One brown-edged crust lay stiff at his ear with the empty place beside it still unnamed and no third beside it in the fold.

He was one hundred and three days old and nobody under that roof had said the number, and there was no word in Grathic that would have carried it if they had. He lay in the half-light with the cold arriving cheek, temple, wrist, the wool riding back from the wrist, his hand outside the wool going up an inch toward the note of water going over water and coming down. Somewhere below the sill a woman was winding wool round a foot that stayed dark inside it and calling it the cold, while a neighbour at the tubs called the water weather; the two facts went past each other in the yard and never met, and the door off the wheel-shed kept its finger's width and no opening came under this roof on the fourth of Seed. Sella touched \lore{the widow's mark} at no hour because a door standing so is not an opening, and she told the fire it was the thaw and turned the coals with the back of a broken pot. The count stood at one hundred and three, unspent.
