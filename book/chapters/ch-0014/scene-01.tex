\daymark{61}{Silt 2, Y.H. 813}

\dropcap{T}{he brook} had come up in the night behind \place{the drying racks}, and the clay came down with it heavier than it had come in Ilmen: brown water with a grit in it that set a man's teeth on edge, a weight that dragged at the racks as though the boards themselves were being asked to carry more than they had been made for. \person{Corin} came into \place{the Clay Yard} in the basket under the fold, carried level as a pot filled is carried, with the wool at his ear and the cold already at his cheek. He was sixty-one days old. Two months gone and one day over. Nobody under the roof gave a figure for the days before the month changed its name, and he kept it exactly, as he had once counted the injured in a field before he put a hand into it. The count went on and could not be spent on a crust.

Under the work of the yard there was a sound that had not been there in Ilmen. It came from behind the racks where the brook was going over its own rise: water over water, a low continual turning with no vessel in it that he could find, the note water makes when it has more of itself than the channel will carry. It was not the early note of \place{the Singing Sill}, felt in the teeth before the ear placed it; it was not the bell told in the dark of \lore{the Long Night}, nor the bell heard at \place{the Cross}, which went four, four and a gap with nobody crossing himself; it was not the breath under his own roof, which missed one in every four and kept the missing place as carefully as the breaths that came. This was the river's own note, and the yard had no word for it that a person could go home warm on. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward it and came down. Nothing within two paces could be reached, and the sound went on without him.

\person{Sella Vail} set the basket at the rack's edge out of the draught, the way a pot is set where the fire will not take it sideways, and gave the instruction to the wool. “Keep it off his ears,” she said. “It's the river wind.” She did not say it to the basket. The little-door burn on the back of her hand closed on nothing and opened again as she settled the fold. A slate lay propped at the end of the rack with its writing open; she went around it at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and came back to the fire without having looked into the writing.

“The brook's up,” a rack-woman said, with her skirts gathered and her hands wet to the wrist. “That'll come through the yard before the month's out.”

“It'll go when it goes,” Sella said to the fire. “It's the thaw.” She fed the fire from the house's dish and did not touch \lore{the widow's mark} at her throat, because a rack was not an opening and a basket was not a door. The mark hung where it always hung on its cord, a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and her hand went past it to the pot as though it were a nail in the wall. She said the house where another woman would have said I, and gave the next instruction to the bucket and the last one to the fire.

The body went on with its small humiliations while she worked. A thread of drool froze at his mouth in the first of the day's cold and nobody wiped it, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left; his head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far; the cold came in its known order, cheek, then temple, then wrist, and the wool did less than the wind wanted. He wanted the bucket and could not reach it. He wanted the number of days spoken aloud by somebody who would know what it counted, and nobody in the yard had any use for it.

The women worked with their sleeves pushed to the elbow, laying wet pots along the boards and turning each one a quarter turn so the slip would not run to one side; the yard smelled of wet clay, tallow smoke and the river coming up through the reeds. A rack-woman set a basket down out of the draught and went back to her tubs, and the boy, who had once stood in a bay where twenty people arrived at once and sorted them without being told, noticed that the yard sorted itself the same way: what could be worked went on being worked, what could be carried was carried, and what could only be waited on was left where it lay and called weather. He had no hands for any of the three.

A second woman came up from the tubs and said the month had come early; the first said it came as it always came. Both said it standing over the pots, and neither gave a figure for what had risen behind them, because the yard had one word for a thing a person could be gone home warm on and the word was weather. The boy laid the two sentences side by side as he had laid others in other months, and left the question of which was true where it stood. He had no \lore{Grathic} word for the brown weight going over itself behind the boards. If he had had one he could not have said it.

Beyond the racks the \place{the Ilm} ran under a skin so thin that the dark current showed in veins, and the water going over water came up through the wicker of the basket and stayed there. Sixty-one days, and the only exact thing in the yard was the one he could not spend.

\scenebreak

At the middle of the day Sella carried him down under the north bridge to \place{the Fishstairs} and set the basket in the shade at the outer edge of the trade, where a pot might be set to keep the wind off it. The steps went down four, four and a landing where a step should have been, and she went down with the basket level and came up no other way. A soft-cuffed clerk sat at the edge of the landing with a slate held to his forearm, his cuff gone grey from washing. His hand went to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls, and he did not speak all the while they were there. He was not the woman in the coat at the flare light, nor the grey-fingered man who had given at the Drawing, nor the soft cuff behind the Court's table. The boy set him on a place of his own and let four places stay four.

\person{Mira Silt} worked the eels at the outer boards with pale slime dried on her sleeves and river silt in the hollow of her neck, her head held a little to the right where the top of her left ear had been. The small smile stayed on her face the way a door stays on the latch. A weir woman asked whether the run would come now the month had turned, and Mira answered the question and then her own: “Does it come now? It comes when it comes. Ask the river.” While she decided she moved eel-bones against her thumb, one and then another, and when the deciding was done she put one away in her pocket, brisk, without having looked into the basket once.

The bridge kept its shade on the boards past the middle of the day and the eel slime in it dried pale on Mira's sleeves; below the landing the water slapped the stones with the same brown weight it had behind the racks, and a brine-gull cried once and went upriver without taking anything. The boy lay with the cold at his cheek and watched the order of it: the woman who kept him did not look into the basket, and the man who would price him did not look into it either, and the two not-lookings were opposite things that stood on separate places in his keeping.

A buyer said a figure for the pair. It went over the basket and into the noise of the stalls and was gone, a number no one in the yard would have carried home in a fold, and the boy did not try to keep it. Mira's thumb went under her apron to the grey wool cord at her waist and stayed there while she decided, and the shifting of a creel showed the second cord twisted twice beneath it, never carried upriver to \place{Nine Pots}, never lifted at a door, the knots on neither counted by any thumb but her own. Her board lay beside \person{Orra Weir}'s \lore{knot-string} on the boards under the bridge and did not touch it, and the shape of the double turn was legible to him without a name in any tongue he could speak.

\person{Berr Clay} came up short at the edge of the shade, as though the ground had told him to stop there. The red wool cord of his office was folded under his coat, the green of tasted glaze sat at one corner of his mouth, and hot clay came off him over the smell of nothing at all, which is the most expensive smell in Grath. He wiped his thumb twice on his apron before touching nothing and held a narrow slate unopened against his forearm, passing a finger over what was written and letting his eyes rest on it a breath longer than the writing required.

“A number goes into a slate, not into a well,” he said. “If the house wants it again, there'll be a slate at the watch.” He took himself out of his own sentence as neatly as a hand out of a field before the dressing goes on, and went off toward \place{High Kiln} with his box still locked in his house and the true deduction inside it. He did not look into the basket.

The boy laid the figure, the cord and the unopened slate in three places that did not touch. The eel price was a sound in a market; the twist in the wool was a shape he could read and not answer; the slate was a thing a man held before he looked at a person. Three orders of reaching, none of them a cause he could write across the others. The clerk's hand on his slate went down a fourth time that he had seen. The brook's note went on under all of it, lower than the talking and not one of them.

\scenebreak

At dusk Sella lifted the basket level and carried him up \place{the Clay Yard} at a half-pace from every open slate. Under \place{the Vail kiln-house} roof she set him on his bed and not in \place{the Debt Loft}, and put the fold right without looking at his face. \person{Garl Vail} was kept as he had been kept for two years: by the salt-stiff coat holding a broader man's shape on its peg, the hollow seat keeping its twenty-year lean, the clean dark line where \lore{the Ash} ridge had turned its corner and been burned and called dealt with, and the new hairline of grey that had gathered beside it in the night and lay there still because the house said it was the thaw. The carried fire lay under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees, and \lore{the kiln's breath} went four, four and a gap through the floor. There had been no opening that day and the mark stayed untouched.

He put water-over-water on a place of its own. Beside it, but never on it, lay the early note from the Sill felt in the teeth, the told bell of \lore{the Long Night}, the heard bell at \place{the Cross}, and the four-four-and-a-gap under the roof --- four low sounds in four places, and now a fifth thing that was not a sound any vessel had given and not a hearing. Six hearings of \lore{the full note} stayed six, five from four sources and the sixth out of a woman the brook had taken twenty-three mornings ago, filed beside the five and never among them; no seventh had come. The two nearly-full answers stayed advanced and not paid. The word stayed a saying on its own tray with the readable shape of a double turn beside it and the weight of a thumb on wicker apart from both. He wrote no cause across any of them. A cause written too early was a thing he had once watched a whole institution sign, with the note beside it unread.

Nothing in the room had been joined. The grey beside the clean dark line was weather; the low sound through the door was weather; the clerk at the steps was a man with a slate; the board at the Weir was a board trodden by nobody; the early note, the told bell and the heard bell were three things in three places. The district had one word that let a person go home warm, and the boy had no word at all and could not go anywhere. He kept the count as the one entry in any room that nobody else had thought to enter, and the gap stayed the part nobody had thought to look at.

One crust lay in the fold where two had lain, brown-edged and stiff past smelling, and the empty place beside it went through another day without a name. He had wanted the breast in the first days and wanted the crust now and could not reach either, and he was the only adult in every room he had ever lain in and the least use in any of them. The door stood a finger's width open. Through the gap came the low new sound of the brook going over itself behind the racks, and through the floor came the fire's missing breath, and the two kept their separate places as the told bell, the heard bell, the early note and the gap had kept theirs for two months. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the water and came down. The count went on.
