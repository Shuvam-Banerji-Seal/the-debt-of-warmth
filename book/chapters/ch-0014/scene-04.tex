\daymark{64}{Silt 5, Y.H. 813}

The house three doors down stood with its door a finger's width open, as it had stood on the morning he was born in its front room and as it had stood on the morning they carried him out of it. \person{Sella Vail} set the basket just inside that gap, at the edge of the light where the floor was trodden ash packed until it had the spring of old bread, and she set it where no slate lay open. She turned it with her knee the way she would turn a pot, level, and left it. She opened no slate. She did not turn the errand into a walk to \place{Slate Row}. When a neighbour's voice came up from the room behind, scrubbing at something in a pot, Sella gave her instruction to the fire of the house and to the wool at the boy's ear and to the bucket by the bench, and never once to the basket. *Keep it off him,* she said to the wool. *It's the river wind.* She said *the house* where another woman would have said I. There had been no opening that morning, and \lore{the widow's mark} at her throat hung where it hung and was not touched. She stood a half-pace off from the bench where the writing lay, with the little-door burn on the back of her hand shut on nothing.

\person{Corin} was sixty-four days old. Nobody under any roof gave a figure for his days, so he kept the count himself, as he had once counted the injured in a field before he put a hand into it, and the count went on and could not be spent. Cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, and the wool did less than the wind wanted; the thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody wiped it, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far. From the low angle of the basket he saw the room the way he had first seen it, from the height at which shelves are highest, and the seeing had not changed in three years.

Above the abandoned board-bed the round pewter tin kept its place. A pale fold showed at the seam of the lid and a thumb's bright rub shone on it where a hand had worried the same spot for three years, and the tin held its scrap of birth-cloth and had not been opened in all that time. He had entered it nowhere on the morning he was taken, and he entered it nowhere now. In the fold of grey wool on the board-bed, where a small pale river stone had once lain against his belly and been left behind when Sella carried him away, there was now only the fold and the hollow in it. He let the stone lie unmentioned as the house let it lie unmentioned. It was not his to enter. A man could spend a lifetime entering things that were not his; he had seen what the slate did to a woman who did it in this room.

The wall between the two women had no door and needed none. It was kept the way the fire was kept, by small acts done at the same hour every morning: the basket set just so, the half-pace held, the question that did not get asked. Sella had never asked what Alda wrote, and Alda had never asked why a child with no house-name lay under a roof that was not his, and in three years the arrangement had acquired the solidity of a thing built, which is to say it had acquired weight and would bear on whoever came to lean on it. From the room behind came the neighbour's pot, set down and taken up, and the low song went on under it, flattened out by work. The boy kept that keeping too, on a place of its own.

Alda came short into the room a little after mid-morning. \person{Alda Reed} had the smell of soap-and-milk gone cold under river silt on her, and lye sour under the soap, and clay under the nails she had scrubbed to the quick; she carried \lore{the birth-slates} under her arm. She went to the bench first. She laid the writing-down on the bench before she turned to the fold, the way she had laid it on this bench three years ago before she turned to the child, and only then came across to the basket. She pressed the fold flat at his shoulder with two fingers, and pressed it a breath longer than a fold requires, the long press of a woman who has lifted four hundred children and knows how much a small body will bear.

Her hand went once inside her bodice and touched the little tin on its cord and came away without opening it. She did not open it in company. She had never opened it in company. The thing inside that tin was in a second hand no Court physician had seen, and it had gone on growing under a floorboard in a house he had no name for and no way to ask after.

Then she saw the red edge of \lore{the birth-flower} where the wool had ridden back at his wrist, and crossed two fingers at it before she knew she had done it. He watched the order of the thing from under the fold: the look, the two fingers, the covering. She covered the sign with the date she had given \person{Dessa Silt} three years ago, the same date, exact and unasked for. “Stone seven,” she said. “The account went up on the eighth, before the paper went out.” She said it to the room, not to him, the way one lays a board over a hole.

“He lies quiet,” Sella said from her half-pace.

“Warm and breathing,” Alda said. “That's all I've set down.” It was exact. It was not true. The middle of the night had been warm and breathing in a room where a child had been colder than anybody said, and the woman who had been told the lie had been in the ground twenty-six mornings and could not correct it, and there was no living person who could correct it for her.

Alda went on with her dates after there was no question left to answer. “A cooper's seventh in \place{Mud End}. A potter's third child in \place{Stonemouth}. The last high water.” Dates were a handle on a thing too large to carry, and she carried it by the handle, going on and on while the room arranged itself around the thing she had not said. She did not say the woman's name. She did not say the third day. The dates came faster than dates had any business coming, and the boy kept the order of reaching as he had learned it: the slate before the child, the tin before the fold, the date before the wonder, the record before the person. He knew the order the way he knew the order of instruments in a field. He could not lift a finger to put any of it back.

What he had not known, and what the room did not tell him, was that this was the morning the sixth page would be written.

Alda took up a narrow slate from under her arm. On it she wrote, in the second hand, what she would not leave lying. The hand was small and close and nothing like the neat upper line a Court clerk would have put on a birth-slate; it was the hand of the notes that began in a year before she was born and had been passed from tin to tin until it came to her, and she had added to it every year since and had never missed a year. She wrote what she had seen in the room three years ago and what she had seen in the rooms since, and what she had carried to the bank and set down on a flat stone at the edge of the water, and what no officer's thumb would ever find turned down where she meant to put it. The scratch of the stylus was the only sound in the front room besides the neighbour's pot in the back.

Sella did not ask what she was writing. She stood a half-pace off with her burn shut on nothing, and the two women kept the arrangement they had kept since the boy was three days old, which was a wall with a door in it that neither had agreed to open. When Alda had done, she turned the slate writing-down into the folds of her grey shawl, and the shawl went in against her side toward a floorboard in a house the boy could not name. The hidden record grew from five pages to six. It would lie there unread, as the five before it lay unread, and it bought him nothing he could lift a hand to. He could not read \lore{Grathic}. He could not ask which house held the floorboard. He could not put out a hand and hand the gesture back to the woman who made it. He kept the two-finger sign and the turned slate on the same tray as two truths arranged in one morning by a woman who was kind, and he did not let one become an account of the other.

Two private records in two materials stood in one room on separate trays and were read by nobody. Above the empty board-bed the round pewter tin kept its pale fold and its thumb's bright rub, holding its scrap of cloth three years old and unopened; going out against the midwife's side, the narrow slate held the sixth page of a second record and was already on its way to a floorboard. The little tin in her bodice stayed shut and went away with her. The mercy-lie told for mercy had curdled all the way through to a woman twenty-six mornings dead, and the lie stood in her face and in the dates and in the scrap of cloth above the bed, and no living person could correct it.

The neighbour scrubbing in the back room struck her pot once against the stone and went on. A low song came up through the floor, flattened out by work. From where he lay the boy could see the ceiling's brown stain and the space on the shelf where a clay jar had once stood, and he set the stain, the space, the tin and the turned slate in separate places and wrote no cause across any of them. A cause written too early was a thing he had once watched a whole institution sign, with the note beside it unread.

\scenebreak

Under \place{the Vail kiln-house} roof the afternoon went the way afternoons went. Sella came back with the basket level as a pot filled that must not be slopped, carried at a half-pace from every open slate, and set him on his bed and not in \place{the Debt Loft}. She put the fold right without looking at his face. She told the fire it was the thaw and fed it from the house's dish, and gave every instruction to the fire, the wool and the bucket, saying *the house* where another woman would say I. There had been no opening, and the mark was not touched. The clean dark line where the Ash ridge had turned its corner lay where it had lain for a year, and the new hairline of grey beside it lay there still, unswept, because the house said it was the thaw. The carried fire lay under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees, and \lore{the kiln's breath} went four, four and a gap through the floor, keeping the missing breath as carefully as the four that came.

He wanted the crust in the fold and could not reach it. One brown-edged crust lay where two had lain, stiff past smelling of anything, and the empty place beside it went through another day without a name. The adult in him called the wanting a failing of the field and the child in him called it hunger, and the hunger was louder. He had reduced a thousand shoulders in a room kept warm by other people's money and he could not keep a blanket on his own chest.

The surgeon in him had a name for what had happened in the front room that morning and kept it behind his teeth, because no word in \lore{Grathic} would carry it and the only mouth he had was a child's. He had once watched a chart with a note turned down and a hand go past it to a signature, and known even then what that hand was worth; he had signed a chart of his own once, and the institution had called it a failing of the system and handed him back clean. Here the hand went to the slate before it went to the child and the woman went home warm on it, and there was no institution to call it anything at all. He set the knowing on its tray and would not let it become a cause.

He kept the count exactly while the day went. Six hearings of \lore{the full note} stayed six: five from four sources and one out of a person, filed beside and never among, and no seventh came under any roof that day. Three near-full answers stayed advanced and not paid. The word stayed a saying on its own tray with the readable shape of the double turn beside it and the weight of a thumb on wicker apart from both. The intervals from Stone 13, Stone 17 and Stone 25 stayed objects on their own place, and he did not let any tray touch another. To the south, beyond the racks, water went over water with a low note that had no word in \lore{Grathic}, and he wrote no cause across it.

The door stood a finger's width open. Through the gap came the low note of the water, and through the floor came the fire's missing breath, and the two kept their separate places as they had kept them for two months. What he kept last was the narrow slate going face-down into the shawl toward a floorboard in a house he could not name, while above the empty bed the pewter tin kept its thumb-bright lid unopened and the woman twenty-six mornings dead kept her lie. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth and came down. The count went on.
