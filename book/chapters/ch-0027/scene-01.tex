\daymark{120}{Reed 1, Firstday, Y.H. 813}

\dropcap{T}{he wind} off \place{the Ilm} came downriver on the first day of Reed with wet mud and the smell of cut grass in it, and \place{Low Kiln} turned its face after it the way a field turns when the light goes off one edge of it. No boundary had been marked. There was no word spoken over the change and no line drawn across the ground to say that one month had ended and another had come on; the planting was behind and the reeds were ahead, and the two things stood in the same yard under the same brown skin the flood had left over the ruts. The wind had a smell he knew before he had any word for it in this country: wet mud, cut grass, and the thin mineral edge the river leaves on everything it touches. It was not strange to him, which was the strange part of it, the sense of coming back to a room after it has been rearranged and finding that you still know where the door stands. The seed stood up in \place{the worked strips} east of the houses, and the hands were out of them, and the ground kept the small dark pits where fists had let seed go and the heel-marks along the drills and the pale dust of clods broken and left where they fell. \person{Sella Vail} carried \person[Corin]{the boy} up from the rack end at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, the basket held steady the way a pot is held that must not be slopped, and gave the instruction to the wool and the wind and never to the basket. “Keep it off his ears. It's the river wind.” The instruction went into the wool and into the wind and nowhere near the child in the basket, and her little-door burn closed on the handle and then on nothing as she walked. He was one hundred and twenty days old and four months, carried where he could not have crawled, and nobody in the yard or under the roof had said so, or would have known what it counted if they had. Cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, the wet wool riding back from the bare joint so the cold found the place it had come for. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and nobody in this yard had been given an instruction about that sound. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had wiped it. He was a man who could have told you by the back of a hand laid on a brow how far a body had gone into the cold, and he could not close two inches on the rim of the basket that held him. Both things were true. The second was the one that was loud.

The reeds were spoken of as a direction and a going-downriver, never as a figure. A man come up from the sluice with his sleeves wet to the elbow said the beds would be wanting hands by the morrow, opened his mouth on what the hands would be, shut it on a direction instead, and went off downriver with the sentence still in his teeth. “Downriver, then,” a woman at the racks said to nobody in particular, shifting a pot an inch along the board so the light fell where she wanted it. “That's where they'll want us.” The woman beside her said “Downriver” back, and neither put a distance to it or a number, because a distance would have wanted a measure and a measure would have wanted a slate, and nobody in this yard had a slate for the reeds. They stood a moment with their hands on their hips looking at work that was not there yet, and went back to their pots. Two women at the rack end spoke of \place{the Weir} as a place the work would go to, not a place anybody here would stand, and of bundles in the way people speak of armfuls when there are any to carry and of glances when there are none. A bundle was a thing you saw go past and judged by the arm that carried it. Nobody set a number on a bundle standing in a field, any more than anybody had set a number on a back bent in a strip. The boy had learned the district's arithmetic early and had never once found a figure in it. It was a counting done in the open air with the hands and the eyes and the mouth, and it arrived as a direction or a pointing or a grunt shaped and swallowed, and the thing it counted was never written down. He had watched a midwife reach for a slate before she reached for a child, and a neighbour reach for a bare board before she looked at him, and a man take a number out of a fist and put it back into a sleeve; he had watched the same refusal performed a hundred times in this yard with nothing but a turned mouth and a wide step. The yard had a way of withholding a number that was older than any slate and more complete. It left the number lying where it was, like a tool left out in the rain, and went on with the work.

\place[the drying racks]{The drying racks} stood in a row along the east edge of the yard with the lowest one set a little apart, and the space beneath it was gone around as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. The broken birth-slates lay under there where they had lain since the flood, entered by nobody and spoken of by nobody, and the yard took them in passing the way it takes in a stone it has decided not to lift. The boy's basket went around the space with the rest of it. Sella's step widened there by the width of a hand and came back again, and the even carry of the basket did not change. At the corner of \place[the Vail kiln-house]{the Vail house} the clean dark line where \lore{Ash} ridge had turned lay unswept, and beside it the new finer grey that had gathered in its place lay unswept too, and the two lines were called \lore{the thaw} and left where they were. A broom stood in the corner beside them and had stood there through the planting month, and nobody had taken it up to either line. The boy had seen the broom given its instruction often enough to know what the instruction was: go around, it cost enough to come in and will cost more to take out. The instruction went to the broom and to the lines, and the lines kept their places and the space under the rack kept its emptiness and the district went on calling the going-around care. Nothing in the yard entered any of it. A place gone around with a look was still a place entered by nobody, and the yard had gone around these three places every day of the planting month and had not once called what it did by its right name.

Out in the worked strips the returning line held its gap. The hands had come out of the seed the day before last; one hand that had gone in on Seed 15 had not come out with the rest. The yard went around the place where it should have stood the way it went around the line and the space under the rack, with a look and a wide step and no number. No body had come up out of the brown ground. No cause had been said over it, no accident named, no hand identified and no figure set at the foot of what the month had cost. The place was held and not entered, carried forward into Reed the way it had been carried through the end of Seed, and the work going-downriver had already begun to arrange itself around it as the planting had. The boy read the going-around as a surgeon reads a chart laid face down on a table: a neat line drawn to close a note that nobody had opened. It was exact. It was complete. It was worth nothing. He had spent two lives learning the difference between a record that tells you what happened and a record that tells you only where to stop reading, and the field's empty place was the second kind, closed as neatly as any slate in \place{Slate Row} and with as little inside it. He had signed such a chart himself once, in a room with better light than this, and had let a note lie turned down under the signature while he went on to the next body, and the shape of that had stayed with him across two lives and crossed with him into a country that had no word for it. Here the chart was a field with a hole in a line and no signature at all, and the not-reading was done in the open air before a whole yard that leaned toward it the way a crowd leans toward a man about to speak low. He set it on a place of its own where he kept such things and did not lay it down beside anything else. Not beside the two unnamed gaps on \place{the loaf shelf} in the room that did not touch this one, not beside the one stiff crust in the fold and the empty place waiting next to it at his ear, not beside the half-beat of a checked stroke or the note of water over water or the hand that had not come back. A likeness laid on a likeness grows a cause. Three places held apart were three places; four laid together would make a cause with a clean hand at the foot of it, and the clean hand was the one object in this world he had spent two lives declining to become. The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the worked strip and came down. Nothing within two paces could be reached.

\scenebreak

A woman come up from the strips with her sleeves pushed above her elbows and the dry mud of the rows worked into the creases of her wrists. She stood a half-pace off, the way people stood off from Sella without knowing they had done it, and wiped one hand on the other and did not wipe it clean.

“What will the reeds cost,” she said, “now the hands are out of them?”

It was the same question the yard had put to the planting at the well and had put to the flood and would put to the next thing that wanted hands, and it went the same way. Sella did not answer it. She glanced once at the clay jar on its wall shelf and let her eyes rest there no longer than the glance required, and her hand went to the little-door burn and closed it on nothing and opened again.

“Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there,” she said. “The house has said it.”

The woman stood with her mouth still open on the next part of the question and found that there was nothing to put into it. She wiped her hand on her other hand again and went off down the yard with the answer she had been given, which was no answer and had the house behind it, and the boy watched her go. She carried the shape of a refusal away in front of her the way a woman carries a pot she has been told is not for her, holding it steady so nothing slops, and she would set it down somewhere out of this yard and it would stand there unanswered. Sella had given the instruction to the pot and not to the woman and not to the basket, and she had said \emph{the house} where another woman would have said \emph{I}, taking herself out of her own sentence as neatly as a surgeon takes a hand out of a field before the dressing goes on, leaving the field open and clean and nobody's. The boy set the once-glanced-at jar and the sent-away question on places of their own in the file he kept where no slate could read it. He had heard this answer before in this same yard and in a better-lit room in another life where a man took himself out of a sentence to leave a figure standing clean in it. Two places, two rooms, the same grammar. He laid no finger across them.

The going of the light came down over the yard the way a cloth comes down over a table that has not been cleared, covering what was on it without moving any of it. The reeds went on being a direction downriver. The seed stood up in the strips with the gap in the returning line gone around by every hand that passed it, and the clean dark line and the finer grey lay unswept in the corner and the space under the lowest rack kept its broken slates and its wide step. Sella carried the basket at the half-pace from every open slate with the basket steady and the burn closing on nothing at the handle, and went around the space beneath the rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. The wet wool had ridden back from his wrist and the cold had found the bare place and gone under the fold at his ear, and the thread at his mouth had frozen where nobody had been instructed to wipe it. His head wandered and made the small wet sound and came back. One hundred and twenty stood where one hundred and nineteen had stood in the small hours, exact, unspent and spoken by nobody who would have known what a number of days counted when they said it. The yard turned downriver toward \place{the reed beds} while the empty place in the worked strip was still gone around with a look and no figure, and his small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water and came down, and nothing was joined, named, paid or ended.
