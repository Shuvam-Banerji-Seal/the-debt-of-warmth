\daymark{122}{Reed 3, Thirdday, Y.H. 813}

The first bundles came into \place{Low Kiln} before the light had settled properly on the yard. Men down from \place{the reed beds} carried cut reed across their shoulders, green at the broken ends and pale grey at the tips where the last frost had taken them, and laid the armfuls in the ruts where the barley had stood a month before. They laid them down and straightened and looked at what they had laid, the way a man looks at a thing he has carried and is willing to have judged by the carrying. A woman come up from the racks lifted two fingers out of a fist and shut them again without showing what they had held. A man with a barrow shaped a number with his mouth and thought better of it and went off with the sentence still in his teeth. Nobody among them put a figure to a bundle standing in the mud. A bundle was a thing you saw go past and judged by the arm that brought it; a day's cutting was a direction downriver and a coming-back at dusk; the question of what it would cost was a question that had not yet been asked in a form anybody here could answer.

“A bundle's counted in the arm that carries it,” a reed-cutter said to the woman at the racks, wiping his palms down his thighs.

“And the hands that cut it?” she said.

He looked at her a moment with his mouth still open, and then he took up another armful and went past her with it, and the question went down the yard unanswered. It was not refused. It was simply not a question the yard had ever learned to put into words that would stay put long enough to be entered anywhere. \person{Sella Vail} carried \person[Corin]{the boy} up from the rack end at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, the basket held level the way a pot is held that must not be slopped, and gave the instruction to the wool and the river wind and never to the basket. “Keep it off his ears. It's the river wind.” The instruction went into the wool and into the wind and nowhere near the child in it, and her little-door burn closed on the handle and then on nothing as she walked.

He was one hundred and twenty-two days old and four months and two days, carried where he could not have crawled, and nobody in the yard or under the roof had said so, or would have known what it counted if they had. Cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist. The wet wool had ridden back from the bare joint and the wind found the place it had come for and went under the fold at his ear. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far and came back. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had been given an instruction about the thread. He read the arm going past with its load as a record kept in the muscle of it --- an entry made in the only material the yard allowed, and one that would be gone by evening when the muscle had cooled and the arm had gone home to its own fire. It was exact. It told a man what had been carried and told him nothing about what it had cost to carry. He set it on a place of its own and did not lay it down beside the empty place in the worked strip that the hands had gone around with a look and no number since the seed came up. A likeness laid on a likeness grows a cause, and a cause in this district always arrived with a clean hand at the foot of it. His small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water and came down.

\scenebreak

The road downriver to \place{the Weir} ran on the bank above \place{the Ilm}, which had gone down inside its banks since the flood and now moved full and brown between the willows with the light coming up along it. Sella carried the basket at the half-pace, going around the clean dark line at the Vail corner and the new finer grey beside it without sweeping either, and around the space beneath \place[the drying racks]{the lowest rack} as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. The grey hairline was called \lore{the thaw} and left where it lay. The two lines had not been crossed by any broom in three days, and the broken birth-slates under the rack were entered by nobody. At the turn of the road she told the pot it would do and said \emph{the house} where another woman would have said \emph{I}, and when a man come up from the sluice asked what the reed work would want before the week was out she gave the answer to the river.

“Ask it,” she said. “It'll tell you when it's had enough. The house has said it.”

The man wiped his wrist on his sleeve and went on down toward the water with an answer he could not spend, and the boy went past him at the edge of the work where no slate lay. That was where Sella had set him all his life when there was work to be looked at: at the edge, where nothing could be written down within reach of him and nothing could be taken up from him without a woman first putting her hand under his back. Below the bank the traps stood in the gaps of the weir with their mouths upriver, and a plank walk went out over the water on posts, and salt had dried white on the posts where the flood had touched them and gone away. The smell of the place came before the sight of it: cold fish, wet rope, tallow smoke and the green mineral edge of the river. A man who walked the traps at dusk went along the boards with his collar up and did not look down at the bank. Nobody spoke his name. He went the way the dusk went on that stretch of water, and the boy did not know him from any other man who used the walk.

\scenebreak

A reed-cutter come along the boards with a bundle on his shoulder and a short length of cord looped inside his coat. He set the bundle down on its side on the flat stone where the hearth-maids had stood to bless the traps in Ilmen, straightened the cord on his thumb, and turned it once so that the boy, low in the basket at the edge of the shade, saw the knots standing along it in the small light. He did not show it to anyone. He did not count it where anyone could hear him count. He tied one knot low down with his teeth and a pull of the thumb, put the length of cord back inside his coat, and went to lift another trap. A weir woman with salt dried white at her wrists came along the boards behind him.

“How many went up yesterday?” she said.

“You can ask the cord,” he said.

“I can't read a cord.”

“No more can the Court,” he said, and went on with the trap.

The boy held the shape of it where he had seen it. It was a tally kept in a material no Court thumb would read: wool or reed-fibre gone grey with handling, knots set at the lengths a man's arm could judge, a record that would burn if it were put to a flame and that no clerk could take up and enter in a slate because there was nothing on it to enter. He had seen such keeping before and had never once been shown it. There was the grey wool cord at \person{Mira Silt}'s waist, touched when a figure was spoken and carrying a second twist beneath it that had never been taken upriver to \place{Nine Pots} or lifted at a door. There was the knot-string lying beside her board, the last knot on it with a double turn and a tucked tail and the small weight of a thumb put there by a woman who watched and got no answer. There were six pages in a little tin turned writing-down toward a floorboard in a house the boy could not enter, written in a second hand no Court physician had ever seen; and there was the round pewter tin above a bed nobody used, with a pale fold at its seam and a thumb's bright rub on the lid, shut and unopened for more mornings than anybody in the district kept count of. Another way of keeping a count in a material an assessor would pass over, gone past him on the plank walk in a man's coat.

He did not count the knots. He did not read a figure into them, and no figure came with them to be read. He did not lay the cord beside the empty place in the strip where a hand had gone in and had not come out, because two absences laid together make an account and an account wants a payer. There was a word he had heard once said low into a basket, a word with a readable double turn standing beside it and no name laid on the turn; it stayed where it had been kept. He held the reed-cutter's cord on a place of its own, beside the other second records and not touching them, carried and not counted, worth nothing and exact.

\person[Orra Weir]{The trap-keeper} stood at the lower boards with her board on its side and a cord at her waist, pulling one knot down with her thumb and setting it among the others. When the weir woman asked her a question about the levy she gave a date instead of an answer and then went back to the knots, and when the reed-cutter lifted his bundle she watched what there was to watch at the edge of the shade and got no answer from the basket. She did not ask, and nobody asked her. The shape of the last knot on her cord was legible to the boy without its name, and he did not spend it.

\scenebreak

Mira worked the eels at the lower boards where the shade gave out, with pale slime dried in long grey strokes up both sleeves and river silt dried pale in the hollow of her neck. She held her head a little to the right where the top of her left ear had been, and her small smile stayed on her face the way a door is kept on the latch. The weir woman asked her whether the run would be thin now that the hands were out of the strips and the reeds were coming down.

“Thin?” Mira said. “Who's to say thin? It comes when it comes. Ask the river.”

She moved eel-bones against her thumb while she decided whether to lie and put one away when the deciding was done. A buyer in a coat with the river still in its hem named a figure for two pair. Mira's thumb went under her apron to the grey wool cord at her waist and stayed there longer than the figure required; shifting a creel showed the second cord twisted twice beneath it, the one that had never been carried upriver to Nine Pots and never lifted at a door. Neither knot was counted by any thumb but her own, and the knot for the thin child upriver was not touched. Her board lay beside the trap-keeper's knot-string and did not touch it.

She did not look into the basket set at the edge of the shade. She had not looked into it on any of the mornings he could remember, and her not-looking was a keeping set on a place of its own, the opposite of the pricing face across a market and joined to no cord and no figure. No crust passed between the boards and the basket, and the old brown-edged crust under the roof stayed where it had lain since the month before, stiff and past smelling of anything, with the empty place beside it still unnamed. The boy laid Mira's keeping beside the two women's records and the reed-cutter's cord and set no finger across any of them. Each keeping on a place of its own, and the place where the hand had not come back out of the strip stayed empty and entered by nobody, touching none of them. The market downriver sold its reeds and its eels over the top of everything it did not enter, and the day went on without a figure being given for any of it.

\scenebreak

By the going of the light the bundles stood grey in the ruts with nobody counting them, and Sella came back up the clay road at the half-pace, going around the space under the lowest rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. She carried him under the Vail kiln-house roof and set him on his bed and not in \place{the Debt Loft}, and put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without once looking at his face, and left the thread of drool frozen and unwiped, because nobody under this roof had been given an instruction about the thread. Her hand rested a half-beat longer than the setting needed before it went away, the way a hand rests on a thing it has carried a long way and cannot bring itself to let go of.

She told the fire it was the thaw and turned the coals with the back of a broken pot. “The house will keep it,” she said, where another woman would have said \emph{I}, and stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand counting something under her breath toward no opening. She pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn, the grey streak pale in the dying light off the coals, and set the pot where it could be found in the dark, and did not look toward the bed. The widow's mark hung where it hung, untouched; no opening had come in three days and a dusk is not one.

Under the roof the boy turned out the file as a man lays instruments on a towel and found every object where he had left it. Six hearings of \lore{the full note} stood beside and never among, with no seventh; three near-full answers stood advanced and not paid; three intervals lay on another tray; the told bell lay beside the heard bell in two places that did not touch; the word stayed unspoken with its readable double turn kept without a name; and the hand that had not come back held a place of its own. To these he added the new object: a length of cord gone past him on the plank walk and carried not counted, its knots unread, its tally kept in a material no Court thumb would read. It lay where he put such things, beside the other second records and touching none of them, held for a far-off day when a hand that did not yet exist might lay the private accounts together and call the joining an account.

One hundred and twenty-two stood where one hundred and twenty-one had stood in the morning, exact, unspent and spoken by nobody who would have known what a count of days counted when they said it. The door stood a finger's width open and the note of water over water came through the gap from a place the reed work had gone down to and come back from. His small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward it and came down. Nothing was joined, named, counted, paid or ended, and the count went on.
