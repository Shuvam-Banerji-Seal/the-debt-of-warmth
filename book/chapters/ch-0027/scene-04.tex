Midday came on over the stalls without anything marking where the morning ended and the other half of the day began. The reeds had come down from the beds in the time it took a man to turn a pot on the wheel: cut bundles stood in the ruts beyond \place{the Cross} where the barley had stood a month before, and hands that had carried seed in shut fists now carried reed in armfuls, and nobody among them put a figure to an armful or a direction. \person{Sella Vail} carried \person[Corin]{the boy} down from \place{Low Kiln} at the half-pace she kept from every open slate and set the basket at the outer edge of \place[the market stalls beyond the Cross]{the stalls}' shade, where the boards gave up their shadow and no slate lay, the way a woman sets a pot down where no passing hip will knock it. The instruction went to the wool and to the running water and never to the basket. Keep it off his ears, it is the river wind; keep it dry, it is running water. She said it the way she said everything to everything, twice, because the wool had not moved and the wind had not listened. Then she went off among the reeds to price a bundle, and the basket stood where she had set it, and no hand under the boards had been given an instruction about the child in it.

The market smelled of cut reed and river silt and the sweetish breath of glaze that came down on a bad wind from \place{High Kiln}, with hot clay under all of it and eel slime somewhere below the bridge. Pigs went over the weighing board and came off with their sides marked in a hand that would not keep the mark; a girl with a creel went over the out-of-line landing sideways, caught the rail, and went on selling; a man with a slate under his arm set the slate against the turnip heap before he looked at the turnips. The weigher put his hand to the slate before his eyes went to the pig, and the buyer put her thumb to her purse before her eyes went to the reeds, and a reed-seller took the bundle by its middle before she looked at the face of the woman who wanted it: three orders of reaching in three parts of one market, and the boy set them on three places and laid no finger across them. Voices came up out of the stalls and went down into them again, and he took them as he had taken everything since the stone at his belly went cold --- the sound first, the sense of it after, the thing itself never. Cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist. The wool had ridden back from the bare joint and the wind under the boards went under the fold at his ear and found the place it had come for. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and nobody at the stalls had been given an instruction about that sound. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and had not been wiped since.

Below \place{the north bridge} at \place{the Fishstairs} the soft-cuffed clerk was still set at the out-of-line landing. His cuff was grey from washing and grey again by midday, the colour of a thing that has been made clean in one place and dirtied in another without anybody asking which. He held his slate to his forearm for a measure nobody had called, and his hand went to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls, every time, the way a man crosses himself before he sits down to meat. The market sold its reeds and its eels over the top of him as though a man with a slate were a thing a landing always had, and nobody stepped around him and nobody asked him what he was set there for. He did not speak. He had not been taken off since the month turned, or if he had been taken off somebody had put him back in the dark, which was the same thing to a market that went on over him.

The steps came back four, four and a landing a hand's breadth out of line where a step should have been, and a whole market walked over it carefully without making a fall of it --- a girl with a creel took it sideways and caught the rail, a man with a pot stepped over and did not look at what he had stepped over, and nobody called it an accident. The boy set the out-of-line landing beside the missing fifth stroke of the bell and laid no finger across them, two things in two places, one walked over and one told in the dark. The grey of the washed cuff went on one place and the slate held at the edge on another, because a man who washes his own cuff has a hunger or a habit and a man who holds a slate where nobody else can see the figure has a use, and a use is a thing that wants a payer. A measure set down before anybody has called it is a measure set down ahead of a death; he kept that as a shape and did not make a rule of it, because the day it wanted a name would come from another direction and another mouth, and there was no hand under the boards that could have written the name down if it came.

At the market's edge, where the reeds thinned out into the ruts and the shade gave up, a foundling stood among the grown faces. He had been there a while and nobody had put him out and nobody had given him anything to do. His clothes were somebody else's cast-offs worn as though they had been measured to him: a man's shoulders on a child's narrow frame, breeches cut off at the knee, a string at his throat with a sliver of green lead-glaze on it. Silt had dried pale in the creases of his wrists, the way it dries on a man who has been down at the water and has not washed, and there was a gap where a front tooth had been. The sliver caught the light when he turned and went dull when he turned back, and he watched the grown faces all morning with the patient attention of a child who has no place in a thing and means to stay in it all the same. He said nothing. Nobody spoke to him, and he did not speak to the pots or the reeds or the eels, and he went on being there the way a board goes on being under a market that walks over it.

A kiln-hand came along the row with a pot on a tray and set it down on the boards a little way from the basket. The boy heard \lore{the full note} in it before the hand had finished setting it --- whole and full under the wall of the thing, the note a vessel gives when nothing has happened to it yet --- and his head went toward the sound as it had gone toward a hundred such sounds since he came into this body, before anyone tapped the pot and before anyone looked at it. A knuckle went against the wall and the note came back full, which was the confirmation any man in the yard would have waited for before he said a thing about the pot. Half a beat after the knuckle, half a beat after the note, the foundling's head went toward the pot too.

It stopped in the middle of the turn. The sliver at his throat caught the light and went dull again as he turned back, and he rubbed it twice with his thumb, the way a glaze-taster rubs a brush clean on an apron while he decides what to say. A kiln-hand at the edge of the row had said the tail of a sentence and left it hanging, and the child's mouth opened on the end of it the way a child's mouth opens to finish a thing heard a hundred times, and nothing came out of it that any clerk with a slate could have taken down. There was no word in the market's language for the thing that had made him turn, which was not the note and came after it, and he swallowed whatever had opened his mouth and went back to watching the grown faces.

The boy laid the delay on a place of its own. It was a thing that came after the knock and was not a hearing, not a seventh and not a vessel's note; it came after the note as a shadow comes after a body and was not the body. He set beside it his own turning toward the pot and the watcher's late turns from the days before, and joined none of them, because a thing that comes after a knock is a different object from one that comes before it and there is no word in \lore{Grathic} for the difference. Two faces turned the same way can mean opposite things: one turns to keep, one turns to price. He had seen a woman across this square turn a cup a quarter and set it back where it stood, and seen another reach past a bare board before her eyes found a child, and he had filed both without a name. Here was a third turning on a third place, and no hand of his went across to join it. He did not call it a sale. A sale is a thing that crosses from one hand to another with a figure in it, and nothing had crossed here --- no loaf, no slate, no secret spoken, no sentence finished. The half-beat stood on its tray where the other delays stood, held for the day it would land and called by a name nobody at the stalls had yet put to it, exact and worth nothing and joined to no kiln.

No loaf passed anywhere within reach of the basket, and no slate was opened within two paces of it. The foundling went back to watching the grown faces and the clerk at the landing went on holding his slate to his forearm, and the two stood in two places that did not touch, one a child with a green sliver and one a man with a cuff grey from washing. The boy's own small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water that came from below the bridge and came down without reaching it. He could not close two inches on the rim of the basket that held him; he had once held pressure in a cavity with both hands while a student counted the pulse out loud and been right about the spleen before the knife went in. Both things were true and the second was the one that was loud. One hundred and twenty-one stood where one hundred and twenty had stood in the morning, exact, unspent and spoken by nobody who would have known what a count of days counted when they said it, and the market went on selling its reeds and its eels over the top of the clerk as though the count and the slate and the hand's breadth out of line were all of them weather.

By the going of the light the stalls had given up their shade and the reed bundles stood grey in the ruts with nobody counting them. Sella came back through the reeds with a bundle under her arm, lifted the basket without looking into it, and carried him up the clay road at the half-pace from every open slate, going around the space beneath \place[the drying racks]{the lowest rack} as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. Under \place{the Vail kiln-house} roof she would set him on his bed and not in \place{the Debt Loft}, put the fold right without once looking at his face and leave the thread of drool frozen and unwiped, because nobody had been given an instruction about the thread; but the last thing in the file as the light went was not the road or the basket or the fold. It was the foundling's head stopping in the middle of its turn half a beat after the pot was set down, the green sliver going dull as he turned back, while below the north bridge the soft-cuffed clerk held his slate to his forearm for a measure nobody had called, his hand going to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls. Two late things in two places that did not touch. The boy laid them where no Court thumb would read them, and went on keeping the count, and nothing was joined, named, paid or ended.
