The afternoon came on without being announced, the way light in Low Kiln goes from the colour of wet ash to the colour of a banked coal and then to nothing. Before she took the basket down \person{Sella Vail} came to \place{the hearth} first, as a woman goes to a door before she opens it, and told the coals it was the thaw in the voice another woman would have used for a child who could not hear her; she turned them with the back of a broken pot and gave the small intake, the hold and the letting-go toward no opening, with neither gap figured and neither made to stand for a number. Then she lifted the basket and carried \person[Corin]{the boy} down the nineteen steps at the half-pace she keeps from every open slate, the instruction going to the wool and the river wind and the banked fire and never to the basket. Three doors down the Clay Yard she stopped at \place{House Silt}, whose plank door stood finger's-width open as it had stood on the day he was born out of it and on the day she carried him away, and set the basket just inside \place{the finger's-width door} at the edge of the light where no slate lay open and no writing could fall across the wicker. She gave it no instruction. A thing in a basket was a thing under a roof, and that was the whole of what she would say of it.

He was three hundred and ninety-three days old, was carried everywhere, and was told by nobody what a day was; the woman carrying him discussed the room with the pot and not with him. The adult in him had once walked into rooms like this one and read them in a single pass --- the bench, the water, the draught at the floor, the place where a hand had been --- and had been paid for the reading in a language nobody under this roof would have understood. The infant in him was held against a shoulder that smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and wanted the crust two inches from his ear. Both were true. The second was louder. Neither won, and no verdict was looked for; the quarrel was the work. He had learned that a room seen from a basket is a room seen and not entered, and that a woman who will not name a thing will still put it where she keeps it, which is a kind of telling if a man has the hands to read it, and he had not.

The surgeon took the room as he had taken it before, by its furniture and by the gap in its count. A bench by the door. A pot on the bench. A draught running along the floor to the corner where the grey lay thick against the skirting. It was one of six rooms in the district that held the same four things, with a finger between it and each of the others, and he had never once let one stand for another; a birth room is a birth room, a kiln-house a kiln-house, and the room where a slate is read on market-day keeps its own gap under its own name. The oiled paper at the window hung slack in its frame. The trodden-ash floor had a board worn smooth where a woman had stood a great many mornings. The low song came up through the floor from the back room, flattened by work until the words were only the shape of words, and under it came the small sour tang of \lore{Ash} and the sharp clean smell of lye water put on with a cloth. On the bench a birth-slate lay turned toward its owner with no figure standing on the side the basket could see.

From the low angle of the basket the pewter tin hung above the empty bed, round and dull, with a pale fold at its seam where a hand had opened it and shut it and left it, and a bright rub on the lid where a thumb had gone often enough to wear the pewter shiny. It was no taller than a man's palm and no wider than a cup's bottom, and it was set high where a child lying on the board-bed could see it and not reach it, which is where women in this yard put the things they mean to keep and not use. Pewter goes grey in the smoke and a thumb brings it back to a small bright place; the bright place is not a repair and does not hide the seam, and the boy had known a hundred vessels that carried their break in full view and went on holding. The bed beneath the tin was a board-bed of grey wool with a hollow where something had lain, and the hollow had kept its shape after the weight went out of it the way a shore keeps the shape of water that has gone off it.

The surgeon in him noted the seam as a man notes a wound dressed by somebody else: pale, dry, drawn tight, with a lip of worn metal on either side and nothing coming out of it, and he had no instrument small enough to take it apart and no leave to stand over it. Grief in this world had no object he could name, and so it had learned to attach to the small hard things a house leaves in place --- a lid worn bright, a fold gone pale, a hollow in wool that holds the shape of a weight that is gone. He let the register go over the tin and move on, because a field read out of order is a field read wrong, and there were seven places in the yard still to go around.

He did not want the tin open. He had wanted a great many things in this life and had been given none of them, and the wanting of the tin was not among them; it was a kept thing, and he had no hand two paces long to lift it and no leave to lift it if he had. The surgeon read it the way he read the wool in the empty place at his ear: a thing left where it lies is still kept, entered nowhere, and the keeping of it costs the hand that left it nothing and the hand that finds it nothing. That was all. He did not turn the errand into a visit to a bank, did not make a quest of a pewter lid, and did not write a cause across the pale fold. There was no name under the seam that he could say, no number in any thumb that he could give, and the lie told for mercy stood as a fact nobody under any roof could correct. A lie told to keep a child safe does not become true because the child grows; it curdles into furniture, and the furniture stays where it was put.

\scenebreak

The neighbour at her cloth did not look up. She had a tub of lye water at her knee and a board across it, and her hands went down and came up and went down again with the rhythm of a woman who has done the same work since before the boy was born, and the low song went on under her breath with the words worn off it. She did not look at the writing on the slate turned toward its owner, and she did not look at the tin. If she knew what the fold at the seam was, she had put the knowing somewhere a Court thumb would not find it, as this yard puts everything it keeps. Sella stood half a pace off with her hands not still, took what the house had come for --- a small wrapped thing that went into her apron without a figure spoken over it --- and straightened a chipped cup on the bench with a finger that knew the place of it without looking. “Don't set that wet on the bench,” she said to the cup, or to the room, or to the woman at the cloth; she had a way of giving an instruction to the furniture so that no person would have to own it. “You'll pay for it in the thaw.” The neighbour's hands went down and came up, and once the song broke the surface into a sentence about the cold and the price of barley and went back under with the words worn off it again, the way the yard says what it has to say and then says nothing more. Neither woman named the room or made a lesson of the visit; what the house had come for went under Sella's apron, and the tin went on keeping what the tin kept, and the slate kept its writing turned to its owner. He did not look at the tin a second time as the basket went out. A thing seen once from a low angle is seen, and a second look would make a habit of the looking, and a habit wants a hand that placed twice where no hand had placed anything but the one that shut the lid.

At the threshold Sella went around the open slate at the half-pace, the way she went around every slate whether it was written on or not, and carried the boy back up the yard saying nothing of the room, the tin, the cup or the cloth. Out in the Clay Yard the grey lay in the places the yard had left it, and she went around the seven walked-around places with a look and a wide step and no number, once and not twice, widest around \place{the returning-line place} where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out. The look stayed the length of a step and then went off the place, as a man's eye goes off a thing he has been told not to keep looking at and is not the same as never having looked. A woman going past with a pail took the long way around the same ground without appearing to know why, and the boy gave her wide step no name and no number: a wide step is a wide step and the yard had no word for what had gone into the place. The clean dark line at \place{the Vail corner} kept its finer grey beside it called \lore{the thaw}, thickened in the dry cold with the faint sour tang coming up where no broom had gone in two years; the space beneath the lowest rack kept its broken birth-slates; the gone pan's round ring kept its round place; \place{the trough} stood bare and dark; \place{the field's empty place} kept its emptiness. The three coverings --- the stayed frost, the skinned trough and the clouded plate on \place{the flat stone by the well} --- were weather entered nowhere with a finger between every pair, and \place{the river} kept its note of \lore{water over water} under \lore{the winter skin}, a covering put on by the cold and taken off by the cold, found where put and not made again. Beside the crust in the fold the wool lay folded once in the empty place, found where put again at the going back, and it was a finding still and not a placing: not a second fold, not a third crust, not a habit (a habit wants a hand that placed twice), not a way (a way wants a third), not a sign, not a gift, not a payment and not a rule.

At \place{the yard well} the day's costs came as directions and went away as weather. A man come down the clay road brought a number up out of a fist toward the pail, looked at it, and put it back into his sleeve so that the figure arrived nowhere; nobody asked him what it was, and the boy gave the fist no interior, for a fist is a fist and not a hand on a record. A woman asked what the day's work had cost and was sent to the pot with \emph{the house has said it}; the pot said nothing, and the question went off with the light as questions do in this yard. Another asked where the weather had been bought and was told to ask the weather, which had laid the cold on and would take it off, and she went back to her pail with the answer she had come for and no figure in it. Under the small sounds the boy filed the order of reaching: hand to the record before eye to the person, and mouth after both. He filed it as anatomy --- the hand arriving first where the damage is, the eyes going to the face only when the record has been read, the mouth opening last of all --- and gave no interior to any of the hands, for a hand moving first is a fact about a hand and not a confession from the person who owns it. He had seen it in a midwife laying slates before turning to a fold, in \person{Berr Clay} wiping his thumb twice before touching nothing, in \person{Hessa Rale} going up the road with the writing turned to her own sleeve, in \person{Ilva} passing a hand toward her sleeve before her eyes went to the asker, and in a collector with a soft cuff and a flat satchel setting his hand to the slate before his eyes went to a bed. The narrow slate up the road stayed unread, the notched strip stayed in its sleeve, and the twelve true subtractions stayed locked in the box up \place{the clay road} with the key gone up in a coat pocket that never came back down and no key in the boy's hand. Nine record-first hands stood on nine trays with a finger between every pair and no tenth stood up in the empty place; the clean voice that had spoken a name on \place{Weighing} stayed taken out of its own sentences on a place of its own and was not made a tenth by being clean.

Sella carried him up the nineteen steps at the half-pace, set him under the roof and never in \place{the Debt Loft}, righted the fold at his ear and under his chin without a look at his face and added no second fold. The thread had gone stiffer in the dry cold and was left where it lay, because nobody under any roof has ever been given an instruction about the thread. Under the floor the fire kept its own breathing and the river went on under its skin beyond the finger's-width door, and the two sounds kept their two places and were not joined.

At the going of the light \place{the towel} came out at the head of \place{the bed} as instruments come out on a towel when a case is done and before the next is called, and he went over the field by the place of each thing, leaving every gap a gap. The crust's gap. The shelf's two gaps. The gone pan's round ring. The field's empty place. The keeping at the fold found where put and still a finding. The told bell's gap. The silence after the name from \place{Weighing}. The place this remembering had not filled. \person{Tarn}'s stopped ear-sentence brought to the calling word at the very end of a term already ended. \person{Jorra Vail}'s promise under \place{the Vail wheel-shed} neither given nor withdrawn. \person{Garl Vail}'s unforged collector sentence lying beside the seventh notch with no name on the man who turned back at the frozen ford and no figure on the debt. Each lay on its own place with a finger between and none was made to stand up and call itself a rule, for a rule is an account that has found a payer and none stood. Six hearings of \lore{the full note} stood beside and never among with the seventh place empty and \place{the Drawing house}'s small flat knock outside them on its own place; the gaps in the yard's stories were the shape of a vessel's intervals and were not made a seventh hearing, and no story gap stood up among the six.

Answer three hundred and sixty-two was filed exact, complete, worth nothing at two inches of air, a restraint and not a payment borrowing no house count and supplying no payer, laid across neither the twelve readings, the bell's gap, the flat knock, the child's grey fingertips, the mother's cup, the covered grain, the handful, the thumb's resting, the held breath, the two bites of iron, the river's skin, \lore{the widow's mark}, the fourth direction nor the keeping at the fold. The widow's mark hung where it hung and was not touched --- an errand, a door, a room, a well, a going of the light and a bed are not openings, seven openings in seven months are seven and not a rule, and no eighth was made due on a holy day. Under the floor \lore{the carried fire} answered four low pulls with the silence where the fifth should have stood, its payer unnamed and its fifth unpaid three hundred and ninety-three days, and nobody lifted the floor and nobody supplied the pull.

The count came to three hundred and ninety-three exact, six months and two hundred and thirteen days told by nobody who would have known what a number counted. The carried fire's fifth was unpaid the same number and Garl's name was unspoken the same number, and the two stood side by side without becoming one. The surgeon took his small clean satisfaction in a field taken as found and left as found, every object in its place and nothing laid across anything else, and it cost him nothing, which was how he knew it was not peace. Peace, in the old life, had always arrived with something spent, a shift ended, a hand washed, a chart signed; this cost nothing because nobody had been asked to pay, and the accountless quiet of it was the same quiet a dry ward had after the last caller had gone and before the next came through the door. Under it, louder, the infant wanted the crust lifted, the thread wiped and a hand on the pot handle, and got none of the three. The hand outside the wool came down and did not stay, and laid itself flat on the fold as a man puts away a tool where the next hand will find it without looking. Three doors down, above a bed nobody now lay on, the pewter tin kept its pale fold at the seam and its thumb-bright lid unlifted, seen once from the low angle and left where it lay, and no name was carried across either threshold.
