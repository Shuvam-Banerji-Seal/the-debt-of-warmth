\daymark{146}{Reed 27, Y.H. 813 (Seventhday)}

Midday came into \place{House Silt} through the finger's width of open door and went no further than the bench. \person{Sella Vail} carried \person[Corin]{the boy} three doors down the yard at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, woodsmoke behind her and the river wind coming round to meet it, and set the basket just inside the door at the edge of the light, away from every place a slate might lie. Keep it off his ears, she told the wool; it's the river wind and it'll go where the water goes. The instruction went to the wool and the river wind and never to the basket. She stood a half-pace off with her little-door burn shut on nothing and asked nothing about the writing on the bench, the tin under a bodice, the sign at a wrist or the date laid over it; a basket set down is a pot set down and left, and nobody in the house had been given an instruction about any of these. One hundred and forty-six days old. Four months and twenty-six days. Neither number came through the finger's width of door with her; there was no word in \lore{Grathic} that would have held either long enough for a woman passing on the clay to set it down.

The room smelled of soap gone cold and of milk gone colder under it, with lye sour under both and the mineral tang of river silt drying on a hearth-maid's hem. The floor was trodden ash packed until it had the spring of old bread, and a grey ridge of \lore{Ash} ran from the bench under the door into the next room and was left there until the Last Day, which nobody in the room had said was near. Above the abandoned board-bed a round pewter tin kept its place with a pale fold showing at the seam of the lid and a thumb's bright rub beside it; nobody had opened it and nobody had said what it held. Below it, in the fold of grey wool, there was only the hollow where a small pale river stone had once lain, and nobody said that was gone either. A chipped cup stood on the shelf by the wall. From the room below, a neighbour scrubbing at a board with lye water sent up a low song flattened out of sense by work, and the words came through the floor the way water's note comes up through a sill, with the sense taken out of them and the labour left in.

From the low angle of the basket the boy read the room the way he had been trained to read a body laid out on a table: what had been touched lately, what had been covered, what had been left where it lay for somebody else to come upon. The carrying arm that had brought him was a record in muscle that told what it had carried and never what it cost, exact, complete and worth nothing, and the hand that reached for the record always went before the hand that reached for the person, an order he had learned where he had been allowed to touch neither. His own hand outside the fold went up an inch toward the pale fold on the tin and came down against the cloth. Nothing within two paces could be reached, not the cup, not the slate propped on the bench with its writing turned under, not the hollow in the wool where the stone had been.

\person{Alda Reed} came short through the finger's width of door with her smell arriving before her, soap-and-milk gone cold under river silt and lye sour under the soap, her nails scrubbed to the quick with clay still under them, the grey wool shawl at her shoulders and the little tin on its cord inside her bodice. She did not look into the basket. Her hand went first to the birth-slates on the bench and laid them writing-down before she turned to the fold, the order she had kept for more years than the boy could count in any tongue: the record before the child, the slate before the person, the flat of the palm knowing the weight and the shape of the stone before the fingers knew the warmth of what lay under the wool. The lower song went on through the floor while she did it, and she neither listened nor stopped it.

She pressed the wool flat at his shoulder a breath longer than a fold requires, and the extra breath was the whole of it: not a caress, not a check for warmth that would have told her anything she did not already know, only a hand left where it had been set for the time it takes to decide whether to take it away. When she took it away her hand went once into her bodice to the little tin and came away without opening it. The grey in her hair was worked in among the black at the temple, and the grey wool at her shoulders smelled of the same lye as the room below.

Then she crossed two fingers at the red edge of the birth-flower at his wrist before she knew she had done it. The sign came first and the knowing came after, as it had on the night of Stone 7 and on every morning since: two fingers laid across the branching red where the wool had ridden back, a small closed gesture made in front of a basket that could not hand it back. She covered it at once with the date she had given three doors down and years ago, laying the words over the mark the way a cloth is laid over a field before the instrument comes down. \emph{Stone seven. The account went up on the eighth, before the paper went out.}

The boy knew the shape of that covering better than she knew she had made it. He had watched a hand lift out of a field before the dressing went on, leaving the field as it had been found and the account standing in its neat margin; here the covering was done with the same care and the field was never opened, and the date stood over the sign where nobody had asked for a date, which was the whole labour of it. He did not make a cause of the two gestures. He kept the sign on one place and the date on another and the small hand that made them on a third, because a likeness is not a cause and a covering is not the thing covered, and a man who lays them together before he has a right to will find he has written an account where there was only a woman being careful.

“He lies quiet,” Sella said from the half-pace, and the words went to the basket as a pot might be addressed, not to the woman bending over it.

\emph{Warm and breathing, that's all I've set down}, Alda said. It was exact and it was not true, and it had been exact and not true for more mornings than any living person in \place{Low Kiln} kept count of. A woman lay three doors down who had believed it once and was past correcting it now; more mornings dead than anyone would count, uncollected, with the lie standing over her in a date no living person could take off. The mercy had curdled into a fact and then into a piece of the house's furniture, something the room went around the way it went around the Ash ridge, and there was no hand alive that could lift it and set it right.

She went on with the dates after the question had stopped, because dates are a handle on a thing too large to carry. A cooper's seventh in \place{Mud End}. A potter's third child in \place{Stonemouth}. The last high water, which had carried a woman away and was called weather in the telling. Each date went down into the room and lay there with the others, exact, complete and answering nothing, the way an armful of reeds is entered in the muscle that carried it and in no slate at the watch. The neighbour in the back room pushed the broom or the brush along a board and the song went on above her with its words worn smooth.

Then Alda took up a narrow slate and wrote what she would not leave lying, in the second hand that no Court physician had ever seen, and turned it face-down into her shawl toward a floorboard in a house the boy could not name. Six pages lay under it in that second hand, all unread, the sixth written long ago and never paid again; no seventh had been written and none was owed to anyone who could spell it. Above the board-bed the round pewter tin kept its pale fold and thumb-bright rub with the lid shut, and below it the grey wool kept the hollow where the small pale stone had lain, and nobody said either was gone. Two private records in two materials stood apart in one room, read by nobody and carried to no door: one under a floorboard in a house he could not enter, one on a shelf above a bed that had been abandoned before he was taken from it.

The boy could not read Grathic in speech or writing. He could not ask which house held the floorboard or hand the gesture back to the woman who made it. He kept the sign, the turned slate and the covered flower on places of their own and made no cause of them, and no name and no number out of the woman three doors down was spoken in the room all morning. The order of reaching he could read without a word: the slates to the bench, the hand to the tin, the fingers to the flower, the date over all of it like a lid laid on a pot that was not going to be opened today. He put the shape of it on the tray where the things he could not enter went, beside the charcoal under another roof's shelf and the grey cord in another man's coat, and wrote nothing across any of them.

\scenebreak

In the afternoon Sella carried him down the river road to \place{the Weir} at the half-pace, the instruction going to the wool and the running water and never to the basket. The reed work was going out of the month with nobody drawing a boundary around it; the last bundles stood grey in the ruts with their tips pale where the frost had bitten them, and a woman who asked what the hands would be paid was answered with the weather and the river, never a figure. The last market of Reed stood five days off and the third Account-day six, and neither was spoken of where the basket lay. The boy went along with his hand outside the fold, hearing the note of water over water come up off the \place{Ilm} before he could see the posts.

The \place{the plank walk} stood on its posts with the gaps between them showing the brown water going, and the flat blessing stone lay where it had always lain with its face rubbed smooth by ten thousand bundles set down and lifted up. A reed-cutter came along the boards with a bundle on his shoulder. He set it on \place{the flat blessing stone}, straightened the short length of grey wool or reed-fibre cord gone grey with the oil of a working thumb, turned it once so the knots stood in the small light, tied one low with his teeth and a pull of the thumb, bit the end off and put the cord back inside his coat, drawing the coat closed over it with the flat of his hand. To a woman who asked what it held he said \emph{You can ask the cord}, and to \emph{I can't read a cord}, \emph{No more can the Court}. He gave no figure, no name and no joining, and went up the boards to lift a trap with the question going down into the water behind him. The tally was carried and not paid, a private record in a material no Court thumb would spell.

\person{Orra Weir} stood at \place{the lower boards} with her board on its side and a grey cord at her waist. She turned the board with the wrist that had turned it ten hundred times, her hands like old rope, the pale eel scars uncovered across both forearms and salt dried white at her wrists; the grey streak showed first through the black hair when she moved her head, and the nasal half-song of the Fen came into her voice where she was tired. She pulled one knot down with her thumb and set it among the others with the small flat knock a knot makes when it is set on wood that knows it. When a weir woman asked what the levy would take now the hands were out in the reeds, Orra gave the date as she always gave it, the ninth of Ilmen, the collector's boat coming round the bend on the flood with six oars and one man at the stern who never touched them, and to the woman who said that was not the levy she said it was a date, that the river took what the Court did not, that a person could shout at either one, and that she had tried. No figure went into the afternoon. Her thumb went to the cord again at the end of the sentence, and the boy could not tell whether that was a lie or the habit of counting and made no cause of it.

The last knot on her cord showed its double turn, tail tucked, tied left over right with the small extra weight of a thumb pressed in, and the word that would have named it stayed unspoken. The forty-first double turn was legible without its name, and he did not spend it. The knot-string went back upriver to \place{the Eel House} where no clerk could spell it, and the reed-cutter's cord went back inside his coat with its knots unread.

The boy kept the two records on the boards apart: the grey cord in a working man's coat with its knots unread, the knot-string at a trap-keeper's waist with its last double turn legible and unnamed. Two private accounts within a dozen paces of the basket and no finger of his long enough to lay one across the other, and no standing to ask either of them what they kept. Side by side and not across, as they had stood on these boards through all the days of Reed, gathering toward a hand that was not his and a day that had not come.

Three doors up the yard six pages lay turned toward a floorboard with both tins shut and nobody reading either, and here on the boards the reed-cutter's cord went back inside his coat with its knots unread. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down against the fold. One hundred and forty-six went on exact and unspent with no thumb upon it.
