By midday the river road had come down off the clay and the light lay on the water in broken plates, each plate turning before it could be counted. \person{Sella Vail} carried \person[Corin]{the boy} at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, going down toward \place{the Weir} with woodsmoke behind her and the river wind coming up to meet it, and the two passed through one another at her shoulders as they had passed all morning. Keep it off his ears, she told the wool; it was the running water and it would go where the water went, and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. The instruction went to the wool and the running water and never to the basket. One hundred and forty-five days old. Four months and twenty-five days. Neither number was spoken on the road, and a woman meeting them on the boards would have had no word in \lore{Grathic} for what it counted if she had been told it.

From the low angle the world was a row of wet hems and the sky come down to the level of a knee. His head went where it wanted and the small wet sound came and went as it wandered and was not set right, and nobody on the road had been given an instruction about that sound, so it went on being made. The arm that carried him was a record in muscle that told exactly what had been carried and never what it cost: level, half-paced, going wide around every open slate. He read it once and set it down. The note of water over water came up off the \place{Ilm} and went into the wool with him, a fifth low thing that nobody counted among the hearings, and his hand outside the fold went up an inch toward it and came back against the cloth. Nothing within two paces could be reached, not the bundle, not the board, not the cord inside the coat.

\place[the plank walk]{The plank walk} stood on posts out over the water, and the boards gave back a hollow small sound under a boot, the sound a pot makes under the knuckle when the glaze has worn through in one place. A reed-cutter came along them with a bundle on his shoulder and a short length of grey wool or reed-fibre gone grey with the oil of a working thumb looped inside his coat. He set the bundle on \place[the blessing stone]{the flat blessing stone}, which kept its cut mark turned away from the light, straightened the cord on his thumb, turned it once so the knots stood, tied one low with his teeth and a pull of the thumb, bit the end off and put it back inside his coat with the flat of his hand. He drew the coat closed over it. The cord went grey in the seam the way a stitch-hole goes grey after a thread has been drawn through it a dozen times, and a Court thumb would have found nothing in it to spell.

A weir woman with salt at her wrists asked what it held. “You can ask the cord,” he said.

“I can't read a cord.”

“No more can the Court.” There was no anger in it and no smile, and he went to lift a trap with the question going down into the water after him.

The boy watched the cord go in and come out. It was a record in wool, exact and complete and worth nothing, and it told what had been carried and not what it cost, the way the carrying arm did. He did not read the knots. He did not lay the cord beside the empty place in the returning line where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out, or beside the eel-woman's two cords down at the boards, or beside the trap-keeper's string of knots that lay a dozen paces off. Three keepings in three materials stood within reach of his eyes and outside the reach of his hand, and none of them was entered anywhere. The reed month went on going out without a boundary drawn at its going, and the tally went up the boards unread.

\scenebreak

\person{Orra Weir} stood at \place{the lower boards} with her board on its side and a grey cord at her waist, turning the board with the wrist that had turned it ten hundred times. Her hands were like old rope, the pale eel scars lying uncovered across both forearms, salt dried white at the wrists; the grey streak showed first through the black hair when she moved her head, and the nasal half-song of the Fen came into her voice where she was tired, as it had come into it every morning since the boy had known her. She pulled one knot down with her thumb and set it among the others with a small flat knock, the knock of a board laid down on a board.

A woman at the rails asked what the levy would take now the hands were out in the reeds. Orra gave a date.

“The ninth of Ilmen. The collector's boat came round the bend on the flood and had six oars. One man stood at the stern and never touched them.”

“That isn't the levy.”

“It's a date. The river takes what the Court doesn't. You can shout at either one. I've tried.” She went back to the knots.

A date given where a figure was asked was an answer with the question taken out of it, a chart laid face-down over a note nobody had opened with the neat margin left showing; the boy set it on its place and made no cause of it. The man at the stern who kept his hands off the oars stood on a place of his own, apart from the midwife's reaching hand, the thane's twice-wiped thumb and the clerk's hand going to a slate before his eyes went to a stall; four shapes in four places, and no finger of his drew them into one. Orra's thumb went to the cord again at the end of the sentence, and he could not tell whether that was a lie or the habit of counting, and made no cause of that either. The last knot on the cord showed its double turn, tail tucked, tied left over right with the small extra weight of a thumb pressed in; legible, complete, worth nothing, and the word that would have named it stayed where it had stayed since the year of the flower, behind her teeth and out of the morning. He read the shape and did not spend it.

\scenebreak

Downriver again, below \place[the Singing Sill]{the sill} where the water went over in a low voice, \place{the fish-trade shade} lay under \place{the north bridge}'s shadow and the eel stalls thinned out at its outer edge. \person{Mira Silt} worked with pale slime dried in long grey strokes on her sleeves and silt dried pale in the hollow of her neck, her head held a little to the right where the top of her left ear had been, her small smile kept on her face the way a door is kept on the latch. A woman come down to the shade with salt white at her wrists asked whether the run would be thin now the last bundles were going down.

“Thin? Who's to say thin? It comes when it comes. Ask the river,” Mira answered her own question, and flicked an eel-bone against her thumb while deciding whether to lie, and put one away when the deciding was done. She said something low about a boat that had come up on the flood and nothing about the hand that had not come out of the strips; the boy could not hear the low part and did not try to.

A buyer in a coat with the river in its hem named a figure for two pair. “Two marks the pair and two short,” Mira said. “You've said it twice and I've counted it twice. There's nothing in the sentence that wants an answer.” Her thumb went under her apron to the grey cord and stayed there longer than the figure required, and shifting a creel showed the second cord twisted twice beneath it, never carried upriver to \place{Nine Pots}, never lifted at a door, the knots on neither counted by any thumb but her own and the knot for the thin child upriver untouched. She brought no crust and did not look into the basket. Her not-looking was a keeping set on a place of its own, joined to no pricing, and the opposite of the pricing face turned the same way across a market; he had no word in Grathic for either. Her board lay beside Orra's knot-string and did not touch it. Three private records stood within a dozen paces of the basket and none could be entered.

\scenebreak

Below the shade at the tubs a child's foot stayed dark inside three turns of wool, and it was the cold to the woman who had wrapped it and weather to the yard. A neighbour come down with a pail said it would go when it would go, and the woman who had wound the wool said nothing, because there was nothing in the yard that had been given an instruction about the foot beyond keeping the wind off it. The boy read it with the part of him that had once read such things before anybody asked him to: the extremity goes before the core, the fingers and toes and the small dark foot giving first while the warm centre holds on and gives last; the face of a person deep in the cold can wear the shape of peace without any peace in it; and the afterdrop waits past a warming that nobody in the yard intended to perform, to take back what the yard thought it had given. He knew the order of it the way he knew the order of a hand going to a record before it went to a person. It worked perfectly and bought him nothing. There was no hand within two paces to put on the foot, no warm stone to set against it, no instruction anybody had given him to give, and his own hand outside the wool went up an inch and came down against the fold. He wrote no silt-leach, no cause, no cure and no recognition across it.

\scenebreak

At \place{the out-of-line landing} below the north bridge the soft-cuffed clerk was still set for a measure nobody called, his cuff grey from washing and grey again by midday, his slate held to his forearm with the writing turned inward, his hand going to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls, whether or not there was a figure to enter. Nobody stepped around him. Nobody had taken him off since the month turned. The market sold its reeds and its eels over the top of him as water goes over a stone that lies where a stone lies. He was not \person{Marra}, and he was not the grey-fingered man at the Drawing, and he was not the child in cast-offs at the market's edge; the boy kept the three apart by refusing to name any one of them into the others, and set the clerk's order of reaching on a place of its own beside the midwife's reaching hand, the thane's twice-wiped thumb and the trap-keeper's thumb going to the cord as a sentence ended. A measure set down before anybody called it is a measure set down ahead of something; he kept that as a shape and did not make a rule of it.

\scenebreak

Sella set the basket at the edge of the work where no slate lay and went half a pace off, and gave the instruction to the wool and the running water and never to the basket. Keep it off his ears, she told the wool; it's the water and it'll go where it goes. Then she stood the way a woman stands over a pot she has set down and will not look into, and the boy kept the three records where they lay. The reed-cutter's tally was inside a coat on the upper boards; Orra's knot-string was at a waist at the lower boards; Mira's grey cord and the second twist beneath it were under an apron at the eel boards. Three cords in three materials on three boards, side by side and not across, with no knot counted among them and no Court thumb set to any. He kept each on a place of its own and did not lay one across another; a joining was a thing he had no standing to make, and no payer had been named to any of them in a hundred and forty-five days.

One hundred and forty-five lay where the day had left it, exact and complete and entered nowhere, carried down the river and back up again in a woman's arm that told what it carried and not what it cost. The word that would have named the last knot stayed behind a trap-keeper's teeth. The reed-cutter drew his coat closed over his cord with the flat of his hand; Orra set one more knot among the others with a small flat knock; Mira put her thumb under her apron and kept it there a breath longer than the figure required. Three cords lay on three boards with no knot counted, and below the shade a child's foot stayed dark inside three turns of wool.
