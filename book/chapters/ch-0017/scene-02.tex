\daymark{79}{Silt 20, Y.H. 813}

\person{Sella Vail} carried him down out of \place{the Clay Yard} at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, the basket held against her hip the way a woman carries a pot filled to the lip and does not trust the road. The mud had dried in the night into a pale skin with long shallow seams in it, and here and there a stone the water had turned lay with its wet side still dark, the way a wound shows under a scab when a hand goes looking. \person{Corin}, seventy-nine days old, went where he was carried with his cheek against the fold and his hand outside the wool. The cold had him by the ear before they reached the road. It came in its known order, cheek and temple and wrist, and the wool rode back from the wrist as though the wind had a hand there and meant to find the place.

“Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. “It's the river wind.”

The wool did less than the wind wanted, and she did not say it to the basket. She said \emph{the house} where another woman would have said I, and she kept the half-pace without seeming to keep it, the way a woman keeps a finger on the edge of a hot pot without looking at the fire.

At \place{the Cross} she set the basket down in \place{the moot-post}'s finger's width of shade with his cheek to the cold iron, and did not look into it. Iron at the cheek is a cold with a shape, flat and without mercy, and he lay with the side of his face against it and measured it against the warm side. A reading was not an opening; \lore{the widow's mark} hung where it hung under her collar and she did not touch it, because no opening came with a figure and a figure was all this morning had to give. She stood a half-pace off with the little-door burn across the back of her hand shut on nothing, facing the square and not the basket, the way a woman faces a pot she has set down and will not be answerable for.

The square had four ways out and he counted them without deciding to: the road down to Slate Row, the steep street up to \place{High Kiln}, the north bridge, and the road that went toward \place{the Bone Orchard}. On Account-day the four ways had been choked with people leaning in; this morning they were not. The crowd stood thinner than at the flood's reading, and the thinness was not in the figure anybody would give for it. It was in the gaps between shoulders, in the stall at the turnip heap where two women did the work of three and did not hurry, in the pig-board with a rail propped under one end where a man's hand had been, in the space where the family from the low houses used to stand and now stood nobody. The water had taken bodies out of the market and the lean week had taken more, and what remained went on with the selling in the same voices, as though a room had lost a corner and the people in it had agreed not to look at the wall.

“There's no Hobb at the trough,” a woman said, setting down a basket with room in it. “Nor his two. The water took the step from under them and they've not come back.”

“They'll come when they come,” the other said, and went on laying out her turnips in a row that had a gap in it she did not close.

The market had a grammar for a household gone as for a step gone: it comes when it comes, and a person goes home warm on the saying. The boy kept the gap in the turnip row on its own place with the gap in the crowd and the space at the end of the loaf shelf up the yard, three absences that did not become one absence because he did not put a hand to any of them.

He had learned in a better-lit room that a chart can close over a thing left lying and still be a beautiful chart, and here the whole square was a chart closed over what the water left. Nobody counted the empty places. The house up the yard kept its days in loaves pushed along a shelf and a space at the row's end nobody would name; the district kept its Account-day in one in twelve printed and unchallenged and the true subtraction spoken by nobody. Two arithmetics, one neat and one that would not be said, and the space between them was where the bodies had gone.

\person{Berr Clay} came up to the block at the middle of the morning. The red wool cord of his office was folded under his coat instead of worn across it, and the green of tasted glaze showed at one corner of his mouth, a small wet leaf of colour that he did not wipe away. He smelled of hot clay and of nothing at all under it, which is the most expensive smell in \place{Grath}. His hands were large and clean. He wiped his thumb on his apron before he touched nothing, and then he wiped it a second time, and only after that did he let his finger pass over the figure on the narrow slate he held against his forearm. The crowd leaned in because he never raised his voice and they had to.

“One in twelve, as the notice says,” he said, low, into the top of the thinner crowd.

He let the words lie a moment so that the households which had heard otherwise could hear they had heard wrong. Then he passed his finger over the figure once more, the way a man checks a clamp before he lets go, and put himself out of his own sentence.

“The levy is called. It is not called by me. There'll be a slate at the watch if the house wants it again.”

He did not say I. He said the notice and the levy and the house, and took himself out of the sentence as neatly as a surgeon takes his hand out of a field before the dressing goes on. The figure stood as read. The crowd went back to the pigs.

All of this went over the top of the basket. The true subtraction went over it too, unspoken, in the space between one shoulder and the next where a man from the low houses had stood last Account-day and a woman with a wet hem had stood before the water. He lay in the moot-post's finger's width of shade and heard the printed figure and the true one pass on two trays that did not touch. A figure read aloud is not the figure paid. A figure let fall from a fist in the direction of a well is not entered either; he had watched one go from a fist to a shoulder and into the general air three days ago and arrive nowhere, and the yard had gone on sweeping around what the water left. The printed figure would go into a slate in a good hand and be read again next Account-day over fewer people, and the empty places would stay exactly where they were, which was in no account at all.

Berr went off toward High Kiln with the red cord folded square and the box in his house still locked and the true deduction inside it. He believed he had done the district a kindness, and there was no cruelty in his face, only the clean thumb and the green at the mouth and a good reason standing where a man should have stood. The boy kept that on its own place and wrote no cause across it, because a better-lit room had taught him what a good reason looks like when it has been worn for fifteen years.

The bell went then. It struck longer than a church bell, four, and four, and a gap where the fifth should have been, and went on ringing in the square after the strokes had stopped. Nobody crossed himself. A woman shifted a pig and a man called a price and the figures on the turnip heap stood side by side without being made to agree. The boy kept the heard bell beside the told bell from the Long Night story, and both beside the early note out of the stone and the kiln's own missing breath under the roof up the yard, four things in four places, and made nothing of the likeness, because four likenesses in a row will grow a cause whether or not a hand puts one there.
\scenebreak

At the Slate Row fringe of the square the \lore{Court} had set its table in the open air, as it did on market-days. The clerk behind it had a cuff gone soft from washing turned back twice from his wrist and ink in two fingers of his writing hand. When a woman came to the table he put his hand to the top slate before his eyes went to her face, and said it was entered as it stood and there was nothing in it the household need correct. The boy watched the order of the reaching from the moot-post's shade: hand, then slate, then person last. He filed it on its own tray. It was not Marra, whom he had known only as a coat and a pause at the edge of flare light in the other life; it was not the grey-fingered collector at the Drawing; and it was not the soft-cuffed clerk who sat down the road at the Fishstairs. Three hands that reached for the record before the person stood in three places on his private file and did not join. No neat dark hand showed itself in the square this morning. The pause he had seen five times in five places did not come a sixth, and he did not go looking for it.

“It's entered as it stands,” the clerk said again to somebody who had begun to ask, and the somebody went away as though answered, which in this district is the same thing as being answered.

In the late morning \person{Sella Vail} lifted the basket off the post and carried him down the road toward the river. The road went downhill and the mud on it had set hard enough to take a heel without giving it back, and she walked as though the basket were a thing that might spill if she hurried. Below the north bridge the \place{Fishstairs} had come back out of the water four steps, four, and a landing a hand's breadth out of line where the fourth step should have met the fifth. A whole market went up and down over the out-of-line landing carefully all morning, with skirts gathered and creels tilted, and nobody made a fall of it and nobody propped it. The boy set the landing beside the weak board in the plank walk that was gone or trodden flat under silt, and beside the hairlines in the ice that had stopped a thumb's breadth short of the edge, three places that did not touch and had no word written across them.

The soft-cuffed clerk sat at the landing with his slate held to his forearm for a measure nobody had called. His cuff was grey from washing and turned back twice, and his hand went to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls, over and over, while the eels were sold above the top of him. Nobody had taken him off. He was not Marra, not the Drawing collector, not the Court-table clerk up at the Cross, and his order of reaching stayed in the fourth place on the tray where the boy had set it, apart from the other three. No sixth sighting of the neat dark hand came with him down the road; there was only the ordinary hand of an ordinary clerk set at a step for an account nobody had asked for, ahead of a death nobody would enter.
\scenebreak

\person{Mira Silt} worked the eels at the outer boards below the bridge with pale slime dried on her sleeves and river silt in the hollow of her neck, her head held a little to the right against the cold in the place where the top of her left ear had been. The small smile stayed on her face the way a door is kept on the latch: it could be shut without anybody noticing the movement. She did not look into the basket when Sella set it down in the shade where no slate had reached.

A weir woman come down the steps with salt white at her wrists asked whether the run would come thin after the water had gone down.

“Thin?” Mira said. “Who's to say thin? It comes when it comes. Ask the river.”

She flicked an eel-bone against her thumb while she decided, moved two more from hand to hand, and put one away when the deciding was done. The answer had been her own, and it had given her the room to choose which answer the woman got.

A buyer in a coat with the river still in its hem named a figure for a pair, and named it again lower, the way a man repeats a weight he hopes will be taken differently the second time. Mira's thumb went under her apron to the grey wool cord at her waist and stayed there while she decided. Shifting a creel showed the second cord twisted twice beneath the grey, never carried upriver to \place{Nine Pots}, never lifted at a door, neither knot counted by any thumb but her own and the knot for the thin child upriver not touched. The two cords lay one above the other and did not change places.

“Two marks the pair and two short,” she said. “You've said it twice and I've counted it twice. There's nothing in the sentence that wants an answer.”

The buyer went away with the eels. Mira put another bone away and went on with her knife. It was six years that Berr paid her two marks short of six and she gave him two eels short of six, and neither had ever put the difference into a sentence; the small war went on under the figure like a seam under a patch, held and entered nowhere. Her board lay beside the knot-string board of \person{Orra Weir} and did not touch it. One woman counted what had been taken and one counted what had been kept, and the boards lay side by side in the fish-smell and the cold with a finger's width of air between them that no clerk had thought to enter. Her not-looking was a keeping where Berr's not-looking at the Cross had been a pricing, and he kept the two on places that did not touch and had no word in \lore{Grathic} for either.

He wanted the crust in the fold and the breast that had gone out of the world before he had a name for wanting it, and the eels went over his head in the noise of the stalls and were gone. One brown-edged crust lay stiff at his ear with the empty place beside it still unnamed, and the buyer's figure went into the noise and was spent there, and the printed figure stood up at the Cross unchanged over a crowd that had fewer people in it to hear it.
\scenebreak

In the late afternoon she carried him back up the road and set the basket off the post again for a moment while she settled the wool with a hand that did not go near his face. The cheek that had been against the moot-post kept its shape of cold after the iron was gone; he carried it up the Clay Yard with him as the body carries a thing pressed into it long after the pressure stops. The square behind them went on with its arithmetic in the same voices, one in twelve and the true subtraction, the heard bell and the told bell, the empty places that nobody entered, the box locked in High Kiln and the mark untouched under a collar. The printed figure and the true one lay on two trays that did not touch. The count went with him exactly, as it had gone through seventy-eight, and the house up the yard would keep its days badly in loaves and glances and a space at the row's end that nobody called a number.

His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the water and came down. The count stood at seventy-nine.
