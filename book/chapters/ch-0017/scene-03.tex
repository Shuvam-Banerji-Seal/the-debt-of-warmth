\daymark{80}{Silt 21, Y.H. 813}

At the middle of the day \person{Sella Vail} carried him three doors down \place{the Clay Yard} at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, the basket held against her hip the way a woman carries a pot filled to the lip and does not trust the road with it. The mud had gone hard in the night and took a heel without giving it back, and the ruts stood with their edges sharp in the light, so that the yard looked less like a place that had been under water than a place that had been cut and left to dry. \person{Corin}, eighty days old, went where he was carried with his cheek against the fold and his hand outside the wool. He was not told where they were going. Nobody tells a basket anything. A pot is carried where it is carried and is not consulted, and he learned the errand the way a body learns a room --- by the light, by the smell, by the thing that is in the place where a thing should be.

She had said nothing all morning that required an I. She said \emph{the house} where another woman would have said I, and gave her instructions to the fire and the bucket before she gave them to anybody, and when she set the basket down nothing in it was addressed. The half-pace held without her seeming to hold it, the way a woman keeps a finger on the edge of a hot pot without looking at the fire. He knew they were going to \place{House Silt} before the door came into view, because the light off the wall of the shed went off at the third door and came back thin through a door standing ajar. He had been carried to that door before and had lain on the floor of that room while it was still a birth room and not a room with an empty bed in it.

The door stood a finger's width open. She set the basket just inside it, at the edge of the light and away from every place a slate might lie --- away from the bench where the writing would be laid, away from the flat stone, away from the shelf, away from the shadow a writing hand might fall into. It was a placement a clerk would not have noticed and a mother would have. She stood a half-pace off with the little-door burn across the back of her hand shut on nothing, facing the room and not the basket, the way a woman faces a pot she has set down and will not be answerable for. She opened no slate. She turned no errand into a walk to \place{Slate Row}. \lore{the widow's mark} at her throat hung where it hung and she did not touch it, because a room with a bed in it is not an opening and a basket is not a door.

From the low angle of the basket the room arranged itself in the order a room arranges itself for a person who cannot lift his head. The bench by the door. The pot on the bench. The draught under the door going along the floor to the corner, felt at the ankle before the cold is felt at the cheek. The brown ceiling stain above the bed, the shape of a spilt thing nobody had scrubbed out. Four nails on the near board, four on the next, and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb. He counted them without deciding to, as he had counted them in this room when the room had been a birth room, and the count had not changed and the room had.

Above the abandoned board-bed the round pewter tin kept its place. He had seen it before from this same height and had entered it nowhere. It held a scrap of cloth from a birth, folded, and the fold showed pale at the seam of the lid, and the lid had a thumb's bright rub where somebody had worried it over years without ever lifting it. The rub was brighter than the tin around it, the way a handled thing is brighter than the shelf it stands on. A hand had gone to that lid often enough to polish it and had never once gone under it. Nobody had opened it. Nobody had said what it held beyond the scrap, and nobody said it now.

Below it the fold of grey wool lay where it had been laid on the third morning of his life, and it held only the hollow where the small pale river stone had once lain. The wool kept the shape of the stone the way a bed keeps the shape of a body that has got up and gone. He knew the hollow without being told; he had lain against the stone when it was warm and had felt it go from warm to not-warm while the women arranged the room around a child who was being given away. The warmth had crossed to him there and had not been entered against anybody, and the stone had been left behind when he was carried under another roof. Nobody said the stone was gone. The house had left the hollow where it was and left it unnamed, and the boy who had been warmed by the stone lay in a basket on the floor and could not reach the fold to put a hand into the place where it had been.

\scenebreak

\person{Alda Reed} came short into the room with the birth-slates under her arm and the smell of soap-and-milk gone cold under river silt coming ahead of her, and under the soap the sour tang of lye. She smelled of a house where somebody else's child had just been washed and somebody else's floor just scrubbed. Her nails were scrubbed to the quick and still had clay under them, the clay that stays under a nail after a day of births and stays there through the scrubbing. She was built like a woman who has spent her life lifting other people's children, with the stoop that comes from leaning over beds, and her hands were steady. When a mother grabs a wrist rather than an arm, it is these hands she grabs.

She went to the bench before she went to the basket. That was the order of her and had not changed in three years and would not change. She laid the birth-slates writing-down on the bench, the writing turned under, and only then turned to the fold. The boy watched the order of the reaching from the floor --- hand to the record first, person last --- and set it on the tray where he kept the other hands that reached that way: the midwife who had delivered him, the neighbour at the well, the clerk behind \lore{the Court}'s table, the man who priced him, Berr passing a finger over a figure, the soft-cuffed clerk at the Fishstairs. The tray was getting full, and none of the hands on it had joined, and he had long ago stopped expecting them to.

“He lies quiet,” Sella said. It was not a question and was not put to Alda exactly; it was put to the room, the way a woman says a thing to the fire.

Alda pressed the fold flat at his shoulder. She pressed it too long --- a breath longer than a fold requires, the way a hand will stay on a thing after the thing has been done to it. Her fingers still had the warmth of the milk in them and the clay under the nails, and they stayed at his shoulder after the wool was already flat.

“Warm and breathing,” she said. “That's all I've set down.”

It was exact and it was not true. She had said it for three years in rooms where a child could not answer her, and the exactness was the lie. A beautiful entry standing where a true one should, and nobody alive to take it off the sign.

Then she saw the red edge of the birth-flower where the wool had ridden back from his wrist and crossed two fingers at the corner of it before she knew she had done it. The sign came into her hand the way a habit comes --- not decided, not addressed to anyone, not even to the child whose wrist it was. She covered it with a date nobody had asked for: the seventh of Stone, the account up on the eighth. It was the date she had given the woman forty mornings gone. She laid it over the sign as a woman lays a cloth over a thing she does not want looked at, and went on with dates after there was no question left to answer.

“A cooper's seventh in \place{Mud End},” she said. “A potter's third child in \place{Stonemouth}. The last high water came a day earlier than the slate had it.”

Sella said nothing to any of it. She stood a half-pace off with her burn shut on nothing, and the room went on with its work. Dates are a handle on a thing too large to carry, and Alda had carried this one three years by the handle and would go on carrying it, and the dates would keep arriving whether anybody asked for them or not.

The mercy she had told for a child had curdled all the way through to a woman forty mornings dead and uncollected, and no living person could take the date off the sign. The woman had believed it to the last --- believed her child spent among children she had never seen, gone in the levy three winters ago for a whole return she could not have read the writing of --- and had gone into the clay-heavy water with the belief standing in her face like a door shut. Three years a kindness, and a kindness entered is a kindness that cannot be un-entered. The number had stayed in her thumb and the name under the sieve-wire scar, and neither had come up out of the water with her, and the date Alda had laid over the sign now stood over a death as though it had been a true account of it. The boy could not read \lore{Grathic} in speech or writing, could not hand the gesture back, could not ask which floorboard in which house held what she wrote. He kept the sign and the date on a place of their own, and the surgeon in him noted that a beautiful entry can stand where a true one should and stay there, and that a room will arrange itself around the beautiful one rather than the true one, and that this is not an accident of rooms but a habit of the people in them.

Her hand went once inside her bodice to the little tin on its cord and came away without opening it. It was the shape of a reaching he had learned to read: the hand goes to the record before it goes to the person, and comes back with nothing, and the person is none the wiser. The tin stayed shut. Above the bed the pewter tin stayed shut. Two private records in two materials stood in one room on separate trays and did not touch, and neither was carried to a door.

On her narrow slate she had written, in a second hand no Court physician had seen, what she would not leave lying about. The slate lay turned face-down toward a floorboard in a house the boy could not name. The hidden record stood at six pages, all unread, and he did not write a seventh for her and did not pay again what had been paid on an earlier day. Six was enough. Six pages of a thing no officer would think to look for, in a hand that was not her own hand, turned down toward a board in a house he could not have found if he had been able to walk. He kept the number six on its own place and did not add to it.

In the next room a neighbour went on scrubbing at the floor with a cloth, the sound of it going along the boards in a steady unhurried way, the way a woman scrubs when the floor has been scrubbed already and she does not want to stop. She did not look up at the writing or the tin. The room had a door standing a finger's width open and a sound going through it and a woman going on with her cloth, and nothing in the next room was entered by anybody. The boy kept the two private records on their separate trays, and beside them a third board standing downriver where \person{Mira Silt} counted what had been taken and \person{Orra Weir} counted what had been kept, her knot-string lying on it, and neither board touched the other. He read none of them. He could not have read a word of any of them, and the shape of them was all he had, and the shape was enough to keep apart and not enough to join.

\scenebreak

At dusk Sella lifted the basket off the floor with her knee and carried him back up the yard at the half-pace, saying nothing about where they had been or what had been written in the room they had left. The pewter tin stayed above the empty bed with its pale fold and thumb-bright rub. The turned slate stayed face-down toward its floorboard in a house he could not name. The hollow in the grey wool stayed where the stone had been, and nobody had said it was gone. Two women had kept the same child that day by writing down things they would not show each other or anyone, and the keeping was exact and the exactness was the lie, and between the two trays there was a finger's width of air that no clerk had thought to enter.

Under \place{the Vail kiln-house} roof she set him on his bed and not in \place{the Debt Loft}, put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without looking at his face, and left the thread at his mouth frozen and unwiped, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. “It's the river wind.” The wool did less than the wind wanted, and she did not say it to the basket.

The door stood a finger's width open. Through the gap came the river's low muttered note, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, and through the floor came the carried fire, four, four and a gap under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, its payer still unnamed. He kept the two sounds in two places as he had kept them for eighty days. Six hearings of \lore{the full note} stood at six, three near-full answers stood advanced and not paid, the word forty lay on a place of its own with the readable double turn beside it, and one brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold with the empty place beside it still unnamed, and none of these had joined and none had been spent.

His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the water and came down. The count stood at eighty.
