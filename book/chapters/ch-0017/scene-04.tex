\daymark{81}{Silt 22, Y.H. 813}

Before the light came into the yard the boy lay on his bed under the \place{Vail kiln-house} roof with the door a finger's width open and the dark divided by it into two places. Through the gap came the river's low muttered note, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming. Through the floor came the carried fire, four, four and a gap under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees and missing the breath that should have been the fifth. He kept the two sounds where he had kept them since the first day and neither went into the other. One brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold at his ear with the empty place beside it still without a name. Cold arrived in its known order --- cheek, temple, wrist --- and the wool did less than the wind wanted, riding back from the wrist so the cold found the place. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the dark and came down. \person{Corin} was eighty-one days old and nobody had told him so.

\person{Sella Vail} lifted him into the basket before the yard had properly woken, the little-door burn across the back of her hand closing first on nothing and then on his weight, and carried him out into \place{the Clay Yard} at the half-pace she kept from every open slate. She said \emph{the house} where another woman would have said I, and gave the morning's instruction to the wool before she gave it to anybody: keep it off his ears, it's the river wind. The mud had gone hard in the night and held the print of a boot without letting go, and the ruts stood with their edges sharp, so that the yard looked less like a place that had been under water than a place that had been cut and left to dry. The sky came up the colour of skimmed milk over the terrace. Behind the drying racks water went over water with the low drag of a thing that had somewhere to put what it carried. A man might have called it weather and gone back to sleep.

The finger's width at the wheel-shed door was already open when they came past it. Men had come in since first light and spoken low in \place{the back room}, and the house had given them no name. The boy read the door the way he had once read a chart laid face-down on a desk: the shape of a thing kept was all he had, and the shape was enough. He smelled slate dust and the cold that comes off a room nobody has warmed, and under it the sweetish edge of lead glaze on a bad wind from the terrace. He was carried past without the room's being entered by anybody. A basket set down is a pot set down and left. A door standing a finger's width open is a door standing a finger's width open and is not an invitation to a child who cannot walk.

She did not ask who had come in, and did not slow at the door or turn her face toward it; the half-pace held. If there is a roll in which a room like that is entered, it is the roll this house keeps and does not show, and the boy was carried past it at the edge of the light with a crust in his fold and no reason given for any of it. He had stopped expecting reasons. Not entering a thing and not knowing it are two different acts, and a house can do the first for years without ever having to do the second.

\scenebreak

\person{Jorra Vail} came through the door before the yard had settled into its noise.

He had grey slate dust worked into both forearms past the bend of his little finger, not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel. That was the first thing. The boy saw it from the low angle of the basket before he saw the face, the way he had learned to see a man's hands before his mouth, because hands know what a face lies about. The dust was fine and grey, finer than the ridge in the corner, and it had been worked in, not laid on. It sat in the creases around the bent finger and in the hollows at the wrist and had gone past the bend of the little finger as though the arm had been buried to the elbow and then pulled out. Slip dried to old bone sits on top of a forearm and can be brushed off with the back of another wrist. Slate dust goes in. These were two different accounts of one morning and the boy set them on one tray without letting them touch, as he had set the clean forearms of the day before beside the dusty ones of other days. He did not ask which slate had been broken. He did not ask whether it had been a birth-slate. Under \place[the drying racks]{the lowest rack} the broken birth-slates lay where they lay and the yard went around the space above them as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and nobody entered them.

He went to the wheel before he spoke to a person and said to it what he had said to it a hundred times, in a flat voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either. “It doesn't care whose hands,” he said. “It cares if they're steady.” The wheel did not answer. It was \person{Garl Vail}'s wheel and had been his for twenty years before the seat wore hollow on the left, and it cared nothing about whose hands were on it, if that was what the dead man had thought and what his son had been told. Garl was kept in this house by a salt-stiff coat on a peg, a hollow seat nobody would let be filled, a clean dark line where the \lore{Ash} ridge had turned its corner, and a proverb about leaving the crack showing, said to pots with no crack and to the fire and never to a basket. The boy had counted him four, four and a gap for eighty-one days and had never once been given the fifth.

A woman at the tubs put her hands back into the water as he passed, and the yard went on with the work it had been doing before the dust came into it. He took a pot off the wheel and told it it would do. “You'll do,” he said, and set it on the bench with the care of a man setting down a thing that has already told him what it is. The \lore{full note} of a Vail pot was in it before it left the wheel, and Jorra knew it there as a man in a better-lit room might hear the whole of a diagnosis in a patient's first breath. The boy knew that he knew it. He had known it since he was eleven and would not say so before a kiln-master come up from \place{High Kiln}, who would call it early and make something of it. The boy kept the knowing where questions are kept for years, which in this house was most of them. He had learned to find the joke funny without laughing, and to keep a blade in the not-laughing that cost less than the laugh.

Sella had not finished telling him the fire was low. “The fire's low,” she said, and got as far as “It wants---” before his bent finger went in ahead of his hand at the damper and moved it, and the end of her instruction arrived at the pot instead of at him. She shut her mouth on the rest and gave it to the fire. “Feed it,” she said to the fire. The household straightened around his silence as cloth straightens when a weight is lifted off it: the broom went back to its stroke, the woman at the tubs went back to her hands, a neighbour at the rack end shifted a tray of wet pots clear of the shadow and carried it on. Nobody had given an order. Nobody had asked a question. The boy had learned by now that a silence could be an obedience and a correction at the same time and that the two would live in one man without either cancelling the other, the way a wheel turns on a hollow seat and keeps its place in a house that will not let the seat be filled.

He did not say the back room was cold. He did not look toward the bed or into the basket. \emph{He's not Vail} stayed where it had been put three years ago, sealed as tightly as a lid nobody had tried to lift, and the promise of the foundling's name to the men who burn slates was neither given nor withdrawn. The boy could read the state of the man's forearms without a word of \lore{Grathic}. He could not ask the question the forearms raised, because he had no words in any language to put to a boy of sixteen who stood between a wheel and a room he did not enter, and because a question asked from a basket travels only as far as the basket. He kept it. Questions lasted for years in this house. He had already learned that dates lasted longer than people and that a line left where it lay was a line kept, and he had no reason to think a question would fare worse.

He set the dust on a tray with the space under the lowest rack, and the two did not touch. The broken birth-slates lay under the space; a pewter tin with a thumb-bright lid stood three doors down; a slate turned writing-down lay toward a floorboard in a house he could not name; downriver two cords lay on a board beside a knot-string and neither board touched the other. He had no hand to lift any of them and no word of Grathic in which to ask for them, and the surgeon in him noted that a file of unread things is still a file, and that a man can keep a record perfectly and never once be allowed to open it.

He noted, too, that a man can hold two kinds of obedience and spend neither, and that the dust on the forearms was the more honest of the two because it could be read at a glance and read correctly. The boy who kept the fire and the boy who went into the room were the same boy, and the house had arranged itself around that fact without ever putting a name to it, as a room arranges itself around a draught under a door and nobody says the draught has a purpose. He wanted to know which slate had been broken. He wanted it with the small hungry nicking of the body that wanted the crust as well, and got no more out of the forearms than out of the crust. Both were records no roll could enter. Both were kept anyway.

\scenebreak

Before the light had properly gone \person{Tarn} set the small bench knife on its sill and told the empty yard where it was. “It's on the sill,” he said. “You can check.” Six years stood in a mark on the handle worn almost flat by his thumb and the seventh was nearly gone and still held, the one entry in this house the house could not check. He did not say the number. He told the kindling what he had told it before, that six were counted and the next he did not count, and went on holding the seventh as a man holds a breath he has decided not to spend though his chest is already tight. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line at the corner and no scrubbing lifted it. The pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back. His eye went to the slack oiled paper in the window before it went to the light, and he said nothing about either.

His broom went over the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector, over and over, without once turning along it. The road lay in the hard mud with the two ruts the flood had cut and the drying had kept, and the broom went across the mouth of it as though the mouth were a thing swept like any other thing. No collector came down it. Tarn did not say that a thing which had not happened had a name, and he did not go for one. He agreed with the woman who wanted the thaw early and the man who wanted it late, saying \emph{Yes, house} to each and agreeing so entirely that no argument could find a foot, and went on wanting the late one because a late thaw puts off the paying, though the paying had stood in the ruts all week.

A vessel gave its note past the door in the middle of the morning, past the wheel-shed and past the racks, and his head did not turn and he did not name the sound in Grathic, and the boy did not call it a seventh hearing. Six hearings of the full note stood at six --- five from four sources and the sixth out of a person --- beside and never among, and an ear that was worth more than the debt that held it went on being kept under the same roof as a loan that had never been called. The boy kept Tarn's not-turning beside Jorra's not-looking and did not let the two touch, because two silences of one shape under one roof are an equality, and an equality is not a cause. Three likenesses in a row will grow a cause whether a hand puts one there or not, and he had learned in a better-lit room to keep them apart.

At midday Jorra went back into the wheel-shed with the grey dust still worked into his forearms and said nothing about where he had been or who had been in the room. The door kept its finger's width of open behind him. Sella carried the boy past the lowest rack at the half-pace, going wide around the space under it and giving the instruction to the bucket and the broom and never to the basket, and the broom went over the collector's road without a turn. The two lines in the Vail corner --- the old clean dark one and the new finer grey beside it, called thaw and left to go when it goes --- lay where they lay, swept by neither. The widow's mark hung where it hung and was not touched, because a midday is not an opening. The boy's hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the bench where the pots stood and came down. One crust lay stiff in the fold with the empty place beside it unnamed. The count stood at eighty-one.
