\daymark{123}{Reed 4, Fourthday, Y.H. 813}

\dropcap{T}{he light} came up off the river with mud in it on the fourth day of Reed, and the yard at Low Kiln took the colour of a thing long handled and set down wet. The wind had come round in the night and brought the smell of cut grass downriver over the sill, and under it the thin mineral edge the Ilm leaves on everything it touches, so that the ruts and the upturned barrow and the backs of \place{the drying racks} all wore the same brown skin. The fourth day had come on without a boundary drawn anywhere in the yard, and the work went into the beds the way the seed had gone into the strips: carried in armfuls, laid in the ruts where the barley had stood, judged by the arm that brought it and by nothing that could be set on a slate. \person{Sella Vail} carried \person[Corin]{the boy} up from the rack end at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, the basket held level the way a pot is held that must not be slopped, and gave the instruction to the wool and the river wind and never to the basket. “Keep it off his ears. It's the river wind.” The instruction went into the wool and into the wind and nowhere near the child inside it. He was one hundred and twenty-three days old and four months and three days, carried where he could not have crawled, and nobody in the yard or under the roof had said so, or would have known what it counted if they had. Cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, the wet wool riding back from the bare joint so the cold found the place it had come for.

Men down from \place{the reed beds} came across the yard with cut reed on their shoulders, green at the broken ends and pale grey at the frost-bitten tips, and laid the armfuls in the ruts. They laid them down and straightened and looked at what they had laid, the way a man looks at a thing he has carried and is willing to have judged by the carrying. A cutter wiped his palms down his thighs before he took up another bundle, and the mud came away in two dark streaks that he did not notice. “A bundle's counted in the arm that carries it,” he said, and it was a statement of the arithmetic the whole yard was using and he made no move to put any part of it into writing.

“And the hands that cut it?” a woman at the racks said.

The cutter took up another armful and went past with the question still in the air behind him, going down the yard the way a thrown thing goes that nobody means to pick up. He did not say whose hands, or how many, or what they had been paid; he did not turn his head. The woman held her gaze a moment on his going and then went back to the pot she had shifted an inch so the light fell where she wanted it, and the question lay where he had left it, unanswered and entered nowhere.

A man come up from the sluice with his sleeves wet to the elbow stood a moment over the laid bundles and asked what the beds would want before the week was out. He asked it in the form of a direction, because that was the form the work had taken since the hands came out of the strips: a going-downriver in the morning and a coming-back at dusk, and a day's cutting measured by how far a man had walked to get it and how far he walked home with it.

“Ask it,” Sella said. “It'll tell you when it's had enough. The house has said it.”

The man turned the answer over, found no figure in it, and went off satisfied, because a direction was what he had asked for and a direction was what he had been given. He had asked what the beds would want and been told to ask the beds; he had asked the cost in the form of a direction and been answered with the weather and the river. Nothing had been priced and nothing had been refused. The cost had been asked in a shape that had no room for a figure in it, and so no figure had come. The boy, carried past them at the half-pace, heard the order of it exactly: the question first, and the answer given to the thing the question was about, and the woman standing between the two with her burn shut on the basket handle and her face turned nowhere. He had spent two lives learning how a person takes himself out of his own sentence; here a whole district took itself out of its own cost.

\scenebreak

The yard went around what it would not enter. The clean dark line lay at the Vail corner where \lore{Ash} ridge had turned, and beside it the new finer hairline of grey that the yard had called \lore{the thaw} after the fact, both unswept since the water went and crossed by no broom in four days. Sella stood over them with the basket steady and swept neither. The space beneath the lowest rack held its broken birth-slates where whole slate should have stood, and the going-around widened there by the width of a hand and came back, the way a woman goes wide around a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. Out in \place{the worked strips} east of the houses the returning line held its gap. One hand that had gone in on Seed 15 had not come out with the rest on Seed 27, and the yard went around the place where it should have stood with a look and a wide step and no number. No body had come up out of the brown ground. No cause had been said over it, no accident named, no hand identified and no figure set at the foot of what the month had cost. A slate would be found and a figure made if a hand came up in a month or a year, and nothing had come up. The place was held and not entered, carried into Reed the way it had been carried through the end of Seed, and the reed work going-downriver arranged itself around it as the planting had.

The boy read the going-around as a surgeon reads a chart laid face down on a table: a neat line drawn to close a note that nobody had opened. It was exact. It was complete. It was worth nothing. Two lives had taught him the difference between a record that tells what happened and a record that tells only where to stop reading, and the yard's empty place was the second kind, closed as neatly as any slate and with as little inside it. Here the chart was a field with a hole in a line and no signature at all, and the not-reading was done in the open air before a whole yard that leaned toward it the way a crowd leans toward a man about to speak low. He set it on a place of its own where he kept such things and laid nothing down beside it.

A woman come up from the strips with dry mud worked into the creases of her wrists stopped at the rack end and asked what the reeds would cost now the hands were out of them. It was the question of the month, asked plainly and in a form that wanted a figure, and the yard went quiet in the small way a yard goes quiet when a thing everybody has been going around is said aloud. Sella glanced once at the clay jar cold under its glaze on the wall shelf and sent the answer to the pot.

“Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there. The house has said it.”

The woman stood a moment with her question still held out, then took it back the way a woman takes back a cup that has not been filled, and carried it away held level so that nothing slopped. She did not ask again and nobody asked for her; the cost went off across the yard in her two hands as carefully as a full pot goes off a shelf, and arrived where it had been asked to arrive, at a pot that had no answer in it. The boy watched the level carry of it and set that on a place of its own too, a keeping set apart and not a price, with no figure in it and no hand laid across it.

\scenebreak

Sella carried him the length of the yard at the half-pace she kept from every open slate. She went around the space beneath the lowest rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, the step widening by the width of a hand and coming back, and around the two unswept lines without crossing either. The instruction went to the wool and to the river wind: keep it off his ears, it is the river wind; keep it dry, it is running water. She said it twice, because the wool had not moved and the wind had not listened, and never once said it to the child in the basket. She said \emph{the house} where another woman would have said \emph{I}, and the boy, listening from inside the basket at the half-pace, heard the taking happen and did not ask for the answer that never came.

Midday came on over the yard without anything marking where the morning ended and the other half of the day began. The reeds went on coming down from the beds in armfuls and standing grey in the ruts; the hands that had carried seed in shut fists now carried reed in armfuls, and nobody put a figure to either. The district's withholding was not kept by one woman in a doorway but performed in front of everybody, in a question sent to a pot and a direction answered with the weather and an empty place gone around with a look; it was the most ordinary thing in the yard, and the boy was the only one in it who could read it as a closed account. Competence without a hand to lay it down was only a more exact kind of helplessness. He had the whole shape of it in his head, the chart closed with its neat line, the note unopened under it, the arithmetic of the arm that carried the bundle and told what had been carried and never what it had cost, and he had no hand to set it down and no voice with which to offer it, and if he had had both he would not have offered it, because an account laid on a table in this district is an account that wants a payer.

He turned the keeping over the way a man lays instruments on a towel and found nothing new to add. \place{the loaf shelf} kept its two unnamed gaps in the next room, through a wall this yard did not reach: the old space at the row's end that had been named as a thing the planting brought in, and the newer palm's breadth that had come in on the night of Seed 6 with no scrape, crumb, print or word. In the fold at his ear one stiff brown-edged crust lay with the empty place beside it still unnamed, and no third had come into the fold in a hundred and twenty-three days, and no third gap stood on the shelf. Two rooms did not touch. He laid no likeness across them, and none across the field's empty place, and none across the place where the hand had gone in. A likeness laid on a likeness grows a cause, and a cause in this district always arrived with a clean hand at the foot of it, and the clean hand was the one object he had spent two lives declining to become.

His small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the worked strips and came down. It had gone up an inch toward the loaf shelf and toward a pot two paces off and toward the note of water through the finger's-width gap, and it came down; nothing within two paces could be reached. In the other life it had closed on clamps and on a phone with a name he would not look at, and had found a vessel in the dark by the note it gave under a knuckle. That hand was gone. This one went up an inch and came down, and the count went on with it, one hundred and twenty-three exact and unspent and spoken by nobody who would have known what it counted when they said it.

Out in the worked strip the yard went around the empty place with a look and a wide step, and the armfuls of reed passed it on their way downriver and would pass it again coming back at dusk, and there was no number anywhere in the line.
