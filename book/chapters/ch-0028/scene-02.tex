Back under \place{the Vail kiln-house} roof the light went out of the oiled paper by degrees, the squares on the floor losing their corners first and their middles last, as though the room were being closed by a hand. \person{Sella Vail} carried \person[Corin]{the boy} in at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, the basket level under her burn-shut hand, and set it down where no slate lay, against the wall and out of the door's draught, the way a woman sets a pot where no passing hip will find it. She did not look into it and she did not speak to it. The instructions went to the things that could not answer, and went first.

“Settle,” she told the bucket. “You've come up out of a dark place. Don't bring it in with you.”

“Go around,” she told the broom. “It cost enough to come in. It'll cost more to take out.”

“Not by the door,” she told the pot. “You'll pay for it in \lore{the thaw}.”

The fire got its instruction last and kept it longest: feed me before anybody is fed. The words went into the bucket, the broom, the pot and the fire, and nowhere near the basket; the basket kept its level carry, and the fold at his ear was put right without a look at his face. She said \emph{the house} where another woman would have said \emph{I}, and the boy, who had spent two lives learning the grammar of a person taking himself out of his own sentence, heard the taking happen and let it lie where it fell. He was one hundred and twenty-three days old and four months and three days, and nobody under this roof had said so, or would have known what it counted if they had said it.

Between the rack end and the wall there was a space where whole slate should have stood. Sella went around it with a step that widened by the width of a hand and came back, the basket held as level as a pail of hot water carried past a child who might knock it. She did not look at the space and she did not sweep it. The broken birth-slates lay where they lay under \place[the drying racks]{the lowest rack}, entered by nobody in four days, and the going-around had the care of a thing seen without being entered in any account.

In the next room \place{the loaf shelf} kept its days the way this house kept most of its days: badly, and in materials no Court thumb would read. Loaves pushed along the board, a glance at the clay jar cold under its glaze on the wall, and spaces left where no figure stood. She moved her lips along the row without a figure, her hand going past the old empty place at the row's end and past the newer palm's breadth that had come in on the night of Seed 6 with no scrape, crumb, print or word, and closed on a loaf beyond both. Two gaps; no third. In the fold at his ear one stiff brown-edged crust lay two inches from him with the empty place beside it still unnamed, and the two rooms did not touch. Two neighbours had laid the old gap and the empty place at his ear under one cause in a single breath and gone off with their pots, and she had heard the sayings and made neither his and made neither hers; a shelf was not an opening, and a likeness laid on a likeness in this district grew a cause with a clean hand at the foot of it.

The \lore{widow's mark} hung at her throat, a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it. She did not touch it. It was touched before an opening and at no other hour, and a shelf was not an opening, nor a strip, a rack, a well, a market, a road, a basket or a bed. Her hand went instead to the strand of coal-black hair at her temple that had come loose in the carrying, and she pushed it back with the wrist that carried the little-door burn, the skin inside the door puckering as the wrist turned. The grey streak showed pale in the dying light off the coals. She had said once that it came in the winter \person[Garl Vail]{Garl} died and had not said it again.

At the fire she told it what it was. “It was the thaw.” The words went down into the coals as instructions went into the bucket, and the coals answered nothing. She turned them with the back of a broken pot, the broken edge lifting and settling, and fed them, and the house kept its warmth the way a cup keeps water with a crack in it nobody will mention.

“The house will keep it,” she said, where another woman would have said I.

Then she stood with the back of the broken pot in her hand and counted something under her breath toward no opening. The lips moved; the count did not arrive anywhere; there was no slate, no door, no crossing of a threshold for it to be entered at. The boy, carried past at the half-pace and set where he could see and not join, read the stopping of the count as a surgeon reads a chart closed over a note nobody has opened: exact, complete, worth nothing. He had learned in two lives to tell a record that says what happened from a record that says only where to stop reading, and this was the second kind, kept in the mouth instead of on slate and more carefully for that.

She set the pot where it could be found in the dark, rim out and handle toward the reaching hand, the way a woman sets a cup for a man who will come in late and not look where he puts his fingers. She did not look toward the bed. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen white in the small hours and nobody under this roof had been given an instruction about the thread, so it stayed where it had been put, a thing kept and not entered.

The going of the light brought the yard in through the oiled paper as a sound before it brought it as a sight: a broom, a foot, a voice saying something about the weather that arrived at the door as a direction. \lore{Ash} lay in the Vail corner where the old ridge had turned, a clean dark line with a new finer hairline of grey beside it, both unswept in four days and both called the thaw. There was no sweeping of either, only the going-around, which was the sort of care that looks like not seeing. Sella stood over them with the pot still in her hand and went on not sweeping them.

Outside at the edge of the yard \person[Tarn Vail]{Tarn} took up the broom only after the broom had been given its instruction; he did not put his hand to a tool that had not been named. He went around the two lines and the space beneath the rack with the stroke that went around and entered nothing, and then he went over the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector, over and over, without once turning his head to look along it. No collector had come down it for a woman the brook took, and he had never said that the thing which had not happened had a name. The small bench knife lay at the very edge of its sill with six years standing in a thumb-mark his thumb had worn almost flat and the seventh nearly gone and still held, the one entry in this house the house could not check. He had told the sill where it was, unasked, and the unforged collector sentence lay beside it where he kept it, neither entered. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line and no scrubbing lifted it; the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back.

His chest had been tight since the dark. The breath he had decided not to spend on Seed 16 was still unspent, and there was a small interval at the top of each reach where it should have gone in, an empty place in the order of a thing a man does without thinking. A woman going past with a pot said the thaw would come early. A man by the upturned barrow said it would come late.

“Yes, house,” Tarn said to the one, and “Yes, house,” to the other, agreeing so entirely that no argument could find a foot to stand on. He kept wanting the late, because a late thaw puts off the paying, though the paying had stood in the ruts since the water went and would stand there when the reeds came in. When he reached the top of the yard the breath that should have gone in did not go in, and he went on with the broom; this was the shape his help took when he was frightened. The tongue of \lore{the damper} stood where it had been left that morning, moved a hair's breadth and standing there still, and he did not ask about the ear and she did not ask about the room. Two silences stood under one roof with nobody putting a word between them, and the loan that could hear was not called. Something must give at the end of the seventh year; it had not given yet.

On his bed under the roof the boy turned out the file as a man lays instruments on a towel, each thing in the place he had put it, nothing added and nothing taken away. Six hearings of \lore{the full note} stood beside and never among: five from four sources --- a cup across a birth room, a pot through \place[the Vail wheel-shed]{the wheel-shed} wall, the district's slate twice at \place{the Cross}, and a man giving at \lore[the Hearth Drawing]{the Drawing} --- and the sixth, the small flat knock out of \person{Dessa Silt} as the men lifted, which came from a person and was filed beside the five and never counted with them. No seventh had come today. The bell that had gone across the square was a told bell, and he put it where it belonged, in its own place and not among the hearings; the pots under the roof gave notes he had learned to leave where they sounded. Three near-full answers stood advanced and not paid, three intervals lay on another tray, and the word forty stayed unspoken with its readable double turn kept without a name. He wrote no cause across any of them. A likeness laid on a likeness grows a cause, and a cause in this district always arrived with a clean hand at the foot of it, and the clean hand was the one object he had spent two lives declining to become.

Through the finger's-width gap came the river's note of water over water, beyond \place{the reed beds} and \place{the Weir}, a fifth low thing kept on a place of its own since he was six days old, never a hearing and never among the six. Through the floor under blind ash the carried fire took its coal and gave back four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for a hundred and twenty-three days. Above him the boards kept four nails, four, and a fifth that was a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts sat in three places and did not become one. A man with an ear for vessels might have laid one gap across another and called them the same absence; he had spent two lives practising the refusal, and the refusal itself was worth nothing to anybody under this roof.

Nobody said he was a hundred and twenty-three days old. He had stopped expecting to be told, which was not the same as not wanting it. The count went on edge to edge against the house's loaves and glances and unnamed spaces, exact and unspent and spoken by nobody who would have known what it counted if they had said it. The cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, the wool riding back from the bare joint so the cold found the place it had come for and went under the fold at his ear.

His small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down. Nothing within two paces could be reached; not the crust, not the pot set rim out for a reaching hand, not the fold, not the bucket, not a slate, not a hand. The hand was his and small, and the count went on with it. River, fire and floorboards kept their three counts in three places. The water went over water beyond the gap, the fire missed its fifth breath under the floor, and one hundred and twenty-three stood where it stood, unspent and spoken by nobody.
