\daymark{124}{Reed 5, Fifthday, Y.H. 813}

Before first grey the \place{Clay Yard} had the look of a vessel that has been set down in the cold and left, and left so often that the leaving has become its use. The racks stood a row of darker uprights against a sky the colour of wet ash, and the light came up off the river with the thin mineral edge the \place{Ilm} leaves on everything it touches, so that the flagstones and the upturned barrow and the shoulders of the men wore one grey skin. The men came up the yard with their collars high and their heads bent together, as though they carried between them something that would break if the wind got under it, and they went low-voiced into \place[the back room]{the room off the wheel-shed}, which stood a finger's width open and did not shut. \person{Jorra Vail} gave the house no name for them and did not say that the room was cold. \person{Sella Vail} set \person[Corin]{the boy} at the edge of the work in \place{the Vail wheel-shed} where no slate lay, the basket level on the boards and his cheek turned to the cold of them, and stood a half-pace from every open slate. She gave the instruction to the wool before she gave it to anybody. “Keep it off his ears. It's the river wind.” The instruction went into the wool and into the wind and nowhere near the child inside it. He was one hundred and twenty-four days old and four months and four days, and nobody in the shed or the yard had said so, or would have known what it counted if they had. Cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist; the wool had ridden back from the bare joint in the grey and the cold found the place it had come for and went under the fold at his ear. The thread at his mouth had frozen where nobody under this roof had been given an instruction about the thread. His head wandered, made the small wet sound, and came back. He did not follow the men. The room was not entered.

Jorra came through the finger's-width door with fine grey slate dust ground small as meal into both forearms past the bend of the little finger, and it was not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel. Slip sits on the hair and dries in pale plates; this had gone into the creases and stayed there, a grey so fine it might have been swept from a slate and breathed in. He kept the dusty forearms and the clean ones on one tray of the morning, the way a man sets two states of the same vessel side by side on a bench and does not mix them, and he asked neither which slate had been broken nor whether it had been a birth-slate. The broken birth-slates lay under \place[the drying racks]{the lowest rack} where the yard went around the space above them as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and entered them into nothing. Nobody went under the rack. The dust came out of the room and the room kept whatever it kept, and the house gave the men no name and the boy took down none.

A woman come up from the racks with a pot in her hands and reed-fibre grey at her wrists stopped at the edge of the boards and asked who had come up the yard before the light. It was a plain question and she asked it plainly, with her eyes on the pot and not on his face, which was the way this yard asked a thing it did not expect to be answered. Jorra did not give her a name. He took the pot from her and set it where the light fell on the rim, and told her the slip at its shoulder was too thick and would want thinning before it went near a firing. The woman took the pot back and went off with her question still in her hands, carried level the way Sella carried a basket, and the boy, who had learned to read a deflected question the way he had once read a chart with the note turned down, put the unanswered thing on a place of its own and wrote no cause under it. No name had been given and no name had been taken.

He spoke to the wheel before he spoke to anyone. “It doesn't care whose hands. It cares if they're steady.” It was the same sentence the boy had heard from the same mouth through the whole length of the planting month, given to the wheel as a man gives a beast its handful, and nothing in it asked who had been in the room or what had been ground into the creases past the little finger. A pot came up on the wheel and Jorra looked at its wall a moment longer than the wall required and said, “You'll do,” in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either. He knew the \lore{the full note} that was in that pot before it ever left the wheel; he had known such notes in pots of this house since he was eleven, and he would not say so in front of a kiln-master come up from \place{High Kiln}, who would call it early and make something of it. \person{Garl Vail}'s proverb had gone to a pot with no crack long ago and cost the more now that there was nobody to say it in his own voice, and the boy, who had spent two lives learning the exact weight of a note before a thing breaks, kept the knowing where questions could last for years. The knowing was worth nothing that anyone could take. It could not be sold, entered, or carried up the yard; it belonged to a boy who could not say it and a wheel-keeper who would not, and the pot went on turning with the note still inside it, uncalled.

A kiln-master come up from High Kiln went along the row of pots with his hands behind his back, looking at the rims and not at the boy, and said nothing that any slate could carry. He had the sweetish edge of glaze on his coat from the terrace, a smell the boy had learned to separate from the damp clay and tallow of the shed, and he stopped at a pot that had a shoulder a hair too thick and passed his eye over it without putting a finger to it. Jorra said nothing either. The note was in the Vail pot before it left the wheel and he had known it there, and a thing said in front of a kiln-master becomes a thing the kiln-master has heard, and a thing heard is a thing entered in some book the boy would never see; the house had taught him that much without ever saying it. The kiln-master went off up the yard with his hands still behind him, and the wheel took up its low note again as though no man had stood over it. The boy set the unsaid knowing on a place of its own and did not lay it beside the dust on the forearms, though the two things had come in on the same morning and from the same door.

\scenebreak

Sella had not finished telling him the fire was low. “The fire's low, Jorra, and the—” The sentence never got to the end of itself, because his bent finger went into the cold iron of \lore{the damper} ahead of it, took the cold out of the fingertip, moved the tongue a hair's breadth and left it standing there. The household straightened around his silence the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted from it: the pot went where the pot was told, the broom stayed where the broom had been put, and the end of the instruction arrived at the pot instead of at the man. He had not answered her with a word and had not looked at her, which was how he obeyed her instantly, and the correction in it was the same correction he had made every morning of the planting month and never once named. She shut her mouth on the rest of the sentence and gave it to the pot, and the yard went on as though a weight had been lifted and the cloth had settled and nobody had seen the hand that did it.

The boy read the iron once more. It was one piece of cold iron with two uses in it, and he had spent two lives learning to tell them apart and had never found a word in \lore{Grathic} that held both mercies at once. The bite that closes on a vessel and holds it whole, so that the warmth stays where the warmth is wanted, is the same bite that can take the hand off a man if the man is slow or careless or told to be quick; the iron does not choose between them and has no need to. Here it was a damper, a tongue of iron moved a hair's breadth by a bent finger, and the reading of it was exact and worth nothing and joined to no kiln. Two men had carried a story into this yard of a counting room shut in the war with the damper closed and the men inside dead of the breath, eighteen leagues east and dated when they told it, and a third voice had only mentioned such a room, with a date in the mention and none of the weight. The boy set the two tellings on their own tray, kept the third mention where it belonged, and set the tray beside the iron without laying one across the other. A likeness laid on a likeness grows a cause, and there was no cause in this yard that any man had earned the right to name; besides, the men had gone home with their bags and their mud, and the kiln had gone on holding its warmth in its own damaged way. Nothing had been said that a slate could carry, and no third telling came to make a rule of the two.

\scenebreak

Outside at the edge of the yard \person[Tarn Vail]{Tarn} took up the broom only after the broom had been given its instruction. Go around, it cost enough to come in, it will cost more to take out; the instruction went into the broom and the flagstones and never into the man, and he took up the tool as a man takes up a thing already named. “Yes, house,” he said to a woman who wanted \lore{the thaw} early, and “Yes, house,” to a man who wanted it late, agreeing so entirely that no argument could find a foot to stand on. He kept wanting the late, because a late thaw puts off the paying, though the paying had stood in the ruts since the water went and would stand there when the reeds came in and after they were cut. His chest had been tight since the dark. The breath he had decided not to spend on Seed 16 was still unspent, and there was a small interval at the top of each reach where it should have gone in, an empty place in the order of a thing a man does without thinking, a breath stopped against iron and never let through.

His seventh year was pressed past the end of the third tend of the planting and still held. The small bench knife lay at the very edge of its sill with six years standing in a mark his thumb had worn almost flat and the seventh nearly gone and never named, the one entry in this house the house could not check; the unforged collector sentence lay beside it where he had kept it since the frozen ford, and neither was entered. \lore{Ash} was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line and no scrubbing lifted it, and the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back. Before he swept he put his hand to the small knife on its sill, moved it a hair's breadth and set it back where it could be checked, and told the sill where it was, though the sill had not asked and the yard had been told a hundred mornings running. Six years stood in the worn mark and the seventh was where the house could not check it. The boy watched the hand go to the tool and come away and set the checking where he set the other record-first hands, kept and not entered; a record kept where no thumb would read it was still a record, and the house's arithmetic had no place in it for the one entry that mattered. To the kindling, later, the account would stay as it had stayed through the planting month: six counted, and the next he did not count. He went around the two unswept lines and the space beneath the lowest rack with the stroke that went around and entered nothing, and then he went out toward the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector, and no collector came, and he did not say that the thing which had not happened had a name.

His stroke went on over the flagstones without the half-check. Behind Jorra's standing finger the bristles passed the place where the stroke had stopped on Seed 22 and did not stop there now, and the stones kept that place all the same, a shallow bright patch in the pattern of the paving that no foot would enter and no broom would call by its name. On Seed 25 the change had been held and not repeated; on Seed 28 and on Reed 2 it had been held again; on this morning of Reed it went on as though the half-beat had never been made, and the two things lay on one tray in the boy's keeping as two objects and not a rule. He set the unchecking stroke beside the earlier half-beat and laid no cause across them.

Jorra's bent finger stood in the cold iron of the damper, and Tarn's bristles went on over the flagstones without the check, the stones keeping the shallow bright patch where the stroke had stopped. Neither man asked about the other's silence. Jorra did not ask about the ear and Tarn did not ask about the room; neither named the other's silence, and the loan that could hear was not called. A loan that can hear is a loan that can be called, and the sentence that would call it had lain under this roof for six years without being said. An ear worth more than a debt is an unsafe thing to own, and the boy, who had spent two lives learning what an uncalled account costs, kept the two silences apart on the tray where they had lain since the night he came, with no finger laid across the space between them. The equality under the roof held at the place it had moved to, neither rising nor falling: one man's obedience arriving before the instruction that called it and being also a correction nobody would name, the other's stroke going on without the check it had once made. Two kept things stood in one room with no hand joining them. Nothing was paid, named, or ended. One hundred and twenty-four stood where one hundred and twenty-three had stood, exact and unspent and spoken by nobody who would have known what it counted, while the wheel kept its low note and the iron kept its bite and the reed work went on downriver without a figure.
