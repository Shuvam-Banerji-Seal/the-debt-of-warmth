The flat knock finished in the room's ordinary noise the way a stone dropped into a full pail finishes: no ring, only a small displacement, and then the surface going still. The women went back to their work. The girl at the hearth moved the pot another inch out of the draught and said something to it about the thaw; the woman at the wrist took up her count again from the beginning, as if a count interrupted were a count spoiled. \person{Sella Vail} stood where she had set herself, a half-pace from the bench and a half-pace from the bed, with her hands folded and the burn shaped like a little door shut tight on nothing. She did not look toward the basket. She had placed \person{Corin} where he could see the work and not be part of it, and she would keep that placing until she lifted him out of it, whatever the room did next.

The collector took his hand off the slate and the slate went with him. He went out as he had come in, without looking at the bed, the bench, or the basket, and the door stood its finger's width open behind him, and the cold came in at that width and went to the floor and stayed there, as cold does in a room that has been warm a long time and has stopped noticing where its warmth is going. The \lore{collection} was done and the room had turned back to what it had been doing before the man came in, which was the work of keeping a child warm.

On a folded quilt at the other side of the hearth lay a child three days returned from a thin account. He had been carried into \place[the Vail kiln-house]{the Vail house} the morning before under a shawl that smelled of smoke and milk, and carried out again in the afternoon, and now he lay here with his head back and his mouth a little open, breathing in a way that used his whole chest and got nothing from it. He was smaller than the boy in the basket, and the grey had come into his fingers from the edges inward the way it comes into clay before the firing. It had come into the nails first and then into the fingers, and the woman at the wrist kept folding the fingers into the wool and uncovering them again to look, and folding them back.

The mother sat on a stool with her hands in her lap and did not hold him. The women had told her that holding a child in this state took warmth out of her and put none into him, and she believed them because she wanted to believe them, and because the alternative was to sit with nothing to do. There was a cup of warm milk on the bench beside her and she had not drunk from it. “Will he know me, if he comes through?” she said, and she said it to her own hands, not to the room. The woman at the hearth told her that a child knows warmth before it knows a face, and that she should keep her voice low and her hands to herself, and went on stirring nothing in the cup. It was an answer to a different question, and it was the answer the room had for all of them. The room smelled of soap and tallow and warm milk, the same smells as a birth room, the same smells as the Vail house, and under them the sour mineral edge that had a corner all to itself.

Two women worked over the child with the concentration of people shelling barley: quick, exact, unhurried, with no wasted reaching. One had her fingers at his wrist and her lips moving; the other pressed a fold of wool flat across his chest with the same certainty the hearth-maid had used at a boy's shoulder three mornings ago, as though a hand could keep the warmth in by holding it down. A third stood at the hearth and did not bring the cup to the child's mouth, because a child in that state must not be woken to drink. They did not raise their voices. This was ordinary work, and the ordinary way of doing it did not include raising voices over a child who might yet come through.

“A graft is settling,” the woman at the wrist said to the quilt. “That's all it is. It comes to some and they come through it and some do not. You wrap the feet and you wait.”

It was \lore[the Second Want]{the second chill}, and it had a name in the room and an order in the room and a thing to be done about it, and the women had known all three before they had ever thought to question the order of it.

From the basket, the boy watched the child the way he had once watched a chart go past him in a bay: not for the story anybody told over the bed, but for the small evidence a body carried and nobody had asked to read. The breath was shallow at the top, then deep, then shallow again, with a little pause at the end that was longer than the breath itself. The fingers were grey from the edges inward. The child's mouth had relaxed into something that looked like contentment, and the corners of it were set in a way he had learned to distrust: the face that smiles when the body is too cold to move, because the muscles have set that way and no one has thought to look beneath the expression.

Alda came across the room when the cup had been set down. \person{Alda Reed} smelled of soap and warm milk and the faint mineral tang of \lore{the Ash} she had promised herself she would sweep tomorrow; the clay under her nails was scrubbed to the quick and still there. She did not go to the child first. She looked for a flat place for her slate, found the bench by the door, set the slate down with the writing turned under, and only then took the child's wrist between two fingers. It was the order he had watched in three rooms now: the slate before the face, the instrument before the person, the thing settled before anybody looked at the thing that could not settle itself.

The mother asked three things in one breath: whether to wrap him, whether to warm the stone, whether to wake him and try the milk. Alda's hand went inside her bodice to the cord and the little tin. Her thumb rested on it for the space of a breath and came out with a date. “Stone eight the account went up,” she said. “Before the paper went out. Don't rub the feet; you'll pay for it in the thaw.” She said it to the quilt, not to the mother. It answered none of the three questions and it closed all of them. The mother wrapped the feet. The room went on with its work, and the exactness of the date went into the air of it the way chalk dust goes into a room and settles on every flat surface. He had heard that exactness before in another mouth, over a chart that said one thing and a note under the chart that said another, and in this room it was the kindest voice present and it was closing a question nobody had opened.

Alda turned the child's wrist over and felt along the inside of the forearm toward the elbow, as though looking for a way, and found what she was looking for and let it go. She took the chalk from behind her ear and wrote on the flat of a second slate two lines in a small upright hand, and when the lines were down she turned the slate writing-down and slid it under the milk cup where no mother would think to pick it up. The boy in the basket knew a hand that turned its own writing down when he saw one; he had watched it across a birth room and at the edge of a market and now beside a death-bed, and he had never once been allowed to see what lay on the other side.

The child had a red mark at the edge of the hairline, no bigger than a thumb-nail, branching where the fair skin showed through the stubble. Alda's two fingers went to its edge and crossed, and went back to the wrist, and she did not know she had done it in front of a room full of women and a basket against the wall. She covered it with the fold. “Warm the stone first,” she said to the pot. “A cold stone is a stone, not a kindness.” Nobody asked her what sign she had made, and nobody asked why the child's wrist had been turned over, and the room went on being ordinary.

\person{Daniel Marchetti} knew \lore{the Afterdrop} without being told the word for it in this country. The patient gets worse at the moment of rescue, when the cold blood comes back from the limbs and the heart must deal with what it brings; the cold patient cannot clot, so a cut that should seal goes on welling; you do not rub a frozen foot, because the ice in the cells has been moved and not melted; and a face that looks peaceful is often a face that has given up. Rewarming has its own treachery, and he had spent twelve years being paid to know its shape.

The white light of \place{Cook County Hospital} in \place{Chicago} came back for two sentences and took the room with it. The light there has no shadow and the body on the table is a girl who was walking an hour ago, and a nurse is holding pressure in a cavity with both hands while the resident waits for the sentence that opens the chest. The chart is on its stand with the nurse's note turned face-down under the clip, and his own hand is going past it toward the signature while the patient warms and the patient worsens and the room wants him to decide.

Then it was the death-house in \place{Low Kiln} again, and the clay yard outside under its rime, and a child on a quilt whose breath did not finish. He had stood at the foot of that table and decided in under a minute, hundreds of times, and carried the decision out of the room behind him; here he was six days old in a basket, the only adult in the room and the least use in it. He knew what was happening to the child better than any woman there knew it, and there was no sentence in the room that would take the knowledge from him. He said nothing. The body opened and shut his mouth for him.

The body wanted the breast and wanted it now, wanted the drool wiped off its chin and a hand to turn it out of the draught; the man inside it wanted ten seconds with the child's wrist and a light he could point where he pleased. Both wants were in the same small chest and neither had any standing in that room. The first was louder and had no words, and won nothing, because nobody had been sent to feed a boy in a basket. He lay and counted the child's breathing on the quilt: shallow, deep, shallow, pause, with the pause the longest of the four. He had made that count in a bay without ever deciding to. Here it was the only thing in the room that was his, and it was no more use than chalk dust.

Every woman corrected him without knowing he had spoken. The one at the wrist drew the grey fingers back inside the wool and told the quilt that a babe mustn't be let to claw at himself. The one at the hearth turned the cup of milk and said that warmth out was warmth lost and a body must be coaxed, not hurried, and that the child had been warm in the morning and would be warm again by morning. A third came from the door with a fresh fold and tucked it under the child's chin, saying that the cold goes in by the mouth and the feet and nowhere else, which was a saying and not an answer. The mother got up, put a clean fold over the child's feet, and sat down again with her hands in her lap. None of them raised a voice. There was nothing to raise a voice about: the room knew what this was and what to do about it, and had known it longer than any of them had been alive, and the only thing in it that disagreed lay under a fold of wool with a thread of drool at the corner of his mouth.

The boy's hand went to the place where a pocket should have been. There was no coat there, no cold rectangle of light with a name on it, no nine summers of a boat promised and never bought. There was only the fold of wool closed over his chest and a small soft hand inside it with milky nails. He did not look at the name. He had not looked at it on the expressway and he would not look at it here, and the absence of it was the one part of him that had not changed in the crossing.

While the women worked he let his eye go along the skirting board. The grey lay there in a ridge, thicker by a finger's width than the ridge under the skirting board at the Vail kiln-house, and it turned the corner by the door without a break and went on into the next room the way water goes on in a channel and does not stop at a stone. Nobody had swept it. Nobody had said what it was. He put it with the ridge at Vail and with the charred edge of the slate in the Vail kiln ash and with the grey barrow that had gone out through the north gate on the day of the reading, and kept the three side by side without joining them, the way he kept the kiln's four, four and a gap. Nobody had explained any of the three, and nobody had asked him to understand them.

The child's breath went shallow and came back shallow, and the women went on with their work. The mother held the cup of milk and did not drink from it. Alda wrote another word on her turned slate and put the chalk back behind her ear. The man on the board-bed lay with his hands crossed on his belly and the pale round place on his arm where the iron had been, and no woman straightened the fold at his chin. Sella Vail had not moved from her half-pace; when the mother looked toward the basket, Sella did not look back, and the mother looked away.

The year's first measure had gone out of the dying in the morning. Before the day was done the newly born in the same room gave as well, and the room called it a graft settling and went on calling the day ordinary. Nobody entered either thing in the account the district kept, and nobody asked the boy why he lay with his hand closed on nothing.

In the corner the grey ridge turned the door's corner without a break, a finger's width thicker than the ridge under the Vail skirting board. The boy's hand lay closed on the fold where a phone would have been, and the count of the day went into the place nobody could tax.
