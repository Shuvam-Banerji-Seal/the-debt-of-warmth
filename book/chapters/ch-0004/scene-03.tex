Stone 12 came in on a dry cold that lifted the oiled paper at the windows, and before the kiln had drawn its first breath \person{Sella Vail} lifted \person{Corin} out of his bed and put him into the basket. She lifted him the way she lifted a pot that is filled and must not be slopped: one hand under the small of his back, the other at his head, the burn shaped like a little door closing first on nothing and then on his weight. The fold had ridden back off his ear in the night; she put it right without looking at him and said something to the wool about the river wind. There was no wind at the door at that hour and none in the room at all, and he knew she was not speaking to the wool. She was speaking the sentence she had said for six days to whatever in the house would hold a sentence and not ask for anything back. The boy wanted the breast. The woman wanted the day kept in its order. Neither got what it wanted, and the basket went out under her arm into the yard.

The year's first \lore{collection} had come into the district. He had had the date by heart since \place{the Cross}: \lore{the Hearth Drawing}, Stone 12 of the twelfth month, with the Ilm under ice and \lore{the Five} spoken of as three days off by people who had counted what they had and did not care to say it. Nobody in the yard had said \emph{collection} or \emph{Drawing} to him; those words were not spoken to baskets. He lay under the fold and carried the date the way he carried the count of the floorboards, without use and without letting go. The Clay Yard was the colour of weak tea under its rime, and the brook behind the kiln had a skin on it that would not bear a stone thrown from the bank. Sella walked with her eyes on the ground ahead of her feet and did not look down into the basket once, the way a woman carrying a full pot does not look into the pot while she is crossing a threshold.

She carried him two doors down the yard to a neighbour's house and went in without knocking, because the door stood its finger's width open and knocking at a door already open was not done in \place{Low Kiln}. The cold came in behind her at that width, went to the floor and stayed there, as cold does in a room that has been warm for a long time and has stopped noticing where the warmth is going. Just inside the door she stopped, turned the basket with her knee, and set it at the edge of the light.

She did not set it where a slate lay open.

There was a slate on the bench by the door, turned writing-down, and she carried the basket past it with her elbow held close and set it down beyond, against the wall, where the boy could see the length of the room and the room, if it looked at all, would see only a bundle of wool and the toe of a woman's boot. He could see the work. He could not be part of it. That was the whole of the placing, made in one movement without a word, the way a woman sets a cup down where a sleeve will not catch it.

“A pot filled is a pot carried,” she said to the girl at the hearth. “Keep the draught off the bench.” She did not say whose pot or whose draught. She pulled the fold over the boy's ear and went to stand where the other women were standing, a half-pace from the bench and a half-pace from the bed, with her hands folded and her little-door burn half-closed on nothing. She had set him where she had set him and she would not move him again, whatever happened in the room.

He knew the room before he had seen it. It was the wrong familiarity that had followed him since the first day: a clay-daubed room with a trodden-ash floor, a bench by the door, a pot on the bench, a draught under the door moving the hem of a woman's skirt with patient, unhurried persistence, the smell of tallow and wet wool and a sour mineral tang he had learned to place in a corner without being told which corner. The grey of \lore{the Ash} lay in that corner. He noticed it and made nothing of it, because nobody in the room had made anything of it either. It was another house and it was the same room he had been born into and the same room he had been kept in, with the bench in the same place relative to the door and the cold in the same place relative to the fire.

The body did what bodies do to him. His head went where it wanted and stayed; a thread of drool gathered at the corner of his mouth and the wool would not take it; the hand outside the fold went up toward his chin an inch and came down. He had spent twenty years telling other people's bodies what to do and could not keep his own face dry. The joke of it had gone flat on the third day and the exactness had not. He counted the nails in the bench: four on the near board, four on the next, and a knot the width of a thumb where a fifth nail should have been. He had counted that shape under another roof and did not make anything of the repetition either. Two rooms with the same gap. A house that keeps a fire and a house that keeps a dying man, both with the empty place left in the count and nobody asked to fill it.

The man lay on a board-bed against the far wall. He was a neighbour, a man whose age the grey had taken off him, and he lay with his hands crossed on his belly and his mouth a little open, breathing in a way that used the whole chest and got nothing from it. His skin had the colour of a pot that has stood too long away from the fire, and the nails of one hand showed blue at the base. Two women sat with him and a third stood at the hearth with her back to the room, and none of them was crying, because this was ordinary work done in the ordinary way. One of them had her fingers on the man's wrist and was counting something there; her lips moved with the count, and she did not look up when the collector came in. The other had a fold of wool in her hands and was pressing it flat over the man's chest with the same certainty Alda Reed had used at a boy's shoulder three mornings ago, as if a hand could keep the warmth in by holding it down.

He did not come with a procession. He came in out of the cold with a flat leather satchel under one arm, set it on the bench without looking at the bed, and the room made room for him the way a room makes room for a man who has been in a hundred rooms like this one and will be in a hundred more. His coat was the colour of wet wool; his cuff was soft at the wrist from much washing and folded back twice so the forearms lay bare. The fingers of his right hand were grey at the tips, the colour of skimmed milk gone off, and he kept that hand closed until he needed it. Sella did not step forward and did not step back. She had placed the basket and would keep the placing, and the collector passed her without appearing to see either the basket or the woman.

He set his hand on the slate before he looked at the man on the bed.

The boy watched the order of it: satchel down, hand to slate, then face. He had watched that order at the edge of a market, where a clerk in a washed cuff had laid his hand on the top slate before he looked at the woman waiting, and he had watched the same order at the edge of a flare light, where a woman in a coat the colour of wet ash had written before she looked up. The hand under his eye now was a man's hand and belonged to neither of them. It was the grammar he had come to recognise before the face: settle the thing first, look at the person after. He had no word for the grammar in \lore{Grathic} and had never heard anybody in a room like this one give the order a name.

From the satchel the collector took a roll of cloth, and from the cloth a tube of \lore{cold iron} the length of a finger, with a cloth stopper in one end and a narrow mouth at the other. The iron had been cold in the cloth and it was colder on the bench; the girl at the hearth moved her cup away from it without being told. Nobody said anything over the tube. Nobody crossed himself. A collector setting a tube on a bench in Low Kiln was an ordinary thing, like a man setting down a tool he means to use and has used before, and the room went on with what it had been doing. The woman at the wrist finished her count and began it again from the start.

\person{Daniel Marchetti} had stood at the foot of tables in a white room and known the order of an operation whether the surgeon said it aloud or not: the field, the way in, the instrument, the response of the thing underneath, the complication, the cost. He had learned that order standing in blood that was not his, and had never once thought to look for it in a room that smelled of tallow. He looked for it now because there was nothing else in this room that belonged to him, and because his mind had nothing better to do with itself than find the shape of a thing he could not touch.

The collector found the field with two fingers and not with his eyes. He turned the man's arm and felt along the inside of the forearm toward the elbow, and stopped where something under the skin could be felt moving against pressure. Dan knew the place without being told. He had found it on a thousand arms in a life where the thing under the skin was blood; here the man was not bleeding and would not bleed, and the collector was not feeling for blood at all. He pushed the cuff further up and wiped the place with a rag from the satchel, unhurried, not clean in the way Dan would have called clean, but careful in the way a man is careful who has done a thing four thousand times and has never once been permitted to hurry. The rag was folded into a square before it was used and folded again when it was done, and the woman at the hearth did not have to tell him where to put it.

He set the tube against the place and held it.

His fingers did not press. They set and held, and the holding was the whole of the work. Dan had seen hands like that in the old life: the hands of a man who has reduced a shoulder a hundred times and knows that the moment of force is the moment you stop using it. The cuff was soft at the wrist, the fingertips were grey, the hand was exact, and exact was not cruel. That was the worst of it. There was nothing in the hand to object to and no part of the room objecting to it, and the room was right to, because the man on the bed had been dying before the collector came in and would have gone on dying if the collector had stayed away. The only thing in the room that objected was a bundle of wool against the wall, and it had no voice and no hand that could reach the length of the bench.

Under the tube the man changed. His breathing went shallow and then deep, the way a sleeper's goes when a cold hand is laid on him; the fingers of his free hand opened; a small sound came out of his mouth that was not a word. The woman at the wrist went on with her count. The girl at the hearth moved the pot an inch further from the draught. The man's eyes were open now and looking at the ceiling, and whatever they saw there they did not ask anybody to explain. Then the man gave.

He gave with the small flat knock.

Dan had heard it four times. He had heard it from a cup across a birth room in \place{House Silt}, and from a pot set down in \place{the Vail wheel-shed}, and twice from the district's own slate at \place{the Cross}: once in the morning under the notice, while two hundred people leaned in to hear a figure, and once in the afternoon when the market was coming apart and the barrow of grey was going out toward the north gate. Four hearings from three things, and nobody had tapped any of them for him, and there was no word in Grathic for the sound, and now a fifth, and this one had come out of a man. He knew \lore{the full note} from its flat shadow and had no word for either in this language or in any other he could reach.

It was not a crash. A dropped pot says crash and everybody turns; this said knock, small and flat, and stopped at the surface, as though the thing that had been holding had only been set down and not emptied. He knew what it was without anybody telling him, the way he had known the shape of a chest going under a spreader and the particular quiet that follows a clamp coming off a vessel that has stopped. The walls going. The thing held going out. The sound finishing where it began. He had known it since he was twenty-four years old and had never once been able to do anything about it, and now he was six days old and lying in a basket against a wall with wool under his chin. The fifth hearing stood with the other four and was not like them, because the other four had come out of clay and stone and this one had come out of flesh that had been warm this morning.

Nobody in the room looked up at the sound. The woman at the wrist went on with her count; the collector kept the tube where it was for the space of two breaths, the way a surgeon holds pressure in a cavity after the bleeding has stopped, because stopping is not the same as finished; then he took the tube away and wrapped it in the cloth. The man's arm lay on the folded rag with a pale round place on it where the iron had been, not a wound, no blood, only a place where the colour had gone out of the skin. The woman pressing the fold flat at the man's chest stopped pressing and set her hands in her lap. The room went on being ordinary with a concentration that Dan recognised: this was how a room carried on when a thing had happened that nobody intended to make into a thing.

Dan knew what had happened better than anybody in that room. He had spent his life knowing it and there was no part of him that could move toward it. His hand outside the fold went up an inch toward the bench, toward the tube in its cloth, toward the slate where the number was going, and came down. The wool closed over it. Nobody saw the hand. Nobody had said a word to the basket since the door had let him in. Correctness was the whole humiliation, and the man with the soft cuff went on being reasonable.

\scenebreak

The collector set the slate square on the bench and wrote. The chalk went down in a neat, ordinary hand, small and upright, the hand of a man who has written the same line ten thousand times and has never needed to improve it. It was not the other hand. There was no pause in it to set beside the three he carried, nothing between the clusters that would have let him put this writing with the writing at the flare and the writing on the birth-slate and the writing at the Cross, and he made nothing of it. He had learned to leave the three where they lay. When the figure was down he passed his finger over it once, the way a man checks a measure he has taken twice and does not expect to find wrong, and the slate lay flat under his palm while the chalk rested in its groove.

He spoke once, and he spoke softly, to the slate and not to the room, and not to the man on the bed, and least of all to the basket. “The measure is banked,” he said. “The account stands. The return is recorded.” He said it in the voice the clerk at the Cross had used, a voice with the verbs taken out of it and nobody left inside the sentence to have done anything. There was a reason in the man's face, and Dan could read a reason in a face the way he read a hand-grip: the collector believed he was keeping the world alive by doing what he had done, and had done it every winter of his working life, and would go home to a warm room when the day's names were all entered. The reason was not cruelty. The reason was the danger of him, which was a smoother thing to live beside than a cruelty would have been, and so more useful to everybody in the room except the man on the bed.

The figure on the slate was the figure the market had leaned in to hear two days ago: \lore{one in twelve}, printed on a notice soft at one corner, read aloud over the heads of two hundred people who went home with it to their pots and their suppers. Here it went down in chalk on a slab of stone beside a bed, with nobody leaning in. At the Cross the number had been a thing people could shout about or agree to or stand side by side with another number and leave disagreeing; the pigs went over the boards and the number went over the heads of the crowd and neither was asked to account for the other. Here it lay under a man's hand with a neat edge to it, and the man it had come out of was breathing in another way now than he had been breathing a little while ago, and no woman in the room straightened the fold at his chin.

He could not reach the tube. He could not read what was written. He could not lift a knuckle toward the bench where the chalk lay in the groove of the slate's frame. He could not have said the word for any part of it in Grathic, and if he had had the word the body under the wool would have made of it a small wet sound and nothing more. The year's first account had been taken and entered where nobody had addressed him; the house that had kept him warm since the third day had carried him to see it done and had not once told him what it was, and the woman who carried him stood a half-pace off and would not be the one to tell him now.

His hand went up an inch outside the fold and came down. It did not reach the bench. It did not reach his own chin.

The women went back to their work. The girl at the hearth set the pot a little further out of the draught and said something to it about the thaw. The collector closed the satchel and laid the slate flat under his hand, the measure entered in a neat, ordinary hand that any officer would have been glad to sign. The flat knock had gone into the room's ordinary noise, with the pot and the draught and the women's low talk. Nobody was tapping anything. The boy lay with his knuckle outside the wool, going up an inch and coming down, and listened to the sound finish.
