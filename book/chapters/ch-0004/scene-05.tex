Dusk came down into \place{the Clay Yard} not as a fall of dark but as a thinning of light through wet wool and woodsmoke, until the rims of the pots on the drying rack went soft and the trodden ash underfoot took the colour of skimmed milk. Behind the house \place{the Slip Brook} went on talking to itself in the dark, and beyond it \place{the Ilm} lay under its skin of ice. \person{Corin} came back under the Vail roof in the basket the way he had gone out of it --- carried, not consulted --- with his chin bad again and a thread of drool at the corner of his mouth that had frozen in the cold and would not wipe itself away. The door did not quite shut behind them. It never did. He had learned to measure the width of its failure by the sound of the wind getting in, and tonight the wind got in at a finger's width and went along the floor to the skirting board, where the grey lay waiting in the corner as if it had been poured there and left to settle.

\person{Sella Vail} set the basket down where the fire could reach it and nowhere near a slate, though no slate was out. She put her little-door hand into the fold and set the fold right at his ear and under his chin without once looking at his face, the way a potter straightens a rim by touch in the dark; then the hand went back to the coals. It did not go to \lore{the widow's mark}, the thumb-length of lead-glazed clay on its cord at her throat. The mark was for the kiln-mouth and the hour before it opened, and the kiln had been shut all day and would stay shut until morning, so the hand stayed where it was. With the back of a broken pot she drew the ash over the coals in a slow sweep, pressing the edges down with the flat of her palm, making a house for the heat to sleep in.

“There,” she said to the fire. “That'll hold.” She did not say where they had been. She did not say what had been taken in the room they had been in, or from whom, or why the boy had come home with a fold that smelled of soap and tallow and the sour edge of another house's corner. She said nothing to the basket at all, because the basket was not a person and had not asked her anything.

He had the rule of the mark now, as he had the rule of the kiln's breathing: touched before the opening, at no other time, not even to answer a man who had spoken her name in a market and called the answering a kindness. He did not know what the rule was for. He knew only that it held, the way a seam holds when the heat is right, and that the household went on keeping its fire inside the rule and would not step outside it to explain anything. A man who had spent his life making people hold still while he counted could respect a rule he could not read. He lay and listened to the coals take.

Outside, \person{Tarn} swept the yard a second time. He had swept it before anyone woke and he was sweeping it again now that the light had gone, because a yard swept twice is a yard nobody can say was left untidy, and a debt who leaves a thing untidy gives the house a handle on him. The boy watched him through the door's finger's width of gap. Tarn moved fast and stopped altogether whenever he was told he was doing well, and no one had told him anything today. Grey \lore[the Ash]{Ash} sat in the creases of his knuckles and the pale ring showed at his left wrist where the iron clip had sat six years ago.

He set the broom against the wall and took the small bench knife out of his pocket and put it back on the bench where a man could count it, and he did this unasked, as he had done it on the morning the boy came under the roof. “I took the small knife. I put it back. You can check.” He said it to the yard. Nobody had asked him to check anything. The boy knew the shape of a sentence said before anyone had asked the question, and had heard it in another life from men who wanted an account settled before it was opened.

Then the broom went back to work, and the boy waited for it to stop. He had learned that Tarn's broom stopped for two strokes when a pot set down in the shed gave its small flat note, and that Tarn never turned his head and never named the sound. It had stopped that morning. It might stop again. The pot on the bench gave nothing now; the note was gone into the house's ordinary noise, and the yard had only the rasp of the bristles and the wind at the door. The broom did not stop. The boy kept the count where it was and did not add to it. There was no word in this country for the note and no breath in him to push a word out, and if he had had both, the sentence would not have been the one Tarn was waiting to hear. A man could keep \lore{the full note} for six years without ever saying it aloud. The boy understood keeping. He had no idea what the keeping cost.

“Yes, house. I'll sweep it. I swept it this morning. I'll sweep it again.” Tarn said it to the ground and went on sweeping, in the tone of a man stating the weather. He had put the knife back where it could be checked and he kept his head down over the broom, and the boy read the lowered head as a man keeping the one thing he owned out of the house's reach. Tarn had said nothing about the note. The boy had said nothing about it either. That made two of them, which was the first thing about this house that he could have called an equality, if he had had a word for it.

\person{Jorra Vail} came in from the back room while Tarn was still sweeping. There was slate dust on his forearms, grey and fine, worked into the creases of the wrist and up past the bend of the little finger, and it was not the bone-coloured slip he wore at the wheel. The boy had learned to tell the two dusts apart the way he had learned to tell one breath of the kiln from the next. Slate dust meant the back room. It meant the low talk and the men whose names were not spoken and the door that was kept closed. Slate dust meant a thing the house did not enter in any roll it showed.

Nobody asked him where he had been. That was the arrangement, and Jorra kept his end of it. He went through into \place{the Vail wheel-shed} and drew his hand over the rim of a pot as if checking a thing he already knew, and said to the pot, “You'll do.” Then, to the room in general and to Sella in particular, without looking at her, “The back room's cold.” It was not an answer. It was not a question. It was a sentence shaped so that nobody could take it further, and Sella did not take it further. She shifted the damper instead, and he shifted it again before she had finished. \person{Garl Vail} was not in the room. His coat kept its salt-stiff shape on its peg and his seat kept its hollow on the left, and the two of them stood for a moment in the silence that had stood there since the boy came under the roof.

The boy watched the dust on the forearms and wanted to know which slate had been broken, and whether it had been a birth-slate, and who had brought it into the back room. He had seen the broken edge of a slate in the kiln ash every day of his life and nobody had ever picked it up. He could not ask Jorra anything, and if he could have asked, Jorra would have answered the pot. So he kept the questions where he kept the rest of it, and the questions went on being questions, which in this house was a thing that could last for years.

\scenebreak

When Sella lifted the basket again to carry him from the door to his bed under the roof, the boy's angle cleared the doorway and the yard opened out beyond it, three doors down to the threshold of \place{House Silt}. \person{Dessa Silt} stood there with her shoulder against the jamb.

The cough came up out of her as it came up every winter, from the pits and the water in the pits, and she put her hand over her mouth and said to the yard, “Weather.” She did not call it anything else. Her thumb went to the pale scar on her left forearm, the long one where sieve-wire had opened her, and stayed there while she decided something, and when she took it away her face arranged itself into the smile her sister called the door shut: nothing further would be said here, by her, to anybody. She was a thin woman worked older than her years, with slip-grey nails and wet clay at the hem, and she stood at the end of a short walk she had taken a thousand times and had not taken today.

The murmur came across the yard on the cold air, too thin for words and shaped like counting all the same. Nineteen steps from the pit to the drying rack, nineteen back, every day for six years, and now the count had gone out of her feet and into her thumb and was running there without her legs. She counted because the count kept a larger thing out of the room. The boy knew a count used as a door when he saw one; he had kept a few of his own. He could not see what lay behind hers.

She did not look into the basket. That was the whole of it, and the boy lay under the fold and took the not-looking in with the cold air. It was the kind that keeps, not the kind that prices; he had learned the two apart without either woman explaining them. The woman at the threshold had taken the figure that was read aloud in \place{the Cross} and had made it a person and laid that person down somewhere out of reach, and she had done it so long ago that the laying down felt like a fact and not a thing she had done. She would not say the number, and she would not say the name, and she had not said either for three years. The boy did not know the number or the name. He knew the shape of an account closed by somebody who had never been allowed to open it.

He wanted something from her that he could not have named and she could not have given if he had. His mouth worked under the wool. A small animal sound came out, and Sella shifted the basket up against her hip and went on toward the bed, and the woman at the threshold went on counting into her thumb, and the yard went on being a yard with two houses in it and a broom at the third.

\scenebreak

Night came on and the house drew in around its fire. The boy lay on his bed under the roof with the fold at his chin and the cold coming in at the door's finger's width, and he set the day's facts side by side the way he set nails in a board: four on the near board, four on the next, and a gap. The Ash ridge along the skirting board of the death-house, thicker by a finger's width than the ridge under this roof, turning the corner without a break. The ridge under this roof, near two thumbs wide, which Sella had not swept in two years and would not let Jorra sweep. The charred edge of a slate lying in the kiln ash, black on one side and eaten into the shape of a strip of bark, with the writing gone off the end. And with them the grey barrow that had gone out through \place{the north gate} on the day of the reading, heaped a little above the sides where the load had shifted, and nobody in the square looking at it twice. He kept them side by side and did not join them, because nobody had explained any of them and nobody had asked him to understand them, and joining them would be a kind of deciding that he had no standing to make.

The figure that had been read aloud in the square --- \lore{one in twelve}, as the notice had it --- had gone out of a person in a room with a pot on a bench and a draught under the door, and the room had gone back to keeping a child warm. The year's first measure had been taken at \lore{the Hearth Drawing}, and the Drawing was behind him now, and the district would go on calling the day ordinary. The boy knew the two things were the same number. He knew it the way he knew a full note from a cracked one, without being told and without any use for the knowing. The household had not changed its arithmetic. The fire was banked. The fold was put right. The debt against the tiles lay where it had been left, uncalled and therefore unpaid and therefore never done with. Nobody in the yard had said what the measure was, and nobody had said where it went. \place{Low Kiln} would say three days to \lore{the Five} when it said anything at all, and would not say what it counted.

He kept the count the way he kept his breath, because there was nothing else in the world that was his to enter. Four, four and a gap. The kiln's four, four and the breath it missed; the floorboards' four and four and the knot the width of a thumb that was not a nail; the bell's four, four and the pause where the fifth stroke should have been. The gap was the part nobody had thought to enter. It lay in the kiln and in the floor and in the bell and in the space between a woman's thumb and her scar, and it was the one place in this house where nothing had been written down. He could not write there either. He could only lie with his hand outside the wool and let the gap be the part he kept.

Sella fed him from the house's own dish, barley gruel thinned with milk and warmed at the fire, and she fed him with a spoon she had wiped on her sleeve and did not enter the spoon or the gruel or the boy in anything. She held his head with two fingers behind the ear because he could not hold it himself, and the warmth went into him through his mouth and belly and stayed, and no slate in the district had a line for it. The house had paid twice for him and had now taken a measure out of the district and had not said so, and it went on keeping him the way it kept the fire: by doing the thing every day and leaving no entry where an officer might look for one.

The coals held under their ash. The little-door burn closed on nothing as Sella put the fold right at his chin and went on to the next thing without looking. Along the skirting board the grey ridge turned the corner without a break. His hand outside the wool went up an inch and came down. Four, four and a gap, and Low Kiln saying three days to the Five without saying what it counted, and the door not quite shutting.
