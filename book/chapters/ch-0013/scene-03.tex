\daymark{57}{Ilmen 28, Y.H. 813}

\person{Sella Vail} stood at the shelf with the little-door burn shut on nothing, moving a loaf along the row the way a woman moves a thing she has already decided about and forgotten she decided. She set it down where it had stood, straightened it, and came back to the same place a second time with her lips working over the days. Under the roof of \place{the Vail kiln-house} the morning still held the yard's cold in it, woodsmoke and wet wool and the faint sour mineral breath of a corner nobody swept. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. To the fire, which was not a person and would hear her: “It's the thaw. Feed it.” To the bucket she gave the instruction she gave everything that had no ears of its own. The basket lay on the bed under the fold and she did not look into it. Three, her lips said, and two, and whatever the moot gives out, and never the other thing, which was the figure. \person{Corin} lay where she had set him, fifty-seven days old, carried everywhere, and heard the days go around in loaves and glances the way a man hears a slate read in a room he has not been allowed to enter.

He kept the number exactly, which was the only work in that house that needed no hands. From Ilmen 28 to Silt 1 was three days, and he counted them in the order a surgeon counts what is left before a hand goes in: the ones he can see, the ones he can reach, the one that will not be there when he gets there. Three. He set them on a tray of their own. He had once stood over a field triage and known, without laying a finger on anyone, how many of the bodies on the ground would still be warm by morning; the arithmetic had been the same then and the hands had been his. Now the arithmetic was the same and the hands were a folded inch of wool outside a blanket. He wanted the number spoken aloud by somebody who could spend it, and nobody spoke it. Knowing it bought him nothing he could lift a hand to, and the dry joke of that lay beside the count like a second loaf nobody had thought to put on the shelf.

\person{Jorra Vail} came in from \place{the Vail wheel-shed} with slip dried to the colour of old bone up both forearms, the bent little finger going in ahead of his hand the way it always went, and no grey worked past the bend of it. He spoke to the wheel before he spoke to anyone, and told it what it had been told a hundred times: it did not care whose hands were on it, only if they were steady. To a pot turning under his palm he said, “You'll do,” in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either. He knew \lore{the full note} of a Vail pot before it left the wheel --- knew it in the wrist before the knuckle ever went near it --- and would not say so before a kiln-master, who would call it early and make something of it. The boy under the fold kept that knowing where questions are allowed to last for years. Three doors down \place{the Clay Yard} a tin kept its shelf and its pale fold, and he did not make a journey of it; here a wheel kept a hollow seat worn by a man who was not Jorra, and the two keepings lay on one tray and did not touch.

Sella had not finished telling him the fire was low. Jorra's bent finger went in ahead of his hand at the damper and moved it, and the end of her sentence arrived at the pot instead of at him, so she shut her mouth on it and gave it to the clay. The scoop and scrape of iron came through the room and the fire took the change without being told twice. He did not say the back room was cold. He did not look toward the bed or the basket. \place{the Debt Loft} kept its ladder and its unmarked clay pot above the shed; the room off the shed kept its finger's width of open, and nobody pushed it wider. \emph{He's not Vail} stayed where it had been set on the hour it was spoken, sealed as tightly as a lid, and the foundling went on being carried under the roof without a name written against him. Jorra went back to the wheel. The yard straightened around his silence without an order.

\person{Tarn} had his small bench knife back on its sill with six years counted and the seventh held back --- the one entry in this house the house could not check, because a man who owns nothing is the only one who knows what a debt is worth. \lore{the Ash} was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the line in the corner, and no scrubbing lifted it; the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back. To the neighbour who wanted the thaw early he said, “Yes, house,” and to the one who wanted it late he said, “Yes, house,” and agreed so thoroughly that the argument had nowhere to put its foot. He went on wanting the late one. A late thaw puts off the paying; that was all of it, and it was enough. When Sella told him he had done well he stopped altogether for two breaths, then set the knife where it could be checked and went back to his broom.

A vessel gave its note past the yard door --- a small flat knock in the clay air, the sound of a pot that has handed over all it had and kept nothing in reserve. Tarn's head did not turn. He did not name the sound, and there was no \lore{Grathic} word in the yard for the thing it was, and he had spent six years not supplying one. The boy under the fold did not call it a seventh hearing. Six hearings stayed six, five from four sources and one out of a person who had been warm that morning three doors down, and this was not a sixth and not a seventh and not anything he had standing to enter. Two ears kept their equality under one roof without a word passing between them, and the loan that could hear stayed uncalled. A man whose ear is worth more than his debt is a man a house might one day learn to count; the sentence lay under the roof all day and nobody said it.

\person{Garl Vail} was kept in the house the way a room is kept by what has not been moved out of it. His salt-stiff coat held a broader man's shape on its peg behind the door, the hollow wheel-seat kept its twenty-year lean that nobody filled, and the clean dark line where the Ash ridge had turned its corner stayed unswept, because a line left where it is is still a line kept. One of his proverbs went to the pot and to the fire and never to the basket: leave the crack showing; people trust a thing they can see has been handled. It went now to a pot with no crack in it and to a fire that had been fed rather than opened. Behind them the carried fire lay under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees and missing one breath in every four --- \lore{the kiln's breath}, named in this house as the thing that takes a man in his sleep. Four, four and a gap. The coal the brine-gull took upriver on Day 45 was entered in no slate and missed in no account, and twelve days had gone over it without a figure.

The saying brought downriver in a stranger's road-mud two mornings ago lay on its own shelf in the boy's keeping: a counting room shut in the last war, a damper closed, the men inside gone of the breath with no way to feed it. The yard had received it as history, a thing that happened eighteen leagues east with a date in it, and the boy had filed it beside the told bell and the heard bell and this gap under his own roof, and had written no cause across any of them. \place{Greybank} was a direction and a day's walk and a man's wet boots; the Vail kiln was a room under whose floorboards he lay, and the two things had been kept apart on the day they were spoken and were kept apart still. A room can be sealed and the bill paid by the bodies inside it. He had stored the sentence without understanding what made it fit, the way a resident stores a fact on a chart and turns back to it years later to find the shape it had been waiting to make. Here the fire had been fed, not sealed; the gap was in the breathing and not in the damper; the house kept its absent man by his coat and his seat and would not call the keeping an account. The likeness was a likeness and not a cause. Six hearings stayed six. He had made that mistake once in a room with better light and had carried the correction into every room since, and he would not make it here for the sake of a sentence a stranger had let fall at a well.

One crust lay in the fold where two had lain, brown-edged and stiff past smelling, and the empty place beside it was carried into the new day without a name. He wanted the crust and could not reach it; he wanted the number spoken and nobody would speak it. The wanting and the knowing sat in him together like two instruments in a case that had lost its hands. The child's body wanted the bread, and wanted it louder; the other want was for a slate with a figure on it that he could read and answer and set down, and the surgeon went on counting while a mouth worked at a crust two inches out of reach. Both were true. The second had been the quieter one for fifty-seven days. A thread of drool froze at his mouth in the yard cold and nobody wiped it, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left; his head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and the cold arrived in its known order, cheek, then temple, then wrist. He was the only adult in the room and the least use in it, a man who had once held pressure in a cavity with both hands and now could not lift a crust to his own mouth. The fold smelled of woodsmoke and wool and nothing of bread that had ever been warm. Beside the crust the empty place kept its seat in the fold as steadily as the hollow seat kept its lean behind the door, and the boy set the two keepings on separate places and let neither become the other.

The house kept its days badly in loaves and glances because to keep them exactly would be to own them, and a thing owned can be taken up by a man with a slate. The boy kept them exactly because counting was the only work left to a body that could not roll over. He had once reduced a shoulder in a room where the light was better and the instrument had been in his hand; here he had the field and the count and no hand, and the count went on being exact and useless with the patience of a thing that has never once been thanked. Three days to Silt, and the month was named for what the river leaves on its banks when it goes down. The district had one word for the water coming up and the stores going down and the empty space at the end of the loaf row, and the word was weather, and it let a person go home warm without owning a day of it.

At the end of the morning Sella moved the loaf along the shelf once more and came back to the same place, her lips going over three and two and whatever the moot gave out. There was a space at the end of the row where a loaf should have stood, and she did not call it a number; a space left where a thing had been is a thing kept, and the boy had learned to read the difference between a keeping and an account without a word in Grathic for either. Six hearings of the full note stayed six, five from four sources and one out of a person, beside them and never among them; the two nearly-full answers stayed advanced and not paid, the word stayed a saying on its own tray, the five unjoined pauses stayed unjoined, and the told bell stayed beside the heard bell with the early note beside both and none of them touching the count of three. He counted the three days in the dark under the roof while the boards took their warmth by degrees and the fire missed one breath in every four. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth and came down.
