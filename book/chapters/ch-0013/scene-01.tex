\daymark{55}{Ilmen 26, Y.H. 813}

\dropcap{T}{he bucket} went over the lip of \place{the yard well} before there was light enough to see the water in it, and the rope came up cold and a little stiff, keeping the shape of the hand that had pulled it. White had written on the stone through the small hours where \lore{the Warm} had lately gone out, fronds and a branching that ran to nothing; by noon it would be gone and leave no slate the poorer. \lore{frost-bloom} came and went in this month the way a figure comes and goes in a clean hand --- entered nowhere, paid by nobody, and gone by the time anyone thought to call a witness. \person{Corin} lay in the basket in the lee of the well's little roof with the fold turned right under his chin, fifty-five days old, and watched the rope come up and the water lean and spill. A skin lay on the water where the bucket broke it, thin as the skin on milk gone cold, and the women at the tubs spoke of that skin to the bucket and not to him. There's a skin on it. Keep it off his ears. The instruction went to the rope and the wool, and the boy under the wool was not part of the sentence.

\person{Sella Vail} set the basket down in the well's shade out of the draught as she would set a pot that must not be slopped, the little-door burn on the back of her hand shutting on the wicker and then on nothing. She had carried him down before the light at a half-pace from every open slate, going around one propped at the rack end as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it over, and she had not looked into the basket since. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. “It's the river wind.” To the fire at home, which was not here and would hear her anyway: “It's the thaw. Feed it.” She said the house where another woman would say I, and gave every instruction to the fire, the wool and the bucket and never to the basket. A basket set down is a pot set down and left; that was the whole of the rule, and the boy had learned to lie under it without expecting to be asked.

A man came down the road from the north with road-mud dried grey on his boots to the calf and the salt-and-hot-clay smell of the east road on him, a smell that opened the yard's nostrils the way a door opens in a warm room. He was not one of theirs. He waited his turn at the bucket with a bag across one shoulder and the patience of a man who has walked eighteen leagues and knows a queue when he sees one, and when the bucket came up he held it a moment before he drank, because a man who has come that far does not put his mouth to water without looking at it first. The yard looked at him and at his mud, and went on with its buckets.

“You'll have come from \place{Greybank},” a woman at the tubs said.

“From east of it,” he said. “Eighteen leagues, near enough. The road's a pot that's boiled over since the thaw.”

He drank, and wiped his mouth with the back of a hand that had carried a load, and then he told them, because a man who has walked eighteen leagues owes the yard a thing to say for the loan of its water. It was a story with a date in it, which is the way such stories are told so that nobody has to believe them.

“Last war,” he said. “There was a counting room in a house east of us. Shut up for the duration, they said. Men inside it keeping the accounts. They shut the damper to save the coals ---” he made a small sound in his throat, iron closing on iron, the sound of a thing with no word for it in his mouth “--- and the men in the room died of the breath. Not the war's hand. Their own heat went out of the room and there was no way to feed it.”

The boy watched the man's hands as he drank. They were the hands of a man who had carried a load and meant to carry another: the knuckles broad, a pale line where a strap had worried the palm, the nails grey with road-dust. He held the bucket with both hands and did not let it rest on the lip, as if water set down was water lost. A pulse beat in his throat, slow and steady, and the breath came short through a nose caked with clay dust. Nothing about him was dangerous. The boy had once sorted twenty bodies in ninety breaths by the exact signs such a man gives --- the grip, the breath, the colour under the nails --- and here he could not sort one stranger out of a queue, could not have tapped his chest and asked him to say the thing again. The skill had gone nowhere. It had simply stopped having a handle.

The yard took the story the way a yard takes a thing that has happened eighteen leagues east. A woman with her sleeves in the wash said the war did for them, which settled it. A man at the racks said a room shut up was a room nobody had counted, and the men who had died in it had died of being unentered, which was a joke that cost nothing because the dead were not there to hear it. Somebody said the frost would take the pits early at this rate, and somebody else said the pits had been worked early already and the colour of the water would tell you. Nobody said kiln. Nobody said Vail. Nobody looked toward the roof of the house behind him where the chimney stood with its breath held under blind ash. The story went around the bucket and came back as a curiosity with a date, and the yard went on with its morning.

Sella, who had heard every word because a woman who keeps a fire hears what is said about heat, did not connect it. She told the fire it was the thaw and told it to be fed. “Cold in the east,” she said to the coals that were not there, and then, to the bucket: “Easy. There's a skin on it.” She had lost a husband to the thing the man had just described and had never once described it that way herself. The house kept its own word for it, a low word that named the danger and the mercy in the same breath, and she had said that word to a boy at a kiln mouth years ago and warned him away. She did not say it now. A room shut up eighteen leagues east was a room shut up eighteen leagues east; the mouth that told it had mud on its boots and no reason to lie, and no reason to be heard either.

But the boy in the shade heard two things that the yard let go past it. He heard the man say the breath, and he heard him say the damper closed. There was no word in \lore{Grathic} that held either thing the way the man held it, not for a room, not for the stopping of air in a room, and the two phrases lay in the basket beside him like tools with no handle. He knew what the man had described. He had known it since he was old enough to lie awake under this roof and count the fire's breathing in the dark --- four low pulls and a silence where the fifth should have been, four and a gap, night after night. He knew the anatomy of it as he knew the anatomy of a wound that closes over a cavity and seals what is in it: a vessel with no way to feed what it holds, the contents cooking their own heat out of the air, the face arranging itself in the ordinary way at the end. He had once stood in a room whose door would not open and known, before anyone said it, how it would finish, and had written it down in a calm voice and gone on to the next body. The surgeon's register came and went without a word of the other life in it. Here there was no word at all, and no room to put one, and no hand to open a door if there had been one.

He filed the Greybank saying on a tray of its own. Beside it, in four places that did not touch, he kept the bell told in the dark of \lore{the Long Night}, four, four and a gap; the bell heard in the square at \place{the Cross}, four, four and a gap with nobody crossing himself; and the breath of \place{the Vail kiln-house} itself, four low draws and a missing fifth, warming the boards by degrees under blind ash. Four low things kept in four places. The early note of \place{the Singing Sill} lay with the bells and the kiln's gap and did not join them. The stranger's room shut up eighteen leagues east went on the newest tray, and he wrote no cause across any of them, because three likenesses laid in a row had once taught him in a better-lit room what a man can do with an answer he wants too much. The tray did not become a cause. It was the whole of the work available to him and it bought him nothing he could lift a hand to.

\scenebreak

Six hearings of \lore{the full note} stayed six, five from four sources and one out of a person who had been warm that morning, beside them and never among them, and no seventh came to the well. The two nearly-full answers stayed on their own tray, advanced and not paid, with no hand to tap the pots and ask them to say the thing again. Five unjoined pauses stayed unjoined. Word, shape, cords, crust, three hands, three near-misses --- each kept its place, and the tray of the Greybank saying was set down among them without touching any. A room can be sealed and the bill paid by the bodies inside; he had seen that arithmetic done in a room with better light and had once performed it himself, with a clamp and a hand held up for silence. Here the arithmetic was done by a stranger over a bucket and called a war. He could not lift a hand to the bucket, could not say a word of it, could not have written the word on the wet stone even if a hand had been given him.

\person{Tarn} came up the yard with his broom and the small bench knife back on its sill before anyone had asked after it, six years of the seven counted and the seventh held where he always held it, the one entry in this house the house could not check. \lore{the Ash} was worked into the creases of his knuckles that no scrubbing lifted, the colour of the clean dark line where the ridge had turned, and the pale clip-ring at his left wrist caught what light there was and gave it back. A neighbour at the rack end said the thaw would come early and the pits would pay for it; another said a late thaw was a longer week of a man's own fire. “Yes, house,” Tarn said to the first, and “Yes, house,” to the second. He agreed so thoroughly with the early and the late that there was nowhere for an argument to put its foot, and went on wanting the late one, because a late thaw puts off the paying. He owned no sentence a house could enter and kept the account he most exactly where nobody could check it. His broom went over the road a man takes when a house wants a collector, over and over, without once turning along it. No collector went down it for \person{Dessa Silt}, seventeen mornings dead in the clay-heavy rise behind the racks, and the omission had no word and had not made a doctrine of itself. A loan that can hear is a loan that can be called; the sentence had never been said under this roof and lay under it all the same.

The stranger finished his water and gave the bucket back with a nod that cost him nothing and was worth something, and the mud on his boots dried lighter where the sun found it. Somebody asked him what year he had put to the story, and he gave a year two wars back and a month the yard did not use, and the answer made it more true, not less, because a date is what a thing has when it has stopped happening to anybody. He shifted the bag on his shoulder and went back down the road the way he had come, with the salt-and-hot-clay smell going off him into the yard's own smell of wet wool and woodsmoke, and the yard took up its buckets where it had left them. A woman at the tubs said the war had been a bad business and went on with her washing. That was the whole of what the story became by the second bucket: a bad business, eighteen leagues east, with a date in it. The man had carried a description of the danger under this roof into a yard that had no use for it and had left it there like a tool somebody else might want, and gone home with his bag and his mud. The boy kept it where it was set down.

The body humiliated him exactly, as it had every morning of fifty-five. A thread of drool froze at his mouth and nobody wiped it. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and cold arrived in its known order, cheek, then temple, then wrist, the wool doing less than the wind wanted. The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the bucket's rim and came down. Reducing a shoulder had been ordinary work in the life that was not this one; keeping his own hand on a lip of cold iron was not, and the joke had not gone stale and had never once been funny enough to say aloud. He was the only adult at this well and the least use in it. He had heard a man describe the way a room kills the men inside it, and had known the description for true before the man had finished saying it, and could not have lifted a finger to write the word for it on the stone lip where the frost had already written and gone.

\scenebreak

At the turn of the wind Sella came for him and lifted the basket level, and carried him up \place{the Clay Yard} at a half-pace from every open slate. Behind her Tarn's broom went over the trodden clay, going and coming, going and coming, without once turning along the road a man takes when a house wants a collector. Under \place{the Vail kiln-house} roof she set him on his bed and not in \place{the Debt Loft}, and put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without looking at his face. She said nothing about the man with the mud on his boots, or the room shut up eighteen leagues east, or the damper closed. If she had connected any of it she would have put the connection where she put the rest of it, in the private roll nobody was shown; but she had not connected it, and there was nothing to put away. She touched \lore{the widow's mark} at no hour of this day, because a well is not an opening and a basket is not an opening, and a story told over a bucket is not one either.

\person{Garl Vail} was absent and kept by objects, which in this house is a way of keeping a man no Court can enter. His salt-stiff coat held a broader man's shape on the peg behind the door and the hollow seat kept its twenty-year lean and nobody filled either. The clean dark line where the Ash ridge had turned its corner was not swept, because a line left where it is is still a line kept, and one of his proverbs went to the pot and the fire and the other to the fire again, and neither went to the basket. Behind the boy the carried fire lay under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees and missing one breath in every four. Four, four and a gap.

He lay on his bed, fifty-five days old, with one crust in the fold and an empty place beside it carried through eight days without a name. The crust from Stone fifteen lay stiff past the smell of anything where it had always lain, unlifted and unnamed, and nobody had said either crust was gone. The Greybank saying lay on its own tray with no cause written across it, and the six hearings stayed six beside it and never among, and the two nearly-full answers stayed advanced and not paid. Nothing had been joined, paid, explained or ended. Somewhere eighteen leagues east a room that had been shut in a war was still a story with a date in it, and under this roof the fire breathed four and missed one, and the two things did not touch. The count went on exactly as the house kept it badly. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth and came down.
