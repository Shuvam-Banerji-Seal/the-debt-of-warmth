\daymark{59}{Ilmen 30, Y.H. 813}

Before light the market was nothing but wet boards and men's breath. \person{Corin} came down into it in the basket under the fold, carried level as a pot filled is carried, with his cheek turned to the cold iron of \place{the moot-post} before there was anybody to see him. It was \lore{Account-day}, the tenth and the last of Ilmen, and Silt stood at the door with mud on its boots. He kept the number exactly, the way he kept all the numbers now, because nobody in the house would give it: one more day and the month would turn, and the month was named for what the river left behind. The district would give out whatever the moot had already decided to pay. He held the one figure that was his to hold and could not spend it on a crust.

\person{Sella Vail} set the basket in the post's finger's width of shade and went around an open slate as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it over. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. “It's the river wind.” She did not say it to the basket. The little-door burn on the back of her hand shut on nothing and opened again as she settled the fold; she did not look into the basket, and she did not look at his face. The boards at \place{the Cross} were wet from the bucket and the pigs would be driven over them by noon, and the square was arranging itself in the manner of every market he had ever lain through. A man set a heap of turnips down and another set a figure beside it, and the two stood side by side without being made to agree. That was the whole of the morning's work, and it would be done again tomorrow with the month's name changed on the notice.

By full light \person{Berr Clay} came to the block with the red wool cord of his office folded under his coat and the green of tasted glaze at one corner of his mouth. He smelled of hot clay over nothing at all, which is the most expensive smell in \place{Grath}. He wiped his thumb twice on his apron before touching nothing, and held the narrow slate where a man holds a thing that must stay clean. “One in twelve, as the notice says,” he said low into the top of the leaning crowd, and said it a second time for the households that came late. “The levy is called for Silt. It is not called by me.” A neighbour began a question and Berr took himself out of his own sentence as a surgeon takes a hand out of a field before the dressing goes on. “A number goes into a slate, not into a well. There'll be a slate at the watch if the house wants it again.”

The true subtraction went over the top of the basket and was spoken by nobody. The number for what the thaw and the flood would cost went from a fist to a mouth to a shoulder and arrived nowhere; a man brought it to the block in his hand and thought better of giving it, and put the hand in his sleeve. The uncalled debt \person{Garl Vail} left against a firing of tiles went around the space where a name might have stood and was not called, as Sella went around \lore{the Ash} in a corner. The crowd leaned, and the figure stood, and the pigs went on being driven over the wet boards.

The boy lay in the post's thin shade and let the figures go past him. He had once stood over a field and sorted twenty bodies into the ones who would live, the ones who would live only if he chose them, and the ones who would die whatever he did; the count had been exact and had bought no one a hand. Here the count was exact too and there was no field, only a square of wet boards and a man with a clean thumb. Six hearings of \lore{the full note} stayed six, five from four sources and the sixth out of a person, filed beside the five and never among them; the two nearly-full answers stayed advanced and not paid. The bell went four, four and a gap and nobody crossed himself. He kept the heard bell beside the bell told in the dark of \lore{the Long Night}, and the early note of \place{the Singing Sill} beside both, and the gap of \lore{the kiln's breath} under his own roof beside all of them, and none of them touched another. There was no \lore{Grathic} word for the flat answer, none for nearly, and none for the thing a district could leave lying where it lay and go home warm. He had learned in a better-lit room what three likenesses laid in a row will do to a man who wants an answer too much, and he would not let the square arrange itself into one. The trays stayed as they were. The count bought him nothing but the knowing.

The road down to \place{the Fishstairs} gave the square back its noise and then took it away again by degrees. There the eels were sold at dawn and the rumours at noon, over boards that went down four steps, four, and a landing where a step should have been. Sella set the basket in the lee of the eel creels, out of the draught, and went back to the half-pace from every open slate as if the whole morning were one room she would not let a figure enter. The boy lay with silt in the air and salt on the wind and the small wet sound of his own head going where it wanted.

The clerk sat at the edge of the steps with a cuff gone grey from washing and a slate held to his forearm. His hand went to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls. He did not speak all the while Sella stood there, and the eels went on being sold over the top of him. Word had come up with the neighbours on other mornings that nobody had taken him off; the measure he sat for had never been called, and the woman the brook took one-and-twenty mornings ago lay in a ground nobody had entered her into. \person{Dessa Silt} had her number in her thumb and her name under a scar, and neither had come up. The boy set the clerk's order of reaching on a place of its own: apart from the woman in the grey coat at the crash, apart from the grey-fingered man who gave at the Drawing, apart from the soft cuff behind the Court's table. Three records of one shape, and a fourth made nowhere. The neat upper line had been seen five times and had no name in his file; no sixth sighting came this morning, and the writer went on being a hand and a pause and nothing more.

\person{Mira Silt} worked the eels at the outer end with pale slime dried on her sleeves and river silt in the hollow of her neck, her head held a little to the right where the top of her left ear had been. She kept the small smile on her face the way another woman keeps a door on the latch. A rack-woman asked whether the run would come now that the month was turning, and Mira answered the question and her own. “Does it come now? It comes when it comes. Ask the river.” While she decided whether to lie she moved eel-bones against her thumb, clicking them together, and when the deciding was done she put one away. She had given her answer before anybody could tell which answer she had chosen.

A buyer said a figure. Her thumb went under her apron to the grey wool cord and stayed there while she decided, and came out with an eel-bone between finger and thumb. Shifting a basket showed the second cord twisted twice beneath the grey, the one that had never gone upriver to \place{Nine Pots}, never been lifted at a door, never counted by any thumb but her own. Her board lay beside \person{Orra Weir}'s \lore{knot-string} and did not touch it. The shape of the forty-first knot was legible to him without its name, tied left over right and set with the weight of a thumb, and the word had not been said again. She brought no crust and did not look into the basket. Her not-looking was a keeping; the man at the block's not-looking had been a pricing. He had no word for either in Grathic and set them on two places that did not touch.

By afternoon a barrow of grey went out through the north gate toward \place{the Bone Orchard}, heaped a little above the sides where the load had shifted, and nobody in the square looked at it twice. It was the afternoon's work, the same work that went out every market-day. The boy put the barrow with the clean dark line under the Vail skirting and the charred slate in the kiln ash and the left-in-place ridge two doors down, and joined none of them. One way of keeping was to burn a thing and call it dealt with; another was to leave it where it lay and let the corner hold its shape. He had no standing to say which the district had done, and a surgeon's eye for the thing a house refuses to look at was the least useful object in that square.

The river began to move under its skin of \place{the Ilm}. It was a stirring felt in the teeth before it was placed in the ear, a low note coming up through the boards where the water ran fast beneath the ice, with the dark current showing through the skin in veins and the hairlines stopping a thumb's breadth short of the edge. The yard called it the water running fast under the ice, and then weather. The same word covered the barrow going out and the water coming up, and a person could go home warm on it and never own the day.

He knew the anatomy of a vessel going thin: the cold from the edges inward, the small extremity first, the skin holding more than it was made to hold while the face arranged itself in the ordinary way. He knew it as exactly as he had once known the look of a man who would die whatever a surgeon did. He had no hand to point with and no word in the district's tongue for the failure, and the word the yard had was weather, which let a man go home. The last market behaved exactly like every market before it: figures unchallenged, flesh weighed, a barrow of grey going out unread. What had changed was under the ice, and nobody had entered what it might take.

The body went on with its small humiliations while the surgeon kept the count. A thread of drool froze at his mouth in the market cold and nobody wiped it, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left; his head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far; the cold arrived in its known order, cheek, then temple, then wrist, and the wool did less than the wind wanted. One crust lay in the fold where two had lain, brown-edged and stiff past smelling, and the empty place beside it was carried through another day without a name, thirteen days now since the barley breath had stopped coming off the water. He wanted the crust and could not reach it. He wanted somebody in the district to speak the number of days to Silt and nobody spoke it; the days went around in fists and mouths and whatever the moot gave out, never in a figure. Everything that went past him went past unspendable: the printed figure, the twice-wiped thumb, the two cords and the uncounted knots, the clerk still set, the barrow of grey, the note coming up through the boards. Knowing any of it exactly had never once given him a hand to lift toward it. He was the only adult in every market and the least use in any of them.

At the turn of the wind Sella lifted the basket level and carried him up \place{the Clay Yard} in \place{Low Kiln} at a half-pace from every open slate. She set him on his bed under the Vail roof and not in \place{the Debt Loft}, and put the fold right without looking at his face. Behind her the coat held a broader man's shape on its peg, the hollow seat its twenty-year lean, and the clean dark line lay under the skirting board where the Ash ridge had turned and been burned and called dealt with, unswept because a line left where it is is still a line kept. Through the floorboards came the low note of the river beginning to move under the ice, and the carried fire kept four, four and a gap. He was fifty-nine days old, with one crust in the fold and the empty place beside it, and his hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth and came down. The count went on.
