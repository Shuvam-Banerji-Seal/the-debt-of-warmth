\daymark{56}{Ilmen 27, Y.H. 813}

\person{Sella Vail} carried him three doors down \place{the Clay Yard} in the flat mid-morning light, the basket held level against her hip, the little-door burn on the back of her hand shut on the wicker and staying shut. She went around a slate propped at the rack end as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it over, and kept the distance she kept from anything with writing on it, which was a half-pace and sometimes more. \person{Corin} lay under the fold with his chin tucked and his ears out of the draught, fifty-six days old, carried everywhere and asked about nothing. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. “It's the river wind.” To the fire at home, which was not here and would hear her: “It's the thaw. Feed it.” She did not say whose house he was, or whose child, and she did not look into the basket.

\place{House Silt} had been a birth room once and was a room kept now, which in this district are the same thing. The trodden ash floor had the spring of old bread under a bare foot; a grey ridge of \lore{the Ash} ran along the skirting board from the bench by the door and went under the door into the next room, left there for the Last Day. There was the bench with its pot, the draught that went along the floor to the corner and was felt at the ankle before the cold was felt anywhere else, a stool by the wall, a hearth with a slip pot, and the board-bed with its fold of grey wool that nobody had moved since the woman who lay on it, \person{Dessa Silt}, was taken eighteen mornings ago by the clay-heavy rise behind the racks. Sella set the basket just inside the door, which stood a finger's width open, at the edge of the light and well away from anywhere a slate lay open. She held it level while she set it down, then straightened the chipped cup on the shelf and did nothing else. There was no errand in the room that a slate could enter, and she did not turn it into a walk to \place{Slate Row}. She did not touch \lore{the widow's mark}; a room with a door a finger's width open is not an opening. The boy was set where he could see and not join, as a pot is set where the light falls on it and the hand that set it goes away.

He set the room beside the others without writing a cause across any of them. The birth room, the kiln-house under whose roof he slept, the death-house two doors down with its ridge a finger's width thicker, the Court's room that had reached him only as a voice through a doorway, the Eel House upriver with salt where this room had ash: a bench, a pot, a draught and a gap in each, five likenesses kept in five places and this one the sixth. Six rooms with the same shape and no door between them that he could find, and he had learned what three likenesses laid in a row will do in a better-lit room.

The room held what it would not say in the manner of a vessel with a seam: the chipped cup straight on the shelf, the fold of wool untouched on the board-bed, a stain on the ceiling above the bed that nobody had washed or covered. The boy read these as a surgeon reads a dressing left in place after the work under it is done: evidence of a hand that had done a thing and then gone on doing it after the reason for it had gone. Nothing in the room was named. The clay jar that had come out of it stood on a shelf under another roof, and the place it had left was a space the light could lie in. Between the two houses a keeping had been divided without a word, and the boy set each half where it lay and would not make a journey of it.

From the low angle of the basket the shelf above the board-bed was a high country. On it stood a round pewter tin.

The pewter had gone dull the colour of water under river ice, all but a small oval on the lid where a thumb had rubbed it bright, a rub the size of a thumbprint and the shape of one. At the seam of the lid a pale fold showed, cloth folded over and pressed, the colour of milk gone cold. The tin still held its scrap of cloth from the birth, and had held it three years, and nobody in this room had opened it or carried it to a bank or said what it was for. The boy took it in the way he took in a seam on a pot: a wall, a closure, a place where a hand had been. He could read the thumb's rub as evidence of a hand, and the fold as evidence of a folding, and could not read past either. The seam of the lid ran all the way round, a closed line like the line where a wound has been brought together and left to heal under a dressing, and the pale fold lay against it like a dressing not yet lifted. He had lifted many such dressings and known what he would find before he lifted them; here he could not raise the lid of a tin, or his hand the inch and a half farther that would have put his fingers on it. The bright place was where a thumb had gone back when there was nothing else to do with it. He did not know what the tin held. He did not know the fold had ever been warm, or at what fire it had been pressed. From where he lay the shelf was beyond the reach of any hand he had, and the hand outside the wool went up an inch toward it and came down.

He set the tin on a tray of its own. It lay there with the bell told in the dark and the bell heard in the square at \place{the Cross}, with the breathing of \place{the Vail kiln-house} and the saying brought downriver in a stranger's mud, with the nearly-full answers and the two cords and the word that was a saying and not a vessel's sound. None of them touched another. He did not write a cause across the tin, because a tin above an empty bed is a tin above an empty bed, and an answer wanted too much had once cost him a life he still carried the shape of. It bought him nothing he could lift a hand to.

The cloth under him was cold and would not be made warm by wanting. A thread of drool froze at his mouth and nobody wiped it, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and the cold arrived in its known order, cheek, then temple, then wrist, the woodsmoke in the wool doing less than the wind wanted. There was nobody in that room older than he was and he could not reach the shelf. He could not read the writing on the slates propped at the bench. He could not roll over to see the tin from another angle, or ask whose hand had rubbed the lid bright, or say any of it in \lore{Grathic}. The clinical eye went on doing what it had been trained to do, and had no field and no instrument and no standing.

\person{Alda Reed} came in short, with her shawl drawn tight against the draught at the door, the smell of soap-and-milk gone cold under river silt on her and lye sour under the soap, \lore{the birth-slates} under her arm. She stopped at the bench before she stopped at the child. Her nails were scrubbed to the quick and still had clay under them, and the stoop in her shoulders was the stoop of a woman who had spent her life leaning over other people's births. She did not say please and she did not say sorry, which was not coldness in her but the correct number of words for a room where somebody had once been in pain. She laid the slates writing-down on the wood, and the soft scrape of stone went through the room, and the boy marked the order of it: the record first, then the child. She pressed the fold flat at his shoulder and kept her fingers there a breath longer than a fold required. The wool rode back from his wrist and showed the red edge of \lore{the birth-flower} where it runs down the forearm and stops short of the hand. Alda crossed two fingers at that red edge before she knew she had done it, and covered the sign before it could be seen with a date nobody had asked for.

“Stone seven; the account went up on the eighth, before the paper went out,” she said.

Sella, standing a half-pace off with her burn shut on nothing, said, “He lies quiet.”

“Warm and breathing --- that's all I've set down.”

It was exact and it was not true. The woman eighteen mornings gone had believed it to the last, and the death stood unentered where no living person could now correct it, and Alda did not say her name or the third day. She went on with a cooper's seventh in \place{Mud End}, a potter's third child in \place{Stonemouth}, the last high water, going on after there was no question left in the room to stop her, because dates are a handle on a thing too large to carry. The dates went on arriving like slates laid one after another on a bench, each flat and clean and each covering the thing underneath it. The boy kept the order of it on its own tray: the writing laid down before the body, the date laid over the sign, the exact words laid over a thing that could not be said. He had seen the same order in a room with better light, where a clean page had been made to cover what nobody wanted read.

Her hand went once inside her bodice and touched the little tin there and came away without opening it. Two tins in one room, both shut, one in pewter above an empty bed and one in cloth against a woman's body; two records in two materials, kept on two trays that did not touch. He could not ask either what the other held, or whether the two had ever been in the same hand.

He watched for the narrow slate and the turning of it down, and neither came. The hidden record stayed where it was, five pages in a second hand no Court physician had seen, face-down toward a floorboard in a house he could not name. No sixth page was added in this room or any other on this day. Sella did not ask what had been written, or what had not; a wall stood between the two women with no door in it, and her private roll stayed where it was, unshown. Nothing passed between them but the child and the weather and the exactness of dates.

Alda took up her slates and went out with the tin still shut inside her bodice, and did not look back at the bed or the shelf, and did not say the woman's name.

\scenebreak

At the turn of the wind Sella lifted the basket level and carried him back up the Clay Yard at the careful distance she kept from anything with writing on it. Under the Vail roof she set him on his bed and not in \place{the Debt Loft}, and put the fold right at his ear without looking at his face. Behind him the carried fire lay under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees and missing one breath in every four. Four, four and a gap. One crust lay in the fold where two had lain, stiff past smelling, and the empty place beside it was carried into a ninth day without a name.

The house kept its absent man the way it kept everything it would not enter: the salt-stiff coat holding a broader man's shape on its peg behind the door, the hollow wheel-seat keeping its twenty-year lean that nobody filled, the clean dark line where the Ash ridge had turned staying unswept. \person{Garl Vail} was three objects and a proverb and a debt nobody called, and the boy put him where he had always put him, apart from the tin three doors down and apart from the woman under the empty bed. A house can keep a man by his things and a room by what it has not moved; he set the two keepings side by side without making either the cause of the other.

Six hearings of \lore{the full note} stayed six, five from four sources and one out of a person who had been warm that morning, beside them and never among them, and no seventh came. The two nearly-full answers stayed advanced and not paid, and the word stayed a saying on its own tray, and the five unjoined pauses stayed unjoined. Three doors down the pewter tin kept its place above the empty bed, with the pale fold at its seam and the thumb's bright rub on the lid, and nobody opened it, and the room kept the shape of a room that has been left where it is. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the shelf and came down.
