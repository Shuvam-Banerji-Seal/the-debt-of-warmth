\daymark{27}{the Long Night, Y.H. 812}

The short day came in under a sky the colour of a banked coal, and nothing was carried up \place{the Clay Yard} in a clay dish. The fire that had come into the house on \lore{Kindling} lay where it had been put, under the back of a broken pot, and the coals kept their own counsel. \person{Corin} woke as he had gone to sleep, carried, with the fold at his chin and the two crusts at his side --- one stiff past the smell of anything, one still giving a thin breath of barley smoke when the wind turned. Twenty-seven days old and going where he was taken. He could not ask the name of the day; he had learned the names of days the way a man learns a room, by the draught under its door and the order in which the people in it moved. This one was \lore{the Long Night}, the third of \lore{the Five}, and the house had already begun to arrange itself around the absence of a flame.

Under \place{the Vail kiln-house} roof the work went on in the ordinary order, which is the order that needs no words. \person{Sella Vail} dried what had been wetted and mended what had been torn, and her hands counted when they had nothing to hold --- spoons, nails, the breaths of the child in the basket --- because a hand that keeps a fire keeps the account of small things. The kiln was not opened that day, and the hand at her throat stayed where it was. She had touched \lore{the widow's mark} before the opening on Kindling and would touch it again only before the next; a day that did not ask the kiln to open asked nothing of the mark. Her little-door burn shut on nothing when her hands were empty and shut on cloth when they were full, and she gave the instruction to the wool and not to the basket: “Keep it off his ears. It's the river wind.”

\person{Tarn} swept the yard before the light cleared the sill and swept it again when the light had, the same work twice and entered nowhere. The small knife went back to its sill and he told the yard where it was, unasked, the way another man states the weather. Six years of the seven were behind him and counted; the seventh he held back, the one entry in this house the house could not check. His broom checked half a stroke at the place on the bench where a pot had once given nothing, and went on. He did not turn his head. He had turned it a degree once, on a day when a vessel went past the door, and had said nothing then either, and the boy under the roof had kept that silence as carefully as he kept his own.

In \place{the Vail wheel-shed} \person{Jorra Vail} worked in slip the colour of old bone, dried to that colour up both forearms, the bent little finger of his left hand going in ahead of him at the damper. He had moved it before Sella had finished telling him the fire was low and had not explained why a low fire needed the damper moved first. He spoke to a pot and not to a person. “You'll do.” The wheel did not care whose hands were on it, only if they were steady; he had said it so often the saying had worn smooth as the seat worn hollow on the left by twenty years of another man's leaning. Of the room off the shed he said nothing, and the door of it kept its finger's width of open, and the boy filed the not-saying away with the other absences that had shapes.

Cold set in early. The oiled paper lifted at the windows and the grey skin on \place{the Slip Brook} held under the wind without breaking, and women at \place{the drying racks} pulled their sleeves down over their knuckles and called it weather. It came at the child in its known order: cheek, then temple, then wrist. The surgeon in him noted how a room cools from the edges inward, boards and sill first, the middle holding longest, and how a small body goes at the extremities before the core will admit to it; the child in him wanted the breast and wanted the crust that was given, and wanted them with a louder voice than any measurement. The two registers quarrelled without a word, as they had quarrelled every cold day of his twenty-seven, and neither want was answered. He had known a room once in which a man pulled from cold water was wrapped too quickly and worsened before he settled, and had learned there to distrust the moment of rescue; here the rescue was a fold put right at his ear and a spoon wiped on a sleeve, and the worsening came on schedule, cheek then temple then wrist, with no one in the room thinking to call it anything but night. The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth of the fold and came down. It was a reach he could not make into a reach, and he had long ago stopped being surprised by the difference. The drool at his mouth went cold and stayed, and nobody wiped it, because a basket at the edge of a board is a pot set down and left.

\scenebreak

At dusk Sella damped the fire and did not relight it. She turned the coals with the back of the broken pot and said to the pot what another house might have said to a person: “Feed the fire before you feed yourself. It'll still be there in the morning.” It was a saying that had belonged to \person{Garl Vail} and had improved in the keeping, and it went to the pot now, and not to the basket. The coat that had been his kept a broader man's shape on its peg in the dark, and the wheel-seat in the shed kept its hollow, and the ridge of \lore{the Ash} along the skirting board ran to the corner and turned it without a break, unswept and left for \lore{the Last Day}. The house sat down in the dark the way a family sits down at a table, in its own places, with the fire out and nothing between the voices but air.

\person{Alda Reed} of \place{House Reed} was among them, with her grey wool shawl and her hands scrubbed to the quick; she smelled of soap and warm milk and of the wish to be somewhere else. She told the story of a child who had come back thin out of \lore{the Return}, thin as a slipped knot, who had known warmth before he knew a face. “He knew the stone before he knew his mother's hands,” she said. “He grew worse on the third day. So did the one before him. Stone seven, the account went up on the eighth, before the paper went out.” She went on giving dates after the question had stopped: a cooper's seventh in \place{Mud End}, a potter's third child in \place{Stonemouth}, the exact day of a birth in a house nobody at the fire would ever enter. Where the story should have opened into wonder she put a date down flat, the way she laid a slate flat on a bench, and covered the thing underneath it. The hearth-maid's dates stood where wonder should have stood, and went on standing after there was no wonder left to ask about.

Tarn told his in the flat words he used for facts that had no feeling in them. “You can't return a man to himself,” he said to the floor. “They tried with my mother. She came back with somebody else's hands.” He did not say what had happened to the hands before they were hers; he did not need to, because everyone at the fire had heard the same shape of story and knew which part of it nobody finished. The boy listened to the space after the sentence the way he listened to the kiln between its breaths. A man had come back with another man's hands, and the hands had gone on being counted as his mother's because a slate had said so, and the space where a true account should have stood was left lying in the middle of the room for anybody to step around.

The boy lay with the voices going over him and counted the dark instead of the flames. There was no fire to count; the flame that had come in on Kindling was under ash and giving nothing to the room. So he counted what the telling left: the space between a question and its answer, the place where a name should have stood and did not, the point where a story stopped short and went on with dates instead. Three gaps.

A woman near the hearth had asked of the thin child, “And what did he call his mother?” and the answer had come two breaths later, and those two breaths lay in the dark between the question and the name that did not come with it. Someone else had begun a sentence with a man's name in it and had left the name out and gone on to the weather, and that left a place the size of a word where the name should have stood. A third voice had been telling of a stone warmed for a cot and had stopped short at the part where the stone did not hold, and the story had gone on with a date instead of the thing that happened. Three gaps, all of them made by people who were kind and had not noticed they had made them.

He set all three on the tray with the bench's interval, the half-heard note past the door and the pot that had cracked in the yard, and kept them from the five hearings of \lore{the full note} from four sources. An interval was not a hearing; a gap in a story was the same kind of object as a gap in a vessel's breath, and both were things left lying where someone had stopped. The count of hearings stayed five. It had stayed five through a market and a reading and a carrying of fire, and no sixth had been added by anybody in this room, and there was no hand in it that could add one.

In one telling of the year the bell came back. A woman whose man had been taken in a levy said it had gone four, four and a gap over \place{the Cross}, longer than any bell hung there, and that nobody had crossed himself when it did. The boy set that bell beside the bell he had heard under \place{the moot-post}'s thin shade and kept the two in two places. They had the same shape: four, four and a space where the closing stroke should have stood. A likeness laid on a likeness will grow a cause whether or not anyone put one there, and he had made that mistake once in a better-lit room and carried the correction for the rest of a life. The told bell stayed a told bell. The heard bell stayed a heard bell. The gap in each of them stayed a gap, and the two gaps did not touch.

He had once sat at a kitchen table in the life before this one while an older man told a story about a man who had died badly, and had noticed even then, with a fork in his hand and no reason to notice, that the story kept a space where the dead man's name should have been. He had not known what the space was. He knew now what shape it was, and knew that shaping it and naming it were two different acts, and that only one of them was his to do. The old life came in that far and no farther: a table, a fork, a voice gone out years ago, and then the wet wool at his ear and the dark under the Vail roof and the two crusts where they had been put.

Tarn said in the dark that six were counted and the next he did not count, and set the knife where it could be checked. “It's on the sill,” he said. “You can check.” The seventh year was the one entry in this house that the house could not check, and he held it back as he had held it back every night of the term, and the dark took the sentence without answering it. Jorra said nothing about the room off the shed. He shifted once where he sat, and the damper was where it should be, and he did not say it was cold. Garl's proverb went to the pot again, not to the basket: leave the crack showing; people trust a thing they can see has been handled. The coat held the shape of a broader man in the dark, and no one took it down, and no one said the name that would have gone with it.

The Long Night ended without fire. It ended the way it had begun, with work in the front of it and voices at the back, and with the boy twenty-seven days old and carried everywhere. The house sat in the dark with the fire out and the boy counting the space between one voice and the next, cold come at his cheek and wrist, the two crusts lying where they had been put, neither eaten nor lifted nor named. \lore{the Remembering} was tomorrow, and the dark kept its own account and gave no name away.
