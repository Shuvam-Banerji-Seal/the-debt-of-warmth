\daymark{25}{Kindling, Y.H. 812}

\dropcap{B}{efore the light} had cleared the eaves the clay dish came up \place{the Clay Yard} with a coal under ash, and nobody said where it had been lit or what it had cost to carry it. It went from hearth to hearth the way a loaf goes round a table when there are more mouths than crusts and everybody must be seen to have had a share. At the first door a woman touched her own fire to it and gave it on. At the next a man held a coal in his cupped fingers, put it under the ash, and did not look at the hand that took the dish away. “Keep it buried,” a woman said, and the words went down the yard ahead of the dish, practical and unkind and ordinary, the sort of thing said to a pot and not to a person. The yard was giving its fire in thimblefuls, each house a little of what it kept, so that what came up the yard was not any one hearth's flame but all of them gathered into the space a thumb could cover. It was \lore{Kindling}, the first of \lore{the Five}, and the year's fire was being carried through \place{Low Kiln} in the dark. Cold ash clung to the rim of the dish, the same grey as \lore{the Ash} ridge under the skirting board, and he had learned by now not to join two things because they had a colour in common. Woodsmoke lay low under the eaves. The thatch had a skin of frost on it, and the wind off \place{the Slip Brook} came under the doors with the smell of wet wool and tallow.

\person{Sella Vail} carried \person{Corin} to the threshold in the basket with the fold at his chin, twenty-five days old and going where he was taken. She set him on the sill, where the door stood a finger's width open and the smoke went out through the gap instead of up, and went to the kiln. From where he lay he could see the boards of the floor and the lower half of the yard and nothing at all of the hands that brought the dish the last few paces. He heard the ash in it shift, a dry small sound like a cough stifled, and felt the stir of the woman who handed it over and the woman who took it, two bodies turning in the dark with a thing between them that neither would name. He could not see into the dish. He could only know that it held something the yard had gone to the trouble of gathering, and that the trouble was ordinary enough that nobody had made a word of it. The fire had come into the house from along the yard, from a door he had never been carried through, from hands that had not once thought to tell him what they were carrying. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound it made when it went too far, and nobody wiped his mouth. Cold came in at cheek, then temple, then wrist, in the order he knew and could not argue with, and the child in him wanted the breast and the crust and the surgeon in him noted the order and made nothing of it.

Before she opened the kiln her hand went to \lore{the widow's mark} at her throat, the thumb's length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it. She held it the space of one breath and let it go. The little-door burn across the back of her hand opened on the iron as she reached for the lid and shut on nothing when the lid was up. She tipped the dish and the coal went in under the fire and the fire took it without a sound, the way a mouth takes a thing it has been waiting for. The dish went back out of the door with darker ash in it than had come up, and nobody looked at the difference. The rule of that hand was exact as a stitch: before the opening and at no other hour, not at a bank, not at a reading, not at a dusk. He had watched it at every opening he had been carried to, and the order never changed --- hand to throat, hand to lid, hand to fire --- which meant the thing it guarded had not changed either and would not while the house stood. A thing kept that exactly had a reason, and the reason was a thing the house could not say in front of a man from \place{Slate Row}. He had no word to ask it with and no standing to ask it even if he had.

The light came up off the fire and went along the wall and into the basket, and he counted it because counting was the one thing the body he was in had left him. Four, and four, and a place where a fifth would be if there were a fifth. Not a gap this time --- a place, an empty shelf where a thing could have stood --- and he set it on the tray beside the interval of the pot turned toward on the bench that had given nothing, and kept the two trays from touching. The bench's interval lay on one tray, the half-heard note past the door on another, the cracked pot in the yard on a third; the empty shelf went beside the first because it was nearest in shape, not because it was the same thing. He had learned that nearness was not sameness in a better-lit room and the lesson had never once been convenient. It was not a hearing. The ear that held five hearings of \lore{the full note} from four sources added no sixth, and there was no hand in the basket to make one with even if a sixth had been there to be made. A place where a fifth would be was an absence with the shape of a presence, and absences were the only things in this house that could be kept without anybody entering them. There was no word in \lore{Grathic} for the empty shelf, and if there had been he could not have said it.

He had known heat moved from vessel to vessel once, in a room with white light and a hand of his own that did not shake, and he had known who paid for the warmth and what it was for. That room was gone and the hand with it. Here the hand that carried the coal belonged to a neighbour whose name he would not be told, the vessel was a dish of clay, and nobody under the roof had a word for what had been carried or a thought to spend on where it had been before it came through the door. He wanted to ask who kept the yard warm enough to give fire away at the end of a Long Cold, and he wanted it with the whole of a man's curiosity and had the throat of a thing that could only gurgle to ask it with. He was the only person in the house who knew that warmth had been moved, and the only one with less standing than the dish to ask who had paid to move it. The household took the gift as it took the weather or the bread: it was there, it was ordinary, it would be there in the morning. Nobody entered its origin and nobody entered its payer. The joke of it had been old in the other life and was new again in this one, and it was funny in the way a wound is funny when you have stopped expecting it to heal.

Somewhere out in the yard a door shut, and then another, and a woman came back into the room with her fist closed around nothing and did not open it. The first measure of the year had been taken while he lay on the threshold with the fold at his chin, and no figure for it was spoken under the roof. She set the shut fist down on the bench as though it were a cup and went to the tubs, and the room went on with its work as though nothing at all had been carried past the door. He learned it the way he learned most of what the house knew --- from the order of a door closing and a fist shut, and from the way a mouth thought better of its sentence when it came near the basket. He could not spend what he had not been told, could not correct it, could not hand it back. The fist stayed shut through the pot and the fire, and the measure went where it had been entered. A number that never reached him was still a number, and the district had already entered it somewhere he would never be taken.

\scenebreak

The work went on around the basket as though a fire had not come into the house at all. \person{Jorra Vail} came in from \place{the Vail wheel-shed} with slip dried to the colour of old bone up both forearms and the bent little finger of his left hand leading the rest of him, and told a pot on the bench that the shoulder was thick and he would thin it. “You'll do,” he said to another pot, in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either, and told the wheel it did not care whose hands were on it, only if they were steady, and did not say the word anyone could use against him for the back room, which kept its cold the way it had kept it all through Stone. He went to the damper before Sella had finished telling the fire it was low, going in with the bent finger ahead of his hand and leaving the end of her sentence where it was. The wheel-shed smelled of damp clay and tallow and the sweet edge of glaze that came up on a bad wind from \place{High Kiln}. The full note was in a Vail pot before it left the wheel and he would not have said so in front of a kiln-master, who would have called it early and made something of it; the boy knew this about him the way he knew the bent finger, and kept both where questions can last for years. He could not reach the wheel and had no way to ask why Jorra never looked toward the bed.

\person{Tarn} came down from \place{the Debt Loft} with Ash in the creases of his knuckles that no scrubbing lifted and the pale clip-ring plain at his left wrist, and set the small bench knife on its sill with the care of a man putting down a thing he can be asked about. “It's on the sill,” he said to the yard, which had nobody in it but the basket. “You can check.” He looked at the kindling stack where four bundles stood and four and a space where a fifth should have been, and took his eyes off it as a man takes his eyes off a woman's face when she has been crying. “Six counted. The next I don't count.” Six years of the seven were behind him and counted every one; the seventh he held back, the one entry in this house the house could not check. “Yes, house,” he said to the ground, and swept where he had already swept, because an argument has nowhere to put its foot when a man agrees with everything. Behind the door \person{Garl Vail}'s coat kept its salt shape on its peg and his seat in the shed kept its hollow on the left, and the ridge along the skirting board ran to the corner and turned it without a break, unswept and not to be swept until \lore{the Last Day}. The charred edge of a slate lay in the kiln ash black on one side and eaten into the shape of a strip of bark, swept around for a year and never picked up. Garl's proverb went to the fire and the pot and never to the basket: leave the crack showing; people trust a thing they can see has been handled.

At the bank Sella fed him barley gruel thinned with milk from the house's own dish, holding his head with two fingers behind the ear and wiping the spoon on her sleeve between the dish and his mouth. The gruel was thin and warm and went down the way warmth goes down in a body that was learning the cold; he took it because it was given and not because he could ask for it. “The house keeps it,” she said, which was as near as she ever came to saying she had kept it herself; and to the fire, “Feed it before you feed yourself,” in Garl's voice improved by twenty years of use, and then to the wool, not to the basket: keep it off his ears, it's the river wind. The wind came off \place{the Ilm}, which still held under its ice, and there was no occasion to look at the river and no way to reach it. In the fold at his side the two crusts lay where they had been put and neither was eaten, lifted or named --- one stiff since Stone 15, brown-edged and gone past smelling of anything, the other still giving a faint breath of barley smoke when the wind turned. She put the fold right at his ear without looking at his face and did not wipe the drool at his mouth. A gift that could not be answered was a gift that could not be refused, and this one had been given twice, once by a woman who would not say she knew him and once by a house that would not say it had taken him in for anything but the keeping.

At dusk she banked the coals with the back of a broken pot and did not touch the mark, because a bank was not an opening and the rule held as exactly at the end of the day as it had before the lid that morning. The little-door burn shut on nothing all the way from the fire to the bed. Nobody had spoken to the basket all day; the door had been shut a finger's width against the draught and the day had gone over the yard with the dish and the fist and the knife on its sill, and not one person under the roof had said a word to the thing lying in the fold. The coat kept its salt shape, the wheel-seat its hollow, the ridge its unbroken corner, and the debt against a firing of tiles lay where it lay, neither paid nor settled nor done with. The year's first measure sat in a fist that had gone back to its work and the fire sat under ash that would be there in the morning, and neither had been spoken of to him. The kiln took its breath under the roof. Four, and four, and a gap.

He lay on his bed with the two crusts in the fold at his side and the new fire somewhere in the ash under the pot, and his hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth and came down on the fold. The surgeon who had once reduced a shoulder in the dark could not lift a crust to his own mouth. The coals hissed once and held. The fire kept four, and four, and a gap.
