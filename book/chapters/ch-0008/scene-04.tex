\daymark{28}{the Remembering, Y.H. 812}

The names went along \place{the Clay Yard} before the light had cleared the sill, carried from door to door the way a coal is carried from one hearth to the next, each house taking the sound into its own mouth and giving it on a little changed. \person{Corin} heard them pass over him and did not know which of the sounds were names. They came in the same low register as the rest of the yard --- the rasp of a broom, the slap of a loaf set down, the small flat knock of a pot gone wrong --- and they left the same spaces behind them. A sound. A space. A sound. A space. He counted the spaces because there was nothing else to count that morning, as he had counted the nails in the boards and the breaths of the kiln and the steps of the woman who carried him. He thought at first that it was the yard breathing. Only later, when the last name had gone up the hill and the quiet lay back over the roofs like a fold of wool put down, did he understand what the district had been doing on \lore{the Remembering}, the fourth of \lore{the Five}: it had been saying the dead, one house to the next, and the spaces were where it stopped for breath.

\person{Sella Vail} had carried him to the threshold and no farther. She held him there with the fold at his chin, her little-door burn shut on nothing, her hip turned so that the morning came at him sideways and the wool took half of what the yard said. She did not set him where a slate lay open, and she did not tell him why she had brought him this far and no farther. The hand at her throat did not go to \lore{the widow's mark}. There had been no opening of the kiln, and a remembering was not an opening; the rule held as exactly on a named day as it held on a market's Account-day. She gave the instruction to the wool. “Keep it off his ears. It's the river wind.” She did not say whose wind, or whose river, or whose ears; and when a name went by that she knew, and the house next door took it up and gave it on, she neither repeated it nor stopped it nor looked toward the basket to see whether he had heard it. She said “the house” where another woman would have said “I”, and of the names she said nothing at all.

Under \place{the Vail kiln-house} roof the day went on by not saying one thing. The coat that had been \person{Garl Vail}'s kept the broader man's shape on its peg behind the door, salt still stiff in the creases of the shoulders, and nobody took it down to carry it past him. In \place{the Vail wheel-shed} the seat kept its hollow on the left, worn by twenty years of a man's leaning, and it stayed hollow. Along the skirting board \lore{the Ash} ran grey to the corner and turned it without a break, as it had turned it every morning of the boy's life and every morning for a year before, unswept and left for \lore{the Last Day}. And the proverb went to the fire and to the pot and never to the basket: feed the fire before you feed yourself; it'll still be there in the morning. The house remembered by keeping the objects and withholding the word. A man could be four things at once and not be named by any of them.

She went around the name the way she went around Ash left in a corner. It was a careful circle, and there was no word for the thing circled. When she spoke to the fire she said feed it and that'll hold, and the sentence stopped where the name would have stood and went on with the pot. When \person{Jorra Vail} came in from the shed she told him the fire was low and did not add the rest, and he moved the damper and did not ask what the rest had been. When she spoke to the wool she spoke of the river wind. Three times in one morning the house came up to the edge of a word and went around it, and the boy lying at the edge of the boards watched her do it with the attention he had once given a surgeon crossing a field he did not want to cross: he knew the shape of the avoidance without knowing the thing avoided. Her hands were never still. They counted spoons, nails, the breaths of the child in the basket. They did not count the name. The lead glaze at her throat kept its own green under her collar, touched at no hour that morning, and the little-door burn closed on cloth and on nothing and gave neither away.

The boy counted the silence where the name should have stood. He did not decide to. It came the way the counting came in that house, unbidden and exact: the space between a woman's sentence and the word she did not put into it had the same shape as the space between a kiln's fourth breath and its fifth. He had been keeping such spaces on a tray of their own since the birth room. There was the pot turned toward on the bench that had given nothing when it was set down; there was the half-heard note that had gone past the door on a day when a vessel was carried; there was the pot that had cracked two paces from his head and had been called a pot gone wrong; there was the gap in the told bell and the gap in the heard bell, kept in two places; and now there was this --- a place the size of a word where the house's own dead man should have stood. He set it with the others and not with the hearings. It was a gap, not a hearing. Of \lore{the full note} there had been five hearings in his ear from four sources, and the fifth of those had come out of a dying man under a cold iron tube, and nobody in this room had added a sixth.

Five. From four sources. No sixth.

\scenebreak

\person{Alda Reed} of \place{House Reed} came under the roof in the middle of the morning with the birth-slates under her arm and soap and warm milk on her, the smell of a room where somebody else's child had just been fed. She laid the slates writing-down on the bench before she turned toward the fold, as she had laid them on every other morning she had come, and only then put her hand to the child. From \place{the birth-flower shrine} down the yard a woman had spoken one of the morning's names aloud and asked whose it was; the question came through the door and stopped at the bench. Alda did not look up from the slate she was straightening.

“Stone seven,” she said. “The account went up on the eighth, before the paper went out.”

It was a date where a name should have stood, and it was the same date she had given at the end of nineteen steps to a woman who had asked for her child. Her hand went once inside her bodice to the little tin on its cord and came away without opening it. The cord showed for a moment at the edge of her shawl, a grey line with a small weight at the end of it, and was gone. She pressed the fold flat at his shoulder and kept her hand there longer than a fold required, long enough for the child's warmth to come up into her palm and for the surgeon in him to note the exact pressure of a woman who wanted something and would not ask. Then the wool rode back at his wrist and the red edge of \lore{the birth-flower} showed, a branch of it running from the third rib down the forearm and stopping short of the hand; and Alda crossed two fingers at the corner of it before she knew she had done it. She covered the sign with the date she had already given, as though a date laid over a thing were the same as a thing not seen. She did not know she had made the sign. She went on giving dates after there was no question left to answer: a cooper's seventh in \place{Mud End}, a potter's third child in \place{Stonemouth}, the exact day of a birth in a house nobody at the bench would enter.

He had once stood in a better-lit room and watched a hand go past a note turned face-down on a chart, and had learned there what a covering looks like when the person doing it believes it is care. He knew the shape of Alda's two fingers and the shape of the date laid over them, and knew they were the same gesture made by two hands that had never met. He could hand neither back. He could not have put a name to the gesture in Grathic even if a mouth had been given him, and there was no word in this house for the thing a woman did when she covered what she would not say. He kept the sign on one place of the tray and the date on another, and did not let them touch. A likeness laid on a likeness will grow a cause whether or not anybody put one there.

In the old register a hand went into a coat pocket and found wool where a small lit rectangle with a name had lain. The hand had been there before, on a night of orange light on wet snow, and the name under the light had not been looked at then either. It had not been looked at now. There had been a promise about a boat on cold water, made across nine summers to a woman who had organised her life around not waiting, and the boat had never been bought; the promise had gone on being carried without the boat, which is a way of carrying a thing that has no handle. The shape of it came and went in the register where such shapes were kept, untagged, with no name looked at and nothing crossing back that a child's hand could hold. The hand that reached was a man's hand and the pocket was a coat's pocket and the wool was the wool at his own ear, and the three did not join. He came back to the fold the way a man comes back from a room he has no business in, with nothing in his fingers and the same small exactness of loss.

\scenebreak

In the kiln ash the charred slate lay where it had lain all year, black on one side and eaten into the shape of a strip of bark, the writing gone off the end. The broom went around it each morning and the ash went around it and nobody picked it up. It was the oldest thing in the room that nobody would name, older than the boy, older than the foundling's arrival, and he had seen its edge every time he was carried past the kiln mouth and had never been near enough to touch it. Two paces was two paces, and there was no hand in the house that would take him the first of them for the sake of a burnt edge. He filed it beside the ridge and the barrows of grey and the thumb laid flat into a neighbour's corner, and made nothing of the likeness.

In the fold the two crusts lay where they had been put. One was stiff past the smell of anything, gone brown-edged and cold since Stone 15; the other still gave a thin breath of barley smoke when the wind turned off the brook. Neither had been lifted. Neither had been named. No third crust had come into the fold on the Remembering, and no woman had asked after one. A gift that cannot be answered cannot be refused, which he had learned first in bread and had since found true of everything else; the lesson sat at his side with the crusts and smelled faintly of the mill.

\person{Tarn} had set the small knife back on its sill and told the yard where it was, unasked, in the tone of a man stating the weather. Six years of the seven were counted; the seventh he held back, as he held it back every day of the term, the one entry in this house the house could not check. He scraped the Ash from his knuckles with the flat of the knife and put the knife down again where it could be checked. His head did not turn today. There had been no vessel going past the door with a note in it, and the silence he kept was the one the whole house kept, and the two silences lay under the same roof without touching.

Jorra came in from the shed with fine grey slate dust worked into the creases of his forearms past the bend of the little finger, and said nothing about where he had been. It was not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel; it was the other grey, the one that came and went without an account, and the boy set this morning's forearms beside the clean ones of other mornings and kept the two states on separate places. Jorra went to the damper before Sella had finished telling him the fire was low, the bent finger going in ahead of his hand, and said to the pot what he did not say to the room: “You'll do.” He did not look toward the bed. Of the name that had not been said in the house all morning he gave no sign at all, and of the seat he meant to fill and the boy he meant to give away he gave none either. The door of the room off the shed kept its finger's width of open.

Under the Vail roof the boy lay twenty-eight days old and carried, with the silence where a name should be lying beside him like a tool put down and not picked up. The salt coat kept its shape on the peg; the grey ridge ran to the corner and turned it without a break; in the kiln ash the charred slate kept its place; in the fold the two crusts lay side by side, one spent and one still smelling of barley smoke when the wind turned. Sella banked the coals with the back of a broken pot and did not touch the mark, because a bank was not an opening and a remembering was not an opening and neither had asked anything of her hand. The fire took its breath in fours and missed one. The Last Day was tomorrow, and the bread would be baked, and the grey would be left where it lay, and the name would still not be said.
