At midday she carried \person[Corin]{the boy} in off the yard and under the roof of \place{the Vail kiln-house} in \place{Low Kiln}, through the door that kept its finger's width of open and did not shut behind her, and the light changed from the grey of the ruts to the brown of a room kept out of the wind. \person{Sella Vail} gave the instruction to the wool before she gave it to anything else --- keep it off his ears, it was the river wind --- and to the pot, and to the broom standing where the broom stood, and to the fire under its blind ash, and never once to the basket. The basket was a pot set down, and a pot set down was left where it was put. Woodsmoke went ahead of her into the room and wet wool went with her, and under both the faint sour mineral tang of the \lore{Ash} that had lived in the corners of this house for two years and would not be swept.

She set him low and a little back, near the bench leg, at the angle at which the underside of a board becomes a roof. It was an angle she had chosen every day of his life without knowing she had chosen it; from the basket he saw what a man standing saw only the top of. \place{the loaf shelf} ran above him with its row kept the way a house keeps its days badly --- loaves pushed along, the hard heels of them going toward the wall, and spaces where a loaf's width should have stood. There were two of those spaces. The old one sat at the row's end and had been there longer than the boy had been under the roof; the second was a palm's breadth that had come in on the night of Seed 6 with no scrape, no crumb, no print and no word, and had been going around the house's mouth ever since as a thing nobody named. The front edge of the board was at his eye level and its underside opened above him like a low roof seen from beneath, the grain running the length of the room with the dark of the day held in it.

The cold iron of the bench bracket was at his cheek. He had been set so near it that the cold came through the wool and did not give, and he lay with the small bare joint of his cheek against it and the grey light going over the top of the shelf where his eyes could not follow. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and nobody under this roof had been given an instruction about the sound. The thread at his mouth had frozen on the walk in and would stay frozen until somebody was told to wipe it, which nobody had been.

Above him she stood with her back to the basket and moved her lips along the row. It was not a counting that any ear could take. The lips went along the loaves and stopped at the spaces and went on, the way a woman's hand goes over a bare board and closes on the loaf beyond it. She glanced once at the clay jar standing cold under its glaze on the wall shelf, and gave no figure. The jar had stood in the house since he was a month old and he had watched her glance at it for a hundred and thirty-five days and never once watched her touch it. Whatever the jar held was held there without a number, and the glance was the whole of the house's account of it. Then she pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carried the little-door burn, the grey streak at her temple showing pale in the light off the coals --- the streak she said had come in the winter \person{Garl Vail} died and would not discuss beyond that --- and told the fire something low that was an instruction and not an account. She told the fire it was \lore{the thaw}.

The dry warm smell of the shelf --- bread and dust and the clay that had been carried past it a thousand times --- lay over the room. For one breath it was crossed by another smell, sharper, mineral, a little bitter: charcoal, the charred stick drawn across the underside of the board and left there. It came from under the shelf where the marks lay, where nobody thought to look for a keeping, and it went as quickly as it came, leaving the bread smell where it had been.

She did not look down at the board. That was the first thing he noted, because he was lying at the angle where the board's underside was all there was to look at, and her face was turned the other way toward the row and the jar and the wall. Without a word she reached a hand behind her and down, and the little-door burn on the back of it closed on the wood's front lip and opened again as the fingers went under. He saw the knuckle set against the grain and the thumb come up, the way a hand goes under a shelf to feel for what it knows is there, not to find out what is there. The finger went under far enough for the knuckle to set. It stayed a breath out and came back. The burn closed on nothing as the hand withdrew, and she did not go back to the shelf.

From the low angle he saw the hand go under and come back and saw nothing of what the marks said. He had seen them before, from this same angle on the first Account-day of Reed: short strokes in groups a finger's breadth apart, some pressed hard enough to leave a smooth track where the stick had dug, some the colour of old smoke and gone almost into the grain, faintest toward the row's end as if the hand that made them had gone away from the task or grown tired of pressing. They did not make \lore{Grathic} words. No slate had been set under them to catch what they meant. He did not count the strokes now. He did not press a thumb where her thumb had been, or lay the underside of the board beside the two gaps above it, or lay either of them beside the one stiff brown-edged crust lying two inches from his ear in the room the shelf did not touch. He did not make a cause of the habit. A hand that goes under a board without looking either knows the board by heart or has been told not to look, and the reason stood between the two and was not entered. The care of the hiding told more than the writing would, and the writing was not his to read, and so he kept the care and the marks on a place of their own and left both where they lay.

A saying of the loaf-shelves held that two gaps stay two gaps until a finger lays them together and an account then wants a name. The saying stood in the room with the two gaps and went unfulfilled, as it had gone unfulfilled every afternoon since the palm's breadth came in on the night of Seed 6. Neighbours had laid the old space and the empty place at a child's ear under one cause in a single breath and gone off with their pots without seeing they had joined two rooms; he had heard the sayings and kept each on its own place, and had not made any of them his own. Now there was a third place under the shelf, and a finger had been under it and come away without a word, and still nothing touched. The keeping was exact and invisible and was the only work in this house that nobody had asked him to do.

He knew the weight of a paper turned face-down on a table because what was written beneath it was meant for one reader and not the room, and he knew that the turning of it mattered more than the words. That was a knowing without a hand to spend it on. Here the hand went under the board without the paper, without the table, without so much as a look to see whether anyone was watching the shelf. A second account kept beneath the first, in a material no Court thumb could spell, on a board a child lay under and a woman stood over with her back turned. He filed the hand beneath the board on the tray where the other record-first hands stood --- the midwife reaching for the slate before the child, the thane wiping his thumb before he touched nothing, the clerk setting his figure down before he looked at the face, the woman at the bare board taking her loaf --- and did not let it become a cause. Five likenesses in a row will stand up and call themselves a doctrine if nobody holds them apart, and a doctrine is an account that has found a payer. Here there was no payer, no figure, no reading and no finger in the district laid the marks below the shelf across the gaps above it, so he held them apart, which was what a man does with instruments on a towel before he loses the use of his hands.

A neighbour come up from the strips with dry mud worked into the creases of her wrists and water standing in the creases let herself in behind Sella and stood with a wet pot against her hip. She did not look into the basket; baskets were not looked into. “What'll the row want before the tend's out?” she asked, and asked it of the room, or of the board, or of Sella's back, and not of any person standing there. It was a question about a figure wearing the coat of a question about the weather, and the house heard it as both.

Sella did not turn from the shelf. “Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there,” she said. “The house has said it.”

The woman shifted the pot from one hip to the other, and the water in the creases of her wrists went dark again where her sleeve rubbed them. “The pot doesn't answer.”

“No,” Sella said. “But it's more use than me.”

That was the whole of it. The woman carried the question away level, the pot held steady against her so nothing slopped, and went back down toward the strips with the dry mud in her wrists and the unanswered question in her mouth, and neither of them entered anything. A cost asked in the form of a direction had been answered with a direction, and the answer had gone home with her and left no figure standing in the room. The boy watched her go and set her question on the place where the yard's other unanswered questions stood --- the reeds judged by the carrying arm, the hands that went in by the look, the weather, the river --- and did not make a sum of them. Account-day stood four days off at Reed 20 and nobody in this room had said the date.

The \lore{widow's mark} hung at Sella's throat where it hung, a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the kiln scratched into it, and she did not touch it. A shelf and an afternoon were not openings, and no opening had come under this roof in a hundred and thirty-five days. The clean dark line where the Ash ridge had turned lay along the skirting board with the new finer hairline of grey beside it, and neither had been swept, and the house called both the thaw and said the grey would go when it went. She told the fire it was the thaw, turned the coals with the back of a broken pot, fed them, and stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand counting under her breath toward no opening, while the grey streak at her temple showed pale and the little-door burn shut on the pot's edge and then on nothing.

Above him the shelf kept its two gaps --- the old space at the row's end and the palm's breadth that had come in on the night of Seed 6 --- and beneath it the charcoal marks lay pressed into the grain where the finger had been and come away without a word. The room that held the shelf and the room that held the crust and its empty place were two rooms, and neither touched the other. He lay at the low angle with the cold iron at his cheek and his hand outside the wool going up an inch toward the note of water through the finger's width of door and coming down against the fold. One hundred and thirty-five days old, four months and fifteen days, he kept the count exact and unspent and spoken by nobody, and the marks under the board waited for a hand that was not his and a day that had not come.
