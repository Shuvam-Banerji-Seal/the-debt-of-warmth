\daymark{137}{Reed 18, Eighthday, Y.H. 813}

The reed month had gone into its last days, and the work went on as it had gone on since the frost broke, in armfuls judged by the arm that carried them and by nothing set on a slate. \person{Sella Vail} carried \person[Corin]{the boy} down the river road to \place{the Weir} at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, giving the instruction to the wool and the river wind and never to the basket, and set him at the edge of the work where no slate lay and the salt stood white where the flood had touched the posts. The reeds stood in long grey ranks in \place{the reed beds}, broken ends green under the frost and tips pale as old bone, and the bundles came down on shoulders and in the crooks of arms, a going-downriver at first light and a coming-back at dusk. A bundle standing in the mud was worth what the arm under it said it was worth, which was a weight and not a figure. The boy watched the shoulders go down and come back and noted the order of them without deciding to: the hand went to the load before the eyes went to the road, and the road got no more of a look than the load got a name.

He knew a record kept in muscle when he saw one. An arm that has carried the same load all morning can tell you how often it has lifted and not what the lifting cost; it is exact, and complete, and worth nothing to a man with a slate. The surgeon's register noted the order of reaching and gave him no standing to enter it. One hundred and thirty-seven days old, four months and seventeen days, and nobody under any roof had said so; his hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down against the fold. Nothing within two paces could be reached, not the bundle on the stone, not the cord going into the coat, not the pot or the crust or the door a league downriver standing its finger's width open. He kept the count where no Court thumb would read it, exact and unspent, and went on watching.

A woman with salt dried white at her wrists asked what the cutters' hands would be paid now the reed was in its last days. The answer came back as the weather and the river, never a figure, and she carried the unanswered question away level, so nothing slopped. “Ask the river,” a cutter said, and that was the whole of it, a direction given where a number had been asked for. The boy set the question on the tray where the unanswered things lay and did not lay it across the clerk's uncalled measure a league downriver, because two accounts with no figure in them are still two accounts.

The reed-cutter came along \place{the plank walk} with a bundle on his shoulder and a short length of grey wool or reed-fibre cord looped inside his coat. The cord had gone grey with the oil of a working thumb, the way a stitch-hole goes grey after a thread has been drawn through it a dozen times; it was the colour of the stone beside it and might have been mistaken for a piece of that same stuff but for the knots. He set the bundle down on \place[the blessing stone]{the flat blessing stone} with its broken ends squared to the edge, straightened the cord on his thumb, and turned it once so the knots stood in the small light. He tied one low with his teeth and a pull of the thumb, bit the end off, and put the cord back inside his coat, drawing the coat closed over it with the flat of his hand.

A weir woman come up from the traps with her sleeves wet to the elbow asked what the cord held. “You can ask the cord,” he said.

“I can't read a cord.”

“No more can the Court.” He lifted the bundle off the stone and went on along the boards toward the traps, and the question went down into the water after him with no figure on it and no name.

The boy watched the coat close over the cord and kept what he had seen on a place of its own. He had read the shape of the knot as a man reads a mark on a chart whose legend he does not carry --- a tie, a turn, a thumb-pulled tail, a record in a material no Court thumb will spell --- and he had not counted the knots or given the cord a figure or a name, and he had not joined it to the empty place in the returning line where a hand went in on Seed 15 and did not come out. The grey of it was a colour on its own place, the colour of stitch-holes and working thumbs; the record was carried and not paid. He did not supply the want that would make it an account. Sella stood a half-pace off with the little-door burn shut on nothing and gave the instruction to the wool again, and did not look into the basket to see what he had been watching.

\scenebreak

At the lower boards \person{Orra Weir} stood with her board on its side, turning it with the wrist that had turned it ten thousand times. Her hands were like old rope, the pale eel scars lay uncovered across both forearms, and the grey streak showed first through the black hair when she moved her head. Salt stood white at her wrists and the smell of river silt, cold fish and tallow smoke went ahead of her; the nasal half-song of the Fen came into her voice where she was tired, as it had come at the blessing and every morning since, and she had never entirely lost it. A cord of grey wool hung at her waist with its knots set close along it, the thing people noticed last. She pulled one knot down with her thumb and set it among the others, and did not look into the basket.

A woman at the rails asked what the levy would take now the hands were out in the reeds.

“The ninth of Ilmen,” Orra said. “The collector's boat came round the bend on the flood and had six oars. One man stood at the stern and never touched them.” The half-song flattened the words and let them out slow, one after another the way a trap is lifted and set down.

“That isn't the levy.”

“It's a date. The river takes what the Court doesn't. You can shout at either one. I've tried.” Her thumb went to the cord again at the end of the sentence, and the boy could not tell whether that was a lie or only the habit of counting, and made no cause of it.

A date given where a figure was asked is an answer with the question taken out of it --- a chart closed over a note whose finding nobody had read, the neat margin left standing --- and he set it where it belonged, on the place where the told bell stood beside the heard bell and the man who keeps his hands off the oars stood apart from the midwife's reaching hand and the thane's twice-wiped thumb. He had no \lore{Grathic} word for the shape of it and did not supply one. The date went on its own place and did not touch the cord in the reed-cutter's coat, though both had passed along the same boards in the same hour.

The last knot on Orra's cord showed its double turn. The tail was tucked, it was tied left over right, and the small extra weight of a thumb had been pressed into it; the shape was legible in the small light and the word that would have named it stayed where it had stayed since the year of the flower. The boy read the shape without the name. He did not count it --- not the last knot, not the ones before it --- and he did not say the word that was not spoken anywhere on these boards, because a word spoken by a child who could not speak would have been a thing entered in an account he had no standing to open. The knot kept its double turn and its name was not given, and that was the whole of what he was allowed to carry away.

Behind her, on the next board down, a second board lay that did not touch hers. \person{Mira Silt} worked the eels over it with pale slime dried in long grey strokes on her sleeves and river silt dried pale in the hollow of her neck, her head held a little to the right where the top of her left ear had been, her small smile kept on her face the way a door is kept on the latch. The two boards lay side by side the whole morning and no edge of one crossed the edge of the other.

\scenebreak

A weir woman come down to the outer boards asked whether the run would be thin now the hands were in the reeds. Mira flicked a string of eel-bones against her thumb while she decided whether to lie, moved two from hand to hand, put one away, and answered the question and then her own. “Thin? Who's to say thin? It comes when it comes. Ask the river.” She said something low about a boat that had come up on the flood and nothing about the hand that had not come out of the strips. The eel-bones went back into her pocket and the smile did not move on its latch.

A buyer in a coat with the river still in its hem named a figure for two pair. Mira's thumb went under her apron to the grey wool cord and stayed there longer than the figure required, while she decided what the figure was worth to the woman who had said it. “Two marks the pair and two short. You've said it twice and I've counted it twice. There's nothing in the sentence that wants an answer.” The buyer took the eels and went. Shifting a creel showed the second cord twisted twice beneath the grey --- the one never carried upriver to \place{Nine Pots}, never lifted at a door --- and then the apron came down over it. Neither knot was counted by any thumb but her own, and the knot for the thin child upriver was not touched.

Her not-looking was a keeping set on a place of its own. She did not look into the basket and had not looked into it all morning, and that not-looking was the opposite of the pricing face a buyer turns across a market, and he had no word in Grathic for either one. The two stood on separate trays and did not touch.

Three private records stood on the boards in wool and teeth and thumb, and all three were visible and none could be entered. The reed-cutter's tally lay in the coat drawn close over it, gone grey where a working thumb had oiled it; Orra's knot-string hung at her waist with its last double turn legible and its name withheld; Mira's second twist lay twice beneath the grey apron, never taken to the door it belonged to. Each was kept by a hand that would not let a figure be written where another hand could read it, and each hand had passed over its record in the same hour within a dozen paces of the basket. The boy saw all three and entered none. He laid them side by side and not across, the way he laid instruments on a towel, knowing each by the wear on its handle and refusing to mistake three handles for one tool --- for three likenesses in a row will stand up and call themselves a doctrine if no hand holds them apart, and a doctrine is an account that has found a payer, and no payer had been named to any of these.

The reed-cutter went up the boards with the cord inside his coat and the knots unread. Orra's last knot kept its double turn, tail tucked, tied left over right with the small weight of the thumb pressed in, and kept its name unspoken. Mira's thumb stayed under her apron longer than the figure required and her board lay beside Orra's without touching it. River wind came up to meet the smell of cold fish and tallow, and the note of water went over water and under the boards where no slate lay.

One hundred and thirty-seven days old, four months and seventeen days, exact and unspent and told by nobody. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water and came down against the fold. Nothing within two paces could be reached, not the cord, not the knot-string, not the second twist beneath the apron; the three keepings went on being kept where no Court thumb would read them, and his count went on going.
