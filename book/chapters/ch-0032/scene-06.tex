The half-pace had brought them out of the Weir shade and up into the square by midday, and the note of water went on under the noise of the stalls the way it went on under everything, a fifth low thing that had never been counted among the four. \person{Sella Vail} set the basket in the moot-post's thin finger of shade at \place{the Cross} where no slate lay, and gave the instruction to the wool and to the river wind and never to the basket: keep it off his ears, it had come all the way down with them and would go all the way back up. \person[Corin]{the boy} lay with his cheek to the cold iron of the post and his small hand outside the wool going up an inch and coming down, not toward anything he could reach but toward the direction of the water, which was the only direction he had. One hundred and thirty-seven days old, four months and seventeen days, and nobody in the square who would have known what the words counted if somebody had said them, and nobody said them.

The square kept its ordinary midday face. The weighing board stood with the morning's mud dried pale in the hollows of it and a straw or two caught in a knot of the grain; the pigs had gone over it earlier and gone off through the north gate, and the going of them had left the place quieter than it had been at first grey. \place{Low Kiln} lay across the river behind them, a smudge of wet wool and woodsmoke that the wind pushed ahead of her when it turned, and \place{clay road} came down into the square from the north terrace with the dust of it on the backs of the barrows. On \place{the pot board} the bowls stood with dust in their hollows and the light going along the rims, and nobody turned one a quarter this day and set it back where it had stood.

He had kept four greens at mouths and five shapes of the colour counting Berr's glaze at Berr's own mouth, and no woman across the square put a fifth to hers this day. The four stayed four. He did not go looking for a fifth to make the count come out even, and he did not call any of them by a name that would have given them a face, or speak an ear with any of them, or lay one across the pale line on the back of a hand that was not here to be looked at. There was no word in \lore{Grathic} that would have made a colour into a person, and he had spent a hundred and thirty-seven days learning the difference between a likeness and a cause. Four objects on four places, five shapes if a man's own glaze was counted, and none of them stood up and said anything.

\person{Berr Clay} came up short out of the clay road with the red wool cord of his office folded under his coat instead of worn across it and the green of tasted glaze at one corner of his mouth, hot clay over the smell of nothing at all. The smell went before him and the man arrived after it, the way a kiln's heat comes out of the door before the hand that opens it. His hands were large and clean. He wiped his thumb twice on his apron before touching nothing, held a narrow slate unopened against his forearm, and passed a finger over the figure with his eyes resting on the writing a breath longer than the writing required, as though he were checking that a seam he had set yesterday had not shifted in the night. The boy set the order of it on the tray where the other record-first hands lay: the hand to the slate before the hand to the person, the finger to the figure before the voice to the buyer. He did not join it to the midwife's reaching hand or to the clerk's, and he had no word for the order except the one his own hands had once kept when he laid instruments out on a towel.

At the eel stall Berr took two pair and paid two marks short, and the stall-keeper put the marks in her palm and did not count them where anyone could hear. “A number goes into a slate, not a bundle,” he said. “If your house wants it again, there'll be a slate at the watch.” Your house, never \emph{I}. He took himself out of his own sentence as neatly as a surgeon takes his hand out of a field before the dressing goes on, and stood a moment with his mouth open on the next part of what he had come to say, and then said it to the stall instead, low, the levy called for the reed and not called by him, one in twelve as the notice says. Nobody leaned in at that hour, so the voice was spent on the boards and went nowhere. He went off toward \place{High Kiln} with the cord folded square under his coat, the narrow slate under his arm, and the box in his house still locked with the true subtraction inside it. He did not look into the basket.

A figure read aloud was not the figure paid. The two marks short went into the stall-keeper's palm without a count, and the two pair of eels went into Berr's basket with no eel short and no word about the six that should have gone there, and the small war between the eel woman and the thane went on being fought in the dark where no slate could enter it. The boy had watched six years of it from under a fold and had never once seen either hand acknowledge the other. He set the two marks on one place and the two pair on another and did not cross them, because a figure that arrives nowhere is still a figure and a pair of eels is still a pair of eels, and putting them in one sentence would have made an account where none had been opened. The green stayed at the man's own mouth on a place of its own. It was the colour of the glaze room up the terrace, a taste that had become a mark, and it belonged to the man who carried it and to nobody else in the square.

Sella carried him down off the square and below the sill to the outer edge of \place{the fish-trade shade} where the stalls thinned out and no Court slate had ever reached, and set the basket at the very end of it where no slate lay. She gave the instruction to the wool and to the running water and never to the basket, and stood a half-pace off. The river wind came up to meet the woodsmoke going off her coat, and under the boards \place{the Ilm} went over its own stones with the low note that he had kept as a fifth thing since he was six days old. Salt stood white where the flood had touched the posts and gone away, and pale eel slime had dried in long grey strokes on the boards and on the sleeves of the women working them.

Below the shade, at the tubs, a woman had wrapped a child's foot in three turns of wool. The foot stayed dark. It was the cold to the woman who had wrapped it and weather to the yard, and the two facts went past each other as they had gone past each other every morning of his life and never met. A neighbour come down with a pail said the water would go when it would go, that it was weather and not a thing a person could carry into a house, and the woman with the wool did not answer her, because there was no answer that would have put the two in the same sentence. The boy read the foot the way he had once read such a thing in a room with white light and no smoke in it: the extremity going before the core, the face putting on its almost-smile that was not peace, the afterdrop waiting on the far side of a warming nobody would perform. He knew the whole order of it. The knowing worked perfectly and bought nothing. There was no figure in any slate that would have named what had fastened there, no cause to write across the water and the wool, no cure he could have carried two paces even if a hand had been put into his to carry it, and no word in the language of the house that would have let him tell the woman what he knew. He did not lay the dark foot beside the story he had heard from other mouths of a child under this roof who had cooled while everybody was kind. A story from a yard and a foot below a sill were two things in two places, and he had spent a hundred and thirty-seven days learning the cost of drawing the line. The foot stayed dark inside its three turns of wool, and the yard went on calling the water weather.

\person{Mira Silt} worked the eels over the board next down with pale slime dried in long grey strokes on her sleeves and river silt dried pale in the hollow of her neck, her head held a little to the right where the top of her left ear had been, her small smile kept on her face the way a door is kept on the latch. Her board lay beside \person[Orra Weir]{Orra}'s knot-string and did not touch it. A weir woman come down to the outer boards asked whether the run would be thin now the reed hands were out at the beds. Mira flicked a string of eel-bones against her thumb while she decided whether to lie, moved two from hand to hand, put one away, and answered the question and then her own. “Thin? Who's to say thin? It comes when it comes. Ask the river.” She said something low about a boat that had come up on the flood and nothing about the hand that had not come out of the strips, and the smile did not move on its latch.

A buyer in a coat with the river still in its hem named a figure for two pair. Mira's thumb went under her apron to the grey wool cord and stayed there longer than the figure required, while she decided what the figure was worth to the woman who had said it. “Two marks the pair and two short. You've said it twice and I've counted it twice. There's nothing in the sentence that wants an answer.” The buyer took the eels and went. Shifting a creel showed the second cord twisted twice beneath the grey --- the one never carried upriver to \place{Nine Pots}, never lifted at a door --- and then the apron came down over it. Neither knot was counted by any thumb but her own, and the knot for the thin child upriver was not touched. The word that would have named the double turn stayed where it had stayed since the year of the flower, unspoken on these boards and unspoken everywhere else he had ever heard it. He read the shape without the name and did not supply the one thing that would have turned a shape into an account.

Her not-looking was a keeping set on a place of its own. She did not look into the basket and had not looked into it all morning, and that not-looking was the opposite of the pricing face a buyer turns across a market and the opposite of the thane's not-looking up at the Cross, and he had no word for any of the three. The three stood on separate trays and did not touch. Below the north bridge at \place{the out-of-line landing} the soft-cuffed clerk was still set for a measure nobody called, his cuff grey from washing and grey again by midday, his slate held to his forearm with the writing turned where nobody else could read it, his hand going to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls. The market sold its reeds and its eels over the top of him as water goes over a stone that lies where a stone lies; nobody stepped around him and nobody asked what he was set there for, and nobody had taken him off since the month turned, or if somebody had, somebody had put him back in the dark. The boy kept the clerk's order of reaching on its own place beside the midwife's, the thane's, the wheel-keeper's and the woman's at the shelf, and did not make a doctrine of them, because a doctrine is an account that has found a payer and no payer had been named to any of these hands.

\scenebreak

The arithmetic of the market and the arithmetic of a cold foot ran on separate trays and had never been laid across one another. A figure read aloud was not the figure paid; a body read exactly was still a body nobody would warm. The boy set the two facts where he set all the others, edge to edge and not touching, the way a man lays instruments on a towel and knows each by the wear on its handle without ever mistaking one handle for another. The four greens stayed four and the five shapes stayed five, and no fifth green had come to a mouth this day to make the count come out even; the word forty lay unspoken with the readable double turn beside it, its name withheld and its knot uncounted; the loan that could hear was still uncalled and the seventh year had not given.

None of it could be spent. He was the only adult under any roof in the district and the least use in any of them: a man who had once laid out instruments on a towel and known every one by the wear of its handle, now a child who could not close two inches of his hand on the rim of the basket that held him. The carrying arm went on keeping its exact account of what it held and saying nothing of what it cost. Berr's finger had gone over a figure and left it where it was; the stall-keeper's palm had taken two marks short without a count; Mira's thumb had gone under her apron to a cord no Court thumb would spell; Orra's last knot kept its double turn with its name unspoken; the reed-cutter's cord had gone up the boards inside a coat drawn close over it; the clerk's hand had gone to his slate before his eyes went to the stalls; and below the sill a child's foot stayed dark inside three turns of wool while the yard called the water weather. Each was exact, each was complete, and not one of them had a payer who could be named. He did not join them. Five likenesses in a row will stand up and call themselves a doctrine if no hand holds them apart, and a doctrine is an account that has found a payer; he had kept his finger off all of them for a hundred and thirty-seven days and would keep it off now, the way a man keeps his finger off a wound he has decided not to open.

The light went along the rim of a bowl on the pot board and came off it, and the market went on selling over the clerk and over the missing step without making a fall of it. River wind came up to meet the woodsmoke. The note of water went over water and under the boards where no slate lay, a fifth low thing never counted among the four, and the foot below the sill stayed dark in its three turns of wool, the afterdrop waiting on the far side of a warming nobody would perform. One hundred and thirty-seven days old, four months and seventeen days, exact and unspent and told by nobody. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water and came down against the fold without reaching it, and the count went on going.
