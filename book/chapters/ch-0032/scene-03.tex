\daymark{136}{Reed 17, Seventhday, Y.H. 813}

Before first grey they came up \place{the Clay Yard} in ones and twos, three or four of them, their collars high against a wind that had not yet made up its mind which way it would blow. \person{Sella Vail} carried \person[Corin]{the boy} out at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, and the dark shapes went ahead of her and turned in at the shadow of \place{the Vail wheel-shed} without a word that travelled. She gave the house no name for them. She did not say that \place{the back room} was cold.

The room off the shed kept its finger's width of open, as it had kept it through every day of his life, and the light came through that thin seam and lay on \place{the flagstones} like a thread laid down and not taken up. A door left a finger open is a door somebody means to come back through without the trouble of opening it properly, and the boy, who had once held the corner of a chart down with the flat of a knife while another hand went in underneath, read the small architecture of the gap and gave it no use. Men went in. Men came out. What they carried in went across the threshold in low voices and came back out as dust on a thrower's forearms, and nothing in the yard was entered.

The collars were high not against the weather but against being known, which is a different use for a collar and one the boy had learned to read on men who went into rooms where a slate lay face-down. He watched them from the level of the basket and saw what the half-pace was for: it kept a carrying arm from coming within reach of a hand that might want to know what it had carried. A low voice is a voice that has decided who may hear it before it decides what it will say, and the house gave the men the whole of its attention in the only way it had --- by going on with its work and giving them no name. Sella's little-door burn closed on the wicker's edge and then on nothing as she passed, and her head did not turn. Whatever the room held, it held it in the dark behind a seam a finger would pass through, and the boy set the seam on the tray where the other kept things stood, beside the charcoal under the shelf and the cord inside the cutter's coat, three materials no Court thumb had been asked to spell. He did not let them touch.

The instruction went to the wool and to the river wind and never to the basket. Keep it off his ears; it had come down with them before the light and would go back up with them at dusk, and a basket set down was a pot set down and left. Woodsmoke went before her and wet wool went with her, and under both the faint sour mineral tang of the \lore{Ash} that had lived in the corners of this house for two years and would not be swept. She carried him past the finger's width of door without turning her head toward it, at the half-pace, going wide around a pan set out to dry and a slate propped where a child might knock it, and if she knew the men had come she had made nothing of their coming that a Court thumb could read.

\person{Jorra Vail} came through the open door of the shed while she was still giving the instruction to the wind. Fine grey slate dust was ground small as meal into both forearms past the bend of his little finger and under the nails, and it was not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel. He kept the dusty forearms and the clean ones on one tray, as a man keeps two states of one morning apart on a counter where nothing is labelled, and he asked neither which slate had been broken nor whether it had been a birth-slate. The questions stood where they had stood for years, and the boy did not put them into words for him. He spoke to the wheel before he spoke to anyone. “It doesn't care whose hands,” he said. “It cares if they're steady.” Then, to a pot turning under his hands with the grey working into the creases of them, “You'll do.” There was no welcome in the voice and no unkindness either, only the flat note of a man telling a vessel what it already was.

He knew \lore{the full note} of that pot before it left the wheel. He had known it three turns before, in the moment when the clay was still settling into its shoulder and the note had not yet been given to the air, and he would not say so in front of a kiln-master come up from \place{High Kiln}, who stood at the shed door with the sweetish edge of lead glaze on his coat and Ash on his cuffs and said the thing was singing before its time. The kiln-master stopped with his mouth open on the next part of it, the way a man stops when what he has said has not been taken up. “You'll want a thinner lip on that,” he said, and then, because Jorra gave him no answer, “A pot that sings early generally sings itself out early.” He drew his thumb across his own cuff, where the sweet glaze had come off on the cloth, and looked at the wheel and not at the basket, which was how a man looked at a basket when he had decided it held no account worth entering. Jorra put one hand back to the clay and said nothing. He let the man have the seeing of it and kept the knowing, and the knowing went on a place of its own in the boy's file, one of the near-full answers that were advanced and never paid, a vessel's note held behind the teeth because a man who said it early would have something made of it. The kiln-master went back up toward the terrace with his coat carrying the sweet smell and his remark spending itself on the boards, and the wheel went on turning under hands that did not report what they knew.

\scenebreak

Sella had not finished telling him the fire was low. “The fire's low, Jorra. It wants---” The end of it never arrived at him. Before she could set the instruction down where it belonged, his bent finger went into the cold iron of \lore{the damper}, ahead of his hand the way it always went, and moved the tongue a hair's breadth and left it standing. The household straightened around his silence the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted off it. Cold iron takes the heat out of a finger faster than water takes it out of a stone, and the bent finger had gone in ahead of the hand because a thrower learns early which part of him can afford the lesson. It came away with the tip gone white for a breath and pink again after, and the boy read the small colour change as an account paid in full and entered nowhere: the tongue moved a hair's breadth, the flame found a new way through, and some pot in the house would be warmer by the width of a hair by midday. There was no charge and no payer named; a house that says \emph{the house} where another woman says \emph{I} can move a damper without telling anybody what it cost the finger that moved it. Nobody gave the finger a word. She shut her mouth on the sentence and gave the end of it to the pot, and the pot, which had not been asked, received the instruction as pots do, without answer and without thanks.

The boy watched the finger come away from the iron. He had watched it a hundred mornings and the iron was not a new thing to him, but he read it again as he read everything that would not stay read: one piece of cold with two bites in it. The bite that closes on a vessel and holds the whole heat in, and the bite that closes on a hand and can take the hand off a man, the same iron doing both according to where it is set and who has the use of it. There was no word in \lore{Grathic} that held the two mercies at once. He had held such a bite in his own hands in another country and known it by one use only, as an instrument that stops a thing from bleeding out; here the same iron had two bites and the kiln put a boy's hand into it before breakfast, and neither the woman nor the man who set it there called either bite by a name. He filed the double bite on the tray where objects lay, exact, complete and worth nothing, joined to no kiln and to no man's silence, and refused, as he refused every morning, to let it stand up and call itself a doctrine.

Beside it, on the same tray and not touching it, lay the two tellings of \lore{the counting room} shut in the war with the men inside dead of the breath, one carried eighteen leagues east with a date, the second carried eighteen leagues east with a bag and a date, both taken by the yard as history and neither taken into this house. No third telling had come. The men who had gone low into the room off the shed had not supplied one; they had supplied dust and quiet and the finger's width of door. The boy kept the two tellings where they lay and did not lay them across the iron, though the word for a room shut with the damper closed and a room with a finger's width of open would have been the same word if he had had a tongue for it and a verdict to spend.

Out in the yard \lore{the thaw} was being argued in two directions at once. A woman come up from the strips with dry mud in the creases of her wrists wanted it early and said the reeds would rot where they stood; a man with a barrow wanted it late and said nothing good had ever come up out of water. \person{Tarn} said “Yes, house” to the first and “Yes, house” to the second, agreeing so entirely that the argument had nowhere left to put its foot, and went on wanting the late one, because a late thaw puts off the paying. The thaw went around the yard as a direction and not a date, and he kept his own date where the house could not check it.

Before the broom he went to the sill and set the small knife at the very edge of it, and told the empty yard where it was, as he had told it every morning of the boy's life: “It's back. You can check.” The yard gave no answer and he did not need one; the telling was the account and not the hearing of it. Kept beside the knife, where no eye was asked to go, lay the unforged sentence about a man sent for a collector in the Long Cold and turned back at the frozen ford --- two shapes of one debt and neither entered. He held the pale clip-ring at his left wrist up to the light for a breath and let it go, and the light came off it and went nowhere that a slate could follow.

He took up the broom only after it had been given its instruction. It stood where it stood and he took it the way a man takes up a tool that has already been named, and the small knife lay at the very edge of its sill with six years standing in a mark his thumb had worn almost flat and the seventh pressed nearer its end and still held where it could not be counted. Six where the house could check and seven where it could not: that was the exact account, and he kept it exact, though something must give at the end of the seventh year and had not given yet. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line where the ridge had turned, and no scrubbing lifted it. His chest was tight. The breath he had decided not to spend on Seed 16 was still unspent, and a small interval stood at the top of each reach where it should have gone in, as exact and as worth nothing as the record in a carrying arm that tells what was lifted and not what it cost. He had carried that breath since Seed 16 and had made no payment of it.

The stroke went out over the flagstones and past the shallow bright patch the stones kept from the day the half-check had come and gone, and it went on without the half-check today, as it had gone on every day since, while Jorra's bent finger stood in the cold iron ahead of Sella's unfinished sentence. The place where the stroke had stopped stayed where it had stopped, a shallow bright patch in the grey paving that nobody swept around and nobody named. Neither man named the other's silence. Jorra did not ask about the ear and Tarn did not ask about the room, and the two not-askings stood under one roof without a finger laid between them, while the loan that could hear went on being worth more than the debt that held it and was still, on the seventeenth day of Reed, uncalled.

Behind them the door off the shed stood a finger's width open, and the light through it lay on the stones like a thread nobody took up.
