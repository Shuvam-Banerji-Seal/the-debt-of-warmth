\daymark{19}{Stone 25, Y.H. 812 (Fifthday)}

Stone 25 was Fifthday and he was nineteen days old, and the cold had come down another degree in the night the way a hand comes down on a sleeping child's quilt and tucks it, without asking whether the child was warm. The oiled paper at the windows of \place{the Vail kiln-house} lifted harder than it had lifted the day before, lifting and letting go against the frame with a dry small sound; \place{the Slip Brook} behind \place{the drying racks} had taken a skin the colour of skimmed milk gone grey, and the skin held under the wind without breaking, which was a thing the children of \place{Low Kiln} read as weather and nothing more. \person{Corin} lay in the basket at the edge of the floor with the cold brown-edged crust at his side where it had been put ten days ago and had not been moved in all that time, and the thread of drool at his mouth had frozen again in the small hours and nobody had thought to wipe it, because a basket at the edge of a board is a pot set down and left. The cold came in at cheek, then temple, then wrist, in the order he knew and could not argue with. The man inside the child marked the order the way he had once marked the order in which a body gives up its heat; the child inside the man wanted the breast and wanted the crust even cold, and the wanting was louder, and neither was answered.

\person{Sella Vail} went to the fire before she went to anything else. Her hand went to \lore{the widow's mark} at her throat, a thumb's length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it, held it the space of one breath and let it go, and only then lifted the lid. The burn shaped like a little door across the back of her hand opened on the iron and shut on nothing, and shut again when the lid was up. She counted the fuel into the last tend with her eyes, three here and two there, and moved her lips along the loaf shelf, and came back to the loaf set a little apart with its brown edge turned up, and never said what the arrangement counted. There was still bread in the house and ash under the pot and a fire that had not gone out in two years, and the household kept the days the only way it knew how: in loaves, glances and the space where a thing should have stood and did not. He carried his own number alongside theirs and did not lose it when theirs changed shape.

She carried him out to the racks after the light had cleared the sill and set the basket down with his face toward the kiln wall and his cheek against the fold, so that the draught off the brook should not go into his ears. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. “It's the river wind.” Then she went three paces to the fuel stack with her little-door burn half closed on nothing, and the yard went on about its work around the basket the way a room goes on around a pot set down and not looked into.

Two paces from his head a pot in \place{the Clay Yard} cracked all the way through.

The woman at the racks had set it down. The pot gave its small flat knock. The hand on the rim stayed a moment too long and came away.

He knew the sound before he knew where it was. It was the dead shadow of \lore{the full note}, the knock a vessel gives when the fault has run from lip to foot and there is no wall left in it to hold with, and he had heard it across a birth room, through a shed wall, and twice from a slate under a bell that did a temple's work. Here it came in the open yard with nothing between the clay and his ear, in the middle of the ordinary morning, and his head went the wrong way.

He tried to turn toward it. The head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound it made when it went too far; the fold caught at his ear; the effort spent itself, and by the time it had the yard had gone on with its work. He had once found a bleeder in a white room by the sound a strip of metal made going into a basin and placed it to the centimetre under a light that did not flicker. He could not place a cracked pot two paces from his own head.

“That's a pot gone wrong,” the woman said to \place{the drying racks}, and went on with her work.

Nobody called the knock a sound. There was nothing in \lore{Grathic} to name what it was except the word for a pot gone wrong, which was a word for the vessel and not for the thing that had happened to it. Nobody tapped a pot for him, or asked the yard to set a name beside the noise, or looked toward the basket to see whether anybody lying there had heard it. The cracked thing was a pot gone wrong, and a pot gone wrong is a thing a household sweeps around and goes past, and that was the end of the matter in the yard.

He put it on the tray with the two notes that did not come --- the pot turned toward on the bench that had given nothing, and the thing half-heard past the door on a carried vessel --- and made a third object there, and did not make a sixth hearing of it. A hearing, in the arithmetic he had kept since the first day under this roof, was a sound with a place: a room, a vessel, a hand that had set it down. This had a place and he could not turn to it, which was a failure of a different kind and therefore its own object. He kept the three intervals on one tray and the five hearings from four sources on another, and the two trays did not touch. The count stayed five. No hand had reached a vessel for him, no slate had been tapped, and the thing he knew exactly had bought him nothing, as it always had.

\person{Tarn} came past with the broom under his arm and \lore{the Ash} worked into the creases of his knuckles that no scrubbing lifted. Six years of the seven were behind him and counted every one, and the seventh he held back, the one entry in this house the house could not check. His broom checked at the place where the pot had stood, half a stroke, and went on; the grey kept moving under the bristles. He did not look at the pot. He did not look at the basket. He said nothing about either, and the boy said nothing, and the two silences --- one under the roof and one in the yard --- kept their usual shape, which was the shape of a loan that could hear and had not been called in. A loan that could hear was worth more than the debt that held a man; nobody in this house had ever put the sentence into words, and it lay in the yard all the same, under the trodden ash, where a man sweeping might have found it if he had been looking for a sentence instead of grey.

The woman swept around the cracked pot the way the house swept around the charred slate in the kiln ash --- leaving it where it lay because a thing left where it lay could be gone past without anybody having to decide what to do with it --- and \person{Garl Vail}'s proverb went to the pot and the fire and never to the basket: leave the crack showing; people trust a thing they can see has been handled. The fault stood open to the light and nobody picked the thing up. His coat kept its salt shape on its peg, his seat in the shed kept its hollow on the left, and the ridge ran to the corner and turned it without a break, and all of them were gone past a dozen times that morning without a word spoken to any of them.

\person{Jorra Vail} came in from \place{the Vail wheel-shed} with slip dried to the colour of old bone up both forearms and no grey worked into the creases past the bend of the little finger, none of the dust that had come back with him on Stone 23. “The shoulder's thick,” he said to the pot on the bench. “I'll thin it.” He went to the damper before Sella had finished telling the fire it was low, the bent finger going in ahead of his hand, and left the end of her sentence where it was. He did not say the back room was cold. He did not look toward the bed. The boy kept the clean forearms and the dusty forearms on separate places on his tray, as he kept two similar bones apart until one of them was proved different, and made nothing of the fact that both had belonged to the same pair of arms two days ago.

The kiln took its breath in fours under the roof and missed one. Four, and four, and a gap. He counted it without deciding to, as he counted the boards and the bell and the step on \place{the Fishstairs} that was not a step; the gap was the part nobody had thought to enter, and it lay in the kiln's breath as it lay in the kindling stack and on the loaf shelf and now on the third place of the interval tray. At the bank that night Sella turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and did not touch the mark, because a bank is not an opening and the rule was exact as a stitch. She put the fold right at his ear without looking at his face, and fed him from the house's own dish with two fingers behind his ear and the spoon wiped on her sleeve, and gave the instruction to the dish and the fire, and the warmth crossed into him and was entered nowhere.

The oiled paper kept lifting at the window. The brook kept its skin under the wind. The cracked pot stood swept around on the trodden ash with the fault showing, and the boy lay under the roof with the cold crust at his side and a third object on the tray of things that did not join, and the count going on because there was nothing else that was his.

\daymark{20}{Stone 26, Y.H. 812 (Sixthday)}

Stone 26 was Sixthday and he was twenty days old, and the house went about an ordinary day. It was not \lore{Account-day}. There was no bell in the morning and no slate read aloud in the yard, no figure set out where two hundred people could lean over it and go home with a fist shut; there was only the work --- mending, drying, sweeping, eating --- which was the work the house did whether anybody read a number over it or not. He woke with the fold at his chin and the crust cold at his side, eleven days where it had been put, and smelled barley smoke in it when the wind turned, and wanted it, and could not lift a hand to it.

The number for \lore{the Five} went around the yards that morning in mouths and fists. It reached the house the way such things reached it, not as a figure spoken to the basket but as an absence with a shape: a mouth stopping short on a word, a hand closed on a latch, a glance that went toward the fold and thought better of it. He had learned the grammar of it in a white room where a chart went around a table and came back to the bed with the speaker taken out of the sentence; here there was no chart, only the yard and the wind off the brook and the loaves on the shelf, and the figure went over the top of him and could not be entered.

“Three days,” a neighbour said at \place{the yard well}, with her slate held to her chest and the bucket not going down yet. “Three, and the Five on us. I've counted it twice.”

“Two,” said a second, come up from the racks with grey on her sleeves. “You've counted the Fifthday twice. It was two yesterday and it's two today.”

“It'll be whatever the moot gives out,” said a third, and set her pot on the stone lip, and went about her business without another word.

The argument was about a figure nobody wanted to own. They passed it back and forth like a cup too hot to hold --- three, two, whatever the moot gave out --- and each of them put it down somewhere else before it could be entered against them. Then the wind came off the brook and somebody said it was a cold snap, and that was the end of it, because weather was a thing a person could say without owning a single day of it. One neighbour who wanted the thaw late said a late thaw was a longer week before the paying, and another who wanted it early said the ice would not wait for the moot, and the yard went on sweeping. The boy heard the figures go past and could not spend them, correct them, or hand them back. He had once sat across a table from a man who wanted a number changed, and had known which line of the form would let the change through and which would not, and had got it through on the wrong line and carried it ever since. Here he lay under a fold with the numbers going over him and no form in the house that would take the weight of his hand.

He kept the one number none of them would give. The days were counted one at a time, and one day after tonight was tomorrow, and after tomorrow there would be another, and when five of them had gone \lore{Kindling} would come. The district could give out three, or two, or whatever it had already decided to pay, and the days would not agree with any of them, and the disagreement was the whole of what was his.

Sella went around the days in loaves and glances rather than a figure. She moved three loaves along the shelf and two back and came to the one set a little apart with its brown edge turned up, and came back to it a second time as she passed, and never said what the arrangement counted. When she fed him from the house's own dish, gruel thinned with milk, with two fingers behind his ear and the spoon wiped on her sleeve, she gave the instruction to the dish and the fire and not to the basket, and the warmth crossed into him and was entered nowhere, as the stone had been and the crust had been and every kindness in this house had been. He had learned that a gift which could not be answered could not be refused, which was a lesson in bread before it was a lesson in anything else.

Jorra came in from the shed with slip dried to the colour of old bone up both forearms and no grey worked into the creases past the bend of the little finger, and said to a pot on the bench that it would do, and went to the damper before Sella had finished telling the fire it was low. He did not say the back room was cold and did not look toward the bed. The absence of dust was a fact in the yard today as the presence of it had been a fact on Stone 23, and the boy kept both facts on the same tray without putting one over the other, because he had learned that two things could be true of the same forearms and neither of them enterable.

Tarn swept the trodden ash twice, once before light and once after the mending, and set the small bench knife on its sill and told the yard it was where it could be checked, though the yard was empty. Six years of the seven were counted and the seventh held back. His broom checked at the place in the yard where the pot had cracked the day before, half a stroke, and went on, and he did not look at the place or say what had happened there, and the boy did not look at him. The loan that could hear stayed where it was, called in by neither man. The cracked pot stood where the woman had swept around it, and everybody went past it, and nobody in the yard called it anything but a pot gone wrong.

The crust lay cold in the fold, stiff at the edges, smelling of smoke and barley when the wind turned. Sella neither took it out nor named it, and the boy neither ate it nor gave it up. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward it and came down on the fold, and the hand that had once reduced a shoulder in the dark could not lift eleven days of bread to his own mouth. He had learned to find the joke funny without laughing, which was a way of laughing that cost less and kept the same blade in it.

Garl's coat kept its salt shape on its peg and the wheel-seat its hollow on the left in the shed, and the ridge ran to the corner and turned it without a break and would not be swept before \lore{the Last Day}. His proverbs went to the fire and the pot: feed the fire before you feed yourself; leave the crack showing, people trust a thing they can see has been handled. Nobody said them to the basket. The uncalled debt against a firing of tiles lay in the space where a sentence about the district's paying should have stood, and the district had gone back to its ordinary day without calling it, which was how a debt stayed alive in this house: by nobody saying the word for it.

At the bank Sella turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and did not touch the widow's mark, because a bank is not an opening and an ordinary Sixthday is not a reading. The kiln took its breath in fours and missed one. Four, and four, and a gap. The loaves were rearranged on the shelf for another day, three here and two there and the brown-edged one set apart and come back to, so that the days to Kindling stood as a shape no collector could take up and no figure could be entered. The count went on because there was nothing else that was his.

The cracked pot stood swept around on the trodden ash where the woman had left it, the fault open to the light and nobody picking it up, and the household's loaves stood rearranged for another day, and the boy lay under the roof with the crust cold in the fold and a number in his head that nobody in the yard would say.
