For five mornings the slip that came back from \place{the Vail wheel-shed} had been the colour of old bone and no grey had been worked into the creases past the bend of the little finger, and \person{Corin} had kept the absence the way he kept the space in the kindling stack --- as a fact with a shape, entered nowhere. He lay in the basket under the fold at the edge of \place{the Vail kiln-house} floor, with the cold brown-edged crust lying where it had been put eight days ago and not moved since, and the drool at his mouth frozen to a thread that nobody thought to wipe, because a basket at the edge of a board is a pot set down and left. Stone 23 was Thirdday and he was seventeen days old, and the fifth clean morning was already going into the tally he had no hand to write.

Then the absence was over. \person{Jorra Vail} came in from the shed with fine grey slate dust worked into the creases of his forearms past the bend of the little finger, grey as the ash under the pot-shovel and not the bone of the wheel, lying in the seams of the skin the way silt lies in the joints of a drying riverbed. He said nothing about where he had been. The boy read the not-looking the way he had once read a resident who kept his eyes on the chart when a senior asked him a question he did not want to answer: not the face, which was arranged, but the part of it that refused to report. The face said the wheel, the pots, the fire. The forearms said a room off the shed with a door that stood shut.

He had sat outside a curtained bed once while a senior asked a resident whether the family had been told, and the resident had kept his eyes on the chart and said the chart would show what had been said, and the boy had known before anybody spoke that the family had not been told at all. It was not a gift. It was a thing a man learned by standing in rooms where the truth had been arranged for him, and he had learned it early and brought it into a country that had no word for the arrangement. Jorra's face was arranged the same way. The dust were the chart.

The cold came in at cheek, then temple, then wrist, in the order he knew and could not argue with, and the body made its small claim against the older one: he wanted the breast, wanted the crust even cold, wanted a hand that could point at the dust and ask the one short question, and got none of the three. He had reduced a shoulder in the dark once and could not keep the wool on his own shoulder now. The joke had a blade in it and he turned it gently, the way a man tests a tooth with his tongue, and it cost him the same as always.

“She'll want a thinner slip,” Jorra said to the pot on the bench, and then, to the wheel through the wall, “The wheel doesn't care whose hands. It cares if they're steady.” \person{Sella Vail} told the fire the fire was low, and he went to the damper before she had finished telling it, the bent little finger going in ahead of his hand and taking the iron with it, and left the end of her sentence where it was. He did not say the back room was cold. He did not look toward the bed.

The pot on the bench was turned a little toward the light and would not be looked at twice. Jorra took it up and set it down again, and the boy turned his head toward the set-down before the hand had quite left the clay, and nothing came --- the same small wooden silence he had filed twice already, and he did not make a third entry of it, because a thing that happens again in the same shape is the same object on the tray and not a new one. The count stayed five from four sources. Jorra's bent finger came off the damper and he went back through the door without a word, and the boy did not turn his head after him, because a head that goes where it wants makes a small wet sound when it goes too far.

There had been low talk in \place{the back room} for three years: men come in whose names the house carried on no roll it showed, speaking in voices lowered for a door, and Jorra coming out with dust in his skin and saying nothing a person could take further. The boy had never seen the men. He had seen only the shape they left behind --- a door kept closed off the shed, dust where slip should have been, and the way the house went around the place with the care a woman uses going around a pot that has been set down and may yet give a sound. Under \place{the drying racks} was a space where whole slate should have stood, and the yard went around that too without entering it. He kept his question where he kept the others, in the place questions could last for years, because a question asked in this house acquired an answer nobody wanted to own, and the answer would be written in a neat hand by somebody else.

Sella got quieter, and the yard straightened around the quiet as a pot settles on its foot. \person{Tarn} swept the trodden ash with his head down and \lore{the Ash} worked into the creases of his knuckles that no scrubbing lifted, and when the broom came to the place by the bench where a pot had once given nothing he kept his eyes on the ash and swept around it.

“I swept it this morning,” he said to the floor. “I'll sweep it again.”

Six years of the seven were counted and the seventh held back, and he had said that to the kindling rather than to a person; the boy had heard it and kept it, and neither of them had said a word to the other about the other thing they both kept. Two silences under one roof were nearer to an equality than anything this house had a name for, and it had no name for it, and the loan that could hear had been exercised again and bought nobody anything.

He counted the creases on the forearms as he counted the nails in the boards, four on the near arm and four on the other, with the grey worked in past the bend where slip had lain for five clean days, and he kept the clean forearms and the dusty forearms as two separate entries on the tray, the way a man keeps two similar bones apart until one of them is proved different. He did not put the dust with the ridge because the ridge had been left where it fell and the dust had been carried in on a man, and the difference mattered more than the colour.

The boy put the grey on a tray of its own beside the ridge along the skirting board, the charred slate lying black in the kiln ash, and the barrow of grey that had gone out through the north gate toward \place{the Bone Orchard}, and made nothing of the likeness between them. Five clean days had made the absence of the dust into a fact; one morning of grey had turned it back into a question with a shape and no hand to write it. A wrong answer entered in a neat hand cost more than the trouble of keeping four similar greys in four separate places on a tray, and he had learned that in a room with better light, where the neat hand had been his own.

The loaf shelf still carried the days to \lore{the Five} as a shape no collector could take up, and the kiln took its breath in fours under the roof and missed one. Four, and four, and a gap. \person{Garl Vail}'s coat kept its salt shape on its peg and his seat its hollow on the left, and his proverb went to the pot and the fire --- leave the crack showing; people trust a thing they can see has been handled --- and never to the basket. At the bank that night Sella turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and did not touch \lore{the widow's mark}; the rule held at the close of a Thirdday as exactly as at an opening. The crust lay cold in the fold. The dust stayed worked into the creases of the thrower's forearms, grey and fine, and the boy lay under the roof with a question he could not ask and no hand to ask it with.

At mid-morning on Stone 24 \person{Alda Reed} came under the roof with soap and warm milk still on her and the birth-slates under her arm, and went to the bench before she went to the basket. The boy had learned the order of reaching in midwives and neighbours and men with iron tubes and women who kept fire, and he had never once seen it vary: the hand to the record before the hand to the person, because the record would still be lying where it had been put when the person had been carried out of the room. Alda laid the birth-slates writing-down on the bench and only then turned toward the fold. Sella stood a half-pace off with her little-door burn shut on nothing, and the two women did not look at each other, and nothing passed between them that could be entered.

The two women had traded a silence since the boy was three days old. Sella had never asked what Alda wrote, and Alda had never asked why Sella took the child, and the arrangement held without a word passing between them, the way a door holds on a latch neither hand will lift. The boy lay at the edge of it and was the thing kept on both sides of it: entered in a roll he had never seen and in notes he would never read, warmed from a dish nobody entered against him, washed in an order that never varied. He had learned to recognise a room where the truth had been arranged before he came into it. This was such a room, and the arrangement had been made by two women who were kind, which was why he could not take it apart in his head without finding his own hands in it.

Alda smelled of the lye she washed in and of milk warmed for somebody else's child, and under those the faint mineral tang of a corner she had promised herself she would sweep tomorrow and had not swept in thirty years. Her nails were scrubbed to the quick and clay still lived under them. When she took the fold at his shoulder she pressed it flat too long, longer than a fold required, and her two fingers stayed on it after the wool had been set right, the way a woman's hand will stay on a stone she has just put down, feeling whether the heat is in it.

Inside her bodice, on its cord, the little tin lay against her ribs. Her hand went to it once and rested there and came away without opening it, which was the whole of the gesture: a record touched in the dark where no officer's thumb would think to look, and not read even by the woman who kept it. The boy had watched that hand go to the tin and come away again and again and had never once seen it open the tin, and kept the two facts on separate places on the tray, because a tin touched and a tin opened were different objects and the cost of confusing them was a wrong answer in a neat hand.

Sella said, “He lies quiet.”

Alda turned the top slate a little, though it was already turned down. “Warm and breathing,” she said, “that's all I've set down.” It was exact, and it was not true. The boy lay with the cold coming in at cheek, then temple, then wrist, and the crust going cold in the fold, and heard the lie delivered as an entry, in the voice a room uses when it wants a chart to read clean.

She saw the red edge of \lore{the birth-flower} at his wrist where the wool had ridden back, and crossed two fingers at the corner of it before she knew she had done it. He had seen the sign made at him again and again, and she had never once known she was making it in front of him. She covered it the way she always covered it, with a date nobody had asked for.

“Stone seven,” she said, “the account went up on the eighth, before the paper went out.”

Then came the cooper's seventh in Mud End and a potter's third child in Stonemouth, and the dates went on arriving after there was no question left for them to answer, faster than a question had any need of them, the way a woman will keep pouring milk into a cup that is full already because pouring is the thing she knows how to do. She gave the dates the way other women set a pot near the fire --- because it was the thing to do and because a room in pain wants something counted --- and the boy, who had counted everything done to him since he could count, took them in and kept them with the rest of the unspendable numbers: one in twelve, a ridge of two thumbs, nineteen steps, a figure that went around the yards in a fist. None of them could be handed back. These were given by a woman with milk on her sleeves and were worth no more than the others. She did not say the sign was a sign, and she did not say why she made it, and the two fingers went back to the slate.

She took up a narrow slate. On it she wrote what she did not leave lying about, in a second hand no Court physician had ever seen, and when she had finished she turned it writing-down into her shawl, toward a floorboard in a house the boy could not name, where a further note would go into the tin still unread. The writing went down as a woman puts a cup down in a room where somebody is sleeping: carefully, and with the flat of her hand, so that nothing should be heard. She did not leave the slate lying about. That was the whole of it --- not a word, not a name, not a figure, only a narrow slate turned face-down into wool and a hand that knew where the floorboard was. The boy had watched a hand reach for a record before it reached for a person in a white room once, and had signed the record himself without reading the note turned down beside it, and here was the same gesture performed by a woman who had washed him and meant him well, and he could no more hand it back than he could lift the slate by one edge.

He smelled the barley in the cold crust at his side and wanted it, and his hand went up an inch and came down on the fold, and the want was childish and loud and true, and the thing he wanted to know was quieter and had been quiet for eighteen days. Both were true, and the second was louder only when the room was still; here a woman was giving dates to a basket and a craft was being hidden in wool, and the child's want won the argument without a vote, as it always did.

He could not read a word of \lore{Grathic} on it. He could not ask which house held the floorboard, or ask why a woman who wrote everything down within three days kept a second record that no mother and no officer would ever read. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the shawl and came down, and the hand that had once put a shoulder back in the dark could not turn the corner of the slate.

Two records had gone into the same morning under one roof: grey dust worked into a thrower's forearms by the breaking of something nobody would name, and a narrow slate turned writing-down into a shawl by a woman who would not say what she had written. Neither had been entered where a Court's thumb would look. Alda went on with the dates after the question had stopped, the birth-slates stacked writing-down on the bench beside her, the little tin still shut inside her bodice; Jorra's grey dust stayed worked into the creases of his forearms in the shed where nobody had asked about it; and the boy lay in the fold with the sign on his wrist that nobody had read.
