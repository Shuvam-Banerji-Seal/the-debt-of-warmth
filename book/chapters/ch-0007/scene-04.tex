Stone 27 was Seventhday and he was twenty-one days old, and the cold had come to stay in the thatch the way a debt comes to stay in a house, not all at once but by degrees, until a man looked up and found it had been there the whole winter and would be there after him. The dry frost ticked in the straw over \place{the Vail kiln-house} with a sound like a woman clicking her tongue at a pot set down wrong, and \place{the Slip Brook} behind \place{the drying racks} carried its grey skin without breaking, and \place{the Ilm} below the sill lay under ice that had stopped being a thing a man looked at and had become a thing a man walked on. \person{Corin} lay in the basket at the edge of the floor with the old brown-edged crust at his side where it had lain since Stone 15, stiff as a shingle and smelling of smoke when the wind turned, and the thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had thought to wipe it, because a basket at the edge of a board is a pot set down and left. Cold came in at cheek, then temple, then wrist. He marked the order as he marked all orders, and the child inside him wanted the crust even stiff and wanted the breast, and the wanting was louder, and neither was answered.

\person{Sella Vail} went to the fire before she went to anything else. Her hand went to \lore{the widow's mark} at her throat, the thumb's length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it, held it the space of one breath and let it go, and only then lifted the lid. The little-door burn across the back of her hand opened on the iron and shut on nothing, and shut again when the lid was up. She counted the fuel into the last tend before \lore{the Five} with her eyes, three here and two there, and moved her lips along the loaf shelf so the days to \lore{Kindling} stood as a shape no collector could take up, and came back to the loaf set a little apart with its brown edge turned up, and never said what the arrangement counted. “Feed it before you feed yourself,” she said to the fire, which was \person{Garl Vail}'s saying improved by twenty years of use, and the fire gave nothing back and the house went on around the basket the way a room goes on around a pot set down and left. \lore{the Ash} lay along the skirting board in a ridge near two thumbs wide that ran to the corner and turned it without a break, and it would not be swept before \lore{the Last Day}.

She carried him out to the racks after the light had cleared the sill and set the basket with his cheek against the fold and his face turned away from the wind off the brook. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. “It's the river wind.” The yard was already arguing.

A woman come up from \place{High Kiln} with sweetish lead-glaze dried at one corner of her mouth stood among the racks with her shawl pulled tight and said the thaw would come early. “It came early last year,” she said. “The ice runs thin where the water's fast. You can hear it under the bridge.”

“You can hear it,” said a neighbour who wanted it late, with her slate held to her chest. “And you'll hear the levy when it comes with an early thaw, sooner than you'll hear your own kettle. Late's the weather for me.”

“Late puts off the paying,” a second said, and that was the whole of the argument, and everybody went on sweeping.

The boy lay with the argument going over him and kept it as a shape, not a cause. He had learned the difference in a better-lit room where two men had set a cluster of facts side by side and declared that one had made the other happen, and he had carried the mistake the rest of a life. The hairline in the river ice ran in from one edge and stopped a thumb's breadth short of the other; a woman wanted an early thaw and a yard wanted a late one because the levy fell in the thaw; the two things stood in the same morning without one of them explaining the other. He put them on the same tray and drew no line between them. Sella, asked, said only that it was weather, which was a word that let a woman go home warm without owning a single day of it.

The ice-mice were found in the thatch before the morning was old. That was what the children of \place{Low Kiln} called the small grey patches that came up in straw in a hard winter --- a thumb's width here, a finger's there, spreading where the wind got under the eaves --- and by noon a child two doors down had the same word on the back of her hand, a numb patch the colour of skimmed milk gone grey that spread while somebody held the hand and looked at it and did nothing to it. “That's ice-mice,” a woman said, and the women at the tubs said yes, that was ice-mice, a thing the cold did, and went back to their work. The hand went on being held. The patch went on spreading. Nobody called it anything but what the cold did, because what the cold did was weather, and weather was the one thing that could take a child's hand without anybody having to answer for it. He watched it the way he had once watched a cooling foot in a room where the nurses kept saying it was only cold, and kept the seeing where questions can last for years.

\person{Tarn} swept the yard with \lore{the Ash} worked into the creases of his knuckles that no scrubbing lifted, and six years of the seven were behind him counted every one, and the seventh he held back, the one entry in this house the house could not check. He stopped under the eaves where the grey patches showed and said to the thatch what he would not have said to a person. “A late one,” he said. “Late's mine. A longer week before the paying.” The thatch ticked in the wind and gave him no answer, which was a relief, because an answer would have been a thing he would have had to enter. His broom checked half a stroke at the place where the cracked pot had stood two days ago and went on, and he did not look at the place, and the boy did not look at him, and the two silences kept their usual shape.

Under the roof \person{Jorra Vail} came in from \place{the Vail wheel-shed} with slip dried to the colour of old bone up both forearms and the bent little finger leading his hand, and told a pot on the bench that the shoulder was thick and he would thin it, and went to the damper before Sella had finished telling the fire it was low, and left the end of her sentence where it was. He did not say where he had been and he did not look toward the bed. The kiln took its breath in fours under the roof and missed one. Four, and four, and a gap. The boy counted it as he had counted it every day since the first, and set it beside the figure going around the yards --- three, then two, then whatever the moot had already decided to give out --- and kept the two numbers apart, because one of them was a shape a household kept in loaves and glances and the other was a shape the fire kept without being asked. They did not agree, and the not-agreeing was the part that was his. Nobody in the yard would have put the breath of a kiln and a figure for the Five in the same sentence, and if they had they would have called it weather, which was what this house called everything that had a shape and no hand to enter it.

At the bank Sella turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and did not touch the mark, because a bank is not an opening and the rule was exact as a stitch. She put the fold right at his ear without looking at his face and fed him from the house's own dish with two fingers behind his ear and the spoon wiped on her sleeve, and gave the instruction to the dish and the fire, and the warmth crossed into him and was entered nowhere, as it always was. Garl's coat kept its salt shape on its peg and his seat its hollow on the left in the shed, and his proverb went to the pot and the fire and never to the basket: leave the crack showing; people trust a thing they can see has been handled. The old crust lay stiff at his side and was neither taken out nor named, and the ice-mice ticked in the thatch, and the kiln kept four, and four, and a gap. He was twenty-one days old and the count went on because there was nothing else that was his.

Stone 28 was Eighthday and he was twenty-two days old, and the smell of the eels came up \place{the Clay Yard} before the woman who carried them, which was how \person{Mira Silt} arrived in any place --- a smell of river silt and salted perch and tallow smoke going ahead of her, and then the small smile kept on her face the way another woman keeps a door on the latch, and then the pale slime dried on her sleeves. \place{House Silt} stood three doors down the yard with the morning smoke going up from it, and the path from its door to the drying racks was nineteen steps long to a woman who had counted them every day for six years, though nobody had counted them this morning yet, and Mira came the other way, up from the fish-trade at the bridge with a basket on her hip. She came past \place{the yard well} without looking into it and went on toward the racks.

Her head was held a little to the right against the cold where the top of her left ear had been, that being the side a hook had taken by her account and a knife had taken by the scar's, and nobody in the yard had ever made anything of the difference. When a neighbour asked her whose basket was going past, meaning the one at the edge of the floor under the Vail wall, Mira answered with a question and then her own answer. “Whose do you think? A basket's a basket. It came from a warm house, that's all, and it's going back to one.” She flicked an eel-bone against her thumb, put it away, and went on with the eels.

A woman at the well said a figure for the Five out loud, three or two or whatever it was that morning, and Mira's thumb went under her apron to the grey wool cord at her waist and stayed there while she decided something, and came out again with another bone between finger and thumb. The boy watched the hand and not what the hand was at. He had learned to read the movement and leave the counting of it to the woman who owned it: he had no word in \lore{Grathic} for a knot, and if he had there would still have been no hand to turn the cord and no standing to ask what the knots were for. The hand went under and came out. That was the whole of what was entered in his file. The smile on her face did not move, which was how he knew the hand had moved.

Then she gave him the crust. There was no speech about it and no pause in which a speech could have been made --- she was three paces off and the basket was at the edge of the floor and she crossed the space with the briskness of a woman putting a bowl down before a child can ask for it, and put a fresh brown-edged barley crust into the fold at his side and went back to the eels without looking in. “It's a crust,” she said over her shoulder to nobody, “not a sermon.” The new one smelled of barley smoke and the Vail fire. The old one lay where it had lain since Stone 15, stiff and cold and smelling the same when the wind turned, and two warmths lay side by side in the fold now, one given and one spent, and neither was entered against anybody, and neither woman named either one. Sella at the fire did not look up and did not take the old one out, and where she was concerned not seeing and not refusing were the same thing. He could smell both and could lift neither. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth and came down on the fold, and the surgeon who had once reduced a shoulder in the dark could not get a crust to his own mouth. He had learned to find the joke funny without laughing, which was a way of laughing that cost less and kept the same blade in it.

When she shifted the basket's weight to her other hip, a second cord twisted twice under her belt showed for a moment below the grey one --- a record of a different account, on a string that had never been taken up the river to \place{Nine Pots} and never lifted at a door. The boy saw the twist of it and kept it in its own place beside the first cord, as he kept two similar bones apart until one of them was proved different, and did not join them. There was a child upriver whose name he had never heard and would not have known how to ask for, and the cord lay in the fold of her apron below the other cord, and the two cords went off up the yard with the eels. The knots on neither were counted. The counting belonged to her, and the record was wool, which no Court's thumb would think to look for and no slate could take up.

Three doors down, at the end of nineteen steps from the pits, \person{Dessa Silt} came to the yard mouth with slip-grey nails and wet clay dried at her hem and her shoulder against the jamb as if the jamb were the only thing holding her up. The cough came up out of the pits in a low short way and she put her hand over her mouth, and when it had passed she said it was weather. “It's the pits,” she said to the ground. “Comes up in the cold. Weather.” Then, because a truth had come near and truths needed a handle, she got specific: nineteen out and nineteen back, four loaves on the seventh of Stone in eight hundred and twelve, three to the levy and one eaten with her feet up, and bits to the woman who came with a basket. Her thumb went to the pale sieve-wire scar up the inside of her left forearm and smoothed it once, the way a woman smooths a fold she does not want to see, and the door-shut smile came down over her face and meant that nothing further would be said. She did not look into the basket going past her shade. She had not said the number in three years and had not said the name in three years, and the boy going past her in the fold of another woman's wool did not know that he was the person the count kept out of the room.

He could not lift either crust to his mouth, could not ask about either cord, could not tell the cough from the weather, and he kept all three in the private file with the others, where nothing was joined and nothing was spent. Two women had put warmth into the fold without a word and kept their accounts in materials no Court could confiscate --- a cord of grey wool and a thumb's worth of steps --- and he was inside neither account and could thank neither woman. He had learned that a gift which could not be answered could not be refused, which was a lesson in bread before it was a lesson in anything else, and the old crust going cold beside the new one was the same lesson said twice. The kiln took its breath under the roof in fours and a gap, and the yard went back to its work, and the boy lay with the fresh crust tucked in beside the cold one, Mira's thumb going back under her apron to the grey cord as she walked off with the eels, and Dessa's door-shut smile at the end of nineteen steps.
