Stone 29 was Ninthday and \person{Corin} was twenty-three days old, and \place{the Clay Yard} was making ready for \lore{the Last Day} the way a house makes ready for a burying it has put off as long as it can --- not with grief, but with brooms. They came out of the sheds in the morning, the stiff ones worn to a stump where the straw had been beaten against doorposts, the new ones still bound with wire, and the dry tick of thatch went along under the \place{the Vail kiln-house} eaves all morning like a woman clicking her tongue at a pot set down wrong. A date was said aloud two or three times, the way a date is said when everybody knows it and wants to hear another mouth say it, and a woman at the tubs told the girl beside her that the grey would be left where it lay until the Last Day and burned the morning afterward and called dealt with, as though a thing called dealt with had ever been a thing finished.

The grey was \lore{the Ash}. It lay along the skirting board under the Vail wall in a ridge near two thumbs wide, running to the corner and turning it without a break, and \person{Sella Vail} laid her thumb into it there and left the thumb a moment, the way a man will lay two fingers flat on a wound before he brings a knife near it, taking the shape first so the hand cannot lie about what it found; then she walked two doors down to the death-house, where the ridge was a finger's width thicker, and laid the same thumb into that, and came back up the yard without ever saying which ridge was which. Both turned their corners without a break. Neither would be swept before the day. The keeping of a house was done with a thumb and a walk and nothing said; he set it on the tray beside the charred slate and the barrow of grey and made nothing of the likeness, because a likeness is not a cause and he had made that mistake once in a better-lit room and carried it ever since.

At the door the uncalled debt against a firing of tiles went around the talk the way a woman goes around a pot left on the floor in the dark --- a careful circle and no word for the thing circled. \person{Garl Vail}'s coat kept its salt shape on the peg behind the door and nobody took it down; his seat in the shed kept its hollow on the left and nobody filled it; his proverb went to the pot and the fire and never to the basket: leave the crack showing, people trust a thing they can see has been handled. The boy lay at the edge of the floor with the old crust stiff at one side and the fresh one given at the other, and the two lay side by side the way two facts lie side by side in a room where neither will be entered, one spent and one still smelling faintly of the Vail fire when the wind turned. Cold came in at cheek, then temple, then wrist. He marked the order as he marked all orders, and the child in him wanted the crust that was given and wanted the breast, and the wanting was louder, and neither was answered.

\person{Tarn} came up from the shed with \lore{the Ash} in the creases of his knuckles that no scrubbing lifted and the pale clip-ring plain at his left wrist, and set the small bench knife on its sill with the care of a man putting down a thing he can be asked about. “It's on the sill,” he said to the yard, which was empty of everybody but the basket. “You can check.” He looked at the ridge where Sella's thumb had been and then took his eyes off it, the way a man takes his eyes off a woman's face when she has been crying. “I can check the width, house. I've a thumb as good as any.” Six years of the seven were behind him counted every one; the seventh he held back, the one entry in this house the house could not check. He said it to the broom and not to a person: “Six counted. The next I don't count.” The broom checked half a stroke at the place where a pot had stood and given nothing two days before, and went on, and he did not look at the place, and the boy did not look at him, and the two silences kept their usual shape.

\person{Jorra Vail} came in from \place{the Vail wheel-shed} with slip dried to the colour of old bone up both forearms and the bent little finger leading his hand, and there was no grey worked into the creases past the bend, not today. The sweeping talk going around the racks had made him quieter, the way a certain quiet in a house is louder than a shout, and the yard had straightened around it without anybody giving the order. He told a pot on the bench that the shoulder was thick and he would thin it, and went to the damper before Sella had finished telling the fire it was low, going in with the bent finger ahead of his hand and leaving the end of her sentence where it was. He did not say the back room was cold and he did not look toward the bed. \lore{the full note} was in a Vail pot before it left the wheel and he would not have said so in front of a kiln-master, who would have called it early and made something of it; the boy knew this about him the way he knew the man's left little finger, and kept both where questions can last for years.

Under the roof the kiln took its breath in fours and missed one. Four, and four, and a gap. The boy counted it as he had counted it every day since the first, and set it beside the thumb in the grey, and beside the date said aloud two or three times, and beside the careful circle around the tiles, and kept all four apart. One of them was a shape a household kept in loaves and glances, one was a shape the fire kept without being asked, and two were shapes a man went around so that they would not have to be looked at directly. None of them was a number anybody would put on a slate, and if they had been put on one he would not have been able to read it, which was the whole humiliation and the whole safety of it. At the bank Sella turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and did not touch the mark, because a bank is not an opening and the rule was exact as a stitch. She put the fold right at his ear without looking at his face and gave the instruction to the fire and the wool, and the day went down over the yard with the brooms standing in the doorways and the two crusts where they had been put, and the ridge turning its corner without a break, and the kiln keeping four, and four, and a gap.

Stone 30 was Tenthday and \lore{Account-day} and the last market of the year, and he was twenty-four days old. Before the light had cleared the sill \person{Sella Vail} went to the fire, and her hand went to \lore{the widow's mark} at her throat, the thumb's length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it; she held it the space of one breath and let it go, and only then lifted the lid. The little-door burn across the back of her hand opened on the iron and shut on nothing and shut again when the lid was up. It was a reading, not an opening; there would be no touching of the mark at \place{the Cross} today, and she carried the basket down with the fold over his face and her half-pace kept from every open slate all the way, the way a woman carries a pot filled that must not be slopped. He had learned the rule of that hand better than he had learned any rule of his own: before the opening and at no other hour, not at a bank, not at a reading, not at a dusk. A thing kept that exactly had a reason, and the reason was a thing a house could not say in front of a man from \place{Slate Row}.

She set him at the north end of the square in \place{the moot-post}'s finger's width of shade, with the cold iron of the post at his cheek and the two crusts in the fold at his side, one spent and stiff from Stone 15 and one given and still smelling of barley smoke when the wind turned. Then she went a half-pace off and stood with her burn shut on nothing, and gave the instruction to the wool: keep it off his ears, it's the river wind. The last market of the year went up around him --- pigs driven over the wet boards, turnips heaped where figures were left standing side by side without anybody making them agree, hoops, eels, the sour smoke of a brazier, the river wind coming under the fold --- and the boy lay with his cheek to cold iron and counted what the square gave him because there was nothing else that was his.

The bell went four, four and a gap, longer than a church bell, and nobody crossed himself. It was the only sound in the square that wanted nothing from him and he listened to it longer than he needed to, counting the strokes the way he counted the nails in a floorboard: four on the near board, four on the next, and a gap where the fifth should have been. A Court instrument doing a temple's work and nobody in \place{Sill} finding the two ends of it worth joining. He set the bell's gap beside the kiln's gap and the floorboards' gap and the kindling's empty fifth place and kept the four in four places, because he had learned that a likeness laid on a likeness will grow a cause whether or not anyone put one there, and he had once signed a page that said a room had done a thing when a person had done it.

\person{Berr Clay} came to the block with the red cord of his office folded under his coat and the green of tasted glaze at one corner of his mouth, and wiped his thumb twice on his apron before he touched the slate. “One in twelve, as the notice says,” he read low into the top of the crowd, and passed a finger over the figure to make sure it was still where he had put it, and said the levy was called for the feast and was not called by him. He went around the uncalled debt against the firing of tiles as carefully as Sella went around Ash left in a corner, set the printed figure where everyone could see it, did not look into the basket, and went back to the pigs by dusk believing he had done the district a kindness. The boy heard the sentence take the speaker out of it the way a chart takes the hand out of a room, and had nothing to answer with. He had once let an institution call a failure of a man a failure of the hospital, and the grammar was the same one, and it went down as easily here as it had there.

The household's stores went into the last tend before \lore{the Five} in loaves and glances and absences --- three here, two there, one set a little apart with its brown edge turned up and come back to and never said --- and the Five were spoken of over the wash of the market as three days off by a district that had counted what it had and did not want to say it. A neighbour said three, a second corrected it to two, a third said whatever the moot had already decided to give out that morning, and the argument ended in the weather the way such arguments did, because weather let a man go home warm without owning a day of it. The figure went around in fists and mouths and came over the top of the basket, and he could not spend it, could not correct it, could not hand it back to anybody. He kept the one number none of them would give: one day after tonight was tomorrow. That was the whole of it, and nobody in the square knew he was keeping it, and the keeping changed nothing except that it was his.

At the fringe of the market, at \place{the Court's table at the Cross}, a collector's clerk with a cuff turned back twice from washing laid his hand on the top slate before he looked at the woman waiting, and said it was entered as it stood and there was nothing in it the household need correct. Slate Row was spoken of from that end of the square as a direction, not a place anybody meant to go that day. The woman went away with her fist shut. The upper line of the slate was in the strange neat dark hand, the one that set a name down before anybody looked at a face, and the small stop between two clusters of strokes came there a fifth time --- at the orange flare light, on the birth-slate, on the square's slate on Stone 10, at this table on Stone 20, now on this slate at the market's edge --- and lay beside the other four without joining them. There was no word in \lore{Grathic} for the small stop and no hand in the basket to make one; the grammar of \lore{the Return} took the woman out of her own business as neatly as it took the speaker out of Berr's sentence, and finished her without raising its voice. The Drawing collector's ordinary hand, the one with no pause in it at all, stayed where it was in its own place, filed apart as a hand that wrote a measure and did not stop, which was a different thing from a hand that stopped and did not finish. A pause was not a hearing and an interval was not a hearing either; the ear that held five hearings of the flat note from four sources had never added a sixth, and the five stops on the slates were not among them and never had been.

For one beat the light broke through without summons and without a name. Orange on wet snow. A hand in a grey sleeve writes and stops between two clusters, and the hiss of snow taking the heat goes up past the writing hand, and a hand goes into a coat pocket and finds wool where a small lit rectangle with a name lay, and he does not look at the name. Then it was past, and he was back in the post's thin shade with the cold iron at his cheek, the spent crust and the given crust in the fold, and the river wind coming under the wool. Nothing had crossed between the two places that he could carry back. He had a fifth sighting and no more than that: no power to decide with, no word to speak, no hand to point with, no standing to enter one stroke of the upper line. If the slate had been laid in the basket he could not have read it, and it was not laid there. If he had known the name in the grey sleeve he could not have said it, and he did not know it. If he had had a hand he would have had to decide what to do with the five pauses, and a decision was the one thing he had kept from making for twenty-four days, because four likenesses laid side by side are four likenesses and the joining is a man's work, and he had no standing for the work. The count changed. Nothing else did. The market went back to its pigs and its turnips and its figures left standing where nobody made them agree, and a barrow of grey went out through the north gate toward \place{the Bone Orchard}, heaped a little above the sides where the load had shifted, and nobody in the square looked twice at it, because it was the afternoon's work and the same work that went out every market-day.

At dusk she lifted the basket off the post and carried him up the yard with woodsmoke and river wind caught under the fold, and set the fold right at his ear without looking at his face, and set him on his bed under the roof and not in \place{the Debt Loft}, and banked the coals with the back of a broken pot. She did not touch the mark. A reading was not an opening, and the rule held at the bank as exactly as it had held before the lid, and the little-door burn shut on nothing all the way up the yard. \person{Tarn}'s small knife was back on its sill where it could be checked, and he had told the yard so once more, unasked, and six years of the seven were counted and the seventh held back. \person{Jorra Vail} had moved the damper and not said the back room was cold, and had not looked toward the bed; the grey dust stayed where it did not show on his forearms, and the bone-coloured slip went on drying to the colour of old bone. The house kept its shape around a man who was not in it: the coat on the peg, the hollow seat, the ridge turning its corner unswept, the proverb going to the fire and the pot, the uncalled debt lying where it lay, neither paid nor settled nor done with. The kiln took its breath under the roof. Four, and four, and a gap.

He lay with the two crusts in the fold at his side, one cold and one given, and the fifth pause lying with the four others and not joined to them, and the Drawing collector's hand with no pause staying in its own place, and the figure for the Five going around the district in fists and mouths, and one day after tonight was tomorrow. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth and came down on the fold, and the surgeon who had once reduced a shoulder in the dark could not lift a crust to his own mouth. The coals hissed once under the ash and held. The door stood a finger's width open, and the fire kept four, and four, and a gap. \lore{Kindling} was tomorrow.
