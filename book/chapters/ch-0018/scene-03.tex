\daymark{87}{Silt 28, Y.H. 813}

\person{Sella Vail} set the basket just inside the finger's-width-open door of \place{House Silt}, at the edge of the light and away from every place a slate might lie. She set it down as she would set a pot down, level, the whole weight of it committed to the floor before she let go, and then stood a half-pace off from it and gave the instruction to the wool and the wind and never to the basket: keep it off his ears, it was the river wind. The little-door burn on the back of her hand shut on nothing and stayed shut. She said the house where another woman would have said I, and the house had carried him three doors down at the edge of the work without once turning the errand into a walk to \place{Slate Row}, and had opened no slate to do it.

\person{Corin} was eighty-seven days old and had been carried everywhere since the day he was born, and the number sat in him with the exactness of a measure set down at night and not entered anywhere. From the low angle of the basket the room had the shape of a room he had been carried into before: a bench by the door, a pot on the bench, a draught going along the floor to the corner, and a place in the figures where a line should stand and does not. He had kept four such rooms in four separate places rather than write a cause where he had only noticed a likeness, and this one was a fifth, set beside the others and not among them. Above the abandoned board-bed the round pewter tin kept its pale fold at the seam and the thumb-bright rub on the lid, unopened, keeping what it kept. Below it the fold of grey wool held only the hollow where the small pale river stone had once lain, and nobody in the room said the stone was gone, and nobody said what the tin held. A chipped cup stood on the shelf by the wall, and a grey ridge of \lore{Ash} ran along the skirting from the bench under the door and into the next room, left where it lay until the Last Day. In the back room a neighbour scrubbed at something on a board with lye water and a cloth, the sound coming up flat and steady through the wall, a low song flattened out of the words by work. He understood every word of it. He could not yet make one.

One brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold against his ear and the empty place beside it had crossed another morning without acquiring a name; no third came. He wanted the crust, wanted the breast, wanted the chipped cup on the shelf two paces beyond his hand, and the three wants sat in him with the strict equality of three tools laid out on a tray. He could reach none. A thread of drool had frozen at the corner of his mouth in the small hours and no hand came to wipe it, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far. Cold arrived in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, the wool riding back from the wrist so the air found the bare place. The hand outside the wool went up an inch and came down. There had been a life in which reducing a shoulder was work done before breakfast and a man put his hand where he wanted it; here he could not lift a hand to his own chin, and the joke had never once been funny enough to say aloud. He had no \lore{Grathic} word with which to ask for the crust, and there was no person in the room who would have known what the number of his days counted if he had had one.

\scenebreak

\person{Alda Reed} came short into the room with her grey wool shawl drawn across her shoulders and the smell of soap-and-milk gone cold under river silt on her, lye sour under the soap, her nails scrubbed to the quick with clay still under them. She came as a woman comes who has been lifting other people's children since she was nineteen, taking the shortest way between a door and a thing that must be done. She went to the bench first. The birth-slates came down off her arm and lay writing-down on the bench before she turned to the fold, one after another, in the order of a hand that reaches for the record before it reaches for the person. Sella stood a half-pace off and asked nothing about the writing, and Alda asked nothing about why the boy was under this roof, and the arrangement between the two women held as it had held since he was three days old, a door neither of them opened.

Then she turned to the fold and pressed it flat at his shoulder, and pressed it a breath longer than a fold requires. He read the length of it from the inside as he had read the full note of a vessel from the outside: a hand that knows exactly how much pressure a thing will bear, and one breath more given after the pressure is right, a small exact tenderness that had no word in this room and no place to put itself except the wool. She crossed two fingers at the red edge of the birth-flower before she knew she had done it, and covered the sign with the date she had given the woman who lay more mornings dead than any living person kept count of, the seventh of Stone, the account up on the eighth, before the paper went out. The gesture and the date were one gesture. She did not say the woman's name. She did not say the number, and she did not say the third day.

“He lies quiet,” Sella said to the pot on the bench.

“Warm and breathing, that's all I've set down,” Alda said. It was exact and it was not true, and it had been exact and not true for three years, and there was nobody living who could take the date off the sign or correct the thing it stood over.

She went on with dates after there was no question left to answer. The cooper's seventh in Mud End. A potter's third child in Stonemouth, the last high water. Dates are a handle on a thing too large to carry, and she carried it by the handle, going on with the names of other people's children long after the room had stopped asking, while the neighbour's cloth went on moving flat against the board in the next room. In the life that was not this one a man could close a chart over a thing left lying, set his name at the foot of it and go home to his supper; here a woman covered a sign with a date and went on with other dates, and both of them would have called it the correct amount of words for a room in which somebody was in pain. The surgeon's reading worked perfectly and bought nothing, as it had bought nothing at the well and nothing below the sill. He lay with the date over him and the fold pressed too long at his shoulder and the little sound of water going over water coming up through the floor, and kept the two things on two places without letting them touch.

\scenebreak

Her hand went once to the little tin on its cord inside her bodice and came away without opening it. It was a small, economical movement, the hand going in and out as a hand goes to a tool it has decided not to use, and she came back to the dates without a pause in them, as if the hand had never moved. The tin stayed shut.

Her sixth narrow slate stayed turned face-down toward a floorboard in a house the boy could not name. Six pages, all unread, written in a second hand no Court physician had seen, the sixth set down on Silt 5 and turned down then and never written again. No seventh page was written in this room. The hidden record stood where it stood, carried and not paid, and nothing in the room had opened it and nothing would.

Two private records in two materials stood apart in one room. The pewter tin above the empty bed kept its pale fold and its thumb-bright rub, and the narrow slate lay face-down toward the floorboard of a house he could not enter, and neither was read by anybody and neither was carried to a door. He set them on separate places and wrote no cause across them. A likeness laid on a likeness will grow a cause whether a hand puts one there or not, and he had learned in a better-lit room what a man can make of two neat hands keeping two neat accounts of the same child; so he kept the tin and the slate apart as he kept the told bell and the heard bell apart, the two tellings of the shut counting room on their own tray joined to no kiln, six hearings of \lore{the full note} beside and never among, the three near-full answers advanced and not paid, and the word forty on a place of its own with the readable shape of the double turn beside it and the weight of a thumb on wicker apart from both. Four low things stayed in four places with the river's note of water going over water a fifth thing on a place of its own and not a hearing at all. The loan that could hear stayed uncalled. Below the sill a child's foot stayed the cold to one woman and weather to a roomful of people who had long ago gone home warm on the word, and the two facts went past each other again in this room and did not meet. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the chipped cup on the shelf and came down. He wanted the number of his days spoken by somebody who would know what it counted, and nobody spoke it.

\scenebreak

Alda gathered her birth-slates writing-down off the bench and held them under her arm the way she had carried them in, and went out short the way she had come in, giving the last of her dates to the door rather than to a person. The neighbour's cloth stopped moving for a breath in the back room and went on again. The pewter tin kept its fold above the bed, the wool below kept its hollow, and the narrow slate lay face-down where it had lain, and the room went on holding three things it did not read and had not been asked to read. Sella put the fold back at his ear without looking at his face and lifted the basket level.

She carried him up at the half-pace from every open slate, going around the space under the lowest rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and set him on his bed under \place{the Vail kiln-house} roof and not in \place{the Debt Loft}. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without looking at his face and left the thread at his mouth frozen and unwiped. She told the fire it was the thaw and fed it, and the fire answered with four low pulls and a silence where the fifth should have been, warming the boards by degrees under blind ash and the back of a broken pot; its payer had gone unnamed all this time and the coal the brine-gull took was entered in no slate and missed in none. She did not touch \lore{the widow's mark}, because a bed is not an opening and a basket is not a door. The clean dark line where the Ash ridge had turned lay where it had always lain, and the new hairline of grey, finer than the old, lay beside it unswept, and the boards overhead kept four nails and four and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts did not meet. Three doors away the tin and the turned slate stood shut and unread, and the hollow in the wool had crossed another day without a name put to it.

The door stood a finger's width open. Through the gap came the river's low muttered note from \place{the Ilm}, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, and through the floor came \lore{the kiln's breath}, four, four and a gap held under blind ash, the two sounds in two separate places as they had come for eighty-seven days. One brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold with the empty place beside it still unnamed, and no third came. Nothing had been joined, paid, explained or ended. Six hearings stayed six beside and never among, the three near-full answers stood advanced and not paid, the word was not spoken, and no collector came. The boy was eighty-seven days old and nobody had told him so. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the water and came down. The count crossed into Silt 29 unspent.
