\daymark{86}{Silt 27, Y.H. 813}

Before the light had decided what it wanted of the morning, \person{Tarn} set the small bench knife on its sill and told the empty yard where it was. He told the sill first and the yard after, because the sill would still be there when he had gone back up the ladder and the yard would not: “It’s on the sill. You can check.” Six years stood in a cut on the handle that his thumb had worn almost flat, so flat that a man had to know where to put his eye before he could find it; the seventh had never been cut at all and was nearly gone and still held. It was the one entry in this house the house could not check, and he kept it where no slate would reach it, in the space between a thumb and a breath. To the kindling he said, as he had said every morning of this last year, “Six counted. The next I don’t count.”

The kindling gave no answer and wanted none. It lay four and four and a space where a fifth should have been, the same old arithmetic the boards overhead kept with their nails and the fire kept under its ash, and Tarn went on holding the breath he had decided not to spend. \lore{Ash} was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line that ran along the Vail corner, and no scrubbing lifted it; he had scrubbed at it since Y.H. 806 and the ridge had only grown the more like it. The pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back, a small circle without a mark on it, the colour of a fingernail pressed flat. He had been bound in the year the clip went on and had counted every day since. A man who owns nothing should know where the small things are, and the small thing he owned was the exact account of what he owed, which the house kept badly and he kept exactly, and which the house could not check. There was a shape that came over him some nights and left him reaching for a tool he had never used; he did not name it, and the counting kept it down as a hand keeps a lid on a pot.

The boy came down into \place{the Clay Yard} at the edge of the work in a basket that was not his, carried level as a pot filled. \person{Corin} was eighty-six days old and had been carried everywhere since the day he was born, and the age sat in him with the exactness of a number set down at night and not entered anywhere. \person{Sella Vail} kept a half-pace from every open slate and set the basket at the edge of the light where no slate lay, and gave the instruction to the wool and the wind and never to the basket: keep it off his ears, it’s the river wind. One brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold against his ear and the empty place beside it had crossed another night without acquiring a name. No third came. He wanted the crust, wanted the breast and the cold iron of a bucket’s rim two inches beyond his hand, and the three wants sat in him together with the strict equality of three tools laid out on a tray. He could reach none. A thread of drool had frozen at the corner of his mouth in the small hours and no hand came to wipe it, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far; cold arrived in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, the wool riding back from the wrist so the wind found the bare place. The hand outside the wool went up an inch and came down. There had been a life in which reducing a shoulder was work done before breakfast and a man put his hand where he wanted it; here he could not lift a hand to his own chin, and the joke had never once been funny enough to say aloud.

\scenebreak

The yard argued the thaw as the light came, in the ordinary way: it had been coming for a month and would come early or late and nobody had owned a day of it. A woman at the tubs wanted it early and said the pits would be green by Tenthday; a man with a barrow wanted it late and said a late thaw was a longer week before the paying, and the paying had stood in the ruts since the water went out of them.

“Green by Tenthday, if it comes,” the woman said to the tubs.

“Late is better,” the man said to his barrow. “Late is longer.”

“The house wants the water down before it wants the pits green,” Sella said, and gave the rest of it to the broom: “Go around what’s left. It cost enough to come in.”

“Yes, house,” Tarn said to the woman.

“Yes, house,” he said to the man.

He agreed so entirely with both that the argument had nowhere to put its foot, and went on wanting the late one all the same, because a late thaw puts off the paying, though the paying had stood in the ruts all week and would stand there whether the pits came green or not. A man can hold two opinions as neatly as he holds two tools and use neither, and a chest held tight against a breath is legible in a worn thumb-place. He had been sent for a collector once, in \lore{the Long Cold}, and come back at the frozen ford and told the house the collector had been sent for; the house had not asked whether he had gone. He kept that sentence where he kept the seventh year, and neither was entered. Below the sill a child’s foot stayed the cold to the woman who had wrapped it twice in wool and pressed it between her palms, and weather to a yard that called the water weather; the two facts went past each other again this morning and did not meet. The boy read the extremity going before the core and the afterdrop waiting on the far side of a warming nobody would perform, and had no hand and no standing to put on the shin. The reading worked perfectly and bought nothing.

His broom went over the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector, over and over, without once turning along it. The road went down toward \place{the Fishstairs}, and a man went down it when a house wanted a figure taken from the living or the dead, and Tarn had gone down it once and come back, and came back no more. He swept the road and did not look along it, and no collector came down it this morning either. The boy, carried at the edge, kept the omission on a place of its own, as he kept the told bell and the heard bell on two places and the two tellings of the shut counting room on a tray where they lay side by side and never touched, the likeness and the difference kept and neither joined to the chimney across the yard, where \lore{the kiln's breath} stood held under blind ash, four low pulls and a gap. Six hearings of \lore{the full note} stayed six — five from four sources and the sixth out of a person who had been warm that morning — beside and never among; three near-full answers stood advanced and not paid; and the word forty was on a place of its own with the readable shape of the double turn beside it and the weight of a thumb on wicker apart from both. He had no hand to tap a pot and ask it to say again, and no \lore{Grathic} word with which to ask. The four low things stayed in four places and the river’s note of water going over water was a fifth thing on a place of its own and not a hearing at all, and the loan that could hear stayed uncalled.

\scenebreak

\person{Jorra Vail} came through the finger’s-width-open door of \place{the Vail wheel-shed} with fine grey slate dust worked into both forearms past the bend of his little finger, not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel. Men had come in since first light and spoken low in \place{the back room}, and he gave the house no name for them and the house asked for none. He had not said the room was cold. The yard went around the space under \place[the drying racks]{the lowest rack} as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and the broken birth-slates lay there still, gone around and entered by nobody.

He spoke to the wheel before he spoke to anyone. “It doesn’t care whose hands,” he said to it, in the flat voice that went to the wheel and the pots and only then to people. “It cares if they’re steady.” He had taken the wheel at thirteen when his father died and nobody came to draw him, and the seat was worn hollow on the left by twenty years of another man’s weight; he had spent three years trying to fill that hollow with his own. The little finger had bent from a splinter off the wheel when he was eleven and he told people it was a fight, because a fight sounded more like him. A pot turned under his hands and he told it without welcome and without unkindness, “You’ll do.” He knew the full note of a Vail pot before it left the wheel — knew it in the wrist and in the thin place under the shoulder and in the air that would be under the note when a knuckle found it — and he would not say so before a kiln-master from \place{High Kiln}, who would call it early and make something of it. The boy lay at the edge of the work and knew that he knew, and kept the knowing where questions can last for years.

Sella had not finished telling him the fire was low. “The fire’s low; the pot wants ---” and his bent finger went into the damper ahead of the sentence, cold iron taking the cold out of his fingertip, and moved it; so the end of her instruction arrived at the pot instead of at him and she shut her mouth on it. The household straightened around his silence the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted: Tarn’s broom found its stroke again, the fire took what it was given, and Sella carried the rest of the sentence to the pot and left it there. He had not answered her with a word and had not looked at her, which was how he obeyed her instantly. The boy read the damper as he would read any iron that stops a thing from running: the same bite that keeps a vessel whole can take the hand off a man, and there is no word in \lore{Grathic} that holds both mercies in one, so he held them in one place without a name and asked nobody to call it either.

He did not say the back room was cold and did not look toward the bed or into the basket. \emph{He’s not Vail} stayed sealed as tightly as a lid nobody had tried to lift in three years, and the promise of the foundling’s name to the men who burn slates was neither given nor withdrawn. He wanted the wheel to stay in the house and the boy outside the name, and had said neither, and the two wants stood in him as the two opinions stood in Tarn, each kept whole by never being spent. The boy kept dusty forearms and clean ones on one tray as two states of one man’s mornings and asked neither which slate was broken nor whether it was a birth-slate, because a question spoken would make an object of an answer and there was no answer here to carry.

Past the door a vessel gave its note: a pot set down on a bench somewhere out of sight, the small flat knock of a whole vessel or the fuller note of one with air under it. He did not turn toward it to find which. Tarn’s head did not turn and he did not name the sound or look toward the basket. The boy did not call it a seventh hearing, because a note that is not turned toward has entered nobody’s keeping, and six stayed six beside and never among. Two ears kept their equality under one roof and two silences kept theirs, of one shape with nobody putting a word between them, and the loan that could hear stayed uncalled. A loan that can hear is a loan that can be called, and the sentence that would call it lay under the roof as it had lain for six years, under the small knife on the sill and the seventh year held where the house could not check it. Something must give at the end of the seventh year, and it had not given here.

\scenebreak

At the going of the light Tarn set the knife back on its sill where it could be checked and told the yard so, unasked. He had carried it in his pocket through the day and brought it back; a man who owns nothing should know where the small things are. The seventh year was still where he held it, nearly gone and not spent, and he went on holding the breath he had decided not to spend, though his chest had been tight since the dark. Sella carried the boy up at the half-pace from every open slate, going around the space under the lowest rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and set him on his bed under \place{the Vail kiln-house} roof and not in \place{the Debt Loft}. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without looking at his face and left the thread at his mouth frozen and unwiped. She told the fire it was the thaw and did not touch \lore{the widow's mark}, because a day’s end under a roof is not an opening and a basket is not a door. The clean dark line lay where it had always lain and the new finer grey lay beside it unswept, and the boards overhead kept four nails and four and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts did not meet. The payer of the coal had gone unnamed; the carried fire kept its four, four and a gap under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees and missing one breath in every four, and its sound came through the floor in its own place while the river’s low muttered note from \place{the Ilm} came through the finger’s-width-open door in another, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, as they had come for eighty-six days in two places that did not touch.

One brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold with the empty place beside it still unnamed. Nothing had been joined, paid or explained. The two tellings lay on their tray and the six hearings stayed six beside them and never among, the three near-full answers stood advanced and not paid, the word was not spoken, and no collector came. The boy was eighty-six days old and nobody had told him so. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the water and came down. The count crossed into Silt 28 unspent.
