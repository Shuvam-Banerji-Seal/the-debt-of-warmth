The afternoon came up off the river and went into the yards ahead of them, so that by the time \person{Sella Vail} carried \person{Corin} in from \place{the Fishstairs} the light had gone thin and the cold had taken on the edge it gets when the sun has lain on water all day and is done with it. She carried him up \place{the Clay Yard} the way she had carried him down, like a pot filled that must not be slopped, her knee turning him at the turn of the path and the fold of grey wool gathered in under his chin so that the wind met the wool and not the ear. She did not look into the basket. A pot carried is not looked into; a pot set down is a pot set down, and the thing to be looked at first when it is set down is the fire. Under the roof she set him on his bed, and not in \place{the Debt Loft}, and put the fold right at his ear with a hand that never once went near his face. The burn shaped like a little door closed on the wool's edge and opened again on nothing. “Keep it off him,” she said to the wool. “It's the river wind.” She gave the instruction to the fire and the pot and the wool and never to the basket, and she said the house where another woman would have said I, and the boy, who understood every word, understood that he was not the one being spoken to.

He lay where she had put him and watched the room come back to itself around him the way a room comes back after an operation, by degrees and in the order of its instruments. The bed was under the roof and the loft was above the shed, and the boy had the bed and \person{Tarn} had the loft, and that was the household's arithmetic before any of its figures were gone into. Nothing about it had ever been said in his hearing. He had once stood in a supply room at four in the morning and read what was left on a shelf by the shape of the gaps, and he read this room now with the same part of him: the count first, the meaning afterward, if it ever came.

Sella went around the month's days the way she went around every number she would not speak. She moved a loaf along the shelf, moved it back, set another with its brown edge turned up, and glanced at the clay jar on the wall shelf that she had brought three doors down from \place{House Silt} and had never once explained. Her lips went along the shelf without making a figure. She came back to the same place twice, the way she always came back, and set the loaf where it had stood before, so that the days that remained to \lore{Kindling} stood as a shape no collector could take up and carry away. Nobody said the stores were thinner than they had been. The house kept its number in loaves and glances and the space where a loaf should have stood, and the boy kept the count exactly beside her bad keeping, four and four and the shape of the days, without ever once letting the two counts touch or speaking a figure between them. He had learned long ago that a number kept badly was still a number kept, and that a number kept exactly and never entered was worth no more than one counted on the fingers of a woman who would not say what she was counting.

In the fold both crusts still lay where they had lain through a death and a blessing and seven days of thaw that everybody called weather. The spent one, brown-edged and stiff since Stone 15, was gone past smelling of anything. The smoky one that \person{Mira Silt} had put there on Stone 28 gave its thin breath of barley smoke when the air moved off the yard, and the breath came and went, and the body wanted it with the whole appetite of a day carried from before light and fed on nothing since. Neither had been eaten. Neither had been lifted. Neither had been named. No third had come into the fold. He could smell the barley and he could not put out a hand to it, and the two paces between the warmth and the hand were as fixed as they had been that morning under the bridge, and as they would be tomorrow. A kindness that cannot be answered cannot be refused. He had learned that in bread, and the learning had given him nothing he could spend.

Before she opened the kiln she put her hand to \lore{the widow's mark} at her throat, the thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and held it for one breath, and let go. It was a rule that held as exactly as the rule about the loaves. She touched it before an opening and at no other hour, not for a pot, not for a date, not for a nearly full sound, and at the dusk bank she would not touch it at all, because a bank is not an opening. The boy watched the hand go up and the hand go down and kept the two gestures in two places, as he kept all the house's gestures, because a rule and the reason for a rule were two objects and he had learned to keep such objects apart.

Behind him the carried fire lay under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, giving its warmth to the boards by degrees. It took its breath in fours and missed one. Four, four and a gap, and the gap was the part nobody had thought to enter. The coal that had gone up the river that morning with the brine-gull was entered nowhere and missed in no account. The fire did not miss it. The house did not miss it. One warmth less had gone out of the world since dawn, and no slate in \place{Slate Row} stood one mark the poorer for it, and the boy lying on his bed under the roof was the only thing in the district that kept the subtraction.

Tarn had the small bench knife back on its sill before the light was fully gone and told the empty yard where it was, unasked, in the tone another man would use to state the weather. “It's on the sill,” he said. “You can check.” He looked at the kindling stack without touching it, four bundles along the near row and four behind and a space at the end where a fifth should have been, and took his eyes off the space as a man takes his eyes off a thing he has no leave to look at. Six years of the seven were behind him and he had counted every one. The seventh he held back, the one entry in this house that the house could not check. Grey \lore[the Ash]{Ash} was worked into the creases of his knuckles the colour of the ridge that had turned the corner and been burned and called dealt with, and no scrubbing lifted it; the pale clip-ring at his left wrist caught what light there was and held it for a moment and gave it back. He called everyone house, including Sella, and said I owe where another man would say I am, and he agreed with everything so thoroughly that an argument had nowhere left to put its foot.

His broom went over the road that runs down toward the Fishstairs, over and over, without once turning to look along it. That is the road a man goes down when a house wants a collector. No collector went down it that afternoon, and nobody in the house sent one, and Tarn did not go. He swept where he had swept, and swept again, and when Sella told him he had done well he stopped altogether for two breaths before he said “Yes, house” and went on, because a kindness he could not enter in any account was a kindness he could not pay back, and he had stopped trying to reconcile the two. He wanted the thaw late. He agreed with the people who wanted it early and with the people who wanted it late and kept wanting the late one all the same, because a late thaw puts off the paying. A loan that can hear is a loan that can be called. The sentence had never been said under this roof, and lay under it all the same, and the boy on his bed kept Tarn's silence beside his own and did not let the two touch, because two silences under one roof are nearer an equality than anything the house had a word for. A loan that can hear is a loan that can be called, and there was a man under this roof worth more than the debt that held him, and nobody had found the figure to enter it with.

\person{Jorra Vail} came in from \place{the Vail wheel-shed} with slip dried to the colour of old bone up both forearms and no grey worked into the creases past the bend of his little finger. The bent finger went in ahead of his hand at the damper. Sella had begun to tell him the fire was low; he moved the damper before she had finished and left the end of her sentence where it was, and she shut her mouth on it and gave the rest to the pot. He spoke to the pot and not to the person on the bed. “You'll do,” he said, in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either. He told the wheel what he had told it a hundred times, that it did not care whose hands were on it, only if they were steady, and the saying had worn smooth as the seat worn hollow on the left by twenty years of another man's leaning. Of the room off the shed he said nothing, and the door of it kept its finger's width of open, and he did not look toward the bed. The boy knew something about Jorra that Jorra would not have said before a kiln-master: that \lore{the full note} was in a Vail pot before it left the wheel, and that to say so in front of a man who would call it early was to have something made of it. He kept the knowing where questions can last for years, as he kept the bent finger and the clean forearms and the absence of dust on them, writing no cause over any of it.

Behind the door \person{Garl Vail}'s coat kept the shape of a broader man on its peg, salt-stiff and never taken down, and the wheel-seat in the shed kept the leftward lean of twenty years that nobody was allowed to fill. Where the grey ridge had turned its corner for a year and two days there was now a clean dark line, and it was not swept, because a line left where it is is still a line kept. His proverbs went to the fire and the pot and never to the basket: feed the fire before you feed yourself, and leave the crack showing; people trust a thing they can see has been handled. The second had gone on being said to the pot after the crack was gone and the ridge was a clean dark line, and nobody in the house had found anything strange in that. The charred edge of a slate lay in the kiln ash, black on one side and eaten into the shape of a strip of bark, swept around for a year and never picked up. The house kept a roll of its own in a careful hand and did not show it, and kept a dead man's place in the shape of a coat and a hollow and a line, and kept a foundling under its roof without a name written against him, and entered none of the three where a man from Slate Row could read it.

The light went off the yard and the evening came on in the order it always came, at the edges of the room first and the middle last, and the fire behind him took its breath in fours. The boy lay on his bed under the Vail roof, forty-five days old, with both crusts still in the fold and no third; the smoky one gave its thin breath and the spent one lay stiff past smelling, and neither was lifted or eaten or named. Behind him the carried fire kept under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees and missing one breath in every four, four and four and a gap. The coal that had gone up with the gull that morning was entered in no account and missed in none. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth that was not within two paces of him and came down. Nothing had been joined, paid or explained. The count went on.
