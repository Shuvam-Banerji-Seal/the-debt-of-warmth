\daymark{45}{Ilmen 16, Y.H. 813 (Sixthday)}

\dropcap{H}{e came} down the river road in the first thin light, carried the way a pot is carried when it is full and must not be slopped, with \person{Sella Vail}'s knee turning him until the fold lay right under his chin and the wool took the wind on its own broad back instead of on his ear. Under \place{the north bridge} the shadow was a room without walls, floored in wet stone and smelling of the cold that came up off \place{the Ilm}; she set him where a pot would be set, out of the draught that ran along the boards, and did not set him where the trade would slop him. Below them \place{the Fishstairs} were awake already, the boards slick with slime and salt, the eel-baskets breathing their cold fish smell into the shade. \person{Corin}, forty-five days old, could not have kept the fold right for himself and could not have said where he was. He lay at the low angle at which a basket sees the world, with the bridge's underside going out above him on its dark beams, and the cold came in its known order, cheek, then temple, then the small wrist outside the wool, and the wool did less than the wind wanted. It was the sixteenth of Ilmen. The blessing was yesterday, and the fish were being sold this morning, and work had come back into the place that had put on its one clean day and taken it off again before noon.

A brine-gull went off upriver with a coal in its beak that was not yet cold. It had taken the coal from somebody's banked fire, from a heap where a house had raked its coals and left them under ash, and it carried the thing low and quick across the water, a dark bird with a red heart in its mouth, and the warmth went up the river with it. The boy watched it go because watching was what was left to him. He knew a taking when he saw one and he knew a payer when he saw one, and here was a warmth leaving the carried fire's account with no slate entered and no hand put out to stop it. Somebody had paid for that coal at Kindling and somebody had banked it and somebody had gone home warm the night before, and now a bird had it and the bird would drop it on a bank or into the water and the Cold would take it, and no account in \place{Slate Row} would show one mark less for the loss. He wrote nothing over it. He had learned early in the other life that a doctrine laid on top of a fact is a way of not looking at the fact, and here there was no fact but a bird and a coal and a warmth going off upriver, which was plain enough without a name. The gull went round the bend and was gone. The morning went on.

At the head of the steps stood the clerk who had been set at the Fishstairs for a measure nobody had called. He had a soft cuff gone grey from washing and a slate held against his forearm, and when a woman came down with a basket of eels he put his hand to the slate before he put his eyes to the stalls. The order of it was the whole of the thing: the hand first, the person after, the writing before the face. The boy had seen that order in a midwife bending over a birth, in a neighbour at a well, in a man who priced him across a market, in Berr Clay passing a finger over a figure, in the soft-cuffed man at the Court's table at \place{the Cross}, and in Alda Reed going to her writing before she went to the child. He filed this clerk on a tray of his own. He was not the woman in the grey coat who had written at the edge of the flare light and paid with her fingers; he was not the collector with the grey fingertips at the Drawing whose hand had no pause in it; he was not even the soft-cuffed man at the Court's table, though he had the same cuff and the same way of holding a slate. He was an ordinary hand set down at the steps for an uncalled measure, and the boy kept him apart from the other hands the way he kept the told bell apart from the heard bell, because a likeness was a likeness and nothing more had been given. No sixth sighting of the neat dark hand came that morning, and the five pauses lay where they had been laid and did not join.

\person{Orra Weir} came down from \place{the Weir} with eels, and the boy knew she had come before he saw her, the way everybody at the Cross knew it: silt and cold fish and tallow smoke moving together, and under that the wet wool smell of a woman who had stood too long on a weir. The grey streak showed first through her black hair. The pale arcs of eel scars lay across both forearms and she had covered neither, and her hands were like old rope that had been wet and dried and wet again for thirty years. She set her basket down where a basket would not be slopped and worked among the eels with the turn of a wrist that had done it ten thousand times, and the \lore{knot-string} at her waist, grey wool gone the colour of river silt where her thumb had worried it, moved with her breathing.

Yesterday, tied low into the cord, there had been a knot she could not account for. The boy could not read \lore{Grathic} in speech or on a slate, and he had no word of his own for the thing she had put into the wool, but this morning, lying in the shade with nothing between him and the cord but the length of a basket, he could read the shape of it. That had changed since yesterday. A double turn with the tail tucked under, tied left over right, the small extra weight of a thumb setting it down where it had not been before: he had held the shape in his head through the night and it had come out in the morning light still whole, like an instrument he had once been able to name and now could only describe to himself. It was the double turn that told him the hand had been a right-handed woman looking at something else while she worked, and it was the thumb that told him the knot had been given a place it did not have before. He had neither the name of the knot nor any word to spend it on, and he kept the shape on its own tray with the saying beside it, not on top of it, because a word and the thing it names are two objects and he had learned to keep such objects apart. \lore{forty} was still a saying and not a vessel's sound; it lay where it had lain since yesterday, low and watched and unanswered, and he did not say it again and nobody said it near him.

Orra worked the eels and watched him watching her. That was the whole of it. She did not say the word a second time and she did not ask what he had made of it, and when he turned his head a degree too far and the small wet sound came out of him, she let her eyes rest on the cord at her waist and then go back to her hands. Once she tied a knot and pulled it down with her thumb and set it among the others, and the boy noted the weight of the thumb and did not count the knots, because counting was an old habit and not a gift, and there was nothing in the count he could have entered anywhere if he had counted. He knew a vigil when he saw one. He had kept a bedside watch through worse nights than this in a country whose words he had not brought with him, and he recognised the particular attention of a person who has set a thing down in front of somebody and is waiting to see whether it will be picked up. He could not pick it up. He had no hand that would reach a league upriver to the hut where the cord hung, and no word to say across the space between a baby's basket and a woman's rope-hand, and the watching went on all the same, patient and half-sung, the way the Fen people did everything, with the story told sideways and the answer put where a date should stand. The cord went back into the hut at the end of the morning where no clerk could spell it and no boy could read it, and nothing was said of it between them.

\person{Mira Silt} worked the eels at the far end of the racks with pale slime dried on her sleeves and silt in the hollow of her neck, her head held a little to the right where the top of her left ear had been. A weir woman asked her whether the run would come with the blessing gone and done, and Mira answered the question and then her own answer. “Does it come now the stone's blessed? It comes when it comes. Ask the river.” She flicked an eel-bone against her thumb and did not yet put it away, because the deciding was not done. When a buyer said a figure for the morning's catch her thumb went under her apron to the grey cord at her waist and stayed there, and shifting the weight of her basket showed the second cord twisted twice beneath it, the one that had never been carried upriver to \place{Nine Pots}, never lifted at a door, with the knots on neither of them counted. She put the eel-bone away when the deciding was done and brought out another, and her small smile stayed on her face the way another woman keeps a door on the latch, able to be shut without anybody noticing the movement. The boy set her two grey cords on a board and Orra's knot-string on a board beside it, and would not let the boards touch. Two women kept the district's private account in a material no officer would think to read, and neither had said what she knew, and the boy had no standing to ask either of them. He watched the hand go to the first cord and never to the second, and kept the watching as carefully as the other keepings, writing no cause over it.

The ear held \lore{the full note} in six hearings and the six stayed six. Five came from four sources: a cup across the birth room, a pot in the Vail wheel-shed heard through the wall, the district's slate twice at the Cross, and a man giving at the Drawing. The sixth had come six mornings back out of a woman lifted wet and heavy from the clay-heavy brook, a small flat knock out of the body as the yard's hands went under her, and it lay beside the five without being among them, from a person, not a seventh and never to be a seventh. No new hearing came this morning under the bridge; the eels slapped the boards and the gulls went over and the clerk's slate lay open at the steps and gave nothing. The nearly full answer of Ilmen 6 lay on its own tray, advanced and not paid, a third sound that was not a hearing and not the thing it answered, and the boy did not let it go near the six. The told bell was still beside the heard bell and never on it; the five pauses did not join; the absence with the outline of a collection, the shape of a thumb on wicker, the word and the knot and the weight of the thumb that set it all stayed each in its own place. He had no Grathic word for the flat note and no Grathic word for nearly, and he had stopped a long time ago expecting the district to give him one. Keeping the trays apart was the whole of his work in the world, and it had never once changed a thing.

In the fold, both crusts still lay where they had lain through a death and a blessing and seven days of thaw that everyone called weather. The spent one, brown-edged and stiff since Stone 15, was gone past smelling of anything. The smoky one that Mira had put there on Stone 28 still gave a thin breath of barley smoke when the wind turned off the water, and the breath came and went with the turning of the wind, and the body wanted it with the whole appetite of a day carried from before light and fed on nothing. Neither had been eaten, neither had been lifted, neither had been named, and no third had come into the fold. A kindness that cannot be answered cannot be refused, which he had learned in bread, and the learning had cost him nothing and given him nothing he could spend. He could smell the barley smoke and he could not put out a hand to it. There was the warmth, and there was the hand, and the two paces between them were as fixed as the space between the basket and the cord upriver, and he had long ago stopped being surprised by the distance.

The body humiliated him exactly, as it had done every morning of his forty-five days. The thread of drool at his mouth froze where the draught found it and nobody wiped it away, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound it made when it went too far, and Sella did not put her hand to it because she was talking to the eel-woman about the weather. Cold came at cheek and then temple in the order it always took, and he could not alter one step of it. The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth that was not within two paces of him and came down. It had gone up an inch every morning since the first morning and it had never once reached anything. He was a surgeon who could not lift a hand to his own chin and the only adult in every room he was carried into, and the fact was not funny and not tragic, only exact; the joke of it, if there was one, cost somebody else and not him, and he had never been able to find who.

Sella turned the basket so the sun was off his face, and did it with her knee while she was looking at the eel-stalls, so that the moving of him was not a thing that had been done to him but a thing the light had done. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. “It's the river wind.” She said the house where another woman would have said I, and gave the instruction to the wool and never to the basket, and stood a half-pace off a slate lying open on a stall and did not set him down where the writing might lie across him. Her little-door burn closed on the basket's wicker and opened again, and she went on bargaining for eels as if she had not moved him at all, which was the care she used and the care she would never name. The boy watched her standing there with her hands never still, counting something on her fingers that she would not have called a count, and he kept her number beside him the way he kept all the household's numbers, badly kept and exactly held, with no figure spoken between them.

The gull was gone upriver with the coal and the warmth out of the carried fire's account, and the boy lay in the bridge's shade with the fold at his chin. On its own tray the shape of the double turn lay beside a word that had not been said again, and the weight of a thumb lay apart from both, and the two cords of the two women stood on separate boards and did not touch. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth that was not within two paces of him and came down. The eels went on changing hands at the stalls. The clerk's cuff went grey in the morning light and his hand kept going to the slate first. Nothing had been joined, paid or explained. The count went on.
