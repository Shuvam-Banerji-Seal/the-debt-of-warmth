\daymark{47}{Ilmen 18, Y.H. 813 (Eighthday)}

The frost came on the stone lip of \place{the yard well} in the hour before the smoke cleared of the morning fires, and it was not the ice that lay on the trough or the rime that grew on the north side of the wall. It stood up in feathers. It grew off the stone in small white fronds, each one finer than the one beside it, the way a mould stands up off a loaf left too long in a damp corner --- only white, and clean, and drinking the first of the light before the sun got over the roofs. \person{Corin}, forty-seven days old, came up from \place{the Clay Yard} in the basket under the fold and was set in the well's shade where a pot would be set, out of the draught that ran off the water. The frost was at the level of a man's knee above him. He could see the fronds from the low angle at which a basket sees the world, and he could no more touch them than he could touch the sun coming up behind the wash-tubs. He had known frost since the first week of his new life. He had never seen it write on a well-lip in Ilmen before.

Where \lore{the Warm} had lately gone out of the stone, the cold wrote its own small hand. That was the whole of the thing. He set the writing on a tray by itself, as he set all the things nobody had entered, and put no cause across it. He knew the difference between a frost that came because the night had been cold and a frost that came because something warm had gone out of a thing and left the shape of its going. Water came up under the sill from somewhere below the town and carried a little heat with it even in the deep of Stone; a hand could feel it through the side of a drawn bucket in the small hours if the hand got there before the bucket stood and cooled. That heat had gone out of the lip in the night and the frost had come in where it had been, tracing the stone in white the way an officer traces a margin on a slate, fine and patient and without a word. By noon the sun would take it off and leave the stone bare, and no slate in \place{Slate Row} would show a mark less for the going, and nobody in the yard would call the writing anything but weather.

A woman with her sleeves rolled came and let the bucket down, and the rope went over the lip and knocked the heads off three fronds and sent them into the water. “It'll go soft before its time,” she said, and looked at the white as if the stone had grown a tooth she had not been told about. A woman at the tubs said no, it would hold; the nights were cold enough yet; there was frost on the turnip grounds and frost would keep. The first woman said the water had been tasting of the Warm all week, had nobody noticed, and the second said a body could taste anything in a thaw and it was only the weather come early, and at that the subject was finished. They went back to their buckets. It had not been an argument anyone meant to win, because there was nothing in it anyone meant to own; it was a way of saying the morning out loud and setting it down again where it had been found. The boy kept the frost on its own tray and did not lay the words on top of it, because a thing said and a thing seen were two objects and he had learned, in a room with better light than this, what happens to a man who lays three likenesses in a row and asks them to stand for a cause.

Cold came at him in its known order --- cheek, then temple, then the small wrist outside the wool --- and the wool did less than the wind wanted. A thread of drool at the corner of his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had wiped it, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went past the point, and the women at the tubs did not look up. He was the only adult in the yard and the least use in it. The woman who had said the ice would go soft worked the bucket up with her arm and set it on the frosty lip for a breath before she carried it away, and the heat that had been in the water a moment ago went off with the bucket and into a house, and was entered nowhere.

Before the smoke was off the yard a figure came up from the road below in a man's fist. It went the way such figures go in \place{Low Kiln}: out of the fist into the mouth of a man at the racks, out of that mouth onto the shoulder of a woman shifting slip, off the shoulder into the next fist, and there it stopped, because no one in the chain had been handed the figure to keep. At the end of it somebody asked a question and the shoulder lifted and the man with the fist said nothing at all. The boy watched a number pass the height of the basket, go over the fold, spend itself in the air and arrive nowhere, and he kept the shape of its going, which was the whole of what was ever on offer. He had seen the same shape at the Cross in a crowd of two hundred and at a market's edge where eels changed hands six for five, and neither time had anybody given him the number to hold.

\person{Berr Clay} came up the yard behind it with the red wool cord of his office folded under his coat and green of tasted glaze at one corner of his mouth. There was hot clay on him under the smell of nothing at all, which is the dearest smell in \place{Grath}, and the women at the tubs made room for him the way people make room for a slate that has been left out in the rain. He wiped his thumb twice on his apron before he touched anything, and what he touched was nothing --- the thumb went through the air twice and came to rest against his own narrow slate, which he held unopened against his forearm. The boy knew the small ceremony. It was a man making sure the record would be clean before he let a figure come near it. Berr passed a finger over what was written and let his eyes stay on it a breath longer than the writing required, as if to check that the figure had not moved while he was looking away.

“A number goes into a slate, not into a well,” he said, in the low voice that made the women lean in. “The house wants it again, there'll be a slate at the watch.”

He did not say I. He said the number and the slate and the house, and took himself out of his own sentence as neatly as a surgeon takes a hand out of a field before the dressing goes on; and the boy, who had once held a retractor for a man who worked that way, kept the twice-wiped thumb on the tray of record-first hands and did not call it the same hand as the midwife's, or a neighbour's at this same well on an earlier morning, or the clerk's behind the Court table, or the man who had priced him across a market, or \person{Alda Reed} laying her birth-slates writing-down before she turned to a child. Five hands and a sixth on six separate places on one tray, and no tray touching another. Berr looked nowhere near the basket. He went off up toward \place{High Kiln} with the box in his own house still locked and the true deduction still inside it, and the figure he had carried in his fist went with him into a slate and stayed there, and the yard went back to its buckets.

\scenebreak

Word came up the yard with the neighbours that the clerk was still set at \place{the Fishstairs} with his slate and his soft cuff for the measure nobody had called. He had been there since the morning after the blessing and there was no sign of his being taken off. He put his hand to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls; he had done it on the first morning and he was doing it still, and the eels went on being sold over the top of him. The boy filed the order on a tray of its own. It was not the woman in the grey coat writing at the edge of the flare light with a pause in her hand that had cost her fingers, and it was not the collector at the Drawing whose hand went to the vessel before it went to the face and had no pause in it at all, and it was not the soft-cuffed man at the Court's table at \place{the Cross}, though the cuff at the steps was the same grey from the same washing. An ordinary hand, set down to write an uncalled measure, was still an ordinary hand on the seventh morning. No sixth sighting of the neat dark hand came with the word, and the five pauses lay where they had been laid and did not join.

He filed something else beside them, and he filed it as an absence. No collector had been called for the woman the brook took nine mornings ago. There had been no draw at her bed, no soft sentence over a slate, no iron tube under her arm, no hand stopping short of a pulse and then going away from it without saying what it had found. The yard had lifted her out and the yard had gone back to its work, and warmth left where it fell and the morning came. He kept the missing collection the way he kept the interval of a pot turned toward and giving nothing: as a thing with the outline of a sound that had not come, filed beside the intervals and never among them. It was not a hearing of \lore{the full note}; it had no vessel and no note and no hour. It was the shape a collection would have had if anybody had thought to call one, and the six hearings stayed six, five from four sources and the sixth from a person, beside them and never among them, and no seventh came into the yard that morning.

\scenebreak

A weir woman came up from the river road with salt white at her wrists and at the cuffs of her sleeves, the salt of a morning spent over the eel racks, and she brought a piece of news the way such news always comes in Low Kiln, as something said to the boards and not to a person. “One board in the walk won't carry a man,” she said. “Not when the river comes up. They'll step on it and they'll find out.”

The walk she meant was \place{the plank walk} at \place{the Weir}, the raised boards that follow the north bank out to the traps and stand over the water on posts, the only dry way to the eels when \place{the Ilm} comes up. She had not said which board. That was the manner of the place: a weakness was spoken of in the third person, and the board itself was left where it was with nobody's weight on it, and the man who stepped over it in the days to come would step over it again without ever knowing what he had stepped over. The boy had seen the boards from the height at which a basket sees the world and had kept one of them as a gap in a sixth room, because the eye had gone to it and then away and he could not make his hand go where his eye had been. He set the news beside the missing step at the Fishstairs --- four steps down and a landing that was not a step --- and beside the hairline in the river ice that ran a while and stopped short of the edge, and he kept the three in three places and did not let them touch. A board that will not bear a man and a breath a fire does not take are the same shape and not the same thing, and he had learned the cost of making a cause out of a shape in a room where the light was better and the stakes were a life. Here there was no light and no leave and no hand, only the shape, which he kept.

The river was beginning to stir. That was what the men come up from the lower road said, though they said it sideways and finished with the weather. Under its skin of ice the Ilm ran a little higher than it had run a tend ago, and the dark veins of the current showed through the thinning skin in a pattern the eye wanted to read as a map and could not. The boy had put the rise in its own place three mornings back and had not moved it since. He added nothing to it today. A thing on its way to breaking is not yet broken, and a man who calls the break before it comes has only made a doctrine out of a board, which is the kind of thing that gets a listener believed and a vessel left unhandled. He kept the board heard-of and not trodden. He did not make it into an accident. Nobody had asked him to, and nobody could have, because he was forty-seven days old and lay in the shade with the fold at his chin and the frost writing above him on the lip of the well.

\scenebreak

Behind \place{the drying racks} \place{the Slip Brook} ran clay-heavy a little higher than it should, the colour of a pot washed into a bucket and left to settle. It came down off the pits with the morning's slip in it and passed along the hedge where the woman had gone into the water, and the yard, which had looked at the place every day since, had stopped looking at it now. The water made a small sound against the stones that a man would not have heard over the buckets, but the boy, who lay at the height where such small sounds carry, heard it under everything: under the rope over the lip, under the women at the tubs, under the talk of the ice and the number and the board. Under all of it, fainter than any of it, came the low note of \place{the Singing Sill}, the fall below the bridges giving its early note through the boards and the water before its time. The note went in under the skin of the morning and stayed there. Nobody at the well said what it was. A woman shifted a basket and said the brook was up with the clay and it would go down again, and that was an end of that subject too.

He kept four shapes and he kept them apart. The honeyed ice of the first days of Ilmen lay in one place, the word for it still going around the yard by mouth and never written. The hairline in the ice that ran and stopped short of the edge lay in another. The higher clay-heavy brook lay in a third. This frost-bloom on the well-lip, white and feathered and already losing its heads to the rope, lay in a fourth. He did not let the four places touch. They were four likenesses of a vessel handing its work to the next thing until the skin held more than it was made to hold, and they were four likenesses and not one cause, because a cause is a thing a man can put his hand to and he had no hand and no word in \lore{Grathic} for the failure and no standing to ask for either. The district's tongue had a word for honeyed ice and a word for a thaw and a word for the weather, and the yard used all three over the top of them and went on warm. He wrote no cause across any of them. The count went on in the only place it could, which was inside him, and it bought him nothing he could spend.

\person{Sella Vail} came back across the yard with the basket at her hip and moved him through the groups the way she moved a pot through a room where things are being carried --- at a half-pace from every open slate, never between a hand and the writing it was going to, never where a figure lay with its face up. There was a slate propped open against a crate at the rack end, and she went around it wide enough that the basket's shadow did not touch the corner of it. When a neighbour began to ask where the figure had gone that had passed from fist to mouth to shoulder, Sella's little-door burn shut on the empty air where a cup would have been and she said the house had sent it up the road, and the neighbour said well, and nothing more came of it. She gave the instruction to the wool and the bucket and never to the basket. “Keep it off him,” she said to the wool, with the wind coming off the water. To the bucket, when she set it down: “That'll do.” She said nothing about where the number had gone or who had paid for the warmth that came up in the water and went off in a bucket into a house that was not anybody's to enter.

The morning went on. The sun got over the roofs and took the frost off the lip, frond by frond, until the stone was bare and looked as if nothing had ever been written on it. The women went back to the tubs, the rack-women went back to the slip, the man who had carried the figure in his fist came back down the road with his hands empty and said nothing about what he had held. \person{Tarn}, down at the shed end with a broom, went once over the road that a man takes when a house wants a collector, and went over it again without once turning his head to look along it, and no collector came down it, and he did not say that the thing which had not happened had a name. Six years of the seven were behind him and he would not count the seventh. \lore[the Ash]{Ash} was worked into the creases of his knuckles that no scrubbing lifted, the pale clip-ring at his left wrist caught what light there was and gave it back. He agreed with the ones who wanted the thaw early and with the ones who wanted it late and went on wanting the late one, because a late thaw puts off the paying. He said nothing about the board, and the boy kept his silence beside his own and did not let the two touch.

By midday the shade had moved a hand's breadth off the well and Sella turned the basket so the sun was off his face, and the boy lay there with the fold at his chin and the stone lip above him, bare now, with the writing gone off it and nobody owning the day. The number had gone past him from fist to mouth to shoulder and had been given to nobody. The clerk was still at the Fishstairs with a slate for a measure nobody had called. The weak board was still at the Weir, heard of and not trodden. The woman in the brook was still uncollected and the omission still had no word. The four shapes lay in four places and did not touch, and the sixth hearing stayed where it was, and the fire under the roof behind him kept four, four and a gap, missing one breath in every four. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the bucket on the lip and came down. Nothing had been joined, paid or explained. The count went on.
