The afternoon took \place{the Cross} apart in the order it had been put together. First the pigs went, and the smell of them went with them, thinning until the eel smell came up from \place{the Fishstairs} and lay over the barley smoke the way a wet cloth lies over a table. Stalls came down board by board; a woman folded a length of oiled paper around nothing and tied it with a string that had been tied and untied so often the paper kept the crease of it, the way a field keeps the furrow after the plough has gone. \person{Corin} lay where \person{Sella Vail} had left him, his cheek to the cold iron of the post, and heard the square lose its people the way a slip pit loses water when the feed is shut off, not all at once but by the lowering of a level, until the heavy things sit on the bottom and the light things go away with the water. He counted what went. Two women. A man with a pole. A boy carrying a crate on his head. A woman with a shawl over her hair. The counts went into the place where the other counts went, and not one of them was a door.

The last pig went over the boards with a weight behind it that he felt travel up through the iron of the post and into his cheek, and after it there was only a man washing the boards with a handful of straw and sluicing grey water from a bucket. At a stall two women stood over a heap of turnips with a bargain between them that had been going since morning and would not be settled before dark. One said a figure; the other said the figure was short by two; the first said two was nothing and the second said two was two, and they went on turning the turnips with their hands while the light changed and the dry cold got into the spaces under the awnings. Nobody called either woman wrong. The figures stood side by side on the heap as the figures had stood all morning, and the bargain went on being a bargain because neither of the two had ever intended it to be settled.

\person{Mira Silt} worked at the edge of the eel stall with her back to the slate on its crate and her face to the open square. Eel slime had dried on her sleeves in long pale streaks that had caught the dust of the morning and turned the colour of old bone; the top half of her left ear was gone, and the cold had got into the place where it had been so that she kept her head a little to the right, as if listening to something on that side. She had eel-bones loose in one hand and flicked one against her thumb as she worked, a small dry tick that came over the other noises of the market and went under them again. She did not look at the basket. She had not looked at it once all day, and the boy, who had spent four days learning the difference between kinds of not-looking, put this one where it belonged without knowing what it was for.

“What's the eel price, Thane,” she said, “two marks the pair or two marks the pair and two short? Don't trouble yourself. It's two marks the pair and two short, and I've counted them twice, and I'll count them again after I've eaten.”

\person{Berr Clay} stood with the red cord folded across his chest and the green at the corner of his mouth, and did not answer, because she had left him nothing in the sentence that wanted an answer. He had come to confirm a kindness that morning in a voice with nobody in it, and here was a woman asking a question that had already swallowed its own reply. She moved an eel-bone from her left hand to her right and said a figure under her breath, and moved another. The boy counted the bones moving and not what they were for. It was not a slate. That was the whole of what he could have said about it in \lore{Grathic}, and he kept it beside the other things that had the same shape and did not join them.

When she turned to go she put the side of her hand against a loose board lying across the path where the barrow men would take it and shoved it clear, brisk, with her face still toward the eels, and went on talking as if she had moved nothing. The jolt of it came up through the post into the basket. It was the way she did everything that had to be done before anybody could make a speech about it. A thread of drool went from his lip to the fold and stayed through the whole of the afternoon, and there was nobody in the square who would have wiped it and nobody he could ask to.

There were two ways of not looking at a basket, and he had cause to know both. The man on the block that morning had not looked because the figure he was reading had nothing to do with what lay under the wool, and a man who will not look at a thing has already decided what it is worth. This woman had not looked because she knew exactly what lay under the wool and had arranged her whole face, and the better part of her body, so that no part of the market would be given the chance to look at it either. One not-looking priced him. The other kept him. He could read the pressure of both and had no word in the language of the square for either, and no person in the square to say it to.

\scenebreak

There was a second reading for the households that had come late, or for those that wanted the figure said again where they could hear it with their own ears. The crowd was thinner now and did not lean. It had leaned once and taken what it came for, and a number that has been heard is a lighter thing to carry than a number that is still wanted, so the square went on with its own business while Berr wiped his thumb on his apron, one short dry stroke on each side, and set two fingers to the stone. He wiped it for the hand and not for the slate. The slate was clean. There was nobody in the thinning market who would have cared if it had not been, and the boy, who had watched a man in a room with good light wipe the rim of a glass before he put it to his mouth, knew that the wipe was not for anyone's benefit but for the man's own sense of how a thing ought to be handled.

“\lore{One in twelve}, as \lore{the notice} says,” Berr said.

He said it low, as he had said it in the morning, and the people at the far edge of the square who had not heard it the first time heard it now and went on with what they were doing. A woman with a basket of turnips put the figure into the same place she had put it three hours ago and did not look up. A man who had been waiting to have a pig weighed went on waiting. Nobody said the figure was wrong, and nobody said it was right; \lore{the levy} went on being spoken around all morning the way a poor room speaks around a hole in the wall, and the second hearing of it lay down in the square as flat as the first.

Then Berr set the slate down on its block. It gave its small flat answer, a dry knock with no body behind it, the shadow of \lore{the full note} stopping at the surface of the stone and going no further. \person{Daniel Marchetti} had heard that sound from a cup across a birth room and from a pot on a bench under a roof that did not ask where his memory came from, and once that morning from this same slate, and now a fourth time from the same stone under the same careful hand. He kept the four hearings side by side the way he kept his numbers and did not make them agree. There was no word in Grathic for the sound and no need of one, and nobody in the square tapped a slate for him. The knuckle that would have done it lay outside the fold of wool with milky nails on it, and went up an inch and came down, and there was no surface within two paces of it that it could have reached.

\scenebreak

A barrow went out through the north side of the square with its shaft down and two men at it. What was in it was \lore{the Ash}, grey and ordinary, heaped a little above the sides in the middles where the load had shifted on the way across the market. One of the men stopped to pull a board out of the path and the other went on, and the barrow went on after him, and nobody in the square looked at it twice. It was the afternoon's work going out toward \place{the north gate} and \place{the Bone Orchard}, the same work that went out every market-day, and there was no more to see in it than in a pot carried to the sink.

He looked at it, which was the difference between him and everybody else in the square. The grey in the barrow was the grey of the ridge along the skirting board under the Vail house, near two thumbs wide, turning the corner without a break, which Sella had not swept in two years and would not let anybody sweep. The same colour. The same plain fact of a thing left where it lay or carried away. He put the barrow with the ridge and the ridge with the charred edge of the slate in the kiln ash, and the three lay together in the place where he kept facts that nobody had explained and nobody had asked him to understand. He had no word for what the grey was, and it did not occur to him to want one. He had learned by now that the things nobody named in this district were the things the district had the most practice at carrying out of sight.

The bell went while the barrow was passing. It went on longer than a church bell would have gone and nobody crossed himself at it, and it was the only sound in the square that wanted nothing from him. He counted the strokes because there was nothing else to count that belonged to him, and the count came out four, four, and a gap where the fifth should have been. Four, four, and a gap. It was the shape of the kiln's breathing through the wall of a house three doors up the yard, and the shape of the nails in the floor where he had lain before he had ever been carried anywhere, four on the near board and four on the next and a knot the width of a thumb where the fifth should have been. He put the bell beside the kiln and the kiln beside the floor and let them lie. He did not make them agree and he did not ask what the gap was for. The gap was the part he kept. It was the only part of any count that nobody had thought to enter.

When the last of the stalls had gone, the number of days to \lore{the Five} was given out as three. It was given out in a voice that had counted what it had and did not want to say it, the way \place{Low Kiln} had been saying three since the ice came, and the square took it the way it had taken the figure at the block: flat, without echo, and went on with the business of going home. Three was a number like the others, a hundred and nine and eight and the two Dessa had counted out and back, a number that could not be spent and could not be argued with and was not his. \lore{The Hearth Drawing} was two days off. He had the date by heart the way he had the shape of the post --- Stone 12, and two days between it and the morning's date --- and nobody in the thinning square had said what the date was for, or what would be drawn, or from whom. Two days, and three days, and the whole district going home to its fires with the numbers lying where they had fallen.

Sella lifted the basket off the post. The move went through his belly before it went through his ears, and his head went where it wanted as she rose, and for half a breath he saw the length of the square emptied of people, the block with the slate lying small on it behind him, the boards washed and stacked, the pale ring on the barrel where a hundred mornings of cups had come and gone. Then the wool took his cheek again and the sight was gone, and there was no way to turn his head far enough to get it back without the small wet sound coming up in his throat. The fold had ridden back at his ear and she put it right without looking, the little-door burn closing on the wool and letting it go, and said something to the wind about the river, which was an instruction to a thing that could not answer and not a word to him.

She turned back up the road toward \place{the Clay Yard} with no word about where they had been or what had been read over the top of him. The market emptied behind them. The slate lay on its block with its second flat answer going into the noise of people taking down their stalls, and the barrow went on toward the north gate with its grey load and no one reading it, and the bell went on being a bell over both. Four, four, and a gap. He carried the new number up the yard with the hand outside the fold unable to lift to any of it, and Low Kiln said three days to the Five without saying what it had counted to make three, and the wool smelled of woodsmoke as the house with the banked coals came back into view, and its door did not quite shut.
