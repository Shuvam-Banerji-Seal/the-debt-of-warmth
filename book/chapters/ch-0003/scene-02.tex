The shouting did not stop. It gathered and leaned. Two hundred people at \place{the Cross} were making one sound, and the sound wanted a figure the way a dry throat wants water and shapes itself around the word before the water comes. Under the one sound ran the small separate noises of the market setting itself up: a board dropped flat, a coin rung on a stone, a pig driven over wet boards with a weight behind it that he felt travel up through the woman's arm and into the basket. Then a man stepped up to the block where the slate lay, and the sound dropped, not because he had asked for quiet but because every person in the square had to lean in at once to hear him, and leaning is a quiet business. Even the pigs seemed to hold still for it, the lean one with its ear in a man's hand, the steam coming off their backs in the dry cold.

He could see very little of it from under the wool: the underside of a chin, the block's near edge, a strip of boot, the sky stripped of colour. A man's height in a crowd is a country to a child in a basket; he knew the size of the thing he was listening to by the way the sound came down to him, from above and a little to his left, and by the way the crowd leaned toward it as a field of barley leans one way in a wind. He had been carried into the middle of the market and set down at its edge, and the market had gone on arranging itself over him all morning without once finding a reason to stop.

\person{Berr Clay} took the slate by its edge. Before he touched it he wiped his thumb on his apron, one short dry stroke across the clay-coloured cloth, and then again on the other side, and only then set two fingers to the stone and turned it a little toward the light, the way a man turns a loaf to see which side took the fire. The slate was clean. He had wiped his thumb anyway. He wore the red wool cord of his office across his chest, folded square where another man might have worn a sash, and it was the only bright thing in the square besides the frost on the smithy roof. He was heavy in the way a wagon is heavy, with a back that had carried clay and grey stubble he had trimmed with a knife before market-day, and a ring of lead-glaze green at one corner of his mouth the colour of old copper gone bad. His hands were large and clean. He smelled of hot clay and, under it, nothing at all, which is the expensive smell of Court business. A man who tastes his own glaze in front of a moot is either thorough or afraid. \person{Corin} had no way of deciding which and had long since given up supposing he could tell from a face.

“\lore{The levy} is called,” Berr said. “It is not called by me.”

He did not raise his voice. That was the frightening thing, if a thing in a basket could be frightened: the moot roared and he did not, and the roar had to come down to him.

“\lore{One in twelve}, as \lore{the notice} says.”

He said it the way another man might cross himself, and said it low, and the crowd took it the way wet ground takes a dropped board, flat and without echo. Behind him a square of paper lifted on a nail at the moot-post, gone soft at one corner, with a row of marks on it that the wind turned over and back. The boy had known the figure before he had ever seen the paper: three of four loaves in a room three doors up the yard, the slip in the pit to be paid for in the thaw, a bed and a warmth for a foundling nobody had agreed to pay for. The figure had had no name in that room either. A midwife had told a woman three days ago that a child had gone in the levy among the children taken for the account, and had given the number no spoken shape at all. Here the number was given a shape and said out loud in a low voice, and nobody withheld it, and it went out into the square and sat down among the pigs.

He could not read the row of marks on the paper, but he knew the shape of a figure held up where everyone could see it and the shape of a hand that would not let it be argued with. A number on a notice is a number that has been settled somewhere before anybody in the square has opened his mouth, and there is no arguing with a thing that has already been written down. The slate on the block was the same shape as the slate on the bench in \place{House Silt}, and the writing on it lay turned up to the light in the same way. He set the two together and then kept them apart, the way he kept all the things that had the same shape and did not yet make a fourth.

“If your household has heard otherwise,” Berr said, “your household has heard wrong. The account stands as read. There'll be a slate at the watch for any household that wants the figure again, and the household can bring its own light.”

He apologised the way a man apologises to a pot he has cracked, by telling it what to do next. He never said I. He said the district and the account and the notice, and each of them stood in for a man who was standing right there on the block, so that the man himself was nowhere in the sentence and the figure was everywhere.

\person{Daniel Marchetti} tried to turn his head to see the face of the man pricing him. The neck would not permit it. Past a point the head went where it wanted and a small wet sound came up in his throat, and the wool rode back off his cheek, and the cold got into the corner of his mouth. He had once watched a resident keep his eyes on his shoes at the review of a chart he had signed, in a room with a long table and a window that would not open, and had read the not-looking then as clearly as a pulse: a man who will not meet your eye has already decided what the room will call the thing that happened. The chart had been his to sign, and the note he had not read had been lying under his own hand. He had read the resident correctly and had still said nothing, which was the part he had never managed to correct. Here the not-looking was done to him, and it was not even a refusal. The man on the block did not look into the basket and had no reason to; the figure went on being read over the top of it, and the boy lay under it like a fact under a stone.

\scenebreak

The figure did its work in the square. A woman near the basket put her hand on her daughter's head and tightened her fingers in the child's hair while the number was spoken, and let go when it had passed, the way a hand lets go of a rope once the load is set down. The girl was old enough to stand still for a figure and young enough not to know what it cost; she shifted her weight from one foot to the other and looked at the pigs. The boy had seen that hand in House Silt three days ago, on a woman who had counted nineteen steps she did not take. Nobody in the market said the word the figure was for. The word was not needed. The figure went into the account and the account held it, and two hundred people went on standing in the dry cold with the thing they had come to hear lying where they could all see it, and the market breathing over it as a kiln breathes over a pot it has decided not to draw.

The moot had wanted its figure the way a thirsty room wants the door opened; now it had the figure and did not know what to do with the wanting, so it put the wanting back where it had been, into the pigs and the turnips and the haggling over short weight. A man lifted a child onto his shoulders so the child could see the slate and then could not get him down again for a while, and the child cried, and nobody looked up at the crying because the figure was lying on the block and wanted looking at. Two women at a stall went on with their hands in a heap of turnips and said two different figures over them, and neither figure was the one that had just been read, and nobody thought the three had anything to do with one another.

Beside the block \person{the Sill assessor} stood with a ledger held shut against his coat, and gave the moot the only other voice it got that morning.

“The figure stands as read.”

He said it to the slate and not to the crowd, and settled the ledger more firmly under his arm, and that was the whole of what the Court had to add. His collar was narrow and clean and his hands were not clay-coloured at all; the boy noted the hands, because hands tell you a man's work before a collar does, and these had never touched a pot or a pig or a slip pit, and had no callus between thumb and first finger where a carrying cord would lie. He could have read the man's grip without being told what the man was. Here there was nothing to read but a figure that stood as read, which is a sentence that closes a matter before the matter has properly opened, and the man who said it had said it so often that it had stopped having a shape at all.

Berr's tongue went once to the green corner of his mouth and came back, a man tasting his own work without appearing to taste it. He waited while the silence ran out, and when it had, his thumb went once more across his apron and he set the slate a fraction straighter on the block. He had the careful hands of a man who has handled a great many things that must not be dropped and has learned that a figure is the heaviest of them.

Eight pigs, two hundred people, one assessor and one slate. The market had its own arithmetic and none of it included the weight in the basket. The cold had got into the boy's mouth again under the wool and the drool went down his chin onto the fold, and there was nobody in that square who would wipe it and nobody he could ask to. The head went where it wanted and the small wet sound came again, and the hand that had once reduced a shoulder in the dark closed on nothing at the edge of the basket and came away. He counted the pigs because the pigs were there and counting was what he had: eight, with a lean one that went clumsy on the wet boards and was hauled up by the ear. Eight was not four and four and a gap, and it went into the private place with a hundred and nine and with the strokes of a bell he had not yet counted, and none of the numbers could be spent. The infant in him wanted the breast and was cold and did not care a figure for any of it.

He counted the ways out as well, because a room has exits and a district has roads and he had never learned to stop telling them apart. There was the road down to the south bridge and Slate Row a quarter league east, the steep street up to High Kiln, the north bridge and the road that went out toward the Bone Orchard. Four ways out of the square and not one of them a door he could open, and the only roof that would keep him lay up the Clay Yard behind him with its coals banked under ash. He put the four beside the others and left them there, untaxable because nobody had asked for them.

\person{Sella Vail} had not set the basket where the slate lay open. She stood a half-pace off from the block with the basket on her hip, the same distance she had kept from the birth-slate in House Silt, and when the figure had been swallowed by the market she did not look at the slate and did not bring the basket nearer. She put her hand to the wool at his chin instead, without looking, the little-door burn closing on the fold and letting it go. The hand smelled of woodsmoke and of the sour edge that came up off her apron when she had been near the kiln, and it was the only hand in that square that had any business near his face, and it did not stay there.

“Keep his ears covered,” she said to the wind, or to the wool, or to the basket. “There's cold off the river.”

She answered nothing else all morning, and the woman who had asked about the mother the morning before was not there to ask again.

When the figure had been read and the moot had nothing more to lean for, Berr Clay set the slate down on its block. It gave a small flat sound, a dry knock with no body behind it, the flat shadow of \lore{the full note} answering and then stopping at its own surface. It was the third flat note in four days. The first had come from a cup across a birth room, a small dead sound that stopped at the clay and would not go through; the second from a pot set down on a bench in a house with a hollow seat and a salt-stiff coat on a peg; and this one came from the district's own account laid under a man's hand. The boy set the three side by side the way he set his numbers, without joining them, and there was no word in \lore{Grathic} that he had for any of them and no need of one. Nobody in the square tapped the slate for him. The knuckle that would have tapped it was a small soft thing with milky nails lying outside the fold of wool, and it could not have reached the block if the basket had been set against it.

He knew what the hearing was worth, which was nothing. A man who hears a vessel answer flat and cannot reach it has heard a fact and gained no purchase on it; he had known that in a birth room and known it in a kiln-house and knew it here with the whole district's account under the same small dead sound. The surgeon in him wanted the slate on a bench with a knuckle to it and the answer taken clean, the way he had once taken the measure of a cup two paces from a bed and done nothing with the measure. The infant in him wanted the breast and was cold and had the wool in his mouth. Both wanted things two paces off and a lifetime away, and the square went on with its morning and did not wait for either.

He had closed a matter like that a hundred times. Signed the chart, let the institution call a thing what it wanted to call it, gone home to a refrigerator with four beers in it and a photograph on the wall he looked at every night without seeing. He knew the shape of Berr Clay's relief standing there on the block, because it was his own relief he was looking at: not triumph and not even cruelty, only the clean settling of a thing that had been given a number and could now be called dealt with. He had been good at that. He had been so good at it that a man with a clean slate and a good reason could stand in a market square and price a child and go home thinking he had done the district a kindness, and Dan would have understood him perfectly, which was the worst of it, and the thing he had never once been able to say out loud to a person who would have understood the words.

The crowd leaned, heard, and went back to the pigs. The roar came up again in its layers: the driver shouting, a pig going over the boards with a weight behind it, a coin rung twice on a stone, a woman calling a price for turnips, the not-a-church bell going on being a bell over all of it. The slate lay where Berr had set it with the figure on it, and the figure stayed where it was, and the district went on breathing around it the way a kiln goes on breathing with the door left a finger's width open. Sella lifted the basket a little higher on her hip and did not look down into it; the fold had ridden back and she did not put it right, which was a thing he noticed and could not ask her about.

He lay with a third flat note in his ear that nobody had asked him to hear and that he could do nothing with, while the pigs were driven over the boards and the market made its ordinary noise. The slate lay open on its block with the figure on it that would carry him whether he could read it or not. \lore{The Hearth Drawing} was two days off, and \place{Low Kiln} said \lore{the Five} were three days off, and he kept a hundred and nine and four and four and a gap and the third note, and none of them was a thing he could say out loud in a square that had already counted him.
