\daymark{91}{Seed 2, Y.H. 813}

\dropcap{T}{he brown skin} the water had left over \place{the Clay Yard} held the print of a boot and gave it back slowly in the morning cold, faithful to a thing that had gone and would not come again in that shape. The first work of Seed had been sweeping. Today, the second day of the planting month, the work had moved east of \place{Low Kiln}, out past the last houses and onto the warmer ground where \place{the barley fields} lay in long strips the boy had carried until now only as a direction. There was no harvest in them yet and no green worth the name, only the brown skin of the turned earth and the pale seam where a broom had gone along it. Cold came off the ground in flat sheets. Wet wool and woodsmoke went before the women; the tallow in the shed had softened in the warm and set again in the cold, and the whole yard smelled of a fire that had been fed and then left. Seed was a name for putting things into the ground, and the ground east of the town had begun to take them.

\person{Corin} was carried at the edge of it, as he was carried everywhere, ninety-one days old and three months, in a basket that was not his under a fold of wool that was not his, and he watched the first planting work begin with a broom and a look and no figure spoken. \person{Sella Vail} had set the basket where the work went past and the wind did not come straight at it, the cold iron of the handle two inches beyond his hand. The women went along the strips with their skirts hitched, and their brooms went around what the sweep had left, and seed came out of a fist in handfuls that nobody counted aloud. A fist went down into a pocket and came up again. A woman pushed the bristles around a stone and looked at the ground a moment longer than the stone required, and that look was the rest of the measuring. There were no slates out here and no figures. The yard had counted what the water took in loaves and glances all through the lean week and had brought the same instruments to the planting: a hand, an eye, and a space nobody would name. In the life that was not this one he had watched a surgeon sort twenty bodies in ninety seconds by the give of one shoulder against another, and the sorting had been exact and had never once been written down. Here the sorting was seed and stone and the weather, and it was just as exact and just as little use to him.

A man with a barrow said the strips would want hands before the week was out, and a woman at \place{the drying racks} said the weather would have the last word, and neither put a number to either thing. “Hands,” the man said, and left the word standing in the cold. “Weather,” the woman said, and went back to her broom. No figure came into the middle between them. A slate takes no seed, and a fist takes no figure. The boy set the strips on a place of their own, a direction still and not a number. A direction can be carried for a month without becoming anything a clerk could enter; a strip is a strip until somebody puts a back into it, and a back is not a figure either. He kept ninety-one exactly, set edge to edge with the ninety before it, while the house kept its account badly in loaves pushed along a shelf, a glance at the clay jar on the wall, and a space at the end of the loaf row that nobody called a number. He wanted the number of his days said aloud by somebody who would know what it counted, and nobody in the field or the yard had any use for it. There was no word in \lore{Grathic} that held a direction and a number in the same breath, and if there had been he had no voice to spend on it.

\person{Berr Clay} came down from the north road to look at the strips, short as a man comes short who has been looking at a thing from a height. He had the red wool cord of his office folded under his coat instead of worn across it, and the green of tasted glaze at one corner of his mouth, and he smelled of hot clay with nothing under it, the most expensive smell in \place{Grath}. His hands were large and clean. He stood a moment over the furrows and looked as if a figure might be lying in them waiting to be picked up, and gave the yard none. He wiped his thumb twice on his apron before touching nothing, and held a narrow slate unopened against his forearm with a finger resting on what was written there. To the man who brought a number up out of a fist and asked what the planting would cost now that seed had gone into the ground, he said, “A number goes into a slate, not into a strip. If the house wants it, there'll be a slate at the watch.” And then, to the yard at large, “The levy is called. It is not called by me.”

The true subtraction went over the strips unspoken. The printed figure stood where it stood, and a figure read aloud was not the figure paid. He never said \emph{I}. He took himself out of his own sentence as neatly as a surgeon takes a hand out of a field before the dressing goes on, and went off toward \place{High Kiln} with the box in his house still locked and the true deduction inside, believing he had done the district a kindness. The man with the barrow went back to his barrow with the number still in his fist, and the women went back to their handfuls, and nothing in the strip had changed its figure because a man had looked at it. The boy put the twice-wiped thumb on a tray of its own, where other record-first hands lay, and did not join it to any of them.

\scenebreak

Back in \place{the Clay Yard} Sella gave the broom its instruction before she gave it to anybody, standing in the Vail corner with the bristles across her two hands and the grey of the ground showing between them. “Round it,” she said to the bristles. “The month's for planting and there's nothing planted here. It cost enough to come in; it'll cost more to take out.” The little-door burn across the back of her hand shut on nothing. Then, and only then, she let \person{Tarn} take the broom out of her hands. She went around the clean dark line where \lore{the Ash} ridge had turned its corner, and around the new hairline of grey finer than the old that lay beside it unswept, and around the space under \place[the drying racks]{the lowest rack} where the broken birth-slates lay, going as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. She swept none of the three. The new grey was the thaw, and the thaw would go when it went; the house had said so all through Silt and said it again at the turning of the month, and a line the house had not swept in a year was left where it lay and called a season. She gave no instruction to the basket. A basket had no ears, and a thing set down was a thing set down.

Tarn set the small bench knife on its sill before the light and told the sill first and the yard after where it was, six years standing in a mark his thumb had worn almost flat and the seventh never cut, nearly gone and still held, the one entry in this house the house could not check. “Six counted,” he said to the kindling. “The next I don't count.” He said it as a man says the weather and went on holding the breath he had decided not to spend, though his chest was already tight with it. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line and no scrubbing lifted it; the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back. “Yes, house,” he said to the woman who wanted an early thaw, and “Yes, house,” to the man who wanted a late. He agreed so entirely that no argument could find a foot, and went on wanting the late because a late thaw puts off the paying, though the paying had stood in the ruts since the water went. His broom went over the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector, over and over, without once turning along it. No collector came. From \place{the Vail wheel-shed} came the low note of the wheel and a word spoken to a pot, \emph{You'll do}, and no figure came out of the shed onto the yard. A vessel gave its note past the door and Tarn's head did not turn, and he did not name the sound, and the boy did not call it a seventh hearing.

Under \place{the Vail kiln-house} roof the carried fire lay under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees and missing one breath in every four. Sella told it it was the thaw and turned the coals with the back of the broken pot and fed it, and the fire answered with four low pulls and a silence where the fifth should have been. Its payer had gone unnamed for ninety-one days. The coal the brine-gull took upriver weeks back was entered in no slate and missed in none; one warmth less had gone out of the world with no account the poorer, and the planting month had come over it without noticing. Every warmth in the house had a payer and none of them stood in the room to be thanked, and the boy had learned to ask who paid before he asked what a thing was worth, which was a surgeon's question in a country that had no use for it. She did not touch \lore{the widow's mark}, because a strip, a broom, a basket and a bed are not openings and no opening had come into this house since the year turned, and she had lost a husband to that danger and had never once described it that way herself. The clean dark line lay where it had always lain and the finer grey lay beside it. The boards overhead kept four nails and four and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts did not meet.

The body humiliated him exactly and in its known order. A thread of drool had frozen at the corner of his mouth in the small hours and no hand came to wipe it, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and came back. Cold arrived at cheek, then temple, then wrist, the wool riding back from the wrist so the air found the bare place and stayed there. One brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold against his ear, gone past smelling of anything, and the empty place beside it had come into Seed 2 without acquiring a name; no third came. He wanted the crust, wanted the breast, wanted the lip of the \place{the yard well} bucket two inches beyond his hand, and the three wants sat in him with the strict equality of three instruments laid out for a procedure he would never perform. The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water going over water behind the racks and came down. He had once known where every vessel in a room lay by the sound of it and could put a clamp on the one that was leaking; here he lay under a fold and could not lift that hand to his own chin. He was the only adult in the field and the least use in it. The joke had never once been funny enough to say aloud.

At the turn of the wind Sella lifted the basket level and carried him up at the half-pace from every open slate, going around the space under the lowest rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and set him on his bed under the roof and not in \place{the Debt Loft}. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without looking at his face and left the thread at his mouth frozen and unwiped. The door stood a finger's width open. Through the gap came the river's low muttered note from \place{the Ilm}, and through the floor came \lore{the kiln's breath}, four, four and a gap held under blind ash, the two sounds in two separate places as they had come for ninety-one days. One brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold with the empty place beside it still unnamed, and no third came. The new finer grey lay unswept beside the old clean dark line. Nothing had been put to a figure in the strips or in the house; the seed was in the ground and the count crossed into the afternoon of Seed 2 unspent.
