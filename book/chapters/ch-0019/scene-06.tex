\person{Sella Vail} set the basket just inside the finger's-width-open door of \place{House Silt}, at the edge of the light and away from every flat place where a slate might lie. She set it the way a pot is set down on a board chosen for it: not in the draught off the threshold, not where a body might pass, not where any writing waited to be read. There was no slate open within reach of the basket, and she had made sure of that with one glance before she bent. Then she straightened and stood a half-pace off, with the little-door burn across the back of her hand closing on the wool of her skirt and then on nothing. She opened no slate. She turned no errand into a walk to \place{Slate Row}. The errand was a basket and the basket was set down, and a basket set down is a pot set down and left, and a pot set down is not told what it is holding.

From the low angle of the basket the room had the shape he had kept four times before and would keep again: a bench, a pot, a draught going along the floor, and a place in the figures where a line should stand and does not. It was a fifth likeness set beside four others and not among them --- the birth room, the room under the Vail roof, the death-house two doors down, the Court's room heard through a doorway, \place{the Eel House} --- and the boy laid the fifth beside the others without letting it join them, because a likeness laid on a likeness grows a cause unless a hand holds them apart, and the only hand available to him lay outside the wool and could not lift a thread from his own chin.

Above the abandoned board-bed the round pewter tin kept its place, with a pale fold at the seam where something had been folded and put away and a thumb-bright rub on the lid where a thumb had gone again and again and had never once lifted the lid. Nobody had opened it in three years and nobody opened it now. Below it the fold of grey wool lay on the board with a hollow in it the shape and the weight of a small pale river stone, and the stone was not in the hollow. Nobody said the stone was gone, and nobody put a hand into the hollow to make sure. The boy lay in a fold of his own under wool of his own and could not have lifted either lid, and the two absences sat in the room as quietly as two pots set down on a shelf and never filled.

In the back room a neighbour scrubbed at a board with lye water and sang a low song with the words flattened out by the work. The boy understood every word and could not yet make one. Cold came in its known order, cheek and then temple and then the bare place at the wrist where the wool rode back, and the hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the sound of water going over water and came down. \person{Corin} was ninety-three days old and three months and nobody in the room had any use for the number. He kept ninety-three exactly while the house went on keeping its days badly, in loaves pushed along a shelf, a glance at a clay jar, and a space at the end of a row that nobody called a figure. One brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold against his ear with the empty place beside it still unnamed, and no third came. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had been given an instruction about the thread.

\person{Alda Reed} came in short, with her shawl drawn tight against the draught at the door, the smell of soap-and-milk gone cold under river silt on her and lye sour under the soap. Her nails were scrubbed to the quick and still had clay under them. The stoop in her shoulders was the stoop of a woman who had spent her life leaning over other people's births, and her hands were as steady as the hands of a woman who has held a great many children at the moment their mothers grab her wrist. She did not say please and she did not say sorry, which was not coldness in her but the correct number of words for a room where somebody had once been in pain.

She stopped at the bench before she stopped at the child. She took \lore{the birth-slates} from under her shawl and laid them writing-down on the wood, and the soft scrape of stone on the bench went through the room. The boy marked the order of it the way he marked everything: the record first, the person after. She laid the slates straight with the edge of one finger, straightening a thing that would have lain straight enough, and only then crossed to the fold.

She pressed the fold flat at his shoulder with two fingers and kept them there a breath longer than a fold requires, long enough that the warmth came up through the wool into her palm and stayed a moment after the hand was gone. It was the small exact tenderness of a woman who had done this thing at a great many beds and knew how much pressure a thing would bear; he could have read the pressure to the ounce and could not have pushed the wool back from his own wrist. Under her hand the wool rode back. The red edge of \lore{the birth-flower} showed there, a branch running from the third rib down the forearm and stopping short of the hand, the colour of a line drawn with a hot wire through wet clay. Alda crossed two fingers at the corner of it before she knew she had done it, in front of a basket and a woman who stood a half-pace off and asked nothing.

Then she covered the sign. She covered it with the date she had given the woman who had asked for her child three years ago and had not been told the truth of it: the seventh of Stone, the account up on the eighth, before the paper went out. That date had stood over a death ever since. The woman had been more mornings gone than any living person kept count of, and she had gone uncollected, and no living person could take the date off the sign now, any more than a board laid over a hole can be lifted without somebody seeing what is underneath. The boy watched the order of the thing from under the fold: the look, the two fingers, the covering. He had no word in \lore{Grathic} for the order and had learned long ago not to look for one.

“He lies quiet,” Sella said. It was not a question, and it was not quite a report; it was a sentence laid on the bench beside the slates so that something could be answered and the answering kept small.

“Warm and breathing, that's all I've set down.” Alda said it exact, the way a woman says a figure, and it was exact and not true. The body under the fold was warm and breathing, which was true, and the thing she had set down about it was exact, and the two truths had never been the same truth and never would be. She said it in the same flat voice she used for a graft settling and a last high water, and went on. “A cooper's seventh in \place{Mud End}. A potter's third child in \place{Stonemouth}. The last high water before the ice.”

She went on with dates after there was no question left to answer, because dates are a handle on a thing too large to carry and a hearth-maid's work is to carry it. The dates arrived faster than any question needed them and went on arriving. Name, number and third day stayed unsaid among them, and the unsaying was not a gap in the dates but the thing the dates were laid over, the way cloth is laid over a shape on a shelf and left there so the shape will not be seen. He listened to the dates as a man listens to a file being closed, and heard the small click at the end of each one when it shut.

Her hand went once inside her bodice to the little tin on its cord and came away without opening it. The cord showed for a moment at the edge of her shawl, a grey line with a small weight at the end of it, and was gone. She had never opened it in company and she did not open it now, and she had not opened it on any morning since he had been able to watch her hands. Under the shawl, on the other side of the room in a house he could not name, her sixth narrow slate lay turned face-down toward a floorboard: six pages written in a second hand no Court physician had seen, all of them unread, the sixth set down on a day three months back and not to be set down again, no seventh written. The hidden record stood at six and went on standing at six.

Two private records in two materials stood apart in one room. The round pewter tin above the empty bed kept its pale fold and thumb-bright rub, unopened; the narrow slate under a shawl going home to a floorboard lay face-down toward the boards, unread. Neither was read by anybody and neither was carried to a door. The boy set them on separate places and wrote no cause across the space between them. He had learned what three likenesses in a row will do to a man who wants an answer, and here were two in one room with a woman between them who did not know she had made either, and he kept them apart with the only instrument he had, which was attention, and it bought him nothing he could spend.

\scenebreak

Alda gathered the slates writing-down and put them back under her shawl, and went out past the bench without a word about the fold or the sign or the date laid over it, the way a woman goes out of a room where she has done exactly what she came to do. In the back room the neighbour's song flattened out and stopped, and the lye water went on at the board. Sella stood a half-pace from the basket with her burn shut on nothing and asked no question about any of it. The two women had traded a silence for three years, and the silence had a door in it and neither of them opened it. The boy lay under his fold and heard the order of the day going out of the room: the slates first, the fold after, and nothing spoken about either, and the waiting done by the record and not by the person.

At the going of the light Sella lifted the basket level and carried him up at the half-pace from every open slate, going around the space under the lowest rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and set him on his bed under \place{the Vail kiln-house} roof and not in \place{the Debt Loft}. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without looking at his face and left the thread at his mouth frozen and unwiped, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left. She told the fire it was the thaw and turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and fed it, and the fire answered with four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been. Its payer had gone unnamed for ninety-three days, and the coal that went up with the brine-gull was entered in no slate and missed in none. She did not touch \lore{the widow's mark}, because a basket and a door and a bed are not openings and no opening had come into this house on Seed 4; she had lost a husband to that danger and had never once described it that way herself.

Under the roof the private file was checked the way a man checks instruments laid out on a towel, and it was unchanged. Six hearings of \lore{the full note} stayed six beside and never among, five of them from four sources and the sixth, the small flat knock out of a woman who had been warm that morning, from a person; no seventh had come and no seventh would be made by wanting one. Three near-full answers stood on their tray, advanced and not paid. The three intervals stayed on another. The word forty was unspoken, and the readable shape of the double turn stood beside it on its own place, and the weight of a thumb on wicker stood apart from both. Four low things kept their four places, with the river's note of water going over water a fifth thing and not a hearing. The two tellings of the room shut in the war stayed on their own tray joined to no kiln, likeness kept and difference kept, breath through the nose against breath made in the throat, both men saying \emph{not the war's hand}. The loan that could hear stayed uncalled. Below the sill a child's foot stayed the cold to one woman and weather to the yard, uncured and unnamed, and the knowing of what it was and what it would do had the usefulness of an instrument kept behind glass.

One brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold with the empty place beside it still unnamed, and no third came. The place at the end of the loaf row and the place at his ear lay in two rooms and did not touch. \lore{Ash} was worked into the creases of a man's knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line, and the new hairline of grey, finer than the old, lay beside it unswept and was called the thaw and would go when it went. The boards overhead kept four nails and four and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts did not meet. Three doors down a tin stayed shut and a slate stayed turned down and a date stayed over a death no living person could lift off; under this roof a fold held one crust and an empty place, and the arithmetic went on in loaves and glances with nobody putting a figure to either.

The door stood a finger's width open. Through the gap came the river's low muttered note from \place{the Ilm}, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, and through the floor came \lore{the kiln's breath}, four, four and a gap under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, the two sounds in two separate places as they had come for ninety-three days. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water going over water and came down. Nothing had been joined, paid, explained or ended. The boy was ninety-three days old and nobody had told him so, and the count stood at ninety-three, unspent, with the tin unopened three doors down and the narrow slate face-down toward its floorboard and the first planting week gone into the ground with the arithmetic unchanged. Day 94 waited in the worked strips with its seed covered and its figure unspoken.
