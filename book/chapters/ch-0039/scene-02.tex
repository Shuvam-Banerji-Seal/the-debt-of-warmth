The afternoon came into \place[the Vail kiln-house]{the kiln-house} sideways, through the finger's-width door and the slack cracks of the oiled paper, and lay on the boards in thin bright strips that stirred when the door stirred and went nowhere. \person{Sella Vail} carried \person[Corin]{the boy} in with the basket on her hip and set him low near the bench leg, at the angle where the underside of \place{the loaf shelf} becomes a roof, and did not look into the basket. She gave the instruction to the wool and the pot and the broom before she gave it to anybody, and the basket got none of it. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. “It's the river wind.” The window was shut and there was no wind in the room, but the saying had been said to the wool every day of his life and the wool had never once answered it.

From the basket's low angle the underside of the board was a ceiling with its own weather: a dry smell of old bread and clay dust, a shadow the length of a hand, the faint sweet edge of \lore{the thaw} coming in from the yard where the new grey lay beside the clean dark line. Above him the house kept its arithmetic the way it kept everything, badly and without variation. Loaves went along the shelf and were pushed into a row. Her eye went once to the clay jar that stood cold under its glaze against the wall, the way a hand goes to an instrument before a case is opened, and went away. There was a space at the end of the row that nobody called a number; a woman's hand would pass over it, find the hard heel of the next loaf, and go on with her work. A shelf was not an event. An absence on a shelf was ordinary work. His own count had neither rim nor handle and would not have sat among them. It stood where it stood. One hundred and sixty, exact, complete and unspent, and nobody under this roof had told him what a number was, and nobody under this roof would have known what to do with the telling if a man had put the words into their mouths.

\scenebreak

A neighbour came up from the strips with a wet pot held in both hands, water dark at her cuffs and a little of it running down her wrist to her elbow, and stopped in the doorway before she had decided what she was asking. The pot was a common pot with a chip at the lip, and she held it level, the way a woman holds a thing that has slopped once and will not slop again. “What does a firing cost,” she said, “set down there?”

She said it to the board, or to the room, or to the back of Sella's head; it was not a sentence addressed to a person, and so it did not require a person's answer. Sella did not turn toward the basket. Her little-door burn closed on nothing as her hand came away from the pot she had been wiping, and opened again when the hand opened.

“Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there,” she said. “The house has said it.”

The neighbour stood a moment with the question still in her mouth, having had it taken out of her hands and handed back in a form that would not carry. Then she carried it away level, pot and all, so that nothing slopped, and went back down the yard with wet cuffs and an unanswered sentence that had changed shape without her noticing. Sella went on wiping. The burn shut when the cloth went down and opened when it came up, two straight sides and a lintel and a threshold doing their ordinary work. An answer that gives no figure is still an answer, and a house that says it once has said it for the year.

\scenebreak

She went to the shelf with her back to the basket and stood at it a while, moving her lips along the row. The boy could see the back of her skirt and the broad fall of her shoulders and the coal-black hair held at the temple by the grey streak, and could not see her face. From his angle the front edge of the board hung over him like the lip of a low roof, and under that lip, where nobody thought to look because nobody stood where he stood, the charcoal lay in short clusters against the grain: some pressed hard enough to have left a shine, some faded nearly into the wood, a keeping put where a keeping is least expected and therefore most likely to last. He had seen the marks before and had not read them. He did not read them now.

Her hand went beneath the front edge.

It went under without a look and without a word, the fingers going exactly where they were going, and came away with nothing in them. She did not go back to the shelf after. She did not glance down at the basket to see whether the movement had been seen, and gave no explanation of it to the wool, the pot, the fire or anybody. A hand that goes under without looking either knows the board by heart or has been told not to look, and the reason stood between the two, with room for both and no way in. He kept the movement on a place of its own, the way he kept a map folded before he knew the country: the hand, the edge, the coming-away, nothing read, nothing copied, nothing joined to the two gaps above it or to the one crust in the other room. A keeping put where nobody expects to be read tells more by the care of its hiding than the writing would. He made no cause of the habit. There was no cause to make that would not want a payer.

The shelf kept its two unnamed gaps, the old space at the row's end and the palm's breadth that had come in on the night of Seed 6 with no scrape, crumb, print or word. No third came into it that afternoon. There was a saying of the loaf-shelves of \place{Low Kiln} that two gaps stay two gaps until a finger lays them together, and that an account then wants a name, and no finger in Low Kiln had laid these together. In the other room one stiff brown-edged crust lay two inches from his ear in the fold, gone leathery in the dry weeks, with its empty place beside it still unnamed and no third beside it either. The round bare ring on the wall where a shallow clay pan had stood out to dry through Reed kept the shape of its absence on a tray of its own. It was not laid across the shelf's two gaps or the crust's empty place or the empty place in the returning line of \place{the worked strips} where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out. It was not laid across the sixth green as a colour joined to no ear, or across the foundling as a shape with no sale. A fourth likeness would make a cause, and a cause would want a payer, and no finger of his drew the line. He had learned early in this house, and in a better-lit room that he carried behind his eyes without ever naming, that three likenesses in a row will stand up and call themselves a doctrine if no hand holds them apart. He held them apart.

\scenebreak

The light went out of the afternoon and came back lower, through the door in one bright strip that ended at the bench leg and did not reach him. Sella lifted him out of the basket with the little-door burn closing on the wicker and opening again, and carried him into the other room. She set him on \place{the bed} under the roof and not in \place{the Debt Loft}, base first and weight settled, and put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without once looking at his face. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the dry heat and kept its line. She left it where it was. Nobody under any roof had been given an instruction about the thread, and a basket set down was a pot set down, and a pot set down was left where it stood.

Her hand rested a half-beat longer than the setting needed before it went away. It was the only thing in the room that admitted anything, and it admitted nothing that could be entered: no figure, no name, no payer, no opening. If it was love, it had been given no instruction and kept no slate, and he had no word in \lore{Grathic} that would hold it without turning it into a debt. He did not try to make one. He lay with the crust two inches from his ear and let the half-beat go where half-beats go.

\scenebreak

At \place{the hearth} she told the fire it was the thaw, and turned the coals with the back of a broken pot. “The house will keep it,” she said, where another woman would have said \emph{I}. She stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand, counting under her breath toward no opening, her lips moving on a row that stood in some other room of her head; then she pushed the coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn, the grey streak at her temple pale off the coals, tipped the carried coal under the back of the pot and set the pot rim out with its handle standing two inches beyond the reaching hand. She did not look toward the bed. \lore{The widow's mark} hung where it had hung since the first firing of the month, a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and her hand did not go to it. A dusk was not an opening. A hearth was not a door. The hand went to the mark before the kiln and at no other hour, and there had been no opening under this roof since the month came up, and there was none at the going of this light.

The coals took what was given without thanks and without question, and answered in their own grammar: four low pulls, and the silence where the fifth should have been. The fire had missed the fifth for a hundred and sixty days and no payer had been named to the silence. The two rooms did not touch. Through the finger's-width door came the river's note of water going over water; through the floor came the fire's four low pulls and its gap. He lay within reach of both and could enter neither.

\scenebreak

In the small hours he turned out his file the way a man lays instruments on a towel before a case begins, each thing going into its own hollow and nothing resting across anything else. Six hearings of \lore{the full note} lay beside and never among --- five from four sources, and the sixth, the small flat knock out of a woman as the men lifted, from a person --- with no seventh and none invented to fill the empty place. Three near-full answers stood on their own tray, advanced and not paid: a pot under the wheel-shed wall, a rack pot lifted a hand's breadth too high and set down again, and \person{Jorra Vail}'s withheld knowing of a Vail pot's full note, among the answers and not among the hearings. The older intervals lay at Stone 13, Stone 17 and Stone 25 with the half-beat behind a standing finger, the unchecking stroke, his own held breath from a market morning. On the widened tray lay nine stoppings --- Reed 5, 8, 11, 14, 17, 20, 23, 26 and 29 --- objects and not a rule, intervals and not payments, no payer named to any, held apart by hand because a doctrine is an account that has found a payer. A tenth had not stood up out of the nine. The seventh year was earned by no man under this roof and had not given, and the loan that could hear was uncalled. \lore{The damper} lay beside them as one piece of iron with two bites joined to no kiln, the holding and the taking with no Grathic word for both mercies; the two shut-room tellings stood side by side with no third; the told bell stood beside the heard bell.

Three counts in three places did not become one: the river's note of water over water through the finger's-width door, the fire's four pulls and its gap through the floor, the boards' four nails, four, and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of the thumb overhead. He had counted them in the dark and would count them again. A fourth likeness would make a cause, and a cause would want a payer. He kept the three where they were, and the pan's gone place where it was, and the charcoal where it was, and did not lay a finger on any of them.

Cold arrived in its known order, cheek, temple, the small bare joint at the wrist where the wool rode back, and stopped there as if it had reached the end of its instruction. Want came up small and importunate for the crust two inches from his ear, and for the number spoken by somebody who would have known what a number was, and got neither. Both were true and the first was louder in the body, and the second was the one he kept. One hundred and sixty stood exact, complete and unspent. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water through the finger's-width door and came down against the fold. The fire missed its fifth under the floor with its payer unnamed for a hundred and sixty days, and the charcoal waited under the shelf for a hand that was not his and a day that has not come.
