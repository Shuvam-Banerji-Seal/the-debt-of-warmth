\daymark{164}{Tallow 15, Y.H. 813 (Fifthday)}

\person[Corin]{The boy} woke with the grey still unborn and the kiln already breathing. It drew in fours behind the wall and left a gap where the fifth breath should have been, the way a man leaves a word out of a sentence and goes on as though the sentence were whole. He had lain one hundred and sixty-four days under that gap and had never heard anybody fill it. Nobody under any roof counted his days aloud; he counted them himself in the dark, the way a man might count the beats of a horse under a blanket and be no wiser for the number.

The basket sat at the angle where the underside of a board becomes a roof, and the underside was his ceiling. Wool at his ear, wool under his chin, the thread at his mouth frozen from the night because no instruction existed about the thread. Hot clay under tallow came down off the banked fires, and the mineral tang of the \lore{Ash} ridge lay along the skirting board where no broom had been told to go. He was in \place{Low Kiln}, under \place[the Vail kiln-house]{the Vail roof}, and the world reached him in two streams: heat through the floor from \place{the hearth}, water over water through the finger's-width door. Neither came within reach. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the river and came down on the fold.

Then the men came.

Three or four went up \place{the Clay Yard} before first grey with their collars high against the sweetish wind, their voices so low that the words arrived at the basket as a shape and not as sound. They went into \place{the back room} off \place{the Vail wheel-shed}, which kept its finger's width of open the way a scab keeps one edge lifted, and the door did not close behind them. \person{Sella Vail} carried past at the half-pace with the basket on her hip. She turned no head toward the door, gave the house no name for the men, and did not say the room was cold. The little-door burn shut on the basket's weight and opened on nothing when she set him down. She told the wool to keep it off his ears and told the wool it was the river wind, and if she had a thought about the men it went where the other unspoken thoughts went, into the private roll nobody was shown.

Two tellings of a shut counting room stood side by side behind the wall: one from a man with a date in his mouth eighteen leagues east, one from a man who had only mentioned the room at a bucket-round and gone off with his bag and his mud. No third had come through all the mornings since. The boy kept the two apart and did not lay one across the other, because two tellings are two tellings and a joining would stand up and ask for a payer. The door kept its finger's width. The room was not entered by him, by her, or by the men who went low into it so early; they entered it and gave nothing out.

Jorra came through the finger's width with the morning on his forearms.

Fine grey slate dust was ground small as meal into both forearms, past the bend of the left little finger and under the nails, not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel. \person{Jorra Vail} was sixteen and built like a gate hinge, tall for a clay boy and narrow, with his hair cut short so that Ash could not nest in it, a thing the district noticed and he never explained. The dust had got there by being broken. The slip that dried to old bone on a working forearm had been mixed on purpose and laid on by hand, and the boy kept the two sorts apart as a matter of making: one a thing undone, the other a thing put together. Jorra kept the dusty forearms and the clean ones on one tray, two states of one morning, and did not make the dust say what it was not there to say.

Jorra asked neither which slate had been broken nor whether it was a birth-slate. The broken birth-slates stayed under \place[the drying racks]{the lowest rack}, gone around by the yard all morning and entered by nobody, and the empty place in the returning line where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out kept its look and its wide step and no number. If the dust had come from one place and not the other, the forearms said so only to a man who already knew how to read them, and the boy was not going to ask a question that would have to be answered in a word.

He spoke to the wheel before he spoke to anyone. “It doesn’t care whose hands. It cares if they’re steady.” He said it to the wheel as another man might say it to a horse before he put his hand to the harness, and the wheel gave nothing back, only went on turning under the weight of his weight in the hollow his father had worn. He did not look toward the basket.

A pot stood near-full on the bench. “You’ll do,” he told it, in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either. He knew the full note that pot would give three turns before it left the wheel, knew the roundness under the knuckle and the true wall and the foot seated square, the note a sound vessel gives when nothing has been left out of it. He did not say so.

That was the shape of the knowing the boy had learned to recognise in the year and more he had lain under this roof: to know the note before the knuckle went to it, to read the chart while the cloth was still over the field. He had been in rooms where heat was kept and counted behind a screen and a man stood at the gate with a towel in his hands and did not go in, and the knowing in those rooms was worth exactly what it cost to keep quiet. Here it was worth a place on a tray. The knowing went to the near-full answers, advanced and not paid, beside the hearings and never among them, and no seventh hearing stood up to join the six. No pot was lost in the yard that morning.

The kiln-master came up on the wrong wind. He was from \place{High Kiln}, with \lore{Ash} pale on his cuffs and the sweetish edge of \lore{lead glaze} on his breath, and he looked at the pot on the bench as a man looks at a thing he has been asked to price. “It’s singing before its time,” he said. “A pot that sings early generally sings itself out early.”

Jorra said nothing. He did not say \emph{I haven't said}, which would have been an answer. He let the saying stand where the wind had put it and went on turning, and the kiln-master, having made his observation, took himself and his sweetish breath off down the yard without waiting to be corrected. He meant nothing by it. Men from High Kiln said such things about pots; it was the kind of thing a kiln-master said when a pot caught his ear and he had a minute to spend.

The boy kept the saying on no tray at all. It was not a hearing and not an answer and not a cost; it was a thing a man had said on a wind and the wind had carried off. He drew no lesson from it and put no figure to it, because a lesson and a figure both want a payer and no payer stood to either. The dust ground small as meal stayed ground small as meal in the creases of a forearm, and the sweetish edge went on coming down off High Kiln whenever the wind turned that way, and the kiln's breath went on in fours with its gap behind the wall. The month's danger gathered in these things the way heat gathers under banked ash, toward an edge that could be paid later. It was not paid now. Nobody in the yard had said a word that could be entered.

\scenebreak

Out on \place{the flagstones} \person{Tarn} stood with the broom in his hand until the instruction had been given to the broom, and only then took it up. “Go around. It cost enough to come in. It’ll cost more to take out.” He said \emph{Yes, house} and swept around the clean dark line where the Ash ridge turned and the new finer hairline of grey beside it that the house called \lore{the thaw}, and around the rack space and the place in the returning line, with a look and a wide step and no number. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line and no scrubbing lifted it, and the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back. Six years stood in a mark worn almost flat by his thumb where the house could check them; the seventh stood at the lip of the sill where it could not. His stroke went over the shallow bright patch without the half-check and over the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector, over and over, without once turning along it. No collector came. He did not ask about the room, and Jorra did not ask about the ear, and the two silences stayed unjoined under the one roof.

At midday she carried him in and set the basket down near the bench leg, at the low angle where the underside of the loaf shelf becomes a roof. She gave the instruction to the wool and to the fire and never to the basket, and said \emph{the house} where another woman would have said \emph{I}. Her voice went low at the hearth, low in the way weather goes low before a shift, and the household read it as it had learned to read Jorra's quiet over three years: not as a thing to be asked about, but as a thing to stand around until it had passed.

It happened again as it had happened on the morning of Day 161, and because it happened again it was no longer a change. She had begun \emph{The fire's low. It wants---} and stopped, and Jorra's bent finger went to the cold iron of \lore{the damper} and stood there while she finished \emph{It wants feeding}. He moved the tongue a hair's breadth and left it standing. The household straightened around a sentence that had been allowed to finish the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted, and nobody said a word about the waiting, and nobody named a payer to it. The equality under the roof stood where it had moved on that other morning and was not moved again today.

The boy did not take the fraction up and lay it beside anything. He had put it on a place of its own when it first came, held and unspoken, not a reconciliation, not a hearing, not a payment, and a thing held is a thing kept where it was put. The iron had two bites, the bite that holds a vessel whole and the bite that can take the hand off a man, and there was no word in \lore{Grathic} that would hold both in one mouthful. He had filed it as one piece of iron joined to no kiln, and he left it where it lay. \lore{The widow's mark} hung where it hung at her throat and she did not touch it, because a hearth at midday is not an opening and the hand goes to the mark before the kiln and at no other hour.

Through the finger's-width door the river kept its fifth low thing, water over water. He put it on a place of its own, not a hearing of \lore{the full note}, not a seventh to stand up beside the six and not invented to fill the empty place, not joined to the kiln's gap under the floor or to the knot the width of a thumb overhead. Three counts in three places, and he would not let any one of them stand up and become the others, because a likeness in a row will call itself a doctrine if no hand holds it apart, and a doctrine is an account that has found a payer. No payer stood to any of these. The crust in the fold lay two inches from his ear with its empty place unnamed and no third; the shelf in the other room kept its two unnamed gaps and its charcoal unread; the round ring where the pan had stood kept its shape on a tray of its own. No fourth likeness was made a cause and no finger of his drew the line.

One hundred and sixty-four stood exact, complete and unspent in the dark of the wool. The reading stood somewhere ahead of it and nobody gave him its date, and nobody gave him the number of his days either, which was the same number and further out of reach than the crust. He had stopped expecting to be told, which was not the same as not wanting it.

The bent finger left the damper standing as it had stood on the morning before. Through the wall the fire answered four low pulls with the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for a hundred and sixty-four days. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water through the finger's-width door and came down against the fold. The morning's work went on around the places nobody entered, and the count went on where nobody could hear it.
