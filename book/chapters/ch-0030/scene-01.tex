\daymark{129}{Reed 10, Tenthday, Y.H. 813}

\dropcap{T}{he clay road} came down into \place{Sill} with the river wind on it, and \person{Sella Vail} carried \person[Corin]{the boy} down it at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, going around a board set out to dry as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. She gave the instruction to the wool and to the river wind and never to the basket: keep it off his ears, it had come all the way down with them and would go all the way back up, and a basket set down was a pot set down and left. The wool rode back from the small bare joint and the cold found the place it had come for and went under the fold at his ear in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody under any roof in the district had been given an instruction about the thread, so it stayed where it had been put. He was one hundred and twenty-nine days old, four months and nine days, and nobody on the road said so. \place{Low Kiln} thinned behind them, with its wet wool and its tallow smoke and the faint sour tang that had worked into the corners and would not come out with scrubbing, and ahead the square opened where the four roads met and the first \lore{Account-day} of Reed was already leaning over its block.

The square had the look of a pot set on to boil with the lid not quite on. Two hundred people leaned toward the reading block, because a man who speaks low makes a crowd come close and being close is its own kind of having been told; the market went on behind them and around them, closing on the wet boards the way mud closes over a boot-print, the figs and the turnips and the pots finding their own level without ever running together. Eight pigs had gone over the wet boards since first grey and the assessor's hand had been on the slate before his eyes went to the rail, entering them before anybody could ask which eight he meant. The reading block stood wet under the slate, the boards dark along their lower edges and the straw trampled into the seams behind, and the mark of the pigs went off it in the thin wind the way a wet footprint goes off a flagstone, leaving the shape first and then less than the shape. There were four ways out: the road down to \place{the south bridge} and \place{Slate Row}, the steep street up to \place{High Kiln}, \place{the north bridge}, and the road toward \place{the Bone Orchard}, and on an Account-day a man stood in every one of them with a figure he would not give. Sella set the basket in \place{the moot-post}'s finger's width of shade where no slate lay open on the boards, and stood a half-pace off. The little-door burn across the back of her hand closed on the wicker's edge and then on nothing. She gave the instruction again to the wool and to the wind and never to the basket, and did not look down into it.

The crowd had an appetite for a number the way a wet room has an appetite for fire: not for what it was but for the having of it. Figures came up out of fists and went into mouths and came out again on the shoulders of men going up the road, and nobody asked the figures to agree. A woman set a heap of turnips on a flat stone and left two figures standing side by side on it without making them equal; a man selling eels named one price back at another and let both stand. Tallow smoke from a pot stall lay low over the turnips and the trays, and hot clay came and went on the wind off the north road, so that the square smelled of a firing nobody had paid for and a breakfast nobody had sat down to eat. The boy lay at the edge of the shade with cold iron at his cheek where the post's iron bracket had been bolted and left, and cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, the wool riding back from the wrist so the cold found the bare place and went under the fold. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far. The surgeon in him read the morning as a chart laid open over a note that had not been turned: the lines were neat, the hand was steady, and nothing under the neat lines had ever been entered. The child in him was cold at the ear and wanted the crust that was not in this square. Both were true. The second was louder, and neither could be handed to anybody.

\person{Berr Clay} came up to the block short, a little before the top of the morning, with the red wool cord of his office folded under his coat instead of worn across it and the green of tasted glaze at one corner of his mouth. Hot clay came off him with the river wind and under it the smell of nothing at all, which is the most expensive smell in \place{Grath}. His hands were large and clean. He wiped his thumb twice on his apron before he touched nothing, and held a narrow slate unopened against his forearm, the writing toward himself, and passed a finger over the figure as a man checks a seam before he trusts it, and let his eyes rest on the writing a breath longer than the writing required. He did not look toward the basket. He never said \emph{I}; he said the notice, the figure, \lore{the levy} and the house, and he used the phrase \emph{as the notice says} the way another man might cross himself, a small sign made with the mouth instead of the hand. His voice was low enough that the two hundred came in closer to hear it, which they did, leaning, the way cloth leans when a weight is laid along one edge.

“One in twelve, as the notice says,” Berr said, and the words went into the top of the crowd and stayed there a moment before going on over the heads and the pigs and the posts and out at the four ways. He set the slate flat on the reading block with the figure uppermost, the way a man sets a dish down where it will not be slopped, and passed the finger over it once more. “The levy is called. It is not called by me.”

A man come up from \place{the worked strips} with dried mud in the creases of his wrists brought a number up out of a fist and held it toward the block, opening the fist enough to show he had one and shutting it again before anyone could see what it held. “Then what's the cost of the hands in the strip?” the man said. “Now they're out of it.”

“A number goes into a slate, not into a strip,” Berr said. “If your house wants it again, there'll be a slate at the watch.” \emph{Your house}. Not \emph{I}. He took himself out of his own sentence as neatly as a surgeon takes a hand out of a field before the dressing goes on, and the man put the fist back into his sleeve and went off with the number still in it, going up toward High Kiln with a shoulder that carried the question and arrived nowhere with it. The figure Berr had read was not the figure paid. The true subtraction sat in a locked box in his house up the clay road, and a figure read aloud into a market is a figure that belongs to everybody and to nobody, exact, complete, and worth nothing anyone could take away and spend.

The bell went while they were still leaning. It came through the noise from the Cross and went out along the four roads longer than a church bell has any business going, four strokes, four, and then a gap where a fifth should have been, and nobody crossed himself at it. A woman with a creel shifted her load and went on weighing eels; a man with a pig by the ear did not stop. The boy kept the heard bell on a place of its own beside the told bell of the Long Night and did not lay one across the other, because a bell that has been heard and a bell that has been spoken of are two objects and not one account, and three likenesses in a row will stand up and call themselves a cause if a man lets them.

Below the bridge the steps came down four, four and a landing a hand's breadth out of line, and a whole market had walked over \place{the out-of-line landing} since first grey without anybody making a fall of it. At the landing the soft-cuffed clerk was still set for a measure nobody had called, his cuff grey from washing and grey again by midday, his slate held to his forearm and his hand going to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls, every time. He had not been taken off since the month turned, or if he had been taken off somebody had put him back in the dark. The market sold its reeds and its eels over the top of him the way water goes over a stone that lies where a stone lies, and did not lower its voice for him; nobody stepped around him and nobody asked what he was set there for. A girl with a creel went over the landing sideways with one hand out to the rail and went on selling without looking at her feet, and nobody made an accident of it. The boy set the washed cuff on one place and the slate held where nobody else could see the figure on another, because a man who washes his own cuff has a hunger or a habit, and a man who holds a slate where nobody can read it has a use, and a use wants a payer. A measure set down before anybody calls it is a measure set down ahead of something; he kept that as a shape and did not make a rule of it.

A man come up from the strips and stood at the edge of the reading with the day's mud dried pale in the creases of his wrists. His eyes went around a place in the returning line, the way a woman's eyes go around \lore{Ash} left in a corner or a broom goes around the space beneath the lowest rack, and his feet went around it when he moved off a moment later, though there was nothing on the boards at that place for his feet to go around. The hand that had gone in on Seed 15 had not come out on Seed 27, and the yard had gone around the space every morning since, with a look and a wide step and no figure, no name, no cause, no accident, no body, no payment. The boy read the going-around before the man's feet moved, as he had once read the afterdrop before the pulse went, and set it on a place of its own, exact and unentered. Eight pigs had gone over the block and a levy had been called over it and the empty place had been gone around on the way to it, and none of the three had been made to stand for either of the others.

He was one hundred and twenty-nine days old and nobody in the square said so. The figure that had gone into the air belonged to everybody and had been given by nobody who could say what it counted; the only one who could have named the day was the one who could not form a word of it, and he lay in the moot-post's shade with the thread frozen at his mouth and the cold at his cheek and the hand outside the wool going up an inch toward the noise and coming down. He had kept the day exact through a hundred and twenty-eight others and would keep this one exact too, not because exactness bought anything but because the alternative was to let it go the way the figures went, from a fist to a mouth to a shoulder and nowhere. He had learned in two lives that a number spoken over a crowd and a number written in a private place are two objects even when they are the same figure, and he kept them on two places with no line drawn between them. Six hearings of \lore{the full note} stood where they had stood, beside and never among, with no seventh; the told bell lay beside the heard bell; the clerk's slate lay beside Berr's twice-wiped thumb and the man's opened-and-shut fist, each on its own place and none of them joined. He set the figure read aloud among these as one more object on a tray, a chart closed over a note nobody had opened, worth nothing to a man who could not read the hand that had written it.

By mid-morning the crowd had stopped looking at the block. The figure had been written in market mud and the mark of it was already drying where the boots and the pigs had stood, the wet going off it in the thin wind until nothing but the shape of a stroke remained and then less than that. The figures the crowd had carried away went with them out at the four ways and left nothing on the boards but the drying mark and a little trampled straw. The basket sat in the moot-post's shade at the half-pace, with the wool riding back and the thread frozen, and nobody in the square looked into it. Sella stood a half-pace off with the little-door burn shut on nothing, and gave the instruction to the wind and never to the basket, and the market went on over the top of both and left them where they lay.
