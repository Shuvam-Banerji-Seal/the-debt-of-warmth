\daymark{131}{Reed 12, Secondday, Y.H. 813}

The morning went downriver with them the way water goes under a bridge that has long since stopped being surprised by it: smoothly, and with the cold still in it. \person{Sella Vail} carried \person[Corin]{the boy} out of \place{Low Kiln} by the river road at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, going around a stone set out to dry as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and gave the instruction to the wool and to the running water and never to the basket: keep it off his ears, it had come all the way down with them and would go all the way back up. The wool rode back from the small bare joint and the cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, going under the fold where nobody had been told to put a hand. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and no instruction had ever been given about the thread, so it stayed where it had been put, stiff and doing nothing.

They came to \place{the Weir} with the light still thin on the posts. Salt had dried white where the flood had touched them and gone away, and the traps stood in the gaps with their mouths upriver, patient as so many open mouths that had learned not to close on one another. Tallow smoke from \place{the Eel House} lay low over the water, and under the noise of the boards ran the note of \place{the Ilm}, a fifth low thing he had kept on a place of its own since he was six days old, heard by nobody who could have told him what it counted. Sella set the basket at the edge of the work where no slate lay and stood a half-pace off with the little-door burn across the back of her hand closing on nothing.

The reed work went on in armfuls. A man came up the boards with a bundle settling into his forearm and judged it by the weight that came into the muscle there, and by nothing that could be set on a slate; a girl with a creel asked a cutter how many stalks went to a bundle, and he held it out at arm's length without once looking at her. “Lift it,” he said. “Your arm'll tell you. It'll tell you what went into it, and that's the whole of what it knows.” The girl lifted it and set it back and went on with her creel, carrying the answer away in the same muscle that had earned it. A record kept in an arm tells what was carried and not what it cost. He had spent two lives watching men hand records to one another, and had never yet seen one hand the cost across with them.

He lay at the low angle where the shade became a roof and the underside of things became the parts that mattered, and watched what the morning did with its hands.

The reed-cutter came along the boards again with a bundle on his shoulder and the short length of wool or reed-fibre cord inside his coat gone grey with the oil of a working thumb, the grey a stitch-hole takes after a thread has been pulled through it a dozen times. He set the bundle on the flat \place[the blessing stone]{blessing stone}, straightened the cord on his thumb, turned it once so the knots stood up in the small light, tied one low with his teeth and a pull of the thumb, bit the end off and put the cord back inside his coat, drawing the coat closed over it. A weir woman with salt at her wrists asked what the cord held.

“You can ask the cord,” he said.

“I can't read a cord.”

“No more can the Court.” He lifted the bundle off the stone and went to his trap with the question going down into the water after him.

No figure came out of it. No knot was counted, no name given, no hand laid on the cord by anyone but the man who owned it. The boy set the cord on a place of its own beside the other private records, and did not lay it across the place in the strip where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out on Seed 27, and did not lay it across the unspoken word either. Three likenesses in a row will stand up and call themselves a cause if there is nobody to hold them apart, and he was developing the habit of being the person who held them apart, which was an invisible labour and bought nothing that anyone could have entered.

\scenebreak

\person{Orra Weir} stood at the lower boards with her board on its side and a cord at her waist, turning the board with the wrist that had turned it ten thousand times and pulling one knot down with her thumb to set it among the others. Her hands were like old rope; the pale eel scars lay uncovered across both forearms, and the grey streak showed first through the black hair when she moved her head. The nasal half-song of the Fen came into her voice where she was tired, as it had at the blessing and every morning since, and she did not seem to know it was there.

A weir woman asked what the levy would take now the hands were out of the reeds.

“The ninth of Ilmen,” Orra said. “The collector's boat came round the bend on the flood and had six oars. One man stood at the stern and never touched them.”

“That isn't the levy.”

“It's a date. The river takes what the Court doesn't. You can shout at either one. I've tried.” She pulled another knot down.

A date given where a figure had been asked was an answer with the question taken out of it: a chart closed over a note nobody had opened, with the neat margin left standing where the finding should have gone. The man at the stern, keeping his hands off the oars while other men pulled, went on a place of his own, apart from the midwife's reaching hand and the thane's twice-wiped thumb, and the boy wrote no cause across the three. Her thumb went to the cord again as the sentence ended. He could not tell whether that was a lie or only the habit of counting, and did not try to tell; a thing done twice in front of him was a thing seen, and the not-knowing he kept whole.

The shape of the last knot on the cord was legible without its name: a double turn, tail tucked, tied left over right, with the small extra weight of a thumb pressed in. The word stayed where it had stayed since the year of the flower. She watched what there was to watch at the edge of the shade and got no answer from the basket, and nobody asked her and she did not ask.

At the outer boards \person{Mira Silt} worked the eels with slime dried in long grey strokes on her sleeves and river silt dried pale in the hollow of her neck, her head held a little to the right where the top of her left ear had been, her small smile kept on her face the way another woman keeps a door on the latch. Asked whether the run would be thin now the hands were in the reeds, she answered the question and then her own. “Thin? Who's to say thin? It comes when it comes. Ask the river.” She flicked a string of eel-bones against her thumb while deciding whether to lie, moved two from hand to hand, put one away, and said something low about a boat that had come up on the flood and nothing about the hand that had not come out of the strips.

A buyer in a coat with the river still in its hem named a figure for two pair. Mira's thumb went under her apron to the grey wool cord and stayed there longer than the figure required; shifting a creel showed the second cord twisted twice beneath it, never carried upriver to \place{Nine Pots}, never lifted at a door, the knots on neither counted by any thumb but her own and the knot for the thin child upriver untouched. “Two marks the pair and two short,” she said. “You've said it twice and I've counted it twice. There's nothing in the sentence that wants an answer.” The buyer took the eels and went with his figure unchallenged, and she did not look into the basket.

Her not-looking was a keeping set on a place of its own, the opposite of a pricing face turned the same way across a market, and he had no word in \lore{Grathic} for either. Mira's board lay beside Orra's knot-string and did not touch it. The reed-cutter's cord had gone back inside his coat with its knots unread. Three private records stood on the boards in wool and teeth and thumb, and the Court's neat slate waited a league downriver for a measure nobody had called. He could see every one of them and enter none of them, and the two boards lying side by side without touching held the whole lesson of the morning as plainly as a thing could be held and still be worth nothing to anyone who might have looked.

\scenebreak

At midday Sella carried him three doors down to \place{House Silt}, at the half-pace, giving the instruction to the wool and the river wind and never to the basket, and set the basket just inside the finger's-width-open door at the edge of the light and away from every place a slate might lie. She stood a half-pace off with her burn shut on nothing, opened no slate and turned no errand into a walk to \place{Slate Row}.

The room kept the shape it had kept since the first morning. The trodden ash floor had the spring of old bread under a boot; a grey ridge of \lore{Ash} ran along the skirting from the bench under the door into the next room, left where it lay; a chipped cup stood on the shelf. On the abandoned board-bed the fold of grey wool held only the hollow where the small pale river stone had once lain, and nobody said it was gone. Above the bed the round pewter tin kept its pale fold at the seam and its thumb-bright rub on the lid, shut. A low song came up through the floor from a woman working below, flattened out of sense by work, and in the back room a neighbour scrubbed at a board with lye water and did not look up at the writing, the tin or the fold.

\person{Alda Reed} came short with soap-and-milk gone cold under river silt and lye sour under the soap, her nails scrubbed to the quick with clay still under them, and laid her birth-slates writing-down on the bench before she turned to the fold. The hand went to the record before it went to the child, as her hand always went, and the boy watched the order of it from the low angle at which a shelf becomes a roof. She pressed the wool flat at his shoulder a breath longer than a fold requires, crossed two fingers at the red edge of the \lore[the birth-flower]{birth-flower} before she knew she had done it, and covered the sign with the date she had given \person[Dessa Silt]{Dessa}: “Stone seven. The account went up on the eighth, before the paper went out.” The date stood over a woman more mornings dead than any living person keeps count of and uncollected, and there was no living person who could take it off.

“Warm and breathing,” she said. “That's all I've set down.” It was exact and it was not true, and had not been true for three years.

Then she went on with the cooper's seventh in \place{Mud End}, a potter's third child in \place{Stonemouth}, the last high water, giving dates after the question had stopped, because dates are a handle on a thing too large to carry. A date laid over a sign is a cloth laid over a field before the instrument comes down and a hand lifted out of a field before the dressing goes on: the covering done with care, the field never opened, the account left standing in its neat margin. He knew the shape of it as he knew the shape of a clamp, and had no word in Grathic with which to hand it to anybody. He set it on its own place and called it neither kindness nor its opposite.

Her hand went once into her bodice to the little tin and came away without opening it. Her sixth narrow slate stayed face-down toward a floorboard in a house the boy could not name: six pages in a second hand no Court physician had ever seen, turned away from the light, read by nobody, with no seventh written. The mercy-lie curdled one day further against a death nobody could correct; the number stayed in a thumb gone into the water, and the name under the sieve-wire scar, and neither was spoken to him. Two private records in two materials stood apart in one room and were carried to no door.

Sella, within hearing of it all, asked nothing about the writing, the tin, the sign or the date; the half-pace and the turned slate stood in for the arrangement again, and neither woman looked toward the other. They were two careful hands entering the same child in two different accounts, and neither account had ever been shown to him, and \lore{the widow's mark} hung where it hung, because a midday is not an opening.

He was one hundred and thirty-one days old and nobody in the room had said so, or would have known what the number would count if somebody had. He set the morning's objects down where they belonged and with nothing laid across them: the reed-cutter's cord, Orra's knot-string, Mira's two cords with the thin child's knot untouched, the date over the sign, the six turned pages, the little tin in the bodice. Each sat on its own place. No figure had been read, no knot counted, no tin opened, no seventh written, no name taken from a dead face, no joining made. Orra's knot-string lay beside Mira's board without touching it; the reed-cutter's cord was gone back inside his coat with its knots unread; and in House Silt the pewter tin above the empty bed kept its thumb-bright rub shut.
