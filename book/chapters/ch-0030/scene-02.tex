The clay road came up from the square with the market noise following and losing hold, the way water dragged off a stone goes thin at the edges and then less than thin. \person{Sella Vail} carried \person[Corin]{the boy} at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, going around a board set out to dry as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, going wide also around the space beneath the lowest rack where the broken birth-slates lay and nobody went. The instruction went with them the way the wool went, given to the wool and to the river wind and never to the basket: keep it off his ears, it had come all the way down with them and would go all the way back up, and a basket set down was a pot set down and left. The little-door burn on the back of her hand closed on the wicker's edge and then on nothing. \place{Low Kiln} came back around them, wet wool and tallow smoke and the faint sour tang that had worked into the corners and would not come out with scrubbing. Cold arrived in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, the wool riding back from the small bare joint so the cold found the bare place and went under the fold at his ear. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours, and nobody under any roof in the district had been given an instruction about the thread, so it stayed where it had been put. He was one hundred and twenty-nine days old, four months and nine days, and nobody on the road said so.

At the \place{Vail kiln-house} she did not set him where the basket usually stood. She set it low and a little back, near the bench leg, at the angle at which a man lying on the floor sees the underside of a board that a man standing sees only the top of. It was not a place she chose for him; it was a place the light chose, coming in low through the finger's-width door and running along the grain under the shelf. She told the fire it was \lore{the thaw}. She gave the instruction to the pot and to the broom and never to the basket --- feed it, turn it, go around what the water left, it cost enough to come in and would cost more to take out --- and she did not look down into the basket. The boy lay at the low angle with cold iron at his cheek where the bench bracket had been bolted and left, and his head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far. The child in him was cold at the ear and wanted the crust two inches off in the fold; the man in him read the angle of the light and the grain of the board before he read either. Both were true. The second was quieter and the first was louder, and neither could be handed to anybody.

At the hearth she set the pot down where the light fell on its rim and turned the handle toward the side of the bench where a reaching hand would find it, and the pot gave its low note as it settled, full beneath the small flat sound that lived in it from the wheel. Six hearings of \lore{the full note} stood where they had stood, five from four sources and the sixth from a person, beside and never among, and he did not make a seventh of the pot under this roof. The wheel's own note came through the wall from the shed and went the same way, heard and not entered; the bell of that morning had been a told bell and a heard bell in two places, and three likenesses in a row will stand up and call themselves a cause if a man lets them. He let none. The surgeon's ear, which had once sorted a cracked vessel from a sound one across a crowded room, sorted the notes and filed them and could spend none of them; the child's ear was cold and wanted the crust.

Above him \place{the loaf shelf} held its row the way a row is held in a house that keeps its days badly: loaves pushed along it, a glance at the clay jar on the wall, and a space at the row's end that nobody called a number. The row kept two gaps. One was the old space at the end, the one that had come in with the planting and that a neighbour could name in one breath without ever seeing it as a thing that was missing. The other was a palm's breadth of bare board that had come in on the night of Seed 6 with no scrape, no crumb, no print and no word, as if a loaf had lifted itself off in the dark and gone. Sella moved her lips along the row and gave nobody a figure. Her glance went once to the clay jar on the wall and came back; the glaze was cold in the warm room, and she did not touch it. The fold at his ear kept one stiff brown-edged crust and an empty place beside it that had no name. That was a record out of the other room, the room under the roof where he slept, and the shelf did not touch it. Two rooms that did not touch. A saying of the loaf-shelves of Low Kiln had it that two gaps stay two gaps until a finger lays them together and an account then wants a name, and no finger in the district had laid these together.

From the low angle the underside of the shelf was a roof, and he lay under it the way a man lies under a bridge and reads the joints nobody thinks to look at from above. A shelf seen from below becomes a roof, and a roof is a thing a man keeps over him without reading the boards. He had spent a hundred and twenty-nine days under roofs and had never once been given a reason to turn one over.

There were marks on it.

Short strokes in charcoal, grouped in little clusters along the grain, some dark and pressed hard enough to leave a smooth track where the stick had dug, some the colour of old smoke and gone nearly into the wood. The clusters sat a finger's breadth apart, and the ones at the end of the row were fainter than the ones near the middle, as if the hand that made them had gone away from the task or had grown tired of pressing. They did not make \lore{Grathic} words and no slate had been set under them to catch what they meant. They were a keeping, put where nobody thought to look, above a row that was kept badly on top. The surgeon's eye went to them the way it went to a note turned down behind a chart --- not because the note was important, but because somebody had taken the trouble to set it where the chart would not show it, and the care told him more than the writing would. He saw the pressure of each cluster the way he had once seen the edge of a wound, dark where the hand had leaned and faint where it had lifted, and he knew before he had finished the first group that he would not be allowed to read them.

He did not read them. He did not count the strokes in a cluster, or hold the shapes in the dark where he could keep them, or turn his head to show them to a man from \place{Slate Row}, because there was no man from Slate Row under this roof and no hand of his that could have pointed. He did not lay the marks under the shelf beside the two gaps above it, or beside the one crust in the fold in the other room, or beside \person{Alda Reed}'s little tin with its six pages turned toward a floorboard, or \person{Mira Silt}'s cords with the second twist twisted twice beneath the grey, or \person{Orra Weir}'s knot-string with the word that would have named its last double turn still unspoken, or the round pewter tin above the abandoned board-bed that nobody entered, or the short grey cord the reed-cutter had put back inside his coat at \place{the Weir}. Neighbours had laid the fold's empty place and the shelf's old gap under one cause in a single breath more than once and gone off with their pots without seeing they had made two bare boards one absence; he had set each saying on a place of its own and had not made any of them his own. The marks under the board would only give those sayings company and let them stand up and call themselves a cause. He set the marks on a place of their own, advanced and unpaid, held for the far-off day when a hand not his laid the district's private records together and an account at last wanted its name.

It was the commonest kind of keeping in the district, and that was why he put it furthest from the others. A slate, a tin, a cord, a jar on a wall, a fist shut on a number --- those were things a person could be caught carrying, and a person caught carrying one looked for somewhere to put it. Marks on the underside of a shelf were set down by somebody who expected no one to look up, and a record nobody expects to be read is the hardest one to lay beside another without making the two agree. He did not know what the clusters counted, and not knowing was a reason to leave them where they lay rather than a reason to bring them near. In the other life he would have called it a note turned down and gone looking for who had turned it; here there was nobody to ask and no hand of his that could have asked, and the looking had nowhere to go.

A neighbour came in with a wet pot, her sleeves dark to the elbow and water standing in the creases of her wrists, and a smell of river water coming off her that was colder than the room, and she stood in the doorway holding the pot against her hip the way a woman holds a thing she has already set down in her mind. “What will the row want before the tend is out?” she said, and her eyes went to the shelf and not to the basket, because the shelf was where a woman looked for an answer in this house and the basket was a pot set down and left. Sella's hand went toward the clay jar on the wall and stopped, and came away without touching the cold glaze, and the end of the instruction went to the pot instead. “Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there,” she said. “The house has said it.” The neighbour carried the question off as level as she had carried the pot in, with nothing slopped and nothing given, and the water went on dripping from her sleeves into the trodden ash behind her and was gone into it. The boy set the asking and the answering on a place of their own: a cost asked in the form of a direction answered with a direction, arriving nowhere as a figure, the two lines meeting nowhere and leaving nobody entered.

The \lore{widow's mark} hung where it hung, a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and Sella did not touch it. She touched it before an opening and at no other hour, and a shelf was not an opening and a day in the middle of a tend was not an opening, so the mark stayed on its cord against her throat while the dry smell of the loaf shelf went up past it and the sharper smell of charcoal went under. The \lore{Ash} lay in the corner where the ridge had turned, the clean dark line and the new finer hairline beside it, unswept and called the thaw; she had told the fire it was the thaw and did not look toward the corner. She turned the coals with the back of the broken pot and gave the end of the instruction to the broom, and the house went on around its two absences without entering either.

Under the shelf the marks waited where they had been put. The boy lay at the low angle with the row above him standing with its two gaps and Sella between him and the board with her back to the basket, moving her lips along the row and counting nothing where nothing was to be counted. The low light went off the underside and the marks went dark in the corner where the grain turned, and the three things stayed each on its own place: the row above, the marks below, and the woman's back between. None of them became an account. He was one hundred and twenty-nine days old and nobody under this roof said so, and the marks under the shelf stayed unread and joined to nothing while the house went on with its day.
