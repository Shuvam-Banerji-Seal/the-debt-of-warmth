\daymark{199}{Ember 20, Y.H. 813 (Tenthday)}

The district had two names for its months and neither of them was wrong. Tallow was the firing month, Ember the kiln month, and a man who used both names in one breath was not corrected, because a correction is the first small entry in an account and there was no account in a pair of names. On the twentieth of Ember \place{the clay road} stood pale in the sun with the dust of a dry week, and \person{Sella Vail} kept the same half-pace from every open slate that she had kept on the first Account-day of the month, walking the same road the same distance to the same square with the same basket under her arm. The instruction went to the wool and to the river wind before it went to anything that might have answered it. “Keep it off his ears,” she said. She did not say it to the basket. The basket was carried without a word, the way a pot is carried that has been filled and must not be slopped, and \person[Corin]{the boy} inside it came into his hundred and ninety-ninth day with nobody under any roof saying so, six months and nineteen days, told by nobody, the count going on in the dark of the wool where no slate in \place{Sarn} could reach it.

At \place{the Cross} she set the basket in \place{the moot-post}'s finger's width of shade where no slate lay, the iron at his cheek cold enough to be a thing and not a promise, and she stood half a pace off, which was the distance at which a woman could be said to be near a thing without having spoken to it. She gave the wool its instruction again and gave the wind its instruction, and gave the basket no reason at all. Her hand went to the cord at her throat and stopped short of \lore{the widow's mark}; a reading was not an opening, and the mark had been touched once before the kiln on Ember 1 and at no shelf, well, road, basket or market since. Her other hand, the one with the little-door burn, shut half over nothing and opened again. She did not look into the basket. A woman who looks into a basket at a reading has begun to ask it a question, and a question asked of a basket goes where all such questions go in this district: to the pot, to the weather, to nobody who could be entered for the answer.

He lay at the low angle of the shade with the fold against his cheek and one leathery crust two inches from his ear, its empty place beside it still without a name and no third, the thread at his mouth frozen from the morning and unwiped because no instruction had ever come for the thread. Above him the square went on making its ordinary noise. Two hundred people leaned to \place{the reading block} with their backs to the sun, and eight pigs went over the wet boards with the mud throwing up in small dark fans, and a man with a goad kept his eyes on the pigs and not on the people, because a man who watches the crowd at a weighing is a man who wants to be entered for the pigs. The boards had been wetted to make the weights true, or so the saying went, and the water on them was called weather as the water in the bare trough was called weather, and neither was ever entered.

He had once stood at the edge of a field and decided which bodies would be carried and which would be left where they lay, and the crowd at a reading was a field of a different kind with the same order in it. He read the backs before he read the faces: the man with his collar held high and the cough he would not spend, the woman with clay dried pale in the creases of her wrists standing where the wind did not reach her, the child with the goad who was hungry and would be fed after the pigs. Nobody in the square knew that a finding was being taken off them in silence and entered nowhere, which was the only kind of finding he could still take. It was worth nothing. He took it anyway, the way he had once taken a blood pressure from a man already dying and written it on a chart nobody would read.

The bell went four, four and a gap. Nobody crossed himself at it. The boy had heard the bell of the Long Night told to him in a clean voice in a room he had never entered, and now he heard this bell with his own ear in a square he could not leave, and he kept the two beside each other and never laid one across the other, the heard beside the told as the told figure would presently lie beside the paid. The gap after the second four was not a fifth stroke and was not meant to be one. It was a place where a stroke had not come, and a place where a stroke had not come is not a stroke. Under a roof he knew well, \lore{the carried fire} would be drawing in fours and leaving the silence where the fifth should have closed, its payer without a name for a hundred and ninety-nine days; overhead in that room the boards kept four nails, four, and a knot the width of a thumb. Here at his ear the river kept its fifth low note of \lore{water over water}, a fifth thing that had never been counted among the hearings. Three counts in three places, and none rose up and became the others, because a man who wanted them to agree would have to put a payer under all three and no payer stood to any of them. He did not make a lesson of the gap. A lesson wants a teacher, and a teacher wants a payer.

\person{Berr Clay} came up short off the clay road a little before the top of the morning, the red wool cord of his office folded square under his coat instead of worn across it, and the green of tasted \lore{lead glaze} at one corner of his mouth where the habit of the old kiln-masters had left it. He smelled of hot clay and, under that, of nothing at all, which is the most expensive smell in \place{Grath}. His hands were large and clean. He wiped his thumb twice on his apron before he touched nothing, once for the slate and once for the figure that would be on it, and held a narrow slate unopened to his forearm with the writing turned toward his sleeve. A finger went over the figure. His eyes rested on it a breath longer than the writing required, the way a surgeon's eye rests on a finding before the chart is closed over it, and the finding stays what it was and the chart is neat. Sella, who had known his wife, read the green at his mouth as nerves; the masters read it as thoroughness. The boy, who had been a surgeon and was now a basket, read it as a habit with a taste in it and put it where a habit goes, not where a cause goes.

“One in twelve, as the notice says,” he said, low into the top of the crowd, and the crowd leaned in because a man who does not raise his voice makes people come to him. The figure went twice over the basket, once for the moot and once for the households that came late, and neither reading entered the basket or the boy in it. “The levy is called. It is not called by me.” To a man who had brought a number up out of a fist and stood with it half given, he said there would be a slate at the watch if \emph{your house} wanted it again --- your house, never I --- taking himself out of his own sentence as neatly as a hand goes out of a field before the dressing goes on. He believed he had done the district a kindness, and went off toward \place{High Kiln} with the box locked and the true subtraction inside it.

The district's arithmetic for the kiln month changed for the second time where two hundred people stood, and could not be changed back. The printed figure stood where it had been read, unchallenged, and a figure read aloud was not the figure paid. There was no shout at the reading and no hand raised against the slate. The pigs went on over the boards. Someone at the turnips put a figure down beside another figure and left them standing side by side without making them agree, which is the whole art of a market, and the boy took the shape of it in without entering it anywhere.

\scenebreak

The reading did not answer the man who had been carried out of High Kiln while the month was young. No figure knew what he had cost. No collector had been called for him and no slate carried his name up the road and back; no cause was spoken over him in \lore{Grathic}, where the words for a sickness of the breath were the words for a wrong wind and a man could die of a direction without anybody making an entry of it. The pale line at his gum stayed where it had been read, at two paces, exact, complete and worth nothing at two paces, on a place of its own. It was not joined to the sweet edge that came up off High Kiln on a wrong wind, nor to the dust ground small as meal in a wheel-keeper's creases, nor to the pot that gave its note before its time and sang itself out, nor to the sixth green at a silent woman's mouth, nor to the foundling's sliver, nor to a pan's gone ring or any empty place in a returning line. Three absences standing long enough would call themselves a cause and a cause would want a payer, and no payer stood to any of them. A breath kept is not an ending. He had watched a man go out of a yard breathing and go on breathing up the clay road, and he had entered no fate for him beyond that, because a surgeon who invents an ending for a body he cannot reach has started to write a story instead of a chart. The market sold over the place where the man had been as water goes over a stone that lies where a stone lies, and nobody stepped around it.

\person{Hessa Rale} came through before noon with Berr's narrow slate held edge-on to her forearm and the writing turned to her sleeve. Her coat was shortened at the skirt for the road's mud, and the mud had dried on it in a pale scurf that she did not brush off, because a runner's coat is a tool and nobody scours a tool for its own sake. She settled the second strap, let out for the long road, with a small flat correction of the wrist, and the thumb lay along the grain of the frame and pressed nothing where the writing was. Her hand went to the slate before her eyes went to the yard. That order had been filed once as the eighth such hand in the district and was not filed twice; eight likenesses left standing in a row would call themselves an account, and an account wants a payer.

Two women asked her what she carried. “I don't read it. I carry it,” she said. “There's a difference, and the road doesn't care which.” To the woman who wanted more she said, “Cross to \place{Greybank}. Reading goes up before noon. I've got a road to walk,” answering the question put and not the one behind it, without rudeness and without apology, and went on at the pace of a person who has made a bargain with distance and keeps it without thinking. No figure came off the slate. The boy watched the hand go and the eyes come after and kept the order on its own place, joined to none, a runner's hand carrying what somebody else had set down and never made answerable for the carrying. She was a runner and not a reader, and he did not make her into an answer to any question he had not asked.

Below \place{the north bridge}, at \place{the out-of-line landing}, the soft-cuffed clerk was still set for a measure nobody had called. His cuff was grey from washing and grey again by midday, and the slate lay against his forearm with the writing inward where no goer-by could take a figure off it. His hand went to the slate before his eyes went to \place{the stalls}. A buyer said a number, and the number went into the slate before the clerk looked up to find the man who had said it. The market sold over him as water over a stone, and nobody stepped around him and nobody took him off. A measure set down before anybody called it is a measure set down ahead of something, and ahead of something is a direction and not a rule; the boy kept the shape and made no rule of it. The clerk was not the grey-fingered collector, not the man at the Court's table, not the shape at the market's edge and not any other hand the boy had filed on a place of its own. He sat where he sat, and the step a hand's breadth out of line went under feet all morning without a fall.

\scenebreak

Across the square at \place{the pot board} a woman stood looking at pots with clay under her nails in small crescents and a pale line across the back of one hand where something had once been worn tight and long enough to leave its mark. The sweetish green of lead glaze lay in the crease beside her lip. She turned a cup a quarter without lifting it and set it back exactly where it had stood, said nothing, and went off with the light. The boy gave the colour no ear and the woman no name, and held the green as a colour joined to no ear, the sixth of its kind and not a seventh, with a finger kept between it and every green behind it. A colour that stands long enough will call itself a likeness, and a likeness that finds a payer becomes a cause; nothing had been paid and no cause stood up.

At the market's edge a small shape in cast-offs worn like a uniform stood with silt dried pale in the creases of its wrists and a gap where a front tooth had been. A green sliver of lead glaze on a string at its throat caught the light when it turned and went dull when it turned back. Its head stopped the half-beat toward a pot that had been set down and stopped in the middle of the turn, and a thumb went to the sliver and rubbed it. No sound came out of it that any clerk could have taken down. No loaf passed anywhere within reach of the basket, no slate came near it, no sale was paid. The boy held the shape where shapes go, after the knock and not before it, not a hearing and not a seventh sighting, and did not lay the green at its throat across the green at the pot board's mouth. A sixth green and a sixth half-turn standing together would call themselves a seventh of each, and a seventh wants a payer, and none stood to either.

From the low angle of the basket he saw the order of the hands before he saw what the hands were for. Berr's twice-wiped thumb and his finger passed over the figure before Berr's eyes took the crowd. Hessa's hand found the slate before her eyes took the yard. The soft-cuffed clerk's hand found his slate before his eyes took the stalls. Each order lay on a place of its own, and the two records going inward stayed two records going inward, the runner's hand carrying and the clerk's hand entering, with nothing laid across either. A row of such hands left standing long enough would call itself an account, and an account would go looking for a payer, and the boy knew better than to leave them standing that way. He had no hand to lay them out with and no slate to enter them on; he kept them by looking, which is a keeping that costs nothing and is worth nothing, and went on keeping it.

The crowd went back to the pigs. The bell's gap stood where it had been and nobody crossed himself at it. The market read the figure, the pigs, the eels, the turnips and the weather, and read everything except the child in the basket at the post's thin shade, whose hand could not close two inches on a pot handle and who had no number spoken over him that anybody had been asked to believe. Berr's box went up the clay road locked with the true subtraction inside. Hessa's slate went after it edge-on, the writing to her sleeve and no figure coming off it. The printed one in twelve stood where it had been read, unchallenged, and the figure read was not the figure paid, and the count in the wool went forward without a word spoken over it.
