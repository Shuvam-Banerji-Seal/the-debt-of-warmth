The afternoon came into \place[Vail kiln-house]{the kiln-house} sideways, through the door that stood a finger's width open, and lay along the floor in a thin strip of light that stopped short of the shelf. The board of \place{the loaf shelf} was bare where it had not been, and the bareness had the colour wood takes after it has held bread long enough to remember the shape of it. A neighbour came in with a dented pot against her hip and reached past the bare board without looking at it first, and her hand went to the board before her eyes went anywhere, the way a hand goes to a chart before the eyes go to a face. She opened the reach for the half-beat it takes to find what is not there, closed on the loaf beyond it, and only then let her eyes come down to the basket where \person[Corin]{the boy} lay. Her thumb left a pale print on the bare wood and went on with the loaf; the print did not stay the shape of anything.

“The old place at the end came in with the planting,” she said, and set the loaf in her pot. She said it to the board, or to the pot, and not to anybody standing there. “A row comes up short. A body gets used to it.”

To \person{Sella Vail}, who had come in behind her with a broom against her shoulder, the neighbour said what the planting would cost now that hands had gone into the strips. Sella moved her lips along the loaf row, pushed a loaf back to where it had been, came to the same place twice, and said the house had said it and the woman should ask the pot. She gave nobody a figure. The neighbour took her loaf and went with her pot, and the half-beat stayed where it had been, on the board, with nobody to enter it.

She went out with the pot held against her hip, and the dent in it caught the light and went dull as she turned; there was no slate on her and no cord at her throat, only a woman taking a loaf home in a pot, and the boy set her going on the tray where other hands had gone before her and wrote no cause across it. Three days ago a breath had held two absences; today one of them had been said alone. He did not supply the one she had left out. It would have been easy --- a child's empty place and a shelf's empty place laid side by side in the mind, with a line drawn through them and a clean hand at the foot to sign it --- and he had declined that hand for a hundred and four days with nobody in the house knowing he had been offered it.

He had learned to read the half-beat before the hand closed on what came after it. It was neither a long pause nor a short one; it was the pause a hand makes when it expects a thing and finds the place of a thing instead, and he had seen it in a midwife reaching for a slate before she reached for a child, in a clerk going to a record before he went to a face, in a woman at a well putting her hand out to keep a child from water. Here it ended in a loaf and a pot and a woman going out with both, and he kept the order on a place of its own. The hand had gone to the board before the eyes had gone to him, and that was the whole of it. There was no cause in it and he wrote none.

In the next room the fold lay on the bed with one brown-edged crust in it, stiff and gone past smelling of anything, and an empty place beside it nobody had named. Between the shelf's two gaps and the fold's one gap there was a wall, and the wall existed in his keeping and nowhere else. He set the shelf's absences on one place and the fold's absence on another and held them there with the only hand available, which was small and outside the wool. A likeness laid on a likeness grows a cause unless a hand holds them apart; he had learned what three likenesses in a row will do to a man who wants an answer, and he had spent his whole life declining to be the clean hand at the foot of the line.

\scenebreak

Sella had given the broom its instruction before she gave it to anybody and now she stood over the clean dark line where the old \lore{Ash} ridge had turned its corner, and over the new finer grey beside it, with the broom in her hand and the bristles resting on the boards. She swept neither. The new grey lay hair-thin along the skirting, finer than the old line and the colour of a pot washed out into water; the house called it \lore{the thaw} and said it would go when it goes. Outside, the yard had gone around what the water left and called the brown skin dry enough to bear a boot; here the two lines lay along the skirting with a clean board between them, and the broom stood against the wall in the hours when no hand was on it so that nobody would take it up by the handle and make an accident of sweeping. The boy had learned what the word did before he had learned what it was said over --- it let a person go home warm without owning the day --- and he kept the grey and the dark line on two places and did not let one become the other. He had seen a woman in this house lay one thumb in a ridge here and the same thumb in a ridge two doors down and carry the knowledge back without saying which was which; he had seen it and had not asked her what she meant by it, because a thumb laid in Ash is an entry in a hand no Court would read. The little-door burn on the back of her left hand closed on the broom handle and then on nothing, and nobody in the room asked what had closed it. The space under the lowest rack went around as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and the broken birth-slates beneath it were entered by nobody. He had no tongue for the question of which slate had been broken and no hand to point with, and beyond that he had decided not to want the answer, because a question that cannot be put becomes a place, and he had places enough.

Her hands were never still; when they had nothing to do they counted spoons, nails, the breaths of a sleeping child, and when the counting had nowhere to go the burn closed on the empty air over a slate and opened again. He had watched it close on a cup handle and on the well-rope and on nothing at all, and he had never once seen it close on the mark. The clay jar on the wall shelf stood cold under its glaze, turned to the little light that came through the door; she glanced at it once and said nothing over it, the way a man glances at a lock he has decided not to try. He had watched that glance for a hundred and four days and did not know what the jar held or why it stood on the wall shelf and not on the bench with the pots. The \lore{widow's mark} hung where it hung at her throat, a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it; people noticed the burn first and the mark last, which was the order she preferred, and she did not touch it now. A shelf was not an opening. A strip was not an opening. A bed was not an opening. She had lost a husband to the one thing in this house that was an opening and had never once described it that way herself; she touched the mark before the kiln was opened and at no other hour, and no opening had come in a hundred and four days.

“Feed first,” she told the fire, which was her husband's saying said to a fire and not to a person, and there was no I in it and no figure. She turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and told the fire it was the thaw, and the fire took the coal and gave back four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, as it had given on every day since he came under this roof. Its payer had gone unnamed for a hundred and four days. The house went on warm on an account nobody would own, and the boy set the missing fifth beside the four as an object and not as a hunger. The warmth came and nobody said whose coal it was; in another district a man might have set a figure beside a gift and called it paid, but here a gift that could not be answered could not be refused either, and the house kept both facts where it kept the clay jar --- on a shelf, within sight, never opened, never explained.

\scenebreak

At the going of the light she carried him up \place{the Clay Yard} at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, going around the space under the lowest rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. She had given the basket no instruction; the instruction had gone to the wool and the pot and the fire, and the basket was carried like a pot filled that must not be slopped. The yard smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and, under them, the faint sour mineral tang that came up from the unswept corner. Under the \place{Vail kiln-house} roof she set him on his bed and not in \place{the Debt Loft}, and put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without looking at his face.

He was one hundred and four days old, three months and fourteen days, and nobody under the roof knew it and nobody under the roof would have known what to count if they had; there was no word in \lore{Grathic} that would have carried the saying. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far. Cold arrived in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, and the wool rode back from the wrist so the cold found the bare place. She did not pull the wool down, because the instruction had gone to the wool and not to the wrist; the cold found the bare place and went on finding it, and he had learned the order of it the way a man learns the order of an instrument tray laid out in the dark --- what comes first, what comes after, what will be wanted before it is asked for. The crust was two inches from his ear and the bucket's lip two paces off, and neither was an opening, so neither was given. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had wiped it, because nobody had been given an instruction about the thread and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. He wanted the crust and could not lift a hand to it; the wanting was quiet now and the counting went on all the same.

In the fold one brown-edged crust lay stiff with its empty place unnamed and no third; in the next room the shelf kept its two gaps. He held them in two rooms that did not touch, and the holding produced nothing and was the whole of the work. A wall is not a thing a boy can build with a hand outside a fold of wool, and he had built one all the same out of not-looking and out of instructions given to pots and never to baskets.

\scenebreak

The door stood a finger's width open. Through the gap came the river's low muttered note from \place{the Ilm}, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, water going over water in a voice the district had no word for and called the thaw. Through the floor came the \lore{kiln's breath}, four, four and a gap under blind ash and the back of a broken pot. The two sounds came in two separate places and had come so for a hundred and four days, and he kept them so; a fifth sound laid beside four would make a count, and a count in this house wanted a payer nobody would name. The note came nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, which was a fact about water and not about anything else, and he kept it as a fact and not as a voice; the district had made one word do the work of the many it had not got around to naming. Four low pulls from the floor and a low turning from the door, and between them the width of a room and a wall that did not join them; he had lain awake on the first night he spent under this roof and heard the two in two places, and he heard them in two places now, and a hundred and four days had added nothing to them but the count.

The boards overhead kept four nails and four and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts did not meet. He had counted the nails on the first night he was laid under this roof and he counted them now, and the count was the only arithmetic in the house that stayed still while loaves went along a shelf and glances went around a yard and the days went badly kept.

His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down. He wanted the crust two inches from his ear, and the breast, and the bucket's lip, and the number of his days said aloud by somebody who would know what it counted, and he could reach none of them and could ask for none. The neighbour's half-beat lay on the board in the other room, and the old space at the row's end lay beside it, and the fold's crust lay stiff under the roof, and none of the three touched. Nothing was joined. Nothing was paid. Nothing was named. The count stood at one hundred and four, unspent, and the river went on over itself in the dark under the roof.
