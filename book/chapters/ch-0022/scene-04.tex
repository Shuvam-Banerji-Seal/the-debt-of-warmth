The afternoon went down the yard in the order the light always went, off the racks and across the trodden clay and into the little shade under the roof of \place{the yard well}, and \person[Corin]{the boy} was carried in it with the fold turned right under his chin. He was one hundred and five days old and going where he was taken. \person{Sella Vail} set the basket on the shaded side of the stone lip, where the cold came up off the wet stone and the rope's own shadow lay across the frost-writing on the rim, and went back to the fire before she went to anything else. The instruction went out ahead of her hand. “Go over it,” she said to the broom that stood against the well-house wall. “Don't leave the ruts. They'll hold water and call it weather.” Only after the broom had been spoken to did she tell the women where to put their feet, and she said the house had sent the word, and did not say I.

\person{Tarn} took the broom then, and not before. He took it the way a man takes a tool that has been handed to him by name, with both hands and a small inclination of the head that was not obedience to a person but obedience to the order of the house. The boy watched the order of it from the basket: the mouth on the instruction, the broom taking it, the man's hands coming after. He had seen that order in a midwife going to a slate before a child and in a clerk going to a record before a face, and here it ended in a broom that had been told its work before any human being had been told theirs. He wrote no cause across it.

At the well the argument went the way it always went, which was nowhere. A woman with her sleeves wet to the elbow said the thaw would come early and the pits would be worked before the Five were out; a man come up from the racks said a late thaw was a longer week of a body's own fire and that no honest house wanted its levy in the mud. The bucket went round and the argument went round with it, and neither of them touched the ground.

“Yes, house,” Tarn said to the first. “Yes, house,” he said to the second, and went back to his broom.

He agreed so entirely that no argument could find a foot. That was the whole of his defence and the whole of his debt: a man who assents to everything has given nothing away, because there is no side left for a slate to find him on. The boy had watched surgeons do the same thing in a room where two men wanted opposite ends of a patient and the chart had to come out clean; the trick was to agree with both and let the hand do what the hand had already decided. He had never once found the trick clean. Here it was done with a broom instead of a chart and cost the same.

And yet Tarn wanted the late. He had wanted it through the lean week and through the flood and through the mud the flood left, and he wanted it now with the paying already standing in the ruts where the water had put it. A late thaw puts off the paying. The paying had not been put off; it had stood there all week with its feet in the rut, and the house had gone around it with a glance and a broom exactly as it went around the clean dark line and the finer grey beside it. He wanted the late because wanting it cost him nothing and buying it would have cost him everything. The boy knew the shape of that wanting. It was the shape of a man holding his breath and calling it patience.

His chest had been tight since the dark. The boy saw it in the way the man set his strokes --- not short, not laboured, but with a small interval at the top of each reach where the breath should have gone in and did not. He knew the interval. He had set his hand on enough chests in a room where the ceiling kept the same height and the breathing did not: the ribs lifting and stopping short, the shoulders doing the work the diaphragm had given up, the face putting on an almost-smile because the body was telling everybody it was comfortable when it was not. Tarn's face had none of that almost-smile. His face did nothing at all, which was the more exact signal of the two.

The small bench knife lay on its sill. Six years stood in a mark on the handle that his thumb had worn almost flat, and the seventh was nearly gone and still held. It was the one entry in this house the house could not check. He had announced the knife to the empty yard before the light and would announce it again at the going of it, because a man who owns nothing must be seen to put the small things back; and to the kindling, which could not answer, he said what he said every morning. “Six counted. The next I don't count.”

The boy read the mark the way he had once read the corner of a chart turned down for a signature. Six clean strokes and a seventh worn to a smear, entered in a hand the house had never audited and never would. The house kept its days in loaves pushed along a shelf, in glances at a clay jar on the wall, in a space at the row's end nobody called a number; it kept its debts in slates with neat margins and a figure that went in and never came out. Here was a third account, kept in the crease of a thumb on a wooden handle, more exact than either and worth less to the man who kept it than the air in his chest. He had known accounts like it. A man will keep a perfect record of a thing he cannot spend just to prove that somebody, somewhere, is still keeping a record.

\lore{Ash} was worked into the creases of Tarn's knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line where the old ridge had turned its corner, and no scrubbing lifted it. He had scrubbed at it with the flat of the knife and with lye water and with the edge of his apron, and the creases had kept their grey the way a pot keeps the smell of something boiled in it years ago. The pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back --- a band of skin the colour of skimmed milk where the iron had sat in Y.H. 806, when he was bound, and the ring had come off and the pale place had stayed. He held it up to the afternoon without appearing to look at it, the way a man checks that a tool is where it should be, and the little light went into it and came out again.

He called everyone house. “I owe the yard a sweep, house,” he said to the trodden clay, and it was not a joke; it was a statement of fact made in the tone that invited somebody else to laugh so that he would not have to. Where another man said I am, Tarn said I owe. There was no word in \lore{Grathic} for the difference between the two, which was perhaps why he had never tried to say it. The boy had heard the grammar all his life in a country that had no word for it either: the speaker stepping out of his own sentence and leaving the debt standing in his place. He had never heard it done so completely. Tarn did not say what he was, or where he had been sent, or what he had found at the end of the road. He said what he owed. It was the only first-person sentence he owned and it was a debt.

The broom went over the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector, over and over, without once turning along it. It went down past the racks and over the rutted clay where the water had stood and back, and the bristles made their dry sound, and the man's head stayed where it was. The road led toward \place{the Fishstairs} and the north bridge and, beyond them, to the houses where a man with a slate and a cold tube would come from if a house had called him. No house had called him. The boy watched the broom go over the same ground and come back over it and go again, and understood that the sweeping was not cleaning. It was a hand passed over a place to keep it from being seen.

He had been sent down that road once, in \lore{the Long Cold}. He had come back at the frozen ford without going on, and told the house it had been done. The boy did not know the ford or the cold or whom he had been sent to fetch; he knew the sentence, because Tarn kept it where he kept the seventh year and entered neither. A man who has told a house that a thing was done and has not done it owns a sentence the house cannot check, just as he owns the mark on the knife handle. The two lay together in him like two instruments laid out on a towel that is never unfolded in daylight. He had carried that sentence for six years the way another man carries a stone in a pocket with a hole in it, and the pocket had worn thin and the stone had not gone.

A loan that can hear is a loan that can be called, and the sentence that would call it was never said under this roof. The boy knew why, with the exact and useless knowledge he had of every dangerous thing in this house: a man who can hear is a loan, and a loan that can hear is a loan that can be called, and Tarn had his two ears under this roof with the loan under the same dark, the two silences lying side by side without touching. Jorra had said nothing about the back room; Tarn had said nothing about the damper; neither had said the sentence that would turn a hearing into a debt due. The sentence lay under the roof like a tool set down in a dark room where a man knows its place by heart and will not go in after it. No collector came down the road. Something must give at the end of the seventh year; it did not give here, in the afternoon of the sixteenth of Seed, and the held breath pressed up to its edge and stayed there.

He knew the pressure. He had felt it in chests on tables in a room he no longer stood in, where the tank ran low and the decision had to be made before the next breath, and he had carried it once in his own chest on a road he did not name, with the snow coming sideways through the light. He did not let the remembering become a room. It was only the shape of a thing under his hand, an anatomy: the top of the breath held and held and the shoulders taking over and the face going quiet because quiet is what a body does when it has decided not to spend. Tarn went on sweeping. He was frightened, and because he was frightened he became helpful, doing three things at once and quietly putting the fourth right, and nobody under the roof said he was frightened and he did not say it himself. At the going of the light he set the broom away where it could be found, and the collector's road had been gone over without once being turned along.

At the going of the light she carried him up \place{the Clay Yard} at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, going around the space under \place[the drying racks]{the lowest rack} as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. Under \place{the Vail kiln-house} roof she set him on his bed and not in \place{the Debt Loft}, and put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without looking at his face. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had wiped it, because nobody had been given an instruction about the thread and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. Cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, and the wool did less than the wind wanted, riding back from the wrist so the cold found the bare place. He wanted the crust two inches from his ear, and the breast, and the bucket's lip two paces off, and the number of his days said aloud by somebody who would know what it counted, and he could reach none of them and could ask for none.

He checked the file the way a man turns out instruments on a towel. Six hearings of \lore{the full note} stayed six, five from four sources and the sixth out of a person, beside and never among and no seventh; three near-full answers stood advanced and not paid; three intervals lay on another tray; the word forty was unspoken with the readable double turn beside it and the weight of a thumb apart from both; four low things kept their four places with the river's note a fifth and not a hearing; the damper's double bite lay filed as an object and the loan that can hear was still uncalled. Nothing had moved. Nothing had been paid. The door stood a finger's width open and the river's low muttered note came through from \place{the Ilm}, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, and through the floor came \lore{the kiln's breath}, four, four and a gap under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, its payer unnamed, the two sounds in two separate places as they had come for a hundred and five days. The boards overhead kept four nails and four and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts did not meet.

On \place{the loaf shelf} in the next room the row kept its two unnamed gaps, and in the fold one brown-edged crust lay stiff with its empty place unnamed and no third. The clean dark line and the new finer grey lay unswept in the corner and were called \lore{the thaw} that would go when it went. \lore{The widow's mark} hung where it hung at her throat and was touched at no hour, because a bed, a shelf, a yard and a well are not openings. No collector had come down the road for the woman in the brook, and the morning had come all the same, and the afternoon had come after it, and the count stood at one hundred and five, unspent.

His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down. The small bench knife lay on its sill with six years in a mark his thumb had worn almost flat and the seventh nearly gone and still held, and the man who kept it had a tight chest and a held breath he had decided not to spend; the broom was set away and the collector's road had been gone over without once turning along it; under the roof the two sounds came in two separate places. Nothing was joined. Nothing was paid. Nothing was named. The count stood at one hundred and five, unspent, and the dark under the roof kept it without a figure.
