\daymark{104}{Seed 15, Y.H. 813}

\dropcap{T}{he brown} skin of the worked strips of \place{the barley fields} east of \place{Low Kiln} had kept the boot-print since the water went, drying into a crust that took a heel and gave it back slowly, and on the fifteenth of Seed the hands the strips had been said to want went into them at last. They went in stooped, a row of backs along the turned ground, and the seed came out of fists in handfuls that nobody called aloud. Brooms went around what the sweep had left --- around the stone the flood had set in the second strip, around the boot-print, around the place where the brown broke into the new finer grey the yard called the thaw --- and the bristles worked over the crust without once crossing the lines. \person[Corin]{The boy} was carried out through \place{the Clay Yard} and set at the edge of the work where he could see it and not be part of it, one hundred and four days old, three months and fourteen days, and nobody in the field had a figure for the hands, the strips or the seed. The work was done with a glance and a count of handfuls, and no number was spoken anywhere.

For a month the thing before him had been a direction, a place a man pointed at from the yard with his chin, and now it was worked ground with bodies in it, and the small lifting of that arrived before he could check it. The thing promised had come; he let himself feel it for the length of one breath. Then the cold came after, as it came after every warming in this district: the hands were here and the number was nowhere, and the only exact thing in the field was a boy who could not lift a hand to his own chin.

\scenebreak

A woman at the head of the first strip let the seed out of her fist a little at a time, and would not say what the fist held. She had counted it into the fist from a basket and let it out into the brown ground and said nothing over it, and when the woman next to her asked how much went to the strip she said the weather would have the last word, which was an answer that cost her nothing and could be entered nowhere. Chaff worked into the creases of her fingers as she went, pale dust in the lines of a hand that knew the seed by feel and not by count; when the fist was empty she closed it again on nothing and went to the next hole with the chaff still in its creases, and the basket was not lifted for a figure.

He read the emptied fist as a chart closed with a neat line and a signature. He had once stood over twenty bodies in the space of ninety seconds and decided, with a part of him that did not argue, which of them would be carried out, which would be left where they lay, and which would be given a mark and set aside for a man with more time; the sorting was a handful placed in a row and the row signed at the foot so that no one afterward could say the decision had not been made, and it had never once felt clean. That was the part of him that read the woman's fist now --- the same dry instrument brought to a seed-strip --- and the reading came exact and complete and worth nothing. It told him the shape of a count that had been made and shut and would not be said. It could not tell him the count, and if it could he would have had no tongue for it and no hand to write it, and the woman had gone to the next hole with the chaff in her creases.

He watched the order of her reaching: to the basket, then the fist, then the ground, and only at the end a glance back at the basket to see what was left. It was the order he had seen in a midwife going to a slate before a child, in a clerk going to a slate before a face, in a neighbour who reached for a shelf before she looked at him. Here it ended in a basket and a strip and nobody the worse for it, and he kept the order on a place of its own and wrote no cause across the distance between the hands. The river wind came off \place{the Ilm} and lifted the loose hair at the nape of his neck; cold arrived in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, and the wool rode back from the wrist so the cold found the bare place. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had been told to wipe it, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left, and nobody had given an instruction about the thread.

\scenebreak

A man stood at the head of the second strip with his hand on the wooden edge of a barrow and opened his mouth on the number of hands the work would want before it was done. He shaped it. The figure was in his teeth and the yard was leaning toward him to hear it, and then he looked at the strip --- at the turned brown ground, at the backs stooped over it, at the broom going around the stone --- and thought better of it, and shut his mouth, and put his weight back on the barrow. The figure went nowhere. It had come up out of the ground into a man's mouth and gone back into the ground without a slate in the field to take it, and he went off along the strip with the barrow, his hand still on the wooden edge, and did not once look toward the boy.

He knew the shape of such a thing. It was the shape of a number that goes from a fist to a mouth to a shoulder and arrives nowhere, except that this one had gone from the strip to a man's teeth and back to the strip, and nothing in the field had changed its figure because a man had nearly said it. He set the near-thing on the place where he set the others and did not join it to the woman's fist or to the barrow or to the direction he had carried for a month. A field that withholds its number in the very act of planting is no different from a market that spends a whole morning inside an account without once addressing the thing it counts, and there was \lore{Grathic} for the weather and for the thaw and none for this. The two sayings he had heard a month ago --- hands wanted by the morrow, the weather having the last word --- had stood then on two places, and he left them standing now and did not add the near-number to them and call it an answer. Three likenesses in a row will make a man who wants a figure go home satisfied with the thing he has made, and the only hand here was small and outside the wool.

\scenebreak

\person{Sella Vail} had given the bucket its instruction before anybody had asked and the broom its instruction before the broom was taken up, and she had given no instruction at all to the basket. “Go around,” she told the broom, the way another woman might have told it to sweep. “It cost enough to come in. It'll cost more to take out.” Only after the broom had been taken up did she tell the women where to put their feet. When one of them asked whose hands these were, she said the house had sent them, and did not say I. She kept her half-pace from every open slate in the yard, and went around the space under \place[the drying racks]{the lowest rack} as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and entered it at no point in the morning. \lore{The widow's mark} hung where it hung at her throat and was touched at no hour, because a strip, a rack and a yard are not openings and no opening had come.

When a woman asked how many days stood between this morning and the next tend, Sella moved her lips along \place[the loaf shelf]{the loaf row}, pushed a loaf back to where it had been, and came to the same place twice, and gave nobody a figure. The house would pay in loaves later; the house had always paid in loaves later, and nobody had said who had laid the loaves there. He watched her go around the slate and around the space under the rack at the same half-pace she kept from every open slate, and saw that the keeping and the planting were one act done in two materials, with a glance for the broom and a glance for the ground and no figure in either. She did not look into the basket and did not look toward him, and she said the house where another woman would have said I, and the field went on with its hands.

\scenebreak

\person{Berr Clay} came down off the north road at midday with the red wool cord of his office folded under his coat instead of worn across it, and the green of tasted glaze at one corner of his mouth, and hot clay over the smell of nothing at all, which is the most expensive smell in \place{Grath}. His hands were large and clean. He wiped his thumb twice on his apron before touching nothing, and held a narrow slate unopened against his forearm, passing a finger over the figure written on it and letting his eyes rest a breath longer than the writing required. “One in twelve, as the notice says,” he said low into the top of the work, and the nearest women leaned in because they had to, and went back to their holes. He said the levy was called and that it was not called by him.

To a man who brought a number up out of a fist and asked what the planting would cost now that hands had gone into it, Berr said a number goes into a slate, not into a strip; if the house wants it again, there'll be a slate at the watch. He said your house and not my account, and took himself out of his own sentence as neatly as a surgeon takes a hand out of a field before the dressing goes on. The man with the number in his fist carried it off again up the road and arrived nowhere with it, and Berr never once looked into the basket and went off toward \place{High Kiln} with the box in his house still locked and the true subtraction inside, believing he had done the district a kindness.

He kept the twice-wiped thumb on a place of its own. He had heard the order it came in --- thumb to apron, thumb to apron, finger to figure, speaker out of sentence --- and he did not join it to the woman's fist or the man's barrow or the slate at the watch; he kept such hands together on the tray where such hands lay and wrote no cause across them. The figure Berr had read aloud was not the figure Berr had paid, and the figure Berr had paid sat in a locked box in a house up the road, and the strip had not changed its figure because a man had looked at it. Nobody in the field crossed himself at any of it, and the brooms went on around the stone.

\scenebreak

He set the direction he had carried for a month beside the number the yard would not give, and held them on two places. He had held them there through the waiting and he held them there now, with the hands in the ground and the seed in the brown skin and not one figure spoken over any of it. The holding was the whole of the work and the work produced nothing, and the nothing was the exact price of keeping the two places clean. He was one hundred and four days old and nobody there knew it, and nobody there would have known what to count if they had; there was no word in Grathic that would have carried the saying.

His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the worked strip and came down. He wanted the brown-edged crust two inches from his ear under the roof, and the breast, and the bucket's lip, and the number of his days said aloud by somebody who would know what it counted, and he could reach none of them; the wanting was no louder now than the counting. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and the cold held at cheek and temple and wrist. The woman's fist closed on the seed with chaff in its creases and the number of hands went nowhere, and the man went off with the barrow and the brooms went around the stone, and the boy was carried at the edge with the direction and the number still two things on two places. Nothing was joined. Nothing was paid. Nothing was named. The count stood at one hundred and four, unspent, and the field went on with its hands.
