\daymark{99}{Seed 10, Y.H. 813}

The reading block at \place{the Cross} stood wet with the market's mud, the boards dark along their lower edges where eight pigs had been driven over them since first grey and the straw trampled into the seams behind them. The pigs were gone now into the noise beyond the post, but their going had left figures standing in the way a cart leaves a rut, and the men who had driven them stood around with their hands on their knees and said nothing about either. \person{Sella Vail} had set the basket in \place{the moot-post}'s finger's width of shade before the crowd thickened and carried it at a half-pace from every open slate on the way, giving the instruction to the wool --- keep the river wind off his ears, let the basket keep what it held --- and never to the basket itself. The little-door burn across the back of her hand closed on the wicker's edge and then on nothing. \person{Corin} lay at the edge of the shade with cold iron at his cheek where the post's iron bracket had been bolted and left, and the cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, with the wool riding back from the wrist so the cold found the bare place. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had been told to wipe it. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the noise of the market and came down. Nothing within two paces could be reached.

A Sill assessor with a slate under his arm stood at the block's end and spoke to nobody in particular about the weight of the things that had passed over it. “Eight over the board and eight off it,” he said, and wrote something down before anybody could ask him which eight he meant. A man at the rail said the pigs had been counted twice and the assessor said, “The slate has them once. That's enough,” and went back to his writing. The boy kept the order of it: the hand to the slate, then the eyes to the rail, then the mouth. He had seen that order in a midwife, at a well, behind a Court table, in a man who priced a basket, in a house that gave its instruction to a pot before a person, and now in an assessor with dry mud on his boots. He kept each instance on its own place and wrote no cause joining them. The crowd leaned in around the block the way wet wool leans toward a fire, without ever quite touching. \lore{Account-day} had come round again on the tenth of the tend and the tenth of Seed, and the district had turned out for it as it turned out for weather: not because it wanted the figure, but because the figure was where everybody stood. The morning went on in a low roar of pigs and voices and the slapping of boards. A woman near the basket had her hand out with a question in it and Sella said to the wool, “Keep it off his ears. It's the river wind,” and to the woman, “The house doesn't answer a figure.” She did not look down into the basket. The boy lay under the post and understood that a market could spend the whole morning inside an account and never once address the thing it counted. He had learned to recognise the shape of that omission the way he had once learned to recognise a chart closed over a neat line with a signature at the foot: an entry made, and nobody at the table who would say what it had cost.

\person{Berr Clay} came up to the block short, with the red wool cord of his office folded under his coat instead of worn across it and the green of tasted glaze at one corner of his mouth, smelling of hot clay and under it nothing at all, the most expensive smell in \place{Grath}. His hands were large and clean. He wiped his thumb twice on his apron before he touched nothing, and only then set the narrow slate flat on the reading block with the figure uppermost, the way a man sets a dish down where it will not be slopped. He did not look toward the basket in the post's shade.

“One in twelve, as the notice says,” he said, low, and the crowd had to lean in to hear him, which was why the roar stopped and why nobody ever thought to ask him to speak up. He said it twice, once for the moot and once for the households that had come late, and on the second reading a finger went over the figure to make certain it was still where he had put it. “The levy is called for Seed,” he said. “It is not called by me.”

To the man at the block's edge who had opened his mouth on a question, Berr said, “A number goes into a slate, not into a strip. If the house wants it again, there'll be a slate at the watch.” He said the house, never my account, and took himself out of his own sentence as neatly as a hand is drawn out of a field before the dressing goes on. The man closed his mouth. Nobody challenged the figure. A woman with a basket on her hip said, “It's one in twelve, then,” and said it again to the girl beside her, as though the repeating of it might make it weigh the same in two mouths; the girl said nothing. Berr set the slate down a second time and it gave back its small flat answer and he did not hear it. The boy heard it and filed it with the other flat answers, and would not call it a hearing of \lore{the full note}, because the thing that had answered was a slate and not a vessel. The figure stood where Berr had set it, and the notice stood on the post with the mud drying at its corners, and neither of them moved.

The true subtraction went over the top of the basket and was spoken by nobody. The man who had asked had come down from the strips with a number held up out of a fist, wanting to know what the planting would cost now that seed was in the ground and hands had gone into it and come back out again; the number went from his fist into Berr's mouth and from Berr's mouth onto the shoulder of a man going up toward \place{High Kiln}, and arrived nowhere. Nothing in the strips changed its figure because a figure had been said over it. A figure read aloud was not the figure paid, and a figure put into a slate was not a figure put into a strip. The boy kept the two facts on two places that did not touch, because he had learned in a better-lit room what a likeness laid on a likeness will do to a man who wants an answer. He was inside the account that went over him and had no standing in it; the figure had passed twice over the top of his basket and had not once come down to where he lay. He was the only adult in the square and the least use in it. He could read the arithmetic of a face turned the wrong way and could not turn his own head far enough to follow the man who carried his number up the road. The crowd went back to the pigs. The instant the figure had been spoken it changed nothing that any of them could carry home.

Berr passed his finger over the figure a final time, shut the slate against his forearm and went off up the clay road toward High Kiln with the box in his house locked and the true deduction inside it. He believed he had done the district a kindness. He had said the notice and the levy and the house, and had never once said I, and the boy had heard the whole grammar of it perfectly and could not have entered a word of it anywhere.

\scenebreak

In the afternoon the bell at the Cross went over the market, four strokes, four, and a place where the fifth should have been, longer than a church bell, and nobody crossed himself at it. The boy kept the heard bell on one place and the told bell of the Long Night on another, in the dark with no fire after dusk, and would not let the two touch although they were the same four and the same missing stroke. Sella shifted the basket a hand's breadth out of the draught and gave the instruction to the wool again, because the wind had shifted round with the afternoon, and said nothing at all about what had been read over it. To a woman who asked whose child it was she said, “The house brings him,” and went on with the market as though no figure had passed within a half-pace of the wicker. The curve of her burn shut on nothing and opened again. The boy wanted the number of his own days spoken by somebody who would have known what it counted, and got no more of it from the afternoon than from the morning. He kept ninety-nine where he kept the rest of it, edge to edge and exact.

At the Slate Row fringe of the square, where \place{Slate Row} was spoken of as a direction and nobody meant to go there that day, a man whose name was on no roll stood at the Court's table and handed a figure to a slate. He had the soft hands of a man who sits at a table and the patient face of a man who can wait in a doorway; his cuff was whole and clean and the slate under his hand was cleaner than the pigs' boards had ever been. He did not raise his voice. The clerk beside him wrote something down in a second hand and did not look up. The boy lay in the moot-post's thin shade and filed the soft hands on a place of their own. He did not join them to \person{Marra}, whom he had never seen in Sill, or to the Drawing collector with grey fingertips, or to the clerk behind the Court's table, or to the soft-cuffed clerk set at \place{the Fishstairs}. He filed them apart from all four, and apart from the child in cast-offs and the warm loaf going on gathering interest in an account the child could not read. He did not call it a sale. A better-lit room had taught him what three likenesses in a row will do to a man who wants an answer, and here were five hands reaching for a record before they reached for a person, and no hand among them that would lay the figure and the loaf together. The boy watched the way the man held the slate at the edge of the table where nobody else could see the figure as he wrote it, and the way his thumb rested on the corner afterward and did not pass over the number as Berr passed over his. Two men, two reaches for a record, and the difference between them was a thing he could read without a word in \lore{Grathic} for it and could not carry to any door. The soft hands went back into their coat and the man waited in the doorway of the afternoon as if waiting were a trade he had worked at for years. Nothing passed that any slate could have called a sale.

On the short turn down to the landing later, when Sella carried him to the edge of the fish-trade shade where no slate lay, the soft-cuffed clerk was still set at the steps with his cuff gone grey from washing and his slate held to his forearm for a measure nobody had called. His hand went to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls, and his eyes went to the stalls without any change in the face of him. A whole market walked carefully over the landing four, four and a hand's breadth out of line without making a fall of it, the way a house walks around a stone it has decided not to lift. Nobody touched the out-of-line place with a broom or a word. A woman came down with a creel on her hip and set her foot down carefully on the far side of it, and lifted the child beside her over rather than let him feel the give of it, and neither of them looked at the place they were stepping around. The boy watched the lifting and kept it on a place of its own, a care that would not become a cause. No sixth sighting of the neat dark hand came. The six hearings of the full note stayed six beside and never among --- five from four sources and the sixth out of a person --- and the word forty was not spoken in the market or anywhere within the sound of it. The boy kept his own turning toward a pot set down on its own tray and the clerk's order of reaching on another, and the soft hands on a third, and the three trays lay side by side and did not touch.

\scenebreak

At the going of the light Sella carried him up \place{the Clay Yard} at the half-pace from every open slate and set him on his bed under the \place{Vail kiln-house} roof, not in \place{the Debt Loft}. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without looking at his face and left the thread at his mouth frozen and unwiped, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left, and a market, a post, a step, a basket and a bed are not openings. She did not touch \lore{the widow's mark}; she had lost a husband to that danger and had never once described it that way herself.

The private file was checked the way a man checks instruments laid out on a towel, and it was unchanged. Six hearings of the full note stayed six beside and never among; three near-full answers advanced and not paid; three intervals on another tray; the word forty unspoken with the readable shape of the double turn beside it and the weight of a thumb apart from both; four low things in four places with the river's note of water over water a fifth and not a hearing; the two tellings of the shut counting room on their own tray joined to no kiln, likeness and difference kept, no third telling. The damper's double bite stayed filed as an object, read perfectly and worth nothing, and the loan that can hear stayed uncalled. The cold foot below the sill went on a place of its own, uncured and uncaused and unnamed, and the half-beat after the knock went on another, and the two places did not touch. Nothing in the file was paid.

She told the fire it was the thaw, turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and fed it, and the fire answered with four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been. \lore{the kiln's breath} came through the floor under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, its payer unnamed for ninety-nine days. In the corner the clean dark line lay where it had always lain and the new hairline of grey, finer than the old, lay beside it unswept and would go when it went. One brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold with the empty place beside it still unnamed, and no third came. \place{the loaf shelf} in the next room kept its two gaps and said nothing about either.

The door stood a finger's width open. Through the gap came the river's low muttered note from \place{the Ilm}, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, and through the floor came the fire's four, four and a gap, the two sounds in two separate places as they had come for ninety-nine days. The bell's fourth stroke had been missing where it should have been over the market, and the soft hands of the man from Slate Row had gone to a slate before his eyes went to the stalls, and both went onto their own trays and lay there with the rest, unspent. Far off in the square eight pigs had gone over a board and the figure on the slate was cleaner than the people it counted. The boards overhead kept four nails and four and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts did not meet. Tallow smoke and wet wool and the faint sour tang of the unswept corner came down under the roof with the dark. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water going over water and came down. He was ninety-nine days old and nobody had told him so, and the figure that had gone over him twice had not known his number. The arithmetic of the house stayed in loaves and glances and a space at the row's end nobody named. He kept ninety-nine exactly, and the count stood at ninety-nine, unspent.
