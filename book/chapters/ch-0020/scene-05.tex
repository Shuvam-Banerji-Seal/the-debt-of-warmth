\daymark{98}{Seed 9, Y.H. 813}

The water had gone out of the low houses and left its work in a child's foot. Below the sill the doorsteps stood dry above a pale line where the river had stopped, and the mud between them had set into a brown skin that took a boot and gave it back slowly. On a step three doors down a woman had a child across her knees and was winding wool round one foot, round and round, the way a woman winds a binding when she has decided the thing is only a cold and wants her hands free for the other one. The foot stayed dark inside the wool. She had wrapped it twice and it had not come back.

\person{Corin} lay in the basket at the edge of the dry ground with the fold laid back from his ear, and the river wind off \place{the Ilm} came over him in small pushes. \person{Sella Vail} had given the instruction to the wool before she set the basket down and to the water after --- keep the river wind off his ears, let the basket keep what it held --- and had given none to the basket itself. The little-door burn across the back of her hand closed on the wicker's edge and then on nothing. She stood at the half-pace from a slate propped against the step and did not go down to the child. The thread at the boy's mouth had frozen in the small hours and lay stiff against his chin; nobody had been told to wipe it. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far. Cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, and the wool had ridden back from the wrist so the cold found the bare place. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water going over water and came down. Nothing within two paces could be reached.

“She was in it up past her knee,” the woman on the step said, “out on \place{the plank walk} at \place{the Weir}, where the boards go over the water on posts. Went in when the water went and came back with that. It's a cold she's taken in it. It'll come back when it comes back. There's nothing in a foot but a cold.”

At the tubs a neighbour knocked the water out of a pot against the rim. “It's the weather,” she said. “It's been in everything this month. That's all it is. It'll go when it goes.”

Two words for one thing, and neither of them the thing. The boy kept them where they fell, the cold on one place and the weather on another, because the yard had one word for what a river did and one for what a body did and no habit of letting the two stand in the same sentence.

He knew the order of it. The ends went first --- the toes, the dark coming up, the blood drawing back toward the middle where a hand could still find heat --- and a person could be warm at the shin and still be going, and the face could put on that almost-smile that is not peace at all. The afterdrop waited on the far side of the warming nobody was going to perform; bring the foot back too fast and the cold would go to the heart and take the heart with it. He knew what should be done and in what order and how long it would take. It was an exact reading and it was worth nothing. He had no word in \lore{Grathic} in which to say any of it, and his hand was not two paces long. He lay at the edge of the shade and let the woman wind the wool, and the foot went on being a foot.

“It's not the cold going to her heart, is it?” the woman said to the neighbour, and answered it herself before anybody could. “No. It's a foot. A foot's a foot.”

It was told of \person{Sella Vail} in the yard that she had once sat three nights with a child of her own who cooled while everybody was kind, and that she had done every warmth the craft knew and the child had gone out of it anyway. She had not told the story herself and would not describe it that way. She shifted the basket a hand's breadth out of the draught and said to the wool, “Keep it off his ears. It's the river wind.” To the woman on the step she said, “The house doesn't go down to a foot.”

“Your house has had practice,” the woman said.

Sella got quieter, and the little-door burn shut on nothing. The boy did not lay the two together --- not the child on the step and not the three nights --- because a likeness laid on a likeness grows a cause unless a hand holds them apart, and there was no hand here that would do it, and certainly not his. The woman wound the wool a third time and lifted the child up onto her hip, and the foot hung dark below the wool and was not cured and was not named and was not entered. Below the sill the yard went back to its pots.

Midday at the edge of the \place{the Fishstairs} market, where the fish-trade shade ended and the open boards began, Sella set the basket at the last finger of shadow before the slate and gave the instruction to the wool and the water, never to the basket. There was no slate under the basket and none within a half-pace of it. A kiln-hand come down from \place{High Kiln} stood at the next board with salt dried white at his wrists and a pot in his hand, and a small foundling in cast-off clothes worn like a uniform stood inside the circle of his voice. The coat had a man's shoulders on it and the breeches were cut off at the knee; silt had dried pale in the creases of the child's wrists; there was a gap where a front tooth had been and he grinned through it, and on a string at his throat he carried a sliver of green lead-glaze that caught the light and went dull when he turned. The boy in the basket did not know his name, and the market required none.

The kiln-hand set the pot down on the board. It gave its small flat note, the sound of a thing with air under it and nothing in it, and the knuckle of the man's hand went with it. The boy's head went toward the note before the pot had finished settling. Half a beat after him the foundling's head went toward it too, after the knuckle and after the note and after the boy, and then stopped in the middle of the turn as if a hand had caught it. The child rubbed the green sliver with his thumb. He could not have said why he had turned, and his face said that he had not meant to, and the grin went on doing its work for him.

“That's a Vail pot,” the kiln-hand said. “It'll hold past its ---” He left the sentence where it stood and went for another pot, and the foundling opened his mouth on the tail of it. Nothing came out that a slate could have taken down. He shut it again, rubbed the sliver a second time, and went back to watching the grown faces with the patience of a child who has learned where the useful ones stand.

No new loaf passed that morning. No slate was opened within reach of the basket and no secret changed hands that a slate could have read. The warm loaf a man from \place{Slate Row} had put into the pocket of a cast-off coat on Silt 16 went on gathering interest in an account the child who carried it could not read; he had entered nothing and did not know he was owed anything. The boy kept the loaf on its own place, filed apart from the half-beat, and wrote no cause across the space between them, because he had learned in a better-lit room what three likenesses in a row will do to a man who wants an answer.

At the landing the soft-cuffed clerk still sat with his cuff gone grey from washing and his slate held to his forearm for a measure nobody had called. His hand went to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls, and a whole market walked carefully over the landing four, four and a hand's breadth out of line without making a fall of it. The boy kept that order on its tray and the half-beat on another, and neither tray touched the third on which he kept his own turning toward the pot. The foundling was not \person{Marra}; he was not the clerk at the landing and not the clerk behind the Court's table at \place{the Cross}. No sixth sighting of the neat dark hand came, and no vessel gave a seventh hearing: the six hearings of \lore{the full note} stayed six beside and never among --- five from four sources and the sixth out of a person --- and the word forty was not spoken in the market or anywhere within the sound of it.

He put the delay where he put such things, on a place of its own after the knock and not before it, not a hearing and not a seventh. A thing that came after the knock was a different object from a thing that came before it; the half-beat was a delay and the boy's own turning was a turning, and one did not become the other for being laid side by side. He had the only instrument there was, which was attention, and it could file the two shapes and could not spend either. He could read a failing foot better than any woman below the sill and could not put a hand two paces out; he could read the half-beat in a child's head better than the child could and could not ask what it was worth. Neither of them could spend what he noticed.

\scenebreak

At the going of the light Sella carried him up \place{the Clay Yard} at the half-pace from every open slate and set him on his bed under the \place{Vail kiln-house} roof, not in \place{the Debt Loft}. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without looking at his face and left the thread at his mouth frozen and unwiped, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left, and a market, a step, a basket and a bed are not openings. She did not touch \lore{the widow's mark}; she had lost a husband to that danger and had never once described it that way herself.

The private file was checked the way a man checks instruments laid out on a towel, and it was unchanged. Six hearings of the full note stayed six beside and never among; three near-full answers advanced and not paid; three intervals on another tray; the word forty unspoken with the readable shape of the double turn beside it and the weight of a thumb apart from both; four low things in four places with the river's note of water over water a fifth and not a hearing; the two tellings of the shut counting room on their own tray joined to no kiln, likeness and difference kept, no third telling. The damper's double bite stayed filed as an object, read perfectly and worth nothing, and the loan that can hear stayed uncalled. The cold foot below the sill went onto a place of its own, uncured, uncaused and unnamed, and the half-beat at the market went onto another, and the two places did not touch. Nothing in the file was paid.

She told the fire it was the thaw, turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and fed it, and the fire answered with four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been. \lore{the kiln's breath} came through the floor under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, its payer unnamed for ninety-eight days. In the corner the clean dark line lay where it had always lain and the new hairline of grey, finer than the old, lay beside it unswept and would go when it went. One brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold with the empty place beside it still unnamed, and no third came. \place{the loaf shelf} in the next room kept its two gaps and said nothing about either.

The door stood a finger's width open. Through the gap came the river's low muttered note from the Ilm, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, and through the floor came the fire's four, four and a gap, the two sounds in two separate places as they had come for ninety-eight days. Far below the sill a foot stayed dark inside three turns of wool on a place of its own; at the market's edge a child's head had gone half a beat after another head and stopped and could not say why, on another; here the boards overhead kept four nails and four and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts did not meet. Tallow smoke and wet wool and the faint sour tang of the unswept corner came down under the roof with the dark. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water going over water and came down. He was ninety-eight days old and nobody had told him so, and the count stood at ninety-eight, unspent.
