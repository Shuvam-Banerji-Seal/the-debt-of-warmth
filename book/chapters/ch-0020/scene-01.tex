\daymark{94}{Seed 5, Y.H. 813}

\dropcap{T}{he broom} went around the stone and not over it, the bristles setting into the brown skin the water had left and coming up with a small dry rasp, the way a hand goes round a sleeping animal and does not wake it. The stone was the size of a man's fist and had been trodden into the mud before the mud set, so that one side of it showed and the rest lay buried, and the woman with the broom pushed the bristles around it twice and then stood a moment longer than the stone required, looking at the ground where it sat. The light came up thin through the barley dust out on \place{the barley fields} east of \place{Low Kiln}, and every stalk that had been beaten down stood at its own angle, and the strips went away in long brown ranks with the seed already in them under a skin of silt. Nothing in the field was counted aloud. A woman's fist opened over a strip and let seed go, closed on the empty air above it, and came back with chaff in the creases.

\person{Sella Vail} gave the broom its instruction before anybody spoke. “Round it,” she said to the bristles, to the stone, to the brown skin holding the boot-print. “It cost enough to come in. It'll cost more to take out.” She said it low, without looking up, the way another woman might have said a grace over a table, and the woman with the broom went on around the stone and did not answer, because the instruction had been given to the broom and not to her and she had not taken it. Sella carried \person{Corin} at the edge of the work in a basket under a fold of wool, going along the strip at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, and she gave the basket no instruction at all. A basket set down is a pot set down and left, and a pot set down is not told what it is holding. When a woman at the next strip asked whose child it was, Sella said the house had brought him, which was an answer and not an answer; the little-door burn on the back of her hand closed on the basket's edge and then on nothing, because her hands had already gone back to the work.

The man with the barrow came up the thin track between the strips with the barrow empty and one wheel running askew, and said to nobody in particular that the strips would want hands before the week was out. The woman with the broom, not looking up from the stone, said the weather would have the last word. Between the two sayings lay the whole field, worked and waiting; hands went around as a word goes around, strips lay where they lay, and the seed had gone out of the fists into the ground without becoming a number by being covered. The boy kept the count because it was the only work available to him. He had no voice to spend on it and nobody had asked him for it.

From the low angle of the basket he watched the woman's fist come up empty. There had been a figure in it a moment before, a small measured amount the hand had known without counting, and now there was chaff and the shape of the giving left in the creases. He read the emptied fist the way he had once read a chart closed with a neat line drawn across the figures and a signature at the foot to make the two agree: a thing entered, a thing paid, both of them accounted for, and nobody in the room the wiser for what the line had covered. It was a clean reading. It was worth nothing. He had once reduced a shoulder in the dark and now could not lift his own hand off the wool; here was a whole worked strip accounted for in fists and looks, and the account would never be asked of him, and if it were he would have no word in \lore{Grathic} to give it and no hand with which to set it down.

The body interrupted the reading in its known order. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had been given an instruction about the thread, so it lay stiff against his chin and did not go away. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and the woman at the strip did not look up, because a head that goes where it wants is a thing a basket is used to. Cold came at cheek and then temple and then the bare place at the wrist where the wool rode back; the hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the low note of water beyond the fields and came down again. He was the only adult in the field and the least use in it. He knew the order in which warmth is given out and the order in which a body loses it, and both orders ran perfectly in a body he could not turn over.

The field went on around the basket without asking anything of it. The woman with the fist moved down the strip and the chaff in her creases brushed off against her skirt, and the bristles went over the last of the seed and covered it, so that the ground looked as though nothing had been done there all morning except a day's work done and gone. He set ninety-four edge to edge against the arithmetic he knew was waiting under the roof: loaves pushed along a shelf, a glance at the clay jar, the space at the row's end that nobody called a figure. None of those things would give him the number. They were the only arithmetic in Low Kiln and they kept nothing exactly. He kept ninety-four exactly and had no place to put it, which was the whole of his dignity and the whole of his use.

\scenebreak

By midday the light had thickened and they had come back into \place{the Clay Yard}, and Sella set the basket at the edge of \place{the yard well} with the cold iron of the handle two inches beyond his hand. She drew with her free hand and gave the bucket its instruction before she gave one to the women standing round: go down easy, come up level, leave the lip as you found it. The women went on with their talk. One of them, with wet sleeves pushed above the elbow and her hair plastered at the temple, asked what the planting would cost now that the seed was in the ground, as though a figure might rise out of the mud if it were asked for in the right place.

Sella moved her lips along \place{the loaf shelf} in the kiln-house doorway, counting what was there in the only arithmetic the house kept, and glanced at the clay jar on the wall shelf. The space at the end of the row she left where it was, with no figure put to it and no name. Then she looked at the woman. “Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there,” she said. “The house has said it.”

The woman looked at the pot, which had nothing to say, and went back to her bucket. No figure had passed between them. The question had gone into the yard and come back out as weather and a proverb, and the seed stood in the strips east of the town with its cost still unspoken. The boy lay in the fold at the edge of the well with the iron two inches from his knuckles and wanted the number of his days said aloud by somebody who would know what it counted. Nobody in the yard knew he had a number, and he could not have asked for it in a language anybody there would have understood him to be speaking.

\person{Tarn} took the broom only after Sella had given it its instruction, and went around the clean dark line at the Vail corner, and the new hairline of grey finer than the old lying beside it, and the space under the lowest of \place{the drying racks}, going as wide around the last as around a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. He swept none of the three. \lore{Ash} was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the line he would not cross, and no scrubbing lifted it; the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back. When he had finished the yard he took the broom over the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector, over and over, without once turning to look along it. He had been sent down that road once in the Long Cold and had come back at the frozen ford and told the house it had been done, and he kept that sentence where he kept the seventh year and entered neither.

The small bench knife lay on its sill with six years standing in a mark his thumb had worn almost flat and the seventh nearly gone and still held. To the kindling he said, “Six counted. The next I don't count,” and went on holding the breath he had decided not to spend. He agreed with the ones who wanted the thaw early and the ones who wanted it late, saying \emph{Yes, house} to both, and went on wanting the late one because it put off the paying, though the paying had stood in the ruts since the water went. A vessel gave its note somewhere past the door and his head did not turn and he did not name the sound, and the boy did not call it a seventh hearing.

\scenebreak

At the going of the light Sella carried him up the yard at the half-pace and set him on his bed under \place{the Vail kiln-house} roof, not in the Debt Loft. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without looking at his face and left the thread of drool frozen and unwiped, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left. Under the roof the private file was checked the way a man checks instruments laid out on a towel, and it was unchanged. Six hearings of \lore{the full note} stayed six beside and never among, five of them from four sources and the sixth, the small flat knock out of a woman who had been warm that morning, from a person; no seventh had come. Three near-full answers stood on their tray, advanced and not paid. The three intervals stayed on another. The word forty was unspoken, and the readable shape of the double turn stood beside it on its own place, and the weight of a thumb on wicker stood apart from both. Four low things kept their four places, with the river's note of water going over water a fifth thing and not a hearing. The two tellings of the room shut in the war stayed joined to no kiln, likeness kept and difference kept, breath through the nose against breath made in the throat, both men saying \emph{not the war's hand}. The loan that could hear stayed uncalled.

Sella told the fire it was the thaw and turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and fed it, and the fire answered with four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been. Its payer had gone unnamed for ninety-four days, and the coal that went up with the brine-gull was entered in no slate and missed in none. She did not touch \lore{the widow's mark}, because a strip, a shelf, a basket, a well and a bed are not openings and no opening had come into this house on Seed 5; she had lost a husband to that danger and had never once described it that way herself. In the corner the clean dark line lay where it had always lain and the new hairline of grey, finer than the old, lay beside it unswept and was called the thaw and would go when it went. Ash was worked into a man's knuckles in the colour of that line, and nobody fetched a broom for either.

One brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold with the empty place beside it still unnamed, and no third came. The place at the end of the loaf row in the kiln-house and the place at his ear lay in two rooms and did not touch. The boards overhead kept four nails and four and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts did not meet.

The door stood a finger's width open. Through the gap came the river's low muttered note from \place{the Ilm}, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, and through the floor came \lore{the kiln's breath}, four, four and a gap under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, the two sounds in two separate places as they had come for ninety-four days. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water going over water and came down. Nothing had been joined, paid, explained or ended. The boy was ninety-four days old and nobody had told him so, and the count stood at ninety-four, unspent, with the seed in the ground and no figure put to it and the fist that had let it out already forgotten by everybody but him.
