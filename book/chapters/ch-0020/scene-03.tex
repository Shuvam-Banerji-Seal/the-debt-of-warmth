\daymark{96}{Seed 7, Y.H. 813}

\place{the back room}, the room off \place{the Vail wheel-shed}, had kept its finger's width of open since before the first grey. Men had come in while the yard still held the small hours in its hollows and had spoken low behind it, and nobody had shut the door after them and nobody had opened it wider. It stood as it had stood on other mornings: a slit a finger could go through and no more, with the cold coming out of it in a thread and going nowhere the house could enter. \person{Jorra Vail} came through it with fine grey slate dust worked into both forearms past the bend of his little finger, not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel. He gave the house no name for the men and did not say the room was cold.

\person{Sella Vail} had set \person{Corin}'s basket at the edge of the light where the shed floor began, with the fold laid back from his ear because the wall here kept the wind off and did not keep off the cold. She had given the instruction to the wool before she set him down and none to the basket. The thread at his mouth had frozen again in the small hours and lay stiff against his chin, and nobody had been told to wipe it. The little-door burn on the back of her hand closed on the basket's edge and then on nothing, and she went to the fire.

Under the lowest of \place{the drying racks} the broken birth-slates lay where they had lain through the flood and through the planting, gone around as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it and entered by nobody. A woman sweeping toward them lifted her broom over the space instead of carrying it across and set the bristles down beyond, the way a person steps over a sleeping dog without looking at it. The boy kept Jorra's dusty forearms and the clean ones of other mornings on one tray, two states of one pair of arms, and asked neither which slate had been broken nor whether it had been a birth-slate. There was no word in \lore{Grathic} that would let him ask, and no standing to ask in if there had been. He had learned to keep a question whole and unspent for years; it was the one arithmetic in this house that nobody else could check and nobody else wanted.

Jorra spoke to the wheel before he spoke to anybody. “It doesn't care whose hands,” he said, laying his palm flat on the worn rim. “It cares if they're steady.”

The wheel took his weight and gave its low rhythm back, the note it had given since \person[Garl Vail]{Garl}'s seat went hollow three years ago, and the floor of the shed answered in the same key. He set a pot up and drew the shoulder thinner with two fingers and told it, “You'll do,” in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either. \lore{the full note} was in the pot already, whole before it left the wheel, and he knew it as certainly as he knew the bend in his little finger. He would not say so before a kiln-master come down from \place{High Kiln} with glaze drying on his sleeve, who would call it early and make something of it.

The kiln-master stood in the doorway with his glazed thumb held away from his coat and looked at the pot turning on the wheel. “That one's singing before it's fired,” he said. “Somebody'll make something of that.”

Jorra did not look up. “It'll do.” He lifted the pot off and set it on the bench, and the note went with it unsaid. The kiln-master waited the length of a breath for more, got nothing, and went back up the terrace with his thumb still held away from his coat. The household went on around the silence as cloth goes on around a weight taken out of it.

Sella had not finished telling him the fire was low. “Jorra, the fire's low---” she had got as far as the words, and his bent finger was already in the damper ahead of the sentence, going in before his hand, the cold iron taking the cold out of his fingertip as it took the heat out of the fire. He moved it a finger's breadth and held it there. The end of her instruction arrived at the pot and at the household instead of at him, and the room straightened around his silence the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted. She shut her mouth on what remained and gave it to the pot. “Lower, then. There's a bake on.”

From the height of the basket the boy watched the bent finger on the iron. He had read the thing in another life with his hands full and the field already lost: any iron that stops a thing from running is the same iron whatever it is that is stopped. A clamp closes a vessel and a clamp takes a hand. A damper holds the heat in a room that must keep it and shuts the breath off in a room that cannot spare it. The bite that kept the pot whole and the bite that would take a man's finger at the knuckle were one bite, and no word in Grathic held both mercies, and nobody under this roof could have been made to understand him if he had found one. He kept the reading where he kept the rest, an anatomy of the house's danger filed as an object: exact, and worth nothing. Three people in this house could read that iron and kept it to themselves for three different reasons --- Sella because a holding is not an opening and she had lost a husband to that danger, Jorra because a name is a thing a kiln-master can carry up the terrace and make something of, Tarn because a loan that can hear is a loan that can be called --- and the house went around all three and called the holding a holding and never a taking.

The two tellings of the room shut in the war stayed on their own tray, joined to no kiln under this roof: breath through the nose against breath made in the throat, both men saying \emph{not the war's hand}. The boy did not lay either telling alongside the iron in Jorra's hand. A likeness laid on a likeness grows a cause unless a hand holds them apart, and there was no hand in the basket. His own went up an inch toward the note of water beyond the door and came down, and the cold arrived at cheek and temple and the bare place at the wrist where the wool had ridden back. He wanted the crust two rooms away and could not reach the hem of the fold.

\scenebreak

In \place{the Clay Yard} the brown skin the flood left held a boot-print in its hollow, and the pale line where a woman had swept around the clean dark mark lay beside a hairline of grey finer than the old, unswept and called the thaw that would go when it went. \lore{Ash} was worked into the creases of \person{Tarn}'s knuckles in the colour of that clean dark line, and no scrubbing lifted it. The pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back.

He took the broom only after Sella had given it its instruction --- go around what the water left, because it had cost enough to come in and would cost more to take out --- and then went over the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector, over and over, without once turning along it. The small bench knife lay on its sill with six years standing in a mark his thumb had worn almost flat and the seventh nearly gone and still held. He had told the sill where it was before he told the yard, and the yard had not answered, and he was satisfied. To the kindling he said, “Six counted. The next I don't count,” and went on holding the breath he had decided not to spend, his chest tight with it under the loose coat.

A woman at the well said the thaw would come early and a man coming up the path said it would come late, and Tarn said “Yes, house” to each, agreeing so entirely that no argument could find a foot to stand on. He went on wanting the late one because it put off the paying, though the paying had stood in the ruts since the water went. He did not say what the paying was and the yard did not ask. He had been sent for a collector once in \lore{the Long Cold}, had come back at the frozen ford without one and told the house it had been done; he kept that sentence where he kept the seventh year and entered neither. A collector who never came was a collector nobody had asked for; an errand reported done was an errand nobody could check. The boy, carried at the edge of the yard with his head going where it wanted and making the small wet sound when it went too far, kept the knife's six and the man's sentence on two places that did not touch, because he had learned what happens when a likeness is laid on a likeness and left there in the sun.

A vessel gave its note somewhere past the door, the small flat sound of a thing set down out of sight. Tarn's head did not turn and he did not name the sound and did not look toward the basket. The boy did not call it a seventh hearing. Six hearings of the full note stayed six beside and never among --- five from four sources and the sixth, the small flat knock out of a woman who had been warm that morning, from a person --- with no seventh anywhere. Two ears and two silences kept their equality under one roof, the ear worth more than the debt that held it and therefore unsafe, and the loan that could hear stayed uncalled. Nobody said the sentence that would call it. \emph{He's not Vail} stayed sealed as tightly as a lid nobody had tried to lift in three years, and the promise of the foundling's name to the men who burned slates was neither given nor withdrawn. Jorra's dust was on its tray by itself and went to no slate; the men in the room off the shed kept the only name they had, which was none.

\scenebreak

At the going of the light Sella carried him up the yard at the half-pace from every open slate and set him on his bed under \place{the Vail kiln-house} roof, not in \place{the Debt Loft}. The private file was checked the way a man checks instruments laid out on a towel, and it was unchanged: six hearings stayed six, three near-full answers advanced and not paid, three intervals on another tray, the word forty unspoken with the readable shape of the double turn beside it and the weight of a thumb apart from both, four low things in four places with the river's note of water over water a fifth and not a hearing, the two tellings of the shut room on their own tray joined to no kiln. He wanted the number of his days said aloud by somebody who would know what it counted, and there was nobody under this roof who knew he had a number. Nothing was joined, paid, explained or ended.

She told the fire it was the thaw and turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and fed it, and the fire answered with four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been. \lore{the kiln's breath} came through the floor under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, its payer unnamed for ninety-six days; the coal that went up with the brine-gull was entered in no slate and missed in none. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without looking at his face and left the thread frozen and unwiped, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left, and a wheel-shed, a damper, a yard, a shelf and a bed are not openings. She did not touch \lore{the widow's mark}; she had lost a husband to that danger and had never once described it that way herself. Her private roll stayed unshown. In the corner the clean dark line lay where it had always lain and the new hairline of grey, finer than the old, lay beside it unswept and would go when it went. One brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold with the empty place beside it still unnamed, and no third came.

The door stood a finger's width open. Through the gap came the river's low muttered note from \place{the Ilm}, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, and through the floor came the fire's four, four and a gap, the two sounds in two separate places as they had come for ninety-six days. The boards overhead kept four nails and four and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts did not meet. In the shed the damper was cold under Jorra's bent finger, and the room off it stood a finger's width open with no name given to the men who had spoken in it and gone. Tallow smoke and wet wool and the faint sour tang of the unswept corner came down under the roof with the dark. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water going over water and came down. He was ninety-six days old and nobody had told him so, and the count stood at ninety-six, unspent.
