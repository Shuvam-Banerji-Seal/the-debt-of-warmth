## Chronicle

On the fifth of Seed the planting became waiting. Women went around the stone the flood had left in the strips east of Low Kiln; a fist let seed go and came back with chaff in its creases, and nobody said what the fist held. Sella Vail glanced at the clay jar and told the woman who asked to ask the pot what a wet thing costs, because the house had said it. The boy she carried, ninety-four days old, read the emptied fist as a chart closed with a neat line and a signature: a clean reading, worth nothing.

On the sixth the loaf shelf stood with two empty places where one had stood. A neighbour's hand passed over the new gap and closed on the loaf beyond without a second look; the house went around the absence. The shelf's gap and the empty place in the fold lay in rooms that did not touch, and nobody asked what had gone. Every warmth had a payer and none was entered; the carried fire missed its fifth breath under blind ash, its payer unnamed for ninety-five days.

On the seventh Jorra Vail came out of the back room, gave the men no name, and set his finger to the damper ahead of Sella's instruction; the household straightened around his silence. On the eighth Mira Silt answered a question with a question and showed a second cord twisted twice beneath the grey when a buyer named a figure; neither knot was counted. Three doors down Alda Reed crossed two fingers at the birth-flower, covered the sign with a dead woman's date and said warm and breathing --- exact, and not true.

On the ninth a child came out of the low water with a foot that stayed dark; one woman called it the cold and the yard called it weather. At the market's edge a foundling turned his head half a beat after the boy toward a pot and rubbed the green sliver. On the tenth, Account-day, Berr Clay read one in twelve twice, called the levy and said it was not called by him, then went off with the box locked and the true subtraction inside. A figure read aloud was not the figure paid; the bell went four, four and a gap, and nobody crossed himself.

On the eleventh the boy was one hundred days old, three months and ten days, and nobody said so. The shelf kept its two unnamed gaps, the fold its one stiff crust and empty place, and the new grey lay unswept and was called the thaw. Sella set him on his bed, put the fold right without looking at his face and left the thread frozen. The river's note came through the door and the fire's four, four and a gap through the floor, in two places as for a hundred days. Nothing was joined, paid, explained or ended. The count stood at one hundred, unspent.
