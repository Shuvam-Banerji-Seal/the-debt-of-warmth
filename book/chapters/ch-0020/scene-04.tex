\daymark{97}{Seed 8, Y.H. 813}

The fish-trade shade under \place{the north bridge} ended where the slate began, and \person{Sella Vail} set the basket at the end of it, in the last finger of shadow before the open boards. She had given the instruction to the wool before she set him down and to the water after --- keep the river wind off his ears; let the basket keep what it held --- and gave none to the basket itself. The little-door burn across the back of her hand closed on the wicker's edge and then on nothing, and she went to the eel boards. \person{Corin}, carried at the edge of the shade with the fold laid back from his ear, had the market's low roof of noise over him and the cold coming up through the boards under him. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and lay stiff against his chin. Nobody had been told to wipe it.

\person{Mira Silt} worked the eels at the outer boards with pale slime dried on her sleeves and river silt dried pale in the hollow of her neck, her head held a little to the right where the top of her left ear had been. Her small smile stayed on her face the way another woman keeps a door on the latch; it could be shut without anybody seeing the movement. From the height of the basket the boy read the set of her head the way a man reads an old injury in a shoulder: the body arranging itself around a thing that is not there. He could not have said what had been taken, and the reading was exact and worth nothing.

A woman come down the steps with salt white at her wrists asked whether the run would be thin now that the planting had begun, and Mira flicked an eel-bone against her thumb.

“Thin?” she said. “Who's to say thin? It comes when it comes. Ask the river.”

She moved two bones hand to hand while she decided whether to lie and put one away when the deciding was done. The weir woman went off with an answer and no figure in it. A buyer in a coat with the river still in its hem named a figure over the creel, and named it a second time, as if the second saying could make the first true.

“Two marks the pair,” he said.

“Two marks the pair and two short,” Mira said. “You've said it twice and I've counted it twice. There's nothing in that sentence that wants an answer.”

Her thumb went under her apron to the grey wool cord at her waist and stayed there while she decided, and the eel knife went on working without her looking at it. Shifting the creel to reach a basket showed the second cord twisted twice beneath the grey, the one that had never been carried upriver to \place{Nine Pots} and never lifted at a door. Neither knot was counted by any thumb but her own, and the knot she kept for the thin child upriver was not touched. Her board lay beside the knot-string of \person{Orra Weir} and did not touch it. Orra watched from where she watched and got no answer and had got none for three years; the boy could read the shape of that watching without its name.

Sella laid two marks on the board for the house and counted them with her eyes, not her fingers, and did not count the boy. The steps below the bridge came back four, four and a landing a hand's breadth out of line where the third step should have stood, and a whole market went over it carefully without making a fall of it. A girl with a creel crossed it sideways, caught the rail and went on selling. At the landing the soft-cuffed clerk still sat with his cuff gone grey from washing and his slate held to his forearm for a measure nobody had called, his hand going to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls. The boy kept that order on a tray of its own, filed apart from \person{Marra} with her neat dark hand, from the Drawing collector with his grey fingertips and from the clerk behind the Court's table at \place{the Cross} --- four hands that reached for the record before the person, four places, and no sixth sighting of the neat dark hand. No two of them touched.

Mira did not look into the basket. Sella had not expected her to and had not asked; the not-looking was a keeping, the same shape as the hand under the apron on the cord. At the strips the week before a man had priced him with a figure over a fist and had not looked into the basket either, and that not-looking had been a pricing. Two faces turned the same way meant opposite things, and there was no word in \lore{Grathic} that would hold either, let alone both. He set them on two places that did not touch, because he had learned what three likenesses in a row will do to a man who wants an answer, and he had spent ninety-seven days refusing to be that man. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water going over water and came down; the wool had ridden back from the wrist and the cold had found the bare place.

\scenebreak

Midday, three doors down in \place{House Silt}, Sella set the basket just inside the finger's-width-open door at the edge of the light where no slate lay, opened no slate, and turned no errand into a walk to \place{Slate Row}. She stood a half-pace off with her burn shut on nothing. In the back room a neighbour scrubbed at a board with lye water and a low song flattened out of its words by work; nobody looked up. From the low angle of the basket the round pewter tin above the abandoned board-bed kept its pale fold at the seam of the lid and its thumb-bright rub, unopened. Below it the fold of grey wool held only the hollow where the small pale river stone had lain, and nobody said the stone was gone. A chipped cup stood on the shelf by the wall and a grey ridge of \lore{Ash} ran along the skirting from the bench under the door into the next room. He had counted the nails in these boards long ago: four on the near board, four on the next, and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts had never met.

\person{Alda Reed} came short into the room with soap-and-milk gone cold under river silt and lye sour under the soap, her nails scrubbed to the quick with clay still under them, the birth-slates under her arm. She reached for the slates before she reached for the child and laid them writing-down on the bench; only then did she turn to the fold. She pressed the wool flat at his shoulder a breath longer than a fold required, and her hand went once inside her bodice to the little tin and came away without opening it. At the red edge of the \lore{birth-flower}, where the wool had ridden back from his wrist, she crossed two fingers before she knew she had done it, and covered the sign with the date she had given a woman more mornings dead than any living person keeps count of. The seventh of Stone. The account up on the eighth. No living person could take it off now.

“Warm and breathing,” she said. “That's all I've set down.”

It was exact and it was not true. Sella said, “He lies quiet,” and Alda went on with dates after the question had stopped --- the cooper's seventh in \place{Mud End}, a potter's third child in \place{Stonemouth}, the last high water --- because dates are a handle on a thing too large to carry. The boy watched the order of her reaching the way he had once watched a hand go for the chart before it went for the patient: the record first, the person after, the tin after that, in a house where no officer would think to look. It was the order of a woman who had delivered four hundred children and could not bear to look at the one she had lied to.

She took up a narrow slate and wrote on it what she would not leave lying, in a second hand no Court physician had seen, and turned it face-down into her shawl toward a floorboard in a house the boy could not name. Six pages now, all unread, the sixth set down weeks ago and not paid again, no seventh written. Two private records in two materials stood apart in this one room, one above the abandoned board-bed and one toward the floorboard, and neither was carried to a door or read by anybody. The boy kept them apart with the only instrument he had, which was attention, and it bought him nothing. He could read the shape of a kept thing in tin and in slate and in grey wool cord, and could enter none of them, while the woman who had made one of them stood over him with soap on her hands and did not look at his face.

\scenebreak

At the going of the light Sella carried him up \place{the Clay Yard} at the half-pace from every open slate and set him on his bed under the \place{Vail kiln-house} roof, not in \place{the Debt Loft}. The private file was checked the way a man checks instruments laid out on a towel, and it was unchanged: six hearings of \lore{the full note} stayed six beside and never among --- five from four sources and the sixth, the small flat knock out of a woman who had been warm that morning, from a person --- with no seventh anywhere; three near-full answers advanced and not paid, three intervals on another tray; the word forty unspoken with the readable shape of the double turn beside it and the weight of a thumb apart from both; four low things in four places with the river's note of water over water a fifth and not a hearing; the two tellings of the shut counting room on their own tray joined to no kiln, likeness and difference kept. A vessel had given its note past the door in the afternoon and neither man under this roof had turned toward it. The loan that could hear stayed uncalled.

She told the fire it was the thaw, turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and fed it, and the fire answered with four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been. \lore{the kiln's breath} came through the floor under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, its payer unnamed for ninety-seven days; the coal that went up with the brine-gull was entered in no slate and missed in none. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without looking at his face and left the thread frozen and unwiped, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left, and a market, a door, a shelf and a bed are not openings. She did not touch \lore{the widow's mark}; she had lost a husband to that danger and had never once described it that way herself. Her private roll stayed unshown. In the corner the clean dark line lay where it had always lain and the new hairline of grey, finer than the old, lay beside it unswept and would go when it went. One brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold with the empty place beside it still unnamed, and no third came.

The door stood a finger's width open. Through the gap came the river's low muttered note from \place{the Ilm}, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, and through the floor came the fire's four, four and a gap, the two sounds in two separate places as they had come for ninety-seven days. Far off, in a room he could not name, a pewter tin kept its thumb-bright rub under the dark; down at the Fishstairs a board lay beside another board and did not touch it, and beneath the grey of a woman's apron a second cord was twisted twice with neither knot counted. Here the boards overhead kept four nails and four and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts did not meet. Tallow smoke and wet wool and the faint sour tang of the unswept corner came down under the roof with the dark. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water going over water and came down. He was ninety-seven days old and nobody had told him so, and the count stood at ninety-seven, unspent.
