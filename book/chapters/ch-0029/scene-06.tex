\daymark{128}{Reed 9, Ninthday, Y.H. 813}

The morning came up the river from the east with a light in it thin enough to drink, and it laid the posts of \place{the plank walk} out one after another as clean as bones, and \person{Sella Vail} carried \person[Corin]{the boy} down the river road at the half-pace she kept from every open slate. She gave the instruction to the wool and the river wind and never to the basket: keep it off his ears, it had come all the way down with them and would go all the way back up, and a basket set down was a pot set down and left. Cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist. The wool rode back from the small bare joint and the cold found the place it had come for and went under the fold at his ear, and the thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had been given an instruction about the thread, so it stayed where it had been put. He was one hundred and twenty-eight days old, four months and eight days, and nobody on the road had said so. \place{Low Kiln} thinned behind them, \place{the reed beds} went grey to the left of the road in long ranks, and the district's doings went on without the number of his days anywhere in them.

By the time they reached \place{the Weir} the morning had come into view the way a room comes into view in the dark, by its smells first: salt dried white where the flood had touched the posts, cold fish, tallow smoke from \place{the Eel House}, and, under all of it, the running note of \place{the Ilm} going over its own stones. The traps stood in the gaps with their mouths upriver. Sella went around a slate propped against the bank as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, set the basket at the edge of the work where no slate lay, and stood a half-pace off. She did not touch \lore{the widow's mark} at a post, a stone, a trap or a board, because none of them was an opening, and she did not look into the basket.

The reed-cutter came along the plank walk with a bundle on his shoulder, walking the boards as a man walks a floor he has laid himself and knows where it gives. He set the bundle down on \place{the blessing stone}, flat and crossed and brushed clean of grit since Ilmen, and took from inside his coat a short length of wool or reed-fibre gone grey with handling. He straightened it on his thumb. He turned it once, and the knots stood up in the small light as a row of small hard facts standing up in a room where nobody else could see them. He tied one low with his teeth and a pull of the thumb, bit the end off, put the cord back inside his coat and drew the coat closed over it. He did not show it to anyone and he did not count it where anyone could hear him count. The boy took the shape of the hand in as a man takes the measure of an instrument laid on a towel before anyone names what it is for: a record kept in a material no thumb of \lore{the Court} could read, exact, complete, worth nothing to anyone who wanted a figure. It was grey with the oil of a working thumb, the grey a stitch-hole takes after the thread has been pulled through it a dozen times, and he read the greyness as the record of a hand that had been there long before any figure was needed, and would be there after the figure was forgotten.

A weir woman with salt dried white at her wrists stopped at the stone with a basket on her hip. “How many went up yesterday?” she said.

The reed-cutter did not turn the cord out of his coat. “You can ask the cord.”

“I can't read a cord.”

“No more can the Court.”

He lifted the bundle off the stone and went to the traps with it, and the question went down into the water with his arm. No figure came up. Nobody counted the knots; nobody gave the cord a name; and the hand that had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out on Seed 27 was not brought up out of the strip and set beside it, nor was the word forty spoken over the boards. The boy set the cord on a place of its own and did not join it to either.

\person{Orra Weir} stood at the lower boards with her board on its side and a cord at her waist, turning the board with the wrist that had turned it ten thousand times. Her hands were like old rope and the pale eel scars lay uncovered across both forearms; the grey streak showed first through the black hair when she moved her head. The nasal half-song she had never lost came into her voice where she was tired, and she gave a date where another woman would have given an opinion. She pulled one knot down with her thumb and set it among the others.

“What will the levy take now the hands are out of the reeds?” a woman at the rails asked, with her own answer already in her mouth.

“The ninth of Ilmen,” Orra said. “The collector's boat came round the bend on the flood and had six oars. One man stood at the stern and never touched them.”

“That isn't the levy.”

“It's a date. The river takes what the Court doesn't. You can shout at either one. I've tried.”

She went back to the knots. Her thumb went to the cord again as the sentence ended, and the boy could not tell whether that was a lie or only the habit of counting, and did not make a cause of it. A date given where a figure was asked was an answer with the question taken out of it, a chart closed over a note nobody had opened, and he set it where it belonged. A man who stands at the stern and keeps his hands off the oars is a man whose work is the counting of other men's pulling, and the boy set that untouched hand on a place of its own, apart from the midwife's reaching hand and the thane's twice-wiped thumb, and wrote no cause across the three. She watched what there was to watch at the edge of the shade and got no answer from the basket; she did not ask and nobody asked her.

Her knot-string lay beside \person{Mira Silt}'s board lower down the shade and did not touch it. The last knot on the cord showed its shape to the boy as a thing showable and not spendable: a double turn, the tail tucked, tied left over right, the small extra weight of a thumb pressed in. He knew the shape and had no name for it in any tongue he had, and the word that would have named it stayed where it had stayed since the year of the flower, unspoken on a morning that had a great many figures in it and not one he could enter. Three accounts of the district stood on the boards in front of him, kept in wool and teeth and thumb, and the Court's neat slate stood ready a league downriver for a measure nobody had called. He could see every one of them and enter none. The keeping was care and refusal in the same gesture, the double bite of \lore{the damper} under a roof downriver, the iron that holds a vessel whole and the iron that takes the hand off a man.

\scenebreak

Lower down the shade Mira worked the eels. The boy had watched her hands at this for more mornings than he could count and still could not tell, from the hand alone, which movement was the lie and which the truth; she made them the same movement on purpose. Pale slime dried in long grey strokes on her sleeves and river silt had dried pale in the hollow of her neck. She held her head a little to the right where the top of her left ear had been, and kept her small smile on her face the way another woman keeps a door on the latch: it could be shut without anybody noticing the movement.

“Will the run be thin now the hands are in the reeds?” a weir woman asked from the steps.

“Thin?” Mira said. “Who's to say thin? It comes when it comes. Ask the river.”

She flicked a string of eel-bones against her thumb while she decided whether to lie, moved two from hand to hand, put one away and said something low about a boat that had come up on the flood, and nothing about the hand that had not come out of the strips. The decision was taken and the slate was not written, and the boy set the hand on its tray and made no cause of it.

A buyer in a coat with the river still in its hem came down the shade and named a figure for two pair. Mira's thumb went under her apron to the grey wool cord and stayed there longer than the figure required, the way a hand stays on a vessel after the note has been heard, listening for the crack that does not come. She shifted a creel from one hip to the other, and the second cord twisted twice beneath the grey showed for a moment and went back under the apron. It had never been carried upriver to \place{Nine Pots} and had never been lifted at a door; neither knot on it had been counted by any thumb but her own, and the knot for the thin child upriver was untouched.

“Two marks the pair and two short,” she said. “You've said it twice and I've counted it twice. There's nothing in the sentence that wants an answer.”

The buyer took the eels and went. She did not look into the basket at the end of the shade. Her not-looking was a keeping set on a place of its own, the opposite of a pricing face across a market, and the boy had no word in \lore{Grathic} for either. Her board lay beside Orra's knot-string and did not touch it; the string of eel-bones went back into her pocket, and whatever the grey wool held went back under the apron with no figure read aloud over it. The smile had stayed shut while the figure went past and while the hand went under the apron, and the boy, who had watched a face held like that in a room where a number was being read, understood that it was not a mask put on but a door kept held, and that a door held shut is a thing somebody must stand beside all day.

\scenebreak

Below the bridge at \place{the Fishstairs}, on \place{the out-of-line landing}, the soft-cuffed clerk was still set for a measure nobody had called. His cuff was grey from washing and grey again by midday, and he held his slate to his forearm with one hand while the other went to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls, every time, as though the figure were due out of the market and not out of a man. Nobody had taken him off since the month turned, or if somebody had taken him off somebody had put him back in the dark. The boy had watched that order of the hand for many mornings and had never once seen it reversed; a clerk whose hand reaches the slate before his eyes reach the stalls is a clerk who already knows what he is waiting for, and a wait with no figure in it is the longest kind. The market sold its reeds and its eels over the top of him. A girl with a creel took the landing sideways, caught the rail and went on selling, and nobody made a fall of it. The steps below the bridge kept their four, four and a landing a hand's breadth out of line, and a whole market walked over them carefully without turning them into an accident.

The boy set the clerk on a place of his own. A measure set down before anybody called it was a measure set down ahead of something; that was a shape, not a rule, and he had learned not to make a rule of it. Hand to slate before eyes to stalls was the order a man uses when the writing is the work and the people are the interruption, and he had seen that order in a midwife reaching for a slate before a child, in a thane wiping his thumb twice before touching nothing, and in a woman reaching for a bare board before she looked at him. Three likenesses in a row will make a cause in a man who wants one, and he had learned in two lives what a cause grows from: a hand laid on a hand, a clean line drawn at the foot of a page. He laid no line. The clerk went on holding his slate for the measure nobody had called, and the measure went on not coming.

The cord went back inside the reed-cutter's coat with its knots unread and its figure given to nobody. Orra's knot-string lay beside Mira's board and did not touch it. Mira's second twist lay under the grey wool with its knots uncounted and the thin child's knot untouched. The clerk's slate lay on his forearm with a figure on it that belonged to no hand in the strip. Four accounts in materials the Court could not spell, and the boy held them all, exact and complete and worth nothing, with one hand outside the wool that could not close two inches on the rim of the basket. Through the finger's-width of shade beside the boards came the river's note of water over water, a fifth low thing on a place of its own, never a hearing and never among the six hearings of \lore{the full note}. The market went on selling over the man with the slate as though a man with a slate were a thing every landing had, and the count went on where it had stood all morning, unspent and spoken by nobody.
