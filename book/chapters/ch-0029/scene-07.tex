\person[Corin]{The boy} was carried up from \place{the Fishstairs} in the slope of the afternoon, when the light lay along the yard in long thin strips and the river wind had come round to the east. \person{Sella Vail} carried him at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, and gave the instruction to the wool and the river wind and never to the basket: keep it off his ears, it had come all the way down with them and would go all the way back up, and a basket set down was a pot set down and left. The wool rode back from the small bare joint and the cold found the place it had come for and went under the fold at his ear in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist. The thread at his mouth had frozen the night before and stayed where it had been put, because nobody under any roof in the district had been given an instruction about the thread. He was one hundred and twenty-eight days old, four months and eight days, and nobody on the road up had said so.

Three doors up the yard she set the basket just inside the finger's-width-open door of \place{House Silt}, at the edge of the light and away from every place a slate might lie, and stood a half-pace off. She opened no slate and turned no errand into a walk to \place{Slate Row}. The front room smelled of soap-and-milk gone cold under river silt, and under the soap the sour mineral tang of lye, and under that the faint dry tang of \lore{Ash} that had worked into the floor and would not come out of it with scrubbing. The floor was trodden ash packed until it had the spring of old bread, and a grey ridge of it ran along the skirting from the bench under the door into the next room, where a woman's low song came up through the boards with its words flattened out by work. Above the abandoned board-bed a round pewter tin kept its pale fold at the seam and a thumb-bright rub on the lid, shut, and the fold of grey wool below held only the hollow where a small pale river stone had once lain. Nobody said either thing was gone.

At a tub in the back room a neighbour scrubbed at a board with lye water and did not look up at the bench, the tin or the fold pressed too long. The song went on under the door and along the floor with its words worn flat, and came up through the soles of the room the way warmth comes up through a cracked pot, by degrees and with a loss nobody entered. Sella gave the instruction to the pot on the bench and never to the basket, and said the house where another woman would have said \emph{I}, and stood with her little-door burn shut on nothing.

\person{Alda Reed} came short into the room with the birth-slates under her arm, smelling of soap and warm milk gone cold under river silt and lye sour under the soap, her nails scrubbed to the quick with clay still under them. She went to the bench before she went to the basket. That was the order of her hand, the record before the person, and he had watched it so often that he could have told the number of steps between the two without counting them: a hand to the slate, then a hand to the child, never the other way about. She laid the birth-slates writing-down on the bench, in the place within her right hand, and only then turned to the fold.

She pressed the wool flat at his shoulder a breath too long. She pressed it with the same two fingers that had made the sign, and the sign came before she knew she had made it, two fingers crossed at the red edge of the \lore{birth-flower} where it ran from the third rib down the forearm and stopped short of the wrist. \lore{Grathic} had a name for the mark and no name for what she did at its edge; he had learned the one and never found the other. She covered the sign with the date she had given the woman: \emph{Stone seven. The account went up on the eighth, before the paper went out.} The date lay over the mark the way a clean line lies over a note nobody has opened. It was exact. It was not true. It stood now over a woman more mornings dead than any living person keeps count of, and no living person could take it off.

“Warm and breathing, that's all I've set down,” Alda said, in the tone a woman uses to state the weather.

She went on with dates after the question had stopped, because dates were a handle on a thing too large to carry: a cooper's seventh in \place{Mud End}, a potter's third child in \place{Stonemouth}, the last high water, the exact day of a birth in a house nobody in that room would ever enter. The dates came faster than dates had any business coming, and the room arranged itself around them the way a market arranges itself around a reading it has not understood. He watched her hand go to the tin once and come away, and knew the shape of that gesture as the shape of a hand that has learned to stop short of the thing it wants to open. A date laid over a sign is a chart closed over a note nobody has read; the clean writing at the foot of it says nothing about what lies under, and a man who trusted the writing would go away satisfied. He had seen a hand cover a field with a cloth before bringing the instrument down, and had seen the same hand lift out of the field before the dressing went on, and had learned from both that the covering and the taking-out are one motion performed on a person who cannot object. Alda's date was such a cloth, laid down over a woman who could no longer pull it off.

On a narrow slate she wrote what she would not leave lying, in a second hand that no physician of \lore{the Court} had ever seen, and turned it writing-down into her grey shawl toward a floorboard in a house the boy could not name. Six pages lay there turned toward the floor and read by nobody, written one at a time across three years, and no seventh had been added on this day.

He kept the three things in three places. The pewter tin above the empty bed held one record in a material no officer's thumb would open, the little tin under the shawl held another, and the grey wool below the first held the shape of a warmth that had gone out of a fold without ever being carried to a door. The hollow in that wool was the only object in the room his sorrow could attach to; in the life he had lived before this one a grief with no name in the country it was spoken in went looking for a thing it could hold, and here the thing was a pale shape pressed into grey wool by a small stone that had long since been carried away. He laid none of the three together. The surgeon in him noted the pressure of the fold at his shoulder, a breath over what a fold required, and the exact instant the two fingers moved; the child in him was cold at the ear and wanted the crust that was not in this room. Both were true. The second was louder, and neither could be handed back.

At the going of the light Sella carried him up \place{the clay road} at the half-pace and set him on his bed under the roof and not in \place{the Debt Loft}. Under the lowest rack the broken birth-slates lay where the yard had gone around them for a year, entered by nobody, and she went around the space as she would go around a pail set where a child might knock it: a look, a wide step, no number and no name. The boy read the going-around as a chart closed over a note nobody had opened, and set it on a place of its own beside the place in the strip where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out on Seed 27; he laid no likeness between them and gave neither a figure. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without once looking at his face, and left the thread of drool frozen and unwiped. Her hand rested a half-beat longer than the setting needed before it went away, the way a hand rests on a thing it has carried a long way and cannot quite bring itself to let go of; the little-door burn closed on nothing. The clean dark line where the Ash ridge had turned lay unswept at the corner, and the new finer hairline of grey beside it lay unswept too, and she told the fire it was \lore{the thaw} and would go when it goes.

She turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and fed them, and said “The house will keep it” where another woman would have said \emph{I}, and stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand counting something under her breath toward no opening. She pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn, and the grey streak at her temple showed pale off the coals, and she set the pot where it could be found in the dark, rim out and handle toward the reaching hand. She did not look toward the bed. She did not touch \lore{the widow's mark}, because a dusk is not an opening, and went to her own bed.

The fire had come up into this house in a clay dish before he was a month old, passed from hearth to hearth along the yard with a coal under ash, and no house had said where it was first lit or who had paid for the burning. It had kept its four low breaths through every morning since and missed the fifth in every four, warming the boards by degrees and taking its coal from somewhere it would not name. She fed it and told it the weather and went to her bed, and the house held what it held without ever entering the warmth.

Under the roof the dark came on in the order it always came on, first the light going out of the oiled paper at the window, then the fire's low red, then the sound of water. Through the finger's-width of the door came the river's note of water over water, beyond \place{the reed beds} and \place{the Weir} and the whole length of \place{Low Kiln}, a fifth low thing he had kept on a place of its own since he was six days old, never a hearing and never counted among the six hearings of \lore{the full note}. Through the floor under blind ash the carried fire took its coal and gave back four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, and its payer had been unnamed for a hundred and twenty-eight days and would be unnamed on the morrow. Above him the boards kept four nails, four, and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts in three places, and none of them became one.

The bell at \place{the Cross} was a told bell and stayed beside the heard bell in a place of its own; the note of water was a fifth low thing and stayed beside the four pulls in another; the knot in the boards was a knot the width of a thumb and had never been counted as a nail. He had learned in two lives how readily three likenesses in a row will stand up and call themselves a cause, and he kept the three counts lying flat where they lay, as a man keeps instruments separated on a towel so that no edge is mistaken for another.

He turned out his file as a man lays instruments on a towel before anyone calls what the operation is for. \place{the loaf shelf} in the next room kept its two unnamed gaps, the old space at the row's end and the palm's breadth that had come in on the night of Seed 6 with no scrape, crumb, print or word; the fold at his ear kept one stiff brown-edged crust and an empty place beside it that had no name. Two rooms that did not touch, and a saying of the loaf-shelves of Low Kiln that two gaps stay two gaps until a finger lays them together and an account then wants a name; no finger in the district had laid these together. Neighbours had laid the shelf's old space and the place at his ear under one cause in a single breath more than once, their hands going to the board before their eyes found him, and he had set each saying on a place of its own and had not made their joining his own. No third gap had come. No fourth likeness had been made into a cause.

Six hearings of the full note stood where they had stood: five from four sources, a cup across a birth room, a pot in \place[the Vail wheel-shed]{the wheel-shed} through a wall, the district's slate twice at the Cross, a man giving at \lore[the Hearth Drawing]{the Drawing}, and the sixth, the small flat knock out of a woman as the men lifted her, filed beside the five and never among them. No seventh had come in three days, and he had not invented one to fill the empty place. Three near-full answers stood on their own tray, advanced and not paid: a cup across a birth room, a pot under the wheel-shed wall, a rack pot lifted a hand's breadth too high and set down again. On the interval tray lay the intervals from Stone 13, Stone 17 and Stone 25, with the half-beat in a man's broom behind the standing finger, the stroke that went on over the flagstones without the check, his own held breath from the market, and the two stoppings of the near-saying: the sentence about an ear worth more than the debt that held it brought twice to the threshold of the word that would call the loan and stopped twice there with no figure and no name. They lay as objects among objects and not as a rule; the loan that could hear was uncalled and the seventh year had not given. The word that would have named the shape of the last knot on \person{Orra Weir}'s cord stayed unspoken with the shape of the forty-first knot still legible beside it. The cold foot below the sill was still the cold to one woman and weather to the yard. The green at a mouth's corner was still a colour and not a name. The clerk at the landing still held his slate for a measure nobody had called.

The reed-cutter's cord had gone back inside his coat with its knots unread and its figure given to nobody; \person[Mira Silt]{Mira}'s grey cord and the second twist twisted twice beneath it had not been lifted at a door, her board lying beside Orra's knot-string without touching it; \person[Berr Clay]{Berr}'s narrow slate held a figure that did not know the hand that had not come back. Every one of them was a record kept in a material no Court thumb could spell, and he held them all, exact, complete and worth nothing, and could spend none of them.

He was one hundred and twenty-eight days old and nobody had said so. The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down. The count went on where it had stood the night before, exact, complete, unspent and spoken by nobody, while the dark went on keeping what it kept and the day crossed into the morrow without being opened, joined, named, paid or ended.
