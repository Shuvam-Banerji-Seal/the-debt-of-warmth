\daymark{42}{Ilmen 13, Y.H. 813 (Thirdday)}

The yard had dried at its edges by mid-morning, dry enough that a broom left a mark, and \person{Sella Vail} carried the basket three doors down with a clay jar held under her apron and the fold tucked at the boy's chin. She went neither to \place{the drying racks} nor to the road. She went to \place{House Silt}, whose door had stood a finger's width open for as long as the yard could remember, where a woman had borne a child in Stone and did not lie in the room now. The jar had gone over with milk in it before the woman went into the water and had not come back; Sella had thought of it at the fire and come for it without saying she had thought of it, and the basket went along because there was nowhere else to put him while a house needed doing. She carried him as she carried a pot filled --- level, with the wrist doing the levelling and the eye on the threshold ahead --- and gave the instruction to the wool before she gave it to the door. Keep it off his ears. It's the river wind.

The neighbour who had the room now opened the door wider with the side of her hand and went back to her tub without looking up. Water stood in the tub with a grey rim at it, and the floor by the hearth was wet where she had worked at it with a cloth, the trodden ash gone dark in patches the colour of slip mixed too thin. Her sleeves were pushed above her elbows and her hands were red at the knuckles, and she worked with her back to the door in the way a woman works who has decided that a basket carried in is not a thing that requires her to stop. Sella set him just inside the threshold, where a pot would be set out of the draught, turned him with her knee until the fold lay right at his chin, and did not look into the basket afterward.

\person{Corin}, forty-two days old, lay where a pot lay and took the room in from the height of a pot.

The bench stood by the door with a pot on it, and the pot had a chip in its rim where somebody had set it down hard in another year and nobody had mended; a chip was left showing here so that people could see a thing had been handled. A draught came under the door and went along the floor to the corner where the wall met the hearth, and it got to his ankles before the cold got to his cheek, which was the order cold took in a room with a door that did not shut. The board-bed stood against the far wall with a fold of grey wool on it lying in the shape of something put down and never taken up; a stool stood where a stool had stood; the birth-slate leaned on the bench with its writing turned under. Nobody turned it. From the floor, shelves are higher than they are from a bed. That was all the thing was at first: a fact of geography, like the distance from the threshold to the tub, or the number of boards between a door and a hearth that a man could count if he had a hand free to count with.

There was a tin on the shelf above the bed.

It was no bigger than a span of two fingers: a round tin the colour of old pewter, its lid shoved a little aside so that a pale fold showed at the seam --- cloth, or paper, or the edge of something that had been folded until it remembered the fold. A thumb had rubbed one place on the lid bright. He knew the shape of the lid, the pale showing at the seam and the rubbed place, and he knew from the angle at which the shelf sat above the bed that a woman lying there could have seen the tin without lifting her head. He did not know what was in it. He did not know that something had been warmed at another fire, across the yard, and carried into this room in a rag, and folded up afterward and put where she could see it from the bed. If there was writing on the tin he could not have read it, and if a man with a slate had stood beside him he would not have asked the man to read it, because a thing handed to an officer is a thing gone out of the room. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the shelf and came down. Shelves are high from a basket.

A man who had spent twelve years opening bodies could have said what that pale fold was and why it had been put where it was put, could have named it in three plain words and been right. He could not say any of them. He could not turn his head far enough to hold the shelf while Sella moved past it, and when his head went where it wanted and made its small wet sound, the tin went out of view and came back as he came back, the way a thing does when it is the only thing at that height. The thread at his mouth gathered cold and nobody wiped it, because a basket set down is a pot set down and cannot be lifted again for a chin. He knew the rubbed place on the lid was made by a thumb going there often, often enough to take the darkness off, and he knew a thumb goes like that only to a thing its owner means to see again. That was the whole of what he was given: a thumb's habit and the height of a shelf. Everything else he would have to be told, and there was nobody in the room who could tell him, and nobody in the room who knew he needed telling.

He entered it nowhere.

Not as a refusal. A refusal needs a hand to refuse with. A slate on a bench can be lifted and read aloud by a man who never held what it counts, but a tin on a shelf above a bed stays where it was put, and an assessor walking through with a roll under his arm would write down a shelf, a bed, a pot with a chip in it and a draught going to the corner, and nothing else. The district had entered him three times without asking: once under a roof, once on a slate read in the square, once in a count of bits kept in a woman's thumb. It had not entered this. He had watched \person{Alda Reed} turn a narrow slate writing-down toward a floorboard in a house he could not name, and watched \person{Mira Silt} keep two cords under an apron with the knots on neither counted, and here was a third kind of keeping in a material no officer would think to read, above a bed in a room nobody entered. Three keepings, three places. He did not lay them side by side and call them one thing. He had made that mistake once in a better-lit room, and had carried it into every room since.

The neighbour scrubbed on. The water went into the cracks between the boards and came back grey. “It's up again behind the racks,” she said to the pot. “It'll be in the yard by night.” She said it as women said everything in this district that they did not want to own, and went on with her cloth. Sella found the jar on the shelf below the other shelf, beside a chipped cup and a little heap of nothing that had once been something, and put it under her apron. She opened no slate. She sent nobody toward \place{Slate Row}, and did not turn the fetching of a clay jar into a walk to a bank, and did not ask after a figure or a collector or a day. There was no errand in her face in that direction. When she lifted the basket again she gave the instruction to the wool and not to the neighbour, and the neighbour did not look up, and the two women passed the whole visit without saying anything either one of them would have entered.

Nobody said the woman's name. Nobody said the number that had stayed in her thumb, nor the four loaves, nor the seventh of Stone in eight hundred and twelve, nor the nineteen steps. The lie told for mercy stood where it had been left: a child spent among children she had never seen, gone in the levy three winters ago, which had been kind when it was spoken and was a fact now that nobody alive could correct. Sella stood with her little-door burn shut on nothing and looked at the bed for the space it takes to put a jar under an apron, and the neighbour went on with her cloth, and the boy lay at the threshold and kept the fact where no correction could reach it. He had once let an institution prefer its version of a thing to the thing itself because the version fitted on a chart, and had carried the letting for the rest of a life. Here the version had gone into the water and come out on the bank as a fact, and the room it had been kept in was being scrubbed by a woman who did not once look at the shelf.

He had five rooms now, and kept them in five places. The birth room he had been carried out of on the third morning; the room under the Vail roof with the kiln breathing behind it; the death-house two doors down where the ridge turned a corner a finger thicker; the Court's room that had come to him as a voice through a doorway, with a place in the figures where a line should stand and did not; and the room two doors the other way where a measure had been taken on Stone 22 and the door had stood this same finger's width open. A board-bed, a bench by the door, a pot on the bench, a draught going to the corner, and a gap where a fifth thing would stand. This was the first of the five again, emptied, with the scrub-water grey in the cracks and a tin on a shelf, and he set it back where it already lay and did not let it join the others. A likeness was a likeness and a cause was a thing that had to be earned, and he had no standing to earn one with his hand outside the wool.

\scenebreak

The walk back up the yard was the length of a pot carried carefully. The \place{Clay Yard} was doing its ordinary work: a woman at a tub, a man going down toward the pits with a basket on his shoulder, \place{the Slip Brook} behind the racks with more water in it than the day before and the colour of slip mixed too thin going over more water in a small continuance that had sound in it. The neighbour's words came up behind him and went into the yard's one word for it. Weather. Sella carried him at a half-pace from every open slate and did not stop where any man stood reading. At the kiln she set the jar down, not on the bench where the pot stood, but on the shelf by the wall, and wiped her hands on her apron and went to the fire before she went to anything else.

Under the \place{Vail kiln-house} roof the afternoon went on in the small sounds it always made. The kiln took its breath in fours and missed one; the salt-stiff coat kept its shape on its peg behind the door and the hollow seat kept the leftward lean of twenty years, and the clean dark line where \lore{the Ash} had turned its corner held nothing and was not swept. Sella turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and did not touch \lore{the widow's mark}, because a bank was not an opening, and the carried fire lay under blind ash and kept the boards warm by degrees, four, four and a gap. The boy lay on his bed with the fold at his ear. In the fold the two crusts lay as they had lain through five mornings: one gone past smelling of anything since Stone 15, one still giving a thin breath of barley smoke when the wind turned off the water. Neither was eaten, lifted or named, and no third came. He wanted the smoky one. The wanting was louder than the knowing, as it had been every morning of his life, and the knowing had nowhere to go.

He kept the tin where he kept things with no hour: not on a shelf with the dates, not beside the sixth hearing of \lore{the full note}, which lay beside the five from four sources and was not among them, but in the place where a shape was kept before it had a name and might never get one. From there it sat above an empty bed with a pale fold showing at the seam of a lid and a thumb-rubbed place shining, and no assessor had entered it and no collector would. Five like rooms stood in five separate places and did not touch. The boy was forty-two days old and the only adult in the room and the least use in it, and his hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth behind him and came down.
