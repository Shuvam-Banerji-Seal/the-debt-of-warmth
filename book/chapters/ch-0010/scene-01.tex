\daymark{38}{Ilmen 9, Y.H. 813 (Ninthday)}

\dropcap{T}{he clay} had come up in the night and taken the woman who stood at the end of nineteen steps. It had come behind \place{the drying racks}, where \place{the Slip Brook} ran higher than a brook had any business running in Ilmen, under a film that no longer held and with the colour of slip mixed too thin going over more water in a small continuance that had sound in it. By the time there was light enough to see, the bank had changed shape. Grey silt lay in a rim along the grass the width of a thumb; a willow stake that had stood upright through the deep cold leaned out into the current; the water had taken on the clay-heavy look of the pits and carried it down toward \place{the Ilm}. \person{Sella Vail} held the basket on the higher ground with the fold tucked at the boy's chin, and she had turned it so that his face went toward the bank and not away from it, as she would have turned a pot so that the light fell where she wanted it. She had not set him down. She stood with her feet apart on the wet clay and both hands under him, and the little-door burn on the back of her hand closed on the wicker and shut.

\person{Corin} was thirty-eight days old. He could not move. He could not speak. He could not look away.

The women at the racks had left their work and come down. Two of them were in the water to their knees and a third held a rope, and a man from the pits came down the nineteen steps at a run and went in without taking his boots off, which is a thing a man does only when there is no time to take his boots off. Nobody called out. That was the first thing the boy registered, that the yard had gone quiet in a way that was not the quiet of early morning but the quiet of people who had come to a thing they had no words for and had decided, without a word, to do it with their hands. Woodsmoke went up somewhere behind him. A pot stood on a rack out of its own shadow. The ordinary work of the yard went on at its edges, because the ordinary work of a yard does not stop for one woman, and the boy lay in the basket and took in the whole of \place{Low Kiln} with the part of him that had once stood at the edge of a different kind of room and decided, in ninety seconds, which bodies were worth opening. His head went where it wanted and made its small wet sound and came back; the cold came in its known order, cheek and temple and wrist; the thread at his mouth was not wiped, because a basket held in two hands is a pot held in two hands and cannot be set down to wipe a chin.

He knew what he was looking at before anyone lifted her. The face had gone the colour of old rope, and the clay had got into the creases beside the mouth and would not come out; there was a wedge of wet wool at the shoulder that had been a petticoat hem; the hands were small and quick and never clean, and one of them was still half shut as though it had closed on something and had not finished closing. Severe cold puts a look on a face that passes for composure, and water puts another look on it that passes for sleep, and he had learned long ago in a room with better light not to trust either. There would be no bringing this one back. He knew the order of it the way he knew the back of his own hand: the cold, the struggling, the gasping that stops, the quiet after, and then the long work of a body handed over to the water the way a vessel hands its work to the next thing and the next until the skin holds more than it was made to hold. He knew it exactly. It was the most exact knowledge he had, and it was the least use.

Then the sound came.

It was a small flat knock, no bigger than a knuckle rapped on a board. It came out of the body as the men lifted, the sound a pot makes when it goes all the way through and the two halves of the crack speak to each other at last --- the note with no ring in it, the note that stops where a full note would carry on. It was not loud. A woman at the tubs said nothing; the man with the rope said nothing; the boy heard it because he heard that sound the way other men hear their own names.

It was the sixth.

He did not explain it. He had never once been able to explain the sound to anybody, and there was no word in \lore{Grathic} for it and no ear in the yard that would have known what he meant if there had been. He had held five hearings of \lore{the full note} from four sources across thirty-eight days: a cup set down across the birth room, a pot on a bench in the Vail wheel-shed through the wall, the district's own slate twice at \place{the Cross}, and a man giving under a cold iron tube at the Drawing --- five from four sources, one of them the first that had ever come out of a person. This was the second from a person. It went beside the five and not among them. It was not a seventh; there had never been a seventh. It was not the nearly-full answer a pot had given on Ilmen 6, which lay on its own tray as an answer to an interval carried since Stone 13, advanced and not paid. This was flatter than that and deader, and it lay beside the five from four sources without joining them, as a thing lies beside a row of things it resembles and is not. The sound had come out of a woman who had stood at the end of nineteen steps and counted her loaves into her thumb, and he had loved her without knowing her, and he could not lift a hand toward her.

The lifting was clumsy and brief and then went on being clumsy for a long time. Hands went under a body heavy with water and could not find a purchase; a forearm that should have gone where the arm told it to went somewhere else; the man from the pits swore and shifted his grip and the body slid back an inch into the silt. Somebody said hold her head and nobody held her head; the water came up out of her mouth in a thin dark thread and went back into the brook that had put it there. They dragged her up the bank by degrees, a section at a time, with the clay sucking at the hem and the petticoat dragging, and set her down badly on the grass, and then came the long undignified interval of people not knowing what to do next. A woman knelt and wiped the clay off the face with her sleeve and the face would not wipe clean; the clay had got into the creases beside the mouth and stayed there. Someone said warm, and someone else said get a stone, and nobody got a stone. The man from the pits put two fingers to the side of the neck where a man feels for a pulse and kept them there longer than a pulse would have needed, and then took them away. He did not say what he had found. There was nothing to find, and the boy knew it and watched the fingers do exactly what fingers do when they are looking for something that is not there.

That was the whole of it: not the knock, which was over in a breath, but the long dull interval afterward, with the drool at his own mouth going cold and nobody wiping it, and his hand outside the wool going up an inch and coming down.

\scenebreak

The shape of her thumb on the wicker came back before the lifting was done.

It had been there the day before, at the end of the nineteen steps below \place{House Silt}: her hand off the jamb and onto the handle of the basket as Sella lifted it past her shade, the thumb along the edge, the small quick grip of a woman who had carried clay up nineteen steps and children down them, staying the space of one breath and letting go. Neither woman had looked at the other. He had kept the shape where he kept the things that had no hour yet, and he had not known, lying there with the drool freezing at his mouth, that it was the detail he would be given to keep and never to spend.

A hand in a grey sleeve went into a coat pocket for a small cold rectangle of light with a name on it. A sentence about a boat on cold water, nine promises old, waited in the pocket and was not said. The name under the light was not looked at. The hand came back with wool.

Nothing crossed into the basket that a baby's hand could carry.

She did not say the number. She did not say the name. The number lay in her thumb and the name under the pale sieve-wire scar up the inside of her left forearm, and neither came up, and the door-shut smile had gone and no other smile had come in its place. To the last she believed him spent among children she had never seen, given up in the levy three winters ago for a whole return she could not have read the writing of. It had been a mercy when it was told. It was a fact now, and there was nobody left who could correct it. He had once known an institution that preferred its own version of a thing to the thing itself because the version fit on a chart and the truth did not; he had let it, and carried the letting for the rest of a life. Here the version had gone into the water with her and come out on the bank as a fact, and there was no chart and no hand to write the correction and no living person who knew there was anything to correct. She had kept him among the bits she counted, the quietest of the accounts that had entered him, because nobody --- least of all her --- had thought it was an account at all.

\person{Alda Reed} came after the vessel had given. She came down from the road with her shawl tight and her hands already smoothing her apron in two passes, and the soap and the warm milk that were usually on her had gone cold under the silt of the river, so that she smelt of lye and wet wool and the mineral edge of the water instead. She went to the slate before she went to the person. It was a narrow slate and she laid it writing-down on a flat stone at the edge of the bank, and only then did she turn to the body, and then to the basket. Her hand went once inside her bodice and touched the little tin and came away without opening it. She did not say the woman's name.

She gave dates where a wonder should have stood. Stone seven, the account up on the eighth, before the paper went out; a cooper's seventh; a potter's third child. The dates arrived faster than any question needed them and went on arriving after there was no question left to answer. She had crossed two fingers at the red edge of \lore{the birth-flower} at his wrist three doors down and covered the sign with a date nobody had asked for, and she had done it again in a market full of people and in a dark room telling \lore{the Return}, and never once known she had done it. Here there was no sign to cross and no wonder to cover, and she laid the dates over the body as she would have laid them over a cradle, because dates were a handle on a thing too large to carry and a hearth-maid's work was to carry it.

The boy lay in the basket and watched the order of her reaching and put it on the tray where he kept the hands that went to the record before the hand went to the person. A midwife, a neighbour at a well, a clerk behind a Court table, a man who had priced him, Berr passing a finger over a figure before he touched nothing --- all of them had that order, and now Alda had it too. He did not call it the same hand as Berr's thumb wiped twice on an apron, and he did not put it with the Drawing man's hand, the collector with the grey fingertips whose hand had had no pause in it at all. Those stayed in their own places. Alda's going to the slate first was a fact of the same shape as theirs and not the same fact, and he had learned what three likenesses laid in a row would do in a better-lit room, and the room here was a bank in the grey before the smoke had cleared and had no light to speak of, which was no reason to be careless with it. He kept the order and wrote no cause beside it. She went on giving dates after the question had stopped, and the tin stayed shut in her bodice, and the woman on the grass kept her clay in the creases beside her mouth.

\scenebreak

Word came up with the neighbours later, in the manner word comes up in a district that has no need to write it down: \lore{the Court} had set a collector's clerk at \place{the Fishstairs} with a slate and a cuff gone soft from washing, and he had been set there for a measure nobody had called for. A woman said it to the pot she was scouring; a man said it to his boots; it went from mouth to mouth the way the number of the ice's crack had gone from a fist to a shoulder in the week before, and nobody claimed it and nobody gave it out. He kept the shape of it on its own tray. There was a clerk at the Fishstairs with a slate, and a woman was dead in the brook behind the racks, and the district had entered neither in anything it would show him.

Sella carried him down in the middle of the morning, past the racks and along the road toward the north bridge, with the basket at her hip and the fold tucked at his chin. She kept her half-pace from every open slate and set him at the edge of the stalls in a finger's width of shade where no slate lay, the same care she had used at the Drawing and at the market's edge and at a hundred places besides. From there he could see the clerk. The man had a soft cuff and a slate and a row of figures on it in a hand the boy could not read, and when a woman came to stand before him the man's hand went to the top of the slate before his eyes went to the stalls. That was the order of it, and the boy put it on its own tray --- not the woman in the grey coat at the flare, whose small pause he had seen five times and could not read, not the grey-fingered man who had drawn from the dying neighbour under the Vail roof, not joined to either of them. The clerk's hand had no pause in it that he could see, or a pause of a different shape, and he did not count it and did not make a sixth sighting of the neat dark hand. He filed the order where he filed such orders, apart from the others, and went on looking at the stalls. Nobody came to the basket with a slate. Nobody asked the house for a figure, and nobody carried him anywhere to have one written down against him, and there was nothing in the morning that anyone could have used to enter him.

\person{Mira Silt} came up from the eels while the basket was in the shade, with pale slime dried on her sleeves and river silt in the hollow of her neck and her head held a little to the right against the cold where the top of her left ear had been. She kept the small smile on her face the way another woman keeps a door on the latch. A neighbour called after her about the water and the colour of it, and Mira answered the silence with her own answer.

“Does the river know who it takes? It takes who it takes. Ask the river.” She flicked an eel-bone against her thumb and put it away.

She did not look at the water. She did not look into the basket. She brought no crust and put nothing into the fold, and no third came into it, and she went back down toward the eels with her head held to the right. The boy set this not-looking beside the others she had given him --- the not-looking at her sister at the end of nineteen steps, the not-looking at the basket in a dozen yards --- and kept them in their separate places, because a not-looking was a shape and shapes were where a mistake began if a man had a hand free enough to follow them. He had no such hand. His lay outside the wool and went up an inch and came down.

At dusk Sella carried him up the \place{Clay Yard} under the \place{Vail kiln-house} roof and set him on his bed, not in \place{the Debt Loft}. \person{Garl Vail}'s coat kept its salt shape on its peg behind the door and the hollow wheel-seat its leftward lean of twenty years, and the proverb went to the pot and the fire and not to the basket: leave the crack showing; people trust a thing they can see has been handled. The clean dark line where \lore{the Ash} had turned the corner for a year and two days held nothing and was not swept. \person{Tarn} set the small bench knife on its sill and told the empty yard where it was, six years of the seven counted and the seventh held back, Ash worked into the creases of his knuckles that no scrubbing lifted. \person{Jorra Vail} came in from the wheel-shed with bone-coloured slip dried to old bone up both forearms and went to the damper before Sella had finished telling him the fire was low, the bent finger going in ahead of his hand. Neither looked toward the bed. They had not been told about the woman in the brook and had not asked, and the house went on around the thing it had not entered.

She turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and did not touch \lore{the widow's mark}, because a bank was not an opening. The carried fire that had come up the yard on \lore{Kindling} was still under blind ash and the broken pot, warming the boards by degrees and missing one breath in every four. Four, four and a gap. The two crusts lay in the fold where they had been put --- one gone past smelling of anything since Stone 15, one giving a thin breath of barley smoke when the wind turned --- neither eaten, lifted or named, and no third. He wanted the second one and could not reach it, and the wanting of it was louder than the knowing.

He lay on his bed under the roof, thirty-eight days old, with the fold at his ear. The shape of a thumb on wicker lay where he kept the things that had no hour yet, which was the only place he had to keep it. The sixth hearing lay beside the five from four sources without joining them, and the count went on, four, four and a gap, into a year the district had entered and had not entered him. A woman was gone into the brook behind the racks and did not come back, and the yard went on with its work as yards do. He was the only adult in the room and the least use in it, and his hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth behind him and came down.
