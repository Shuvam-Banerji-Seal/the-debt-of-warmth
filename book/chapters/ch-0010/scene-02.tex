\daymark{39}{Ilmen 10, Y.H. 813 (Tenthday)}

The small knife was on its sill when the basket was lifted. \person{Tarn} had set it there before light and told the empty yard where it lay, six years of the seven counted and the seventh held back where he always held it, and now he stood aside from the door with Ash worked into the creases of his knuckles and the pale clip-ring at his left wrist catching what light there was. He did not look into the basket. He said nothing at all, which in that house was a way of saying the whole of it: the debt kept its place, the foundling kept his, and the two were not to be put in one sentence. \person{Sella Vail} came through behind him with the basket at her hip, the little-door burn on the back of her hand shut on the wicker, and the wool folded at the boy's chin before she had cleared the threshold. She did not touch \lore{the widow's mark}. The kiln had not been opened and the mark had its hour and no other.

\person{Corin} was thirty-nine days old. He had been thirty-eight the day before, and the district had entered neither number, which was a fact he kept without resentment and without much hope of its changing. The fold was at his chin; the two crusts lay where they had been put, one gone past smelling of anything since Stone 15, the other still giving a thin breath of barley smoke when the wind turned off the yard, neither eaten, lifted or named, and no third. His head went where it wanted and made its small wet sound and came back. The thread at his mouth froze a little in the morning and nobody wiped it, because a basket held in two hands is a pot held in two hands and cannot be set down for a chin. Cold arrived in its known order, cheek and temple and wrist. He wanted the smoky crust. He could not reach it. The wanting was louder than the knowing, as it had been every morning of his new life.

She carried him down \place{the Clay Yard} like a pot filled that must not be slopped, turning him with her knee so that his face went toward the yard and not toward the writing a neighbour had tucked under her arm at the gate. She kept a half-pace from every open slate without appearing to keep it, going around the writing the way a woman goes around a pail set in a doorway. A pot stood on a bench along the yard with its mouth to the sky, and Sella said to the pot, “Feed the fire before you feed yourself. It'll still be there in the morning. You won't.” The pot gave no answer; a proverb said to a pot never expects one. It was not said to the basket, and the basket was not the pot, and the boy had learned by now to keep those two facts in different places.

A woman followed them as far as the yard's end and said, “That's a good basket. Whose is it?”

“The house's,” Sella said. Then, to the wool, “Keep it off his ears. It's the river wind.” She did not say \emph{I}. She did not say \emph{my}. The instruction went to the wool and the wind and not to the thing under it, and she went on walking with her burn shut on the wicker.

They came down to \place{the Cross} in the middle of the morning, when the slate was being read and the pigs were going over the weighing board. The square had four ways out of it: the road down to \place{the south bridge} and \place{Slate Row}, the steep street up to \place{High Kiln}, the \place{north bridge}, and the road that went away toward \place{the Bone Orchard}. The boy counted them from the low angle of the basket because they were there and because nothing else in the square could be counted without somebody wanting a figure for it. The bell went through the morning longer than any church bell, four, four and a gap, and nobody crossed himself at it.

Sella set him in \place{the moot-post}'s finger's width of shade, the only shade at that end of the square, choosing the place with her knee as she would choose a place for a pot, setting it level and out of the draught and not looking into it afterward. Cold iron came into his cheek through the wool. Her hand did not go to the mark. A reading was not an opening and she had carried that rule through a year and into this one without once explaining it to anybody, least of all to the child at her hip.

\lore{Account-day} had come round again and the market went on as markets do, as though a woman had not gone into the brook behind the racks the day before and lain uncollected on the bank. Eight pigs went over the board with their legs tied and their small hard eyes shut; eels came up from the water in shallow trays that left a wet mark on the boards; pots stood among turnips in heaps that nobody tried to make agree. The levy was called for the thaw. The question of what the thaw would cost went around the square and would not stand still long enough to be given.

A man come back from looking at the ice held his fist shut with the thumb tucked under, which is the way a man holds a thing he has decided not to give. A woman at the turnip heap said, “Well?”

“It's come a hair further,” the man said, and put the fist into his coat pocket.

“A hair,” the woman said.

The man said nothing. A third man going past with a yoke on his shoulders lifted one shoulder at her, and that was the whole of the figure the square was going to get. It went from a fist to a mouth to a shoulder and came to nothing. Somebody said the river only did what rivers did in Ilmen, and that let everybody go home without owning the day. The boy lay with cold iron at his cheek and kept the shape of the number exactly, which was more than the yard did with it.

\person{Berr Clay} came to the block with the red wool cord of his office folded under his coat and the green of tasted glaze at one corner of his mouth, his hands large and clean. He wiped his thumb twice on his apron before he touched his slate, twice as he had wiped it on Stone 30 and on a dozen days besides, and the crowd leaned in, because a low voice makes two hundred people quieter than a shout would.

“One in twelve, as the notice says,” he said. Then, because a household might have heard otherwise, “If your household has heard otherwise, your household has heard wrong.” The levy was called for the thaw; it was not called by him. He passed a finger over the figure to make sure it was still where he had put it. The figure stood as read. There would be a slate at the watch if a house wanted it again.

A man near the pigs, who had been told something by somebody and wanted it made public, asked what the thaw would stand at.

“A number goes into a slate, not into a well,” Berr said. “If your house wants it, there'll be a slate at the watch.”

\emph{Your house}, not \emph{my account}. The speaker stepped out of his own sentence as neatly as a surgeon takes his hand out of a field before the dressing goes on, and the boy knew the movement better than he knew the face that made it. Berr put the slate down and did not look into the basket, which lay at a half-pace from his boots and under the figure. One in twelve stood printed and unchallenged. The true subtraction went over the top of the wicker and stayed unspoken, and the boy could no more hand it back or correct it than he could lift the crust in the fold to his mouth.

Somebody at the pigs said there was a firing of tiles up the terrace that had never been paid for, and somebody else said that was a year ago and more, and the man with the yoke said the pigs had come in heavy, and so it went: the debt went around the square the way Sella went around ash in a corner, neither paid nor settled nor done with, and nobody said the name that belonged to it. The proverb lived in the house's mouth and went to the pot and the fire, never to the basket.

At the Slate Row end of the square stood a table of \lore{the Court} in the open air. The clerk had a cuff gone soft from washing and ink in the creases of two fingers, and when a woman came to stand before him his hand went to the top slate before his eyes went to her. He said, “It's entered as it stands. There's nothing in it the household need correct,” in a soft sentence with no edge on it, and the woman went off down the row with her fist shut and nothing in it she could spend. The boy watched the order of the reaching and put it on the tray where he kept the hands that went to the record before the person: \person{Alda Reed} reaching for her slate before the fold, a neighbour at the well reaching for hers before her child, Berr's twice-wiped thumb, this clerk with his soft cuff. He did not make one fact of the four. They stayed four facts of the same shape in four places, and he wrote no cause beside any of them. He did not see the upper line of the slate and did not count a pause in it; the clerk's order went on its own tray, and the clerk was a clerk and nothing more.

While the yard kept its number badly, the boy kept his exactly. Four ways out of the square. The bell's four, four and a gap with nobody crossing himself. The six hearings of \lore{the full note} stood where he had put them: a cup set down across a birth room, a pot on a bench in a wheel-shed through a wall, the district's own slate twice at the Cross, a man giving under a cold iron tube at the Drawing, and yesterday's small flat knock out of a woman heavy with water. Six, and not seven. The sixth lay beside the five from four sources and never among them, and there had been no new hearing in the market to move it. The nearly full answer of Ilmen 6 lay on its own tray, advanced and not paid; the intervals lay apart; the told bell lay beside the heard bell and never on it. He had once stood in rooms where a thing like yesterday's was given a number and a name and a place on a chart, and had signed his own name to a version of it that fit the chart better than the body did. Here he lay in a market shade with cold iron at his cheek and was not asked to sign anything at all, because nobody had thought to enter him.

A trauma surgeon who could not reach a cup had become a surgeon who could not reach a crust; the trade had gone down in the world and the patient had not improved. He kept the joke without laughing, which was a way of laughing that cost less and kept the same blade in it. The pigs went over the board; the number went from the fist to the mouth to the shoulder; the bell struck and missed its fifth; the crowd leaned in to hear a figure it already knew and would not repeat. He was the only adult in the square and the least use in it, and the crust lay in the fold at his side with a thin breath of barley smoke going out of it whenever the wind turned.

In the afternoon the figure was read a second time for the households that had come late, and the same sentence went over the basket a second time, and one in twelve was not challenged a second time either. Sella stood a half-pace off with her burn shut and said nothing to anybody and nothing to him. When the light went long she lifted the basket off the post, put the fold right at his ear without looking at his face, and carried him up the yard under \place{the Vail kiln-house} roof, saying nothing about where they had been and nothing about what had been read over the top of him. At the door the small knife was back on its sill and the yard was swept around the places it did not enter. The clean dark line where \lore{the Ash} had turned its corner for a year and two days held nothing and was not swept.

She set him on his bed, not in \place{the Debt Loft}. The two crusts lay in the fold where they had been put, neither eaten, lifted or named, and no third. The fire that had come up the yard on \lore{Kindling} was under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees and missing one breath in every four. Four, four and a gap. He lay with the fold put right at his ear and the sixth hearing lying beside the five from four sources without joining them, and the count went on, four, four and a gap, into a year the district had entered and had not entered him. His hand outside the wool, after a moment's consideration that was not a decision, went up an inch toward the warmth behind him and came down.
