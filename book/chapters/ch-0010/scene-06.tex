\daymark{43}{Ilmen 14, Y.H. 813 (Fourthday)}

The wind had come a league up the \place{Ilm} and had not warmed itself on the way. \person{Sella Vail} carried him down before the light was fair on the \place{Clay Yard}, the basket held level at her hip and the fold tucked at his chin, and she gave the instruction to the wool as she gave it every morning. Keep it off his ears. It's the river wind. \person{Corin}, forty-three days old, could not keep it off anything; the wool did what wool could do, which was less than the wind wanted, and the small cold went at his cheek and then his temple in the order it always took. He had learned the order without being told it and could not alter one step of it.

At \place{the Cross} the morning was already doing its work. Men came up from \place{the Fishstairs} with baskets of eels slung across their shoulders and water dripping from the wicker in a slow dark line, and the water on the boards was not all river water; some of it was salt, and the salt had got into the cracks and lay there white in the shade. Two women with bundles of rushes went past talking of \lore{the Fish Blessing} as if it were tomorrow, which it was not, and a man answered them with a date instead of an opinion, because in this district a date is a thing a person can say without owning it. Reed fifteen. The hearth-maids would stand on the stone and the traps would be blessed and the eels would run whether anybody blessed them or not. Somebody said the last part and laughed, and the laugh went off across the square and nobody joined it.

Sella set the basket in the moot-post's shade where no slate lay open, turned him with her knee until the fold lay right, and did not look into it afterward. She stood a half-pace from the nearest open slate all the while she traded, which was her way of standing in a market. When a figure went over the top of the basket from a man with his fist shut around something, she did not ask him to open it. The figure went from the fist to a mouth and then to a shoulder, and the shoulder said it was weather, and that was the end of it. He kept the pieces he had heard of it and spent none of them.

Across the square a woman stood looking at pots.

She had green at one corner of her mouth, the colour of lead glaze gone wrong in the firing, and she looked at the pots the way a woman looks at things she has priced already in her head, turning one with her finger, putting it down, taking up another. She did not buy. She looked a moment longer at a pot with its handle mended, and then she was gone into the press at the \place{Slate Row} end, and the green went with her, and nothing about her had wanted an answer. The boy watched the place where she had been until it was a place again. He put the green on one tray and the looking on another, because a thing looked at and a thing that did the looking are not the same object and he had learned to keep such things apart. Why the looking had stayed with him he could not have said. He had no word for it and no hand to set beside it, and he made neither.

The weir women at the next stall spoke of \person{Orra Weir} as a woman speaks of a roof she has stood under in a storm: not fondly, but with the exactness of somebody who knows which boards will hold. Orra's eels came down in baskets with the salt still wet on them, and Orra's traps were being lifted upriver, and Orra would know whether the stone was ready because Orra knew a thing the day before it happened. The boy entered the name on the tray where names went before faces and left it lying there. \person{Mira Silt} was somewhere along the stalls with slime dried pale on her sleeves and her head held to the right; when she shifted a basket the grey cord showed at her waist and the second cord twisted twice beneath it, and the knots on neither were counted. She did not look toward the moot-post's shade. He set her grey wool on its own board and did not let it touch the green or the looking or anything else.

Sella lifted him before the square had finished its shouting and took the \place{river road} up out of the town. The eels had to be salted before the light went, and the hut at \place{the Weir} had a bench that wanted wiping and a pot that wanted a fire under it. She did not tell the basket why they were going. She gave the instruction to the wool and walked, and the boy went upriver without being told and without asking, which was how most things arrived at him.

\scenebreak

The road was packed clay rutted by the frost, and it held the water in its ruts in long brown mirrors that broke under the heel and came back behind the foot. The Ilm ran alongside under a skin so thin that the current showed through it in dark veins, and it ran a little higher than it had on the day before. A man come down from upriver said the river was up and called it weather, and the woman with him said the thaw had come early and called it weather too, and neither of them said what the word was for the thing under the skin. The boy kept the honeyed ice of Ilmen two and the hairline that stopped short of the edge and this new rise in three places, and did not let the three places touch. A likeness was not a cause. He had made that mistake once in a better-lit room and had carried it ever since, and here there was nobody to be wrong in front of and no hand to be wrong with.

A woman with dried eel slime on her sleeves came down the road toward them, and it was Mira, with a basket of eel-bones at her hip and the small smile kept on her face like a door on the latch. “You going up?” she said to Sella, and answered it herself. “Then you'll see her. She'll have the cord out before the stone's swept.” She flicked a bone against her thumb and put it away when the deciding was done, and her head went to the right against the cold where the top of her left ear had been. When she shifted the basket the second cord showed twisted twice under the grey, never carried upriver to \place{Nine Pots}, never lifted at a door. “Ask her what night the boat came,” she said. “Don't ask her what she's owed. She'll tell you the oars.”

Sella said the house had eels to salt and went on. The boy did not see Mira look into the basket, which was the only answer she had ever given about it.

Orra was at the top of the rise where the road came level with the water, standing with her sleeves wet to the elbow and her hands on a trap that she was lifting out of the weir. The grey streak through her black hair showed first, as people said it did; the thing they noticed last was the \lore{knot-string} at her waist. Her hands were like old rope, and the pale arcs of eel scars lay across both forearms and she had covered neither, and she smelled of river silt and cold fish and tallow smoke so that a person at the Cross would have known she had come before they saw her. She lifted the trap with a turn of the wrist that had done it ten thousand times and set it on its side on the boards, and only then did she look at the basket.

“That's the Vail boy,” she said, slow, and the nasal half-song of the Fen came into it where she was tired. “You've got her eyes and somebody else's listening. How old is it? No --- don't tell me. It'll be the fourteenth of Ilmen, in eight hundred and thirteen. It's always a date with you people.”

Sella said the eels wanted salt and set the basket down where a pot would be set, level and out of the draught. She did not answer the question about the boy and did not look into the basket.

Orra wiped her hands on her apron and came over, and when she spoke again it was sideways, the story starting in the middle where somebody was already in trouble. “Collector's boat came on the third night of the frost,” she said. “Four oars. One of them rowed with his left hand over his right, which is a thing a man does when he's carrying something he doesn't want the others to see. That's the sort of night it was.” Her thumb went to the cord at her waist and stayed while she said it, and she tied a knot in a length of grey wool that the boy could not see from where he lay. He did not know the knot was a lie. He saw the thumb go to the wool and come away, and he filed the movement where he filed hands that went to a record before they went to a person, and did not join it to anything.

“The levy,” Sella said.

“The river takes what the Court doesn't,” Orra said. “You can shout at either one. I've tried.” She gave a date, then, where an answer should have stood, and went back to her trap.

The boy lay in the shade and kept the grey wool of her cord on a board beside the grey wool of Mira's, and would not let the two boards touch. Two women kept accounts in a material no officer would think to read, and one of them had a knot for a thing she would not say, and neither had ever put a name on a slate. He did not know what either cord counted. Not knowing was not the same as having nothing to enter, and he entered the shapes and left the counts alone.

\person{Alda Reed} came up the road with her shawl drawn tight and soap-and-milk still on her from the morning's work, and for a moment the two women stood together in the lee of the \place{Eel House} with the salt wind going past them. Orra said the river was up and Alda gave the date of the last high water and neither said what either one was thinking. Alda's hand went into her bodice once, to the little tin she never opened, and came out empty; Orra's thumb went to her cord and tied nothing that the boy could see. When a figure came near them --- some number for the traps, for the eels, for what the blessing would cost --- Alda said the weather had turned and Orra said the river would do what the river did, and the subject changed so neatly that nobody could have said it had been there at all. Nothing was said of the third day. The boy lay with the shape of the change in front of him like a door shut quietly in another room, and he did not know what room it was, and he did not try to name it.

\scenebreak

The Weir was another room.

The Eel House stood on the \place{plank walk} with its door propped open and a bench just inside and a pot on the bench with salt crusted white around its rim, and a draught came along the boards and went under the door to the corner where the wall met the ground. Sella set the basket on the boards outside, where a pot would be set out of the wind, and the boy took the room in from that height: the bench, the pot, the draught, and a gap where a fifth thing would stand if there were a fifth. He had a birth room and a kiln-house and a death-house and the Court's room heard through a doorway and the room with the tin above the bed, and now he had this one, with salt instead of slip on the floor and river under the boards instead of trodden ash. He set it in its own place and did not let it join the others. A likeness was a likeness. That was all he had ever been given and all he would allow himself to spend.

On the \place{blessing stone} on the north bank a crossed mark had been cut into the flat face of it, and a woman was brushing the salt off the stone with the side of her hand so that the mark showed. The boy could not see the mark from where he lay, and if he had seen it he would not have known what it meant, and nobody there would have told him. The \place{eel traps} had been lifted and set along the gaps of the weir with their mouths upriver, and the \place{eel racks} beside the hut stood bare and salted, waiting for the day's catch to be hung. Orra went from one to the next with her rope-hands and her uncovered scars, and the salt got into the creases of her knuckles and stayed there.

A man went along the plank walk ahead of her, and at one place he put his foot over a board and did not set it down, stepping to the next board with his weight held off, the way a man steps where he knows a board will not bear him when the river comes up in Silt. He did not say anything about it and neither did Orra, and the boy filed the board with the other gaps. He did not know yet what Silt was or why a board would fail in it. He knew the shape of a thing left out of a count, which was enough for today.

\place{the Singing Sill} gave its low note early, coming up through the boards under the basket before the light had gone, and a woman at the racks said the river was singing early and called it weather, and went on with her salt. The boy had heard the note through the boards of the kiln-house and now he heard it through the boards of the Weir, and he kept the two hearings in two places because one had come through a house and one had come through a weir and a weir is not a house, no matter what a basket makes of it. The sixth hearing of \lore{the full note} still lay beside the five from four sources and was not among them, and no seventh had come, and nothing had joined.

The river went a little higher under its thinning skin while they worked, and the men at the traps said it would be up by night and called it weather, and Sella said the house would want its eels and did not look at the water. She turned the basket so that the sun was off the boy's face, and the salt wind went past them both, and the cord of knots hung in the hut behind them where he could not enter and could not read, and tomorrow the hearth-maids would stand on the stone and bless the traps. He lay in the shade with the fold at his chin, forty-three days old, and kept the three rises in three places and the two grey cords on two boards, and the count went on.
