\daymark{41}{Ilmen 12, Y.H. 813 (Secondday)}

They carried him down before the light was properly in the yard, and the house went with him in the small sounds it made behind the wall: the kiln taking its breath in fours, the gap where the fifth would stand, and \person{Garl Vail}'s saying going to the pot because there was nobody to say it to --- feed the fire before you feed yourself; it'll still be there in the morning. \person{Sella Vail} lifted the basket with the little-door burn closing first on nothing and then on the wicker, and gave the instruction to the wool: keep it off his ears, it's the river wind. She said the house where another woman would have said I. At \place{the Fishstairs} she chose the shade the way she chose a place for a pot, with her knee and not her eye, and set him down level and out of the draught, the fold at his chin, and did not look into the basket afterward. \person{Corin}, forty-one days old, lay where a pot would lie and watched the boards above him go grey with the coming morning.

The steps down to the landing counted themselves without anybody's leave: four, four, and a place where a step should have been. He had learned that count at the edge of the fish-trade and had never once found the missing step with his hand, because a hand outside the wool could go up an inch and no further. The river did what rivers did in Ilmen and the yard called it weather, and he kept the missing step in its own place, apart from the kiln's gap and the bell's gap and the told gap that belonged to a story and not to any bell that had been struck. Cold arrived in its known order, cheek and temple and wrist. The thread at his mouth gathered a little frost and nobody wiped it, because a basket set down is a pot set down and cannot be lifted again for a chin. His head went where it wanted, made its small wet sound, and came back. In the fold two crusts lay as they had lain: one gone past smelling of anything since Stone 15, the other still giving a thin breath of barley smoke when the wind turned off the water. He wanted the smoky one. He could not reach either. The wanting was louder than the knowing, as it had been on every morning of his life, and neither crust was lifted or named.

\person{Mira Silt} came up from the eels with the dawn still on her sleeves. The slime had dried pale on them in the cold, and silt sat in the hollow of her neck as though it had been poured there and forgotten. She held her head a little to the right, not turned toward anything, only held, the way a man holds a bad joint quiet through a long shift; the top of her left ear was not there and had not been there for eight years, and she said a hook had taken it, and the scar said a knife, and nobody in the district had ever found it worth making anything of. The small smile stayed on her face the way another woman keeps a door on the latch --- it could be shut without anybody seeing the movement. She smelled of river silt and salted perch and the smoke of tallow, and under that the cold off the water. His eyes, which were good for nothing beyond what two paces could show him, took the measure of her hands before she spoke: the eel knife hand, the counting hand, the thumb that went where it went when a figure came near. He knew the shape of the wound under the held head without being told --- a clean loss at the rim, long healed, the small nerves beneath still carrying cold when the rest of her face had gone used to it. A man could read such a thing across a room and could do nothing about it from a basket, and the knowing sat where it always sat, with nowhere to go. She held her head to the right because the cold came in at the place where the ear had been, and the cold came in, and she went on selling eels.

A woman at the stall wanted to know whether the eels ran early this year. Mira did not look at the woman. “Do they run early?” she said. “They run when they run. Ask the river.” The answer came out of her own mouth in the space where the other woman's answer would have stood, and the other woman took it and went away satisfied, which was the whole of the art. Then somebody farther along the boards said a figure for the morning's take, and Mira's thumb went under her apron to the grey cord at her waist and stayed there while she decided what the figure was for. The boy watched the thumb go and watched it stay. He had watched it go on other mornings, at a well and at the end of nineteen steps, and had never once seen it go to the other cord.

She shifted the weight of a basket that was not hers and was not the one at her feet, and the second cord showed beneath the grey. It was twisted twice, the grey over it and the other under, and it had never gone up the river to \place{Nine Pots} and had never been lifted at a door. Nobody's thumb had counted the knots on the one or the other, and the boy, who could count the nails in a floorboard without being asked, could not lift a hand to either. He put the grey cord on one board in the private file and the cord twisted twice beneath it on another, and would not let the two boards touch. Two records, one shape of keeping, and a woman standing between him and them with her smile shut on the latch. A mark on a slate can be taken up and read aloud by a man who never held the thing it counts; a knot in wool has to be looked at by the woman who tied it, and stays where she left it. He did not know which of the two cords went toward a child and which kept the account of what had been paid out for one, and made no cause of the not knowing. He had no word in \lore{Grathic} for an account kept in wool, and would not have spoken it if he had, because to name a thing before an officer is to hand it over, and there was no officer here, and still he kept the boards apart on purpose, the way he kept the told bell from the heard one. Two things that look alike are not the same thing, and joining them would have been a deciding he had no standing to make.

Eel-bones clicked against her thumb while she decided. She flicked them one after another, a dry little sound like rain on a stone, and put one away when the deciding was done, and neither the flicking nor the putting away was entered anywhere. “Six for five or five for six?” she said to the woman who wanted eels. “Don't trouble yourself. I've counted them twice and I'll count them after.” The eels were small and dark and went through the hand like wet rope. The woman gave marks and took eels, and the eels went into a basket, and Mira made nothing of the difference between six and five, because a small war carried on for six years is carried in the hand and not in the mouth. The boy kept the marks going one way and the eels going the other and did not join them to the cord, though both were accounts and both were in the same woman's hands.

At the edge of the boards a soft cuff came down on a slate and stayed there while the clerk looked at nothing in particular, the hand before the eyes as the hand is always before the eyes. The boy set that order on its own tray with the other hands that had reached for the record first, a midwife's and a market clerk's and a man who priced him, and joined it to none of them. He had never been allowed a name for the hand that wrote the upper line, and there was no upper line in sight this morning, only a cuff and a slate and the ordinary reaching.

She brought no crust. There had been a crust once from her hand, a brown-edged thing with barley in it and a little smoke, put into the fold briskly so that nobody could make a speech about it; there had been mornings since when none came, and there was none this morning. The spent crust and the smoky one lay side by side where they had been put and neither was eaten, lifted or named, and Mira did not look into the basket to see whether they were still there. A gift that cannot be refused cannot be answered, and a gift that is not looked at is the same gift, and he had learned both facts in bread. He wanted the smoky one. The wanting went on under the knowing like a second river.

The women at the next board spoke of \lore{the Fish Blessing} tomorrow, when the hearth-maids would go upriver to \place{the Weir} and stand on the flat stone to bless the traps, and of the number that went with the eels, because a blessing in this district has a number tied to it the way a pot has a crack. The number went from a mouth to a shoulder and came back as a shape nobody gave out. One woman said it with her fingers shut inside her sleeves; another said it by looking at the river and closing her mouth on the rest. A girl beside them had salt dried white at her wrists and did not once look down toward the shaded edge where the basket lay. They ended in the weather, as everybody ended in the weather, and the eels went on running whether anybody blessed them or not. The boy kept the shape of the number on its own tray. He could not spend it, correct it or hand it back, and he had stopped expecting a figure to arrive in a form a baby's hand could hold.

\scenebreak

Back up the yard the light had got into the clay and the trodden ash, and \place{the Slip Brook} ran behind \place{the drying racks} the way it had been running for seven mornings, clay-heavy and a little higher than it should, with a film on it that no longer held the wind. A child who went near the bank was called back by the middle of the yard, and the water went on over water without fetching anybody. Sella set the basket level at the edge of the racks and out of the draught, turned the fold with her knee, and gave the instruction to the wool and not to him. She did not look in, and did not say what the water was doing, and there was nobody in the yard who would have called it by its name if she had.

The boy put the brook where he put the honeyed ice under the mud at \place{the Ilm} and the hairline that had opened out of the thaw and stopped short of the edge: three objects in three places, and no cause written between them. A likeness was a likeness and a cause was a thing that had to be earned; he had made that mistake once in a better-lit room and had carried it into every room since. The brook had taken a woman three mornings ago and nobody had entered the taking, and the yard called the rise weather because weather was the word that let a person go home without owning a day of it. He kept the taking on its own tray, beside the shape of a thumb on wicker and the sixth hearing of \lore{the full note}, which lay beside the five and was not among them, and wrote no cause beside any of them.

He lay at the edge of the racks with the fold at his chin and the cold coming at cheek and temple and wrist, and kept what had been given him. The two cords were out of sight now; from where he lay he could not see the grey one or the cord twisted twice beneath it, and the knots on neither had been counted by anybody, and no door had been lifted to the child upriver. He kept them on their two boards all the same, side by side and not touching, the way he kept the two not-lookings that had the same shape and were opposite things. In the fold the two crusts lay where they had been put, and no third came into the yard that morning, and neither was lifted or named. The number from the stall went around outside him as a shape nobody gave out, and the count went on inside him as it had gone on since the first room: four, four and a gap. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the house behind him, where the carried fire lay under blind ash and missed one breath in every four, and came down. He was forty-one days old and the only adult in the yard and the least use in it, and the river went on doing what rivers did while the district found a word for it and went home.
