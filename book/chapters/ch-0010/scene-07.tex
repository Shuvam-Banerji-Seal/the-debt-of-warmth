\daymark{44}{Ilmen 15, Y.H. 813 (Fifthday)}

The boards of \place{the plank walk} had been swept before light, and the salt they swept went over the edge into \place{the Ilm} without anybody watching it go. It was the fifteenth of Ilmen, \lore{the Fish Blessing}, and \place{the Weir} had put on its one clean day the way a worked house puts on a shirt it will take off again by noon: \place{the eel traps} lifted and set in the gaps with their mouths upriver, \place{the eel racks} bare and salted, and \place{the blessing stone} on the north bank flat and brushed free of grit so that the cut mark showed. \person{Sella Vail} carried the boy up from \place{the river road} in the first thin light and set him where a pot would be set, out of the draught that came along the boards, and turned him with her knee until the fold lay right under his chin. Keep it off his ears. It's the river wind. \person{Corin}, forty-four days old, could not keep it off anything. The wool did what wool could do, which was less than the wind wanted, and the cold went at his cheek and then his temple in the order it always took. He had learned that order without being told it and could not alter one step of it.

The hearth-maids came out of \place{the Eel House} with their sleeves damp and salt in the creases of their hands, and stood on the stone with the crossed mark cut into the flat face of it under their feet. The boy could not see the mark from where he lay. If he had seen it he would have put it on the tray of shapes and not on the tray of meanings, because a shape was what he was given and a meaning was what other people made of shapes when they had a slate in reach. One of the maids sprinkled salt along the weir head with a flat hand, and another said the old words in a low half-song, and the women at the racks stopped their work and stood with their hands held loose in front of them, the way people stand when something is being done to a thing they cannot thank. A man behind him said the eels would run whether anybody blessed them or not, and somebody laughed, and the laugh went off down the boards and nobody joined it.

An old weir woman with her apron wet to the knee said the saying of the place as she worked: the brook takes what no slate will enter; the knot keeps what no clerk can spell; the eels run whether anybody blesses them or not. She said it to the boards, not to the boy, and went on with her salt. He set the three clauses on three trays, because they were three objects and not one sentence, and because the districts he had lived in put figures in fists and mouths and weather and had never once handed him a figure whole. Six mornings back \place{the Clay Yard} had lifted a woman out of a clay-heavy brook and had entered her nowhere. Here a flat stone was being thanked for fish that had not asked to be thanked. He did not join the two things. He had learned what joining two things in a better-lit room could cost, and here there was nobody to be wrong in front of and no hand to be wrong with.

\person{Orra Weir} came down the boards when the blessing was done, and the boy knew she had come before he saw her, the way everybody at \place{the Cross} knew it: silt and cold fish and tallow smoke moving together along the plank walk, and under it the wet wool smell of a woman who had stood too long on a weir. The grey streak showed first through her black hair; the pale arcs of eel scars lay across both forearms and she had covered neither; her hands were like old rope that had been wet and dried and wet again for thirty years. She took the scars for granted the way another woman takes a wedding ring, and she had no slate in her hand and would not have sat down at a table with one lying open on it. When a figure came near the racks she stood up instead of answering it, and when she spoke of names she said them into the cord at her waist and not into the air.

She crouched by the basket with her rope-hands on her knees and looked at the boy the way a trap-keeper looks at a trap that has come back up full. “You're the Vail boy,” she said, slow, and the nasal half-song came into it where she was tired. “You've got her eyes and somebody else's listening. How old is it? No --- don't tell me. It'll be the fifteenth of Ilmen, in eight hundred and thirteen. It's always a date with you people.”

Sella set a pot down for her and went back to the eels, and the boy was handed over without a word, the way a thing is handed from one pair of hands to another when both pairs know what the thing will bear. Orra did not ask Sella anything. She told the pot instead. “An eel goes in,” she said. “It can't see the way out. That's all a trap is. Don't make it more than that.” Her thumb went to the cord at her waist while she said it, and came away. He knew the shape she had described better than he knew any word in \lore{Grathic}: one way in, no way back, a vessel that holds what enters it until somebody lifts it. That was anatomy. It was not a doctrine, and he wrote no doctrine over it.

The blessing went on without him in the way all work goes on around a basket set down out of the draught. Salt got into the creases of Orra's knuckles and stayed there. The hearth-maids came down off the stone. The weir women spoke of the number of eels the traps would hold and then of the weather, which was how a number went around this district without arriving anywhere; when one of them said a figure for the blessing a man turned the conversation to the river, and the figure lay where it had fallen and nobody picked it up. The boy lay at the low angle at which a basket sees a plank walk, with the boards going out above him on their posts and a gap in one of them that a man stepped over without putting his weight down. He filed the gap with the other gaps. He had seen a likeness between a board that would not bear a man and a breath that was missing in a house's count, and he had kept the two apart on purpose, because a likeness was a likeness and that was all he had ever been given.

\scenebreak

The \lore{knot-string} hung at Orra's waist, grey wool gone the colour of river silt where her thumb had worried it. There were knots at the end of it enough to make a row, and she took a fresh length between her rope-hands and tied one more while she told a story sideways, the way she told everything, starting where somebody was already in trouble.

“It was the year of the flower,” she said, and the half-song went down with the words. “The night they took them, I stood at the end of the weir and watched a woman come up the Clay Yard with a bundle under her coat. Covered it was. Small. Warm --- I could see the steam off it in the cold, or I told myself I could. It had a flower on it. I've never known what to call it, so I tied it.” She pulled the knot down with her thumb and set it among the others, and the new one did not sit like them.

Then she said the word. She said it low, almost under her breath, half-sung the way the old words on the stone had been half-sung, and she said it to the basket and not to the women at the racks. “\lore{forty}.”

She watched what the boy did with it. That was the whole of it: a woman who had kept a count in wool for three years said the number aloud where a baby lay with the fold at his chin, and watched to see whether the baby would do anything at all. He did nothing. The thread of drool at his mouth froze and unstuck and froze again; his head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound it made when it went too far; his hand outside the wool lifted an inch toward the warmth that was not within two paces of him and came down. Somewhere in the fold the smoky crust gave a thin breath of barley smoke when the wind turned, and the body wanted it, and the body was louder. He wanted the crust and he wanted to lift a hand to the cord and he could do neither, and both wants were true at once and the second had no hands.

The surgeon in him took the other register. He noted the shape of the new knot --- a double turn with the tail tucked under, tied left over right, which is the way a right-handed woman ties when she is looking at something else --- and he noted the weight of her thumb as she set it, the small extra pressure that means a thing has been given a place it did not have before. He noted that the word had been said low and had been watched, which told him it was a count before he knew what it counted. He could not read Grathic in speech or on a slate and could not have lifted a knuckle to touch the wool. He kept the word on its own tray, unread, with the shape of the knot laid beside it and not on top of it, because a word and the thing it names are two objects and he had learned to keep such objects apart.

The ear held \lore{the full note} in five hearings from four sources, and the sixth from a person had come six mornings back and lay beside the five without being among them, and no seventh had come. The word on its tray did not go near them. It was not a sound of a vessel giving way; it was a thing a woman had said into the air and watched, and he filed it as a saying, and left the question of what it counted where the question stood, which was out of reach. Nothing joined. He had no Grathic word for nearly and no Grathic word for the flat knock and now no word of his own for the thing she had put in the cord, and he had stopped a long time ago expecting the world to give him one.

\person{Mira Silt} came up from the eels at the far end of the racks with pale slime dried on her sleeves and her head held to the right where the top of her left ear had been. A weir woman asked her whether the run would come with the blessing and Mira answered the question and then her own answer. “Does it come when you bless it? It comes when it comes. Ask the river.” She flicked an eel-bone against her thumb and put it away when the deciding was done, and when somebody said a figure for the morning's catch her thumb went under her apron to the grey cord at her waist and stayed there; shifting the weight of her basket showed the second cord twisted twice beneath it, never carried upriver to \place{Nine Pots}, never lifted at a door, the knots on neither counted. She did not look into the boy's basket. He set her grey wool on its board and Orra's knot-string on its board beside it, and would not let the two boards touch. Two women kept the district's private account in a material no officer would think to read, and neither had said what she knew, and the boy had no standing to ask either of them.

Orra put the cord back under her apron and went to lift a trap, and the new knot went with it into the hut where the boy could not enter and could not read. He had been given the shape of it and the weight of a thumb and a word spoken low, and none of the three could be spent. A man who has spent his life opening vessels cannot open a knot with a baby's hand; a man who has read a hundred charts cannot read a cord he cannot reach. He kept what he had been given. That was the whole of his work in the world, and it had never once changed a thing.

\scenebreak

The traps came up full. Orra tipped one and the eels went into the basket with the small slap of wet bodies against wicker, and a boy three paces away could not have lifted one of them, and the surgeon in him noted the way the eels went head-first into the mouth of the trap and could not find the mouth again once they were in. That was all a trap was, she had said. He watched her lift the next one and did not make more of it than that, though the shape of it stayed with him because shapes stayed with him, and because she had tied a knot over it. The eels would run whether anybody blessed them or not. The blessing had taken the morning and the eels had gone on doing what eels do, and the stone would be unswept by tomorrow and no slate had entered any of it.

The river went a little higher under its thinning skin while they worked, and a woman at the racks said the Ilm was up and called it weather, and the men at the traps said it would be up by night and called it weather too. \place{the Singing Sill} gave its low note up through the boards before the light had gone, and that was weather as well. The boy kept the honeyed ice, the hairline that stopped short of the edge, the higher clay-heavy brook and this new rise in four places, and did not let the four places touch. The district had a word that let a person go home warm without owning a day, and he had no word at all and had to carry the day entire. Sella turned the basket so the sun was off his face, and the salt wind went past them both.

When the light thinned toward dusk Sella came for him without being called, lifted him from the boards where a pot had stood and carried him down the river road. She did not speak to the basket on the way. She gave the instruction to the wool when the wind came round the bend, and said the house would want its eels, and once, passing a woman with a slate under her arm, she stood a half-pace off with the boy held level at her hip and did not set him down where the slate might lie open. Up the Clay Yard the lamps were being lit, and under \place{the Vail kiln-house} roof she set him on his bed and not in \place{the Debt Loft}, and put the fold right without looking at his face.

The two crusts lay in the fold where they had lain through a week of thaw and a death and a blessing. The spent one was stiff past smelling of anything. The newer one still gave a thin breath of barley smoke when the wind turned through the door that did not quite shut. Neither had been eaten, neither had been lifted, neither had been named, and no third had come into the fold that morning or any morning. He wanted the smoky one with the whole appetite of a body that had been carried all day and fed on nothing, and he could no more reach it than he could reach the knot in the hut a league upriver. A kindness that cannot be answered cannot be refused, which he had learned in bread, and the learning had cost him nothing and given him nothing he could spend.

Behind him the carried fire kept under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees. It took its breath in fours and missed one. Four, four and a gap. The clean dark line where \lore{the Ash} had turned its corner for a year and two days was not swept, because nobody in the house had ever swept it and because a line left where it is is still a line kept. Sella turned the coals with the back of the pot and did not touch \lore{the widow's mark}, because a bank is not an opening. She said something to the fire about the thaw and went on with her hands, and the boy lay where she had put him, forty-four days old, with the day entire inside him and nothing outside him that he could move.

Nothing had been joined. The word forty lay on its own tray with the shape of the knot beside it and the weight of a thumb on wicker laid apart from both, and none of the three had been spent or explained or carried into any account a Court could enter. Six hearings of the full note stood five from four sources and one from a person, and the number had not made a seventh and had not joined the six. Up the river a cord of knots hung in a hut with a new knot on it that no clerk could spell, and the boy did not know what it counted. He knew the shape of a thumb on a handle and the shape of a knot on a cord, and he had been given a word spoken low and watched, and he had done nothing with it, which was what the woman had come to see. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth behind him and came down. The count went on.
