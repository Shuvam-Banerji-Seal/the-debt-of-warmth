\daymark{109}{Seed 20, Y.H. 813}

Account-day came up over \place{the Cross} without anybody calling it, the tenth day of the tend and a market-day as all the tenth days were, and the square had been wet since first grey with the mud the pigs had carried in. \person{Sella Vail} brought \person[Corin]{the boy} down at the half-pace from every open slate, going around the boards where a clerk had set a figure out to dry as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and set the basket in \place{the moot-post}'s thin shade before the crowd thickened. She gave the instruction to the wool and to the wind and never to the basket. “Keep it off his ears. It's the river wind.” Then she took her hands off it and stood a half-pace away with the little-door burn shut on nothing, the stance of a woman who has set a pot down where it may be seen and not slopped and will not be the one to say what is in it. Beyond the post \place{the barley fields} lay open to the morning, the brown worked skin of the strips east of \place{Low Kiln} holding the boot-print and the stone and the new finer grey, and the hands that had gone into it on Seed 15 were gone now, leaving the ground with whatever figure the ground had kept.

The reading block stood dark along its lower edges where eight pigs had gone over it since first grey, and the straw lay trampled into the seams behind them. The pigs had been driven off into the noise beyond the post, but their going had left figures standing the way a cart leaves a rut, and the men who had driven them stood about with their hands on their knees and said nothing about which eight they meant. At the \place{Slate Row} fringe of the square a Court table had been set out in the open air, and a clerk wrote a figure down in a second hand, his hand going to the slate before his eyes went to the rail and then to his mouth. Two hundred people leaned toward the block in the top of the morning, and the market went on behind them with its own figures going from fist to mouth to shoulder and arriving nowhere. There were four ways out of the square: the road down to \place{the south bridge} and Slate Row, the steep street up to \place{High Kiln}, \place{the north bridge}, and the road toward \place{the Bone Orchard}, and on Account-day every one of them had somebody standing in it with a number he would not give. \person{Corin} lay at the edge of the shade with cold iron at his cheek where the post's iron bracket had been bolted and left, and the cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, with the wool riding back from the wrist so the cold found the bare place. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had been told to wipe it. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the noise and came down.

He had learned to read a figure before he could read a word, and what he read now was not on the block. It was in the order of the day: the tenth of the tend, the date on the notice, a number that had been written down somewhere before anyone in the square had opened his mouth, and a crowd leaning in to hear a man read it low. The district had withheld its number in fistfuls all through Seed. A woman at the head of the first strip had let seed out a little at a time and would not say what the fist held, and had closed the fist again on nothing with chaff in its creases; a man with a barrow had shaped the number of hands, looked at the strip, thought better of it and shut his mouth, so that the figure went from the strip to his teeth and back to the strip and arrived nowhere. The boy had read the emptied fist as a chart closed with a neat line and a signature at the foot over a note that had not been read, exact, complete and worth nothing. Now the number was coming at them from the other direction, from a slate at a date, and nobody in the square thought to set the two beside each other and ask why a thing the ground would not give had been given so neatly from a piece of stone. A figure let fall from a fist into a strip was entered nowhere; a figure read from a slate at a date was entered everywhere, and the two were the same missing thing wearing different clothes.

\person{Berr Clay} came up to the block short, with the red wool cord of his office folded under his coat instead of worn across it and the green of tasted glaze at one corner of his mouth. He smelled of hot clay and, under it, nothing at all, which is the most expensive smell in \place{Grath}. His hands were large and clean. He wiped his thumb twice on his apron before he touched nothing, and held a narrow slate unopened against his forearm with the figure turned away, passing a finger over it and letting his eyes rest a breath longer than the writing required, the way a man checks a dish he has already set down to be sure it will not slop. He did not look toward the basket. He did not raise his voice.

“One in twelve, as the notice says,” he said low into the top of the crowd, and the crowd leaned harder to hear him, so that a man who might have shouted and been heard at the far end of the square was heard instead by everybody at the near end, which is a better kind of hearing and costs nothing. He read it twice, once for the moot and once for the households that had come late, and laid the figure where everyone could see it and nobody could challenge it. “The levy is called. It is not called by me.”

The sentence went around the square like a bucket passed from hand to hand, and nobody in it thought to ask who was carrying it. A woman near the post said one in twelve the way another woman might say the weather, and two men feeding the pigs said it after her, and by the time the boy had counted the sayings the figure had stopped being a number anybody owed and had become a thing the morning had always been. That grammar was the whole of his Account-day. A man takes his own hand out of his sentence as neatly as a surgeon takes his hand out of a field before the dressing goes on, and the sentence closes around the space he left without a seam showing. The boy had watched a midwife reach for the record before the person, a neighbour reach for a shelf before she looked at him, a clerk behind a Court table and a clerk set at \place{the Fishstairs} for a measure nobody called; here was the same order with the speaker taken out of it as well, and no word in \lore{Grathic} for the two absences at once. He set the grammar on a tray of its own and did not join it to any of the others.

\scenebreak

A man at the edge of the crowd brought a number up out of a fist and asked what the planting had cost now that hands had been in it. The fist had carried the number all the way from the strips and had let nothing out of it on the road; the man opened it as a man opens a hand to show a thing he has been keeping warm, and the thing was a figure nobody had said aloud. The crowd went quiet in the small way a market goes quiet when a man asks a figure in earshot of the man who has it. Berr did not look into the fist. He wiped his thumb once more, though it had been clean a moment before, and said, “A number goes into a slate, not into a strip. If the house wants it again, there'll be a slate at the watch.”

\emph{Your house}, he had said. Never \emph{I}. He apologised in the form of an instruction, which is the only apology a Thane need make, and the man with the fist had nothing to answer because there was no speaker in the sentence to answer him.

The man shut his fist and went off with the number still in it, and the boy watched the order of it: strip, fist, mouth, slate, and the speaker gone out of the middle of it before the dressing went on. The number went up the road with Berr and arrived at the watch, which was a place and not a person and could not be asked why. Nothing in the worked strips changed its figure because a man had looked at it. The brown ground east of Low Kiln kept its boot-print and its stone and its finer grey, and the hands that had gone in by the look, in glances and handfuls and never in a figure, did not become a count because a slate had a date on it. A chart signed at the foot does not make the note above it true; it only makes the chart difficult to argue with. The boy knew there was a figure read aloud and a figure paid and that they were not the same; he knew the true subtraction was going over the top of the crowd unspoken and sitting in the locked box in Berr's house up the road, and that the box had a key and the key had a pocket and the pocket had an owner who believed his own sentences. He could not read either figure and could not carry the box, and the knowing bought him exactly what the knowing had bought him on every Account-day of his life, which was nothing he could spend and one more thing he could not forget.

\person{Garl Vail}'s uncalled debt against the firing of tiles went around the space where a name might have stood and was not called. Berr went around it the way Sella went around Ash left in a corner, with his attention on the block and his care not to touch what lay beside it, and the debt lay where it lay, neither paid nor settled nor done with. The boy kept it on the place where the left things were kept, beside the crust gone out of the fold and the stone gone out of the wool and the coal that had gone up with the bird, and wrote no cause across any of them. A kindness that could not be answered had become an absence that could not be answered, and the man who had done it went on believing he had done a kindness. Garl's salt-stiff coat kept his broader shape on its peg up the yard and his hollow seat kept its twenty-year lean, and neither of them had anything to say to the figure read over the ground where his son's hands had once gone.

In the afternoon the bell at the Cross went over the market, four strokes, four, and a gap where the fifth should have been, longer than a church bell, and nobody crossed himself at it. It did a temple's work with a Court's hand and asked nobody's leave. The boy kept the heard bell on one place and the told bell of the Long Night on another, in the dark with no fire after dusk, and would not let the two touch although they were the same four and the same missing stroke. No seventh hearing came into the square on Seed 20. Six hearings of \lore{the full note} stood beside and never among --- five from four sources and the sixth out of a person --- and the bell was not one of them and never had been. A near-full answer did not come either, and none of the three on its tray was paid.

Word came up through the crowd that the soft-cuffed clerk was still set at the Fishstairs with his slate to his forearm for a measure nobody had called, his cuff grey from washing and his hand going to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls, and that nobody had taken him off. The boy filed the order apart from Berr's twice-wiped thumb, apart from the man who priced the basket and the woman who laid a date over a sign, apart from \person{Marra} whom he had never seen in \place{Sill} and from the Drawing collector with grey fingertips and the soft-handed man at the Court's table. Five hands reaching for a record before a person, five trays, no two touching, and no sixth sighting of the neat dark hand came into the square. A measure set down before anybody called it is a measure set down ahead of a death; he kept that as a shape and did not make a rule of it, because a rule is a thing a man can carry into a room and hand to somebody who will write it down. The soft-cuffed clerk sat on for a measure that had not been asked for, and the market sold its eels over the top of him as though a man with a slate were a thing a landing always had.

Berr set the slate flat on the block and passed his finger over the figure once more, and his eyes rested a breath too long on it, as if the number might have moved since he put it there. It had not moved. He took the red wool cord folded from under his coat and did not put it on, folded it again instead, and went off toward High Kiln with the box in his house still locked and the true subtraction inside, believing he had done the district a kindness. He never looked into the basket in the moot-post's shade. The twice-wiped thumb went over the figure and the hand went up the road, and between the figure and the hand there was no seam, no note, and no name for the difference between what had been read and what had been paid.

Sella put the fold right at the boy's ear without looking at his face, and carried the keeping back up the yard at the half-pace from every open slate, going around the space under the lowest rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. The bell's fourth-and-fifth gap hung over the market behind her with nobody in it crossing himself, and under the post the boy kept the heard bell beside the told bell in two places, added one hundred and nine to the count and spent none of it. The true subtraction went up the clay road in a locked box and the worked strips kept whatever figure they had kept, and nothing in the square had joined the two.
