Midday came into \place{House Silt} sideways, through a door that stood a finger's width open, and \person{Sella Vail} set the basket just inside it at the edge of the light and away from every open slate. She set it down as a woman sets a pot where it will not be slopped and will not be stepped in, and then took her hands off it. She opened no slate. The errand that had carried her three doors down the \place{Clay Yard} was an errand to a room, and she did not turn it into a walk to \place{Slate Row}, though Slate Row lay up the road and most of what this room held would have read better there. She stood a half-pace off with her little-door burn shut on nothing, and gave the instruction to the wool. “Keep it off his ears. It's the river wind.” The instruction went into the wool and not into the basket and not into \person[Corin]{the boy}. He lay where a pot is laid, within reach of no hand.

The room had a bench under the door, a chipped cup on the shelf by the wall, and a grey ridge of \lore{Ash} running along the skirting into the next room. Above the abandoned board-bed a round pewter tin kept its place, with a pale fold showing at the seam of the lid and a thumb's bright rub worn into it. Below it the fold of grey wool lay as it had been laid and held only the hollow where the small pale river stone had once lain. Nobody in the room said that either thing was gone. In the back room a neighbour scrubbed at a board with lye water, and a low song came up through the floor with its words flattened out by work. From the height of a basket the shelf is the highest thing in a house, and the boy had entered the tin nowhere in three years.

Then Alda came.

She came short, the way a woman comes who has been called out of a room where somebody else has just been fed: soap-and-milk gone cold under river silt, lye sour under the soap, her nails scrubbed to the quick with clay still under them. \person{Alda Reed} had her birth-slates under her arm and a narrow slate of her own in the fold of her shawl. She crossed to the bench and laid the writing-down flat before she turned toward the fold. It was the order of a hand that reaches for the record before the person, and it did not vary; he had watched it from a basket at a market's edge and from a bed, and had never once seen her reverse it. He had watched a senior man lay his hand on a chart before he looked at the patient under it, and had learned there what the order meant: the record was the thing that would still be there when the person was carried out. There was no word in \lore{Grathic} for the order, and he had no voice to spend on it, and he saw it performed again now in a room where the person was not going to be carried out at all.

She put her hand to the fold at his shoulder and pressed it flat a breath longer than a fold requires. Not tucking it, not smoothing it: pressing it, the way a hand holds pressure on a graft until the vessel takes. The small weight of her palm came through the wool and stayed and went away. His head went where it wanted under the fold and made the small wet sound it made when it went too far, and she did not put it right, because the slate was already on the bench and the fold was already pressed and there was no instruction left over for a head.

Her hand went to the red edge of the birth-flower at his wrist. Two fingers crossed there, at the corner where the branching mark nearly reached the joint, before she knew she had done it. The sign was not in her face; it was in the hand, and the hand went on. Then, as a shutter comes down over a window, she covered the sign with a date. “Stone seven,” she said, to the bench, or to the fold, or to the room. “The account went up on the eighth, before the paper went out.”

The date laid itself over the sign the way a slate laid over a crack lays itself flat. It was the date she had given the woman who bore him, and it now stood over a death more mornings gone than any living person kept count of, and a woman uncollected whom no living person could correct. Where a name should have stood there stood a date. She had put it there for mercy three years ago and had gone on putting it there ever since, and it had curdled through to a fact that nobody alive could take off. The boy lay under the wool with the birth-flower going down his forearm and the date standing over it where he could not reach either, and he kept his finger off both.

“Warm and breathing,” she said. “That's all I've set down.” It was exact. It was not true. He had no way to tell her so and no standing if he had, and she did not wait for an answer; she went on with dates after the question had stopped, because dates are a handle on a thing too large to carry: a cooper's seventh in \place{Mud End}, a potter's third child in \place{Stonemouth}, the last high water, the exact day of a birth in a house nobody in this room would ever enter. The dates came faster than dates had any business coming, and the room arranged itself around them the way a market arranges itself around a reading it has not understood.

Her hand went once inside her bodice to the little tin on its cord and came away without opening it. She took up the narrow slate from her shawl and wrote on it with a charcoal stub what she did not leave lying about, in the second hand no Court physician had ever seen, and turned it writing-down into the shawl toward a floorboard in a house the boy could not name. Six pages, all unread, written one at a time over years and never carried to a door; no seventh had been added and nothing was paid again today. Above the board-bed the pewter tin kept its pale fold and thumb-bright lid shut, and below it the hollow in the wool kept the shape of a stone nobody mentioned. Two private records in two materials stood in one room on two separate places, and a third thing lay at his wrist under a date, and the boy set all three apart and wrote no cause across the space between them. A likeness laid on a likeness will grow a cause whether or not anybody put one there. He had learned that in a better-lit room and had spent three years in this one keeping his finger off the places where it would grow.

She went out as she had come in, short, with the birth-slates under her arm, and Sella did not ask what had been written. A half-pace and a turned slate stood between the two women for the whole of the visit, which was the arrangement, and neither had put a word into it in three years. Sella lifted the basket level and carried him back out at the half-pace from every open slate, and did not look into it, and did not turn the errand into a walk to Slate Row on the way back either.

The afternoon went on at \place{the loaf shelf} under \place{the Vail kiln-house} roof, where the house kept its days badly: loaves pushed along a bare board, a glance at the clay jar on the wall, and a space at the row's end that nobody called a number. A neighbour came in with a pot in her hand and reached past the board for a loaf. Her hand went to the empty place first and opened for the half-beat it takes to find what is not there, and then closed on the loaf beyond it. Only after that did her eyes go to the boy.

“That old place at the end came in with the planting,” she said, to the board, or to her pot, or to nobody. “A row comes up short and a body gets used to it.” She said it in one breath, the planting and the short row, and went off with her pot and never saw the empty place at his ear.

The boy filed the saying beside the one already waiting there. A neighbour had stood at this same board and named the old gap as a thing the planting brought in, and had gone off with her pot without seeing the place at his ear; this one said it again with a short row to finish it, and went off the same way. Two tellings of one absence, laid on two places and kept from touching, and no third came and no finger in \place{Low Kiln} had laid them together. There was a saying of the loaf-shelves that two gaps stay two gaps until a finger lays them together and an account then wants a name. No finger had laid these together. He had spent his whole life declining to be the clean hand at the foot of a figure, and he was not going to begin at a loaf shelf with a neighbour's pot in the room.

The shelf kept its two unnamed gaps --- the old space at the row's end that came in with the planting, and the new palm's breadth that had come in the night of Seed 6 with no scrape, crumb, print or word --- in a room that did not touch the fold's one stiff brown-edged crust and its empty place. Three absences in three places, and if a line were drawn through them it would be a cause with a signature at the foot of it. He kept his finger off the shelf and off the fold and the line was not drawn.

Sella moved her lips along the loaf row and gave nobody a figure. She glanced at the clay jar on the wall shelf, cold under its glaze, and did not touch it or explain it, and moved her lips back over the same row and came to the same place twice. “The house has said it,” she said to a woman who had asked, which was true and was not a number. She did not touch \lore{the widow's mark} at any hour of the afternoon, because a shelf and a strip and a bed are not openings, and she had lost a husband to that danger and had never once described it that way herself. Her private roll stayed unshown.

She told the fire it was \lore{the thaw} and turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and fed it. The fire took the coal under blind ash and gave back four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, as it had for a hundred and seven days, its payer unnamed. Every warmth in the room had a payer and none of them stood in it to be thanked. She said \emph{the house} where another woman would have said \emph{I} and did not look toward the basket.

At the going of the light she carried him up at the half-pace from every open slate, going around the space under \place[the drying racks]{the lowest rack} as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and set him on his bed under the roof and not in \place{the Debt Loft}. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without looking at his face and left the thread of drool at his mouth frozen and unwiped, because nobody had been given an instruction about the thread and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. The crust in the fold stayed stiff with its empty place unnamed and no third.

The keeping bought him nothing and kept the places clean, which was his exact and unspendable dignity. The date over the sign and the neighbour's planting were two sayings on two places, and the two records in tin and slate stayed shut in a room he could not enter. There was no reach in the hand outside the wool for the crust two inches from his ear, no voice to ask for the number of his days, and no tool in the world that would have taken any of it out of a neighbour's mouth and set it right.

The door stood a finger's width open. The river's low muttered note came through the gap from \place{the Ilm}, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, and through the floor came \lore{the kiln's breath}, four, four and a gap under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, the two sounds in two separate places as for a hundred and seven days. The boards overhead kept four nails and four and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts did not meet. Cold came in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, and the wool rode back from the wrist so the cold found the bare place. He wanted the crust, the breast, the bucket's lip two paces off, and the number of his days said aloud by somebody who would know what it counted, and could reach or ask for none of them. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down. The count stood at one hundred and seven, unspent, and the dark under the roof kept it without a figure.
