Midday came down off the terrace and went along with them down the river road, the light lying flat on the clay and having no shadow in it worth the naming. \person{Sella Vail} carried \person[Corin]{the boy} at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, the basket level against her hip, the fold laid over him so that the running water spoke to the wool and not to the basket. Keep it off his ears, she told the wool; it had followed them all the way down from the yard and would go all the way back up before the light was gone, and she had said it once, because a wind takes an instruction and carries it away and there is an end of it. The instruction went to the wool and to the running water and never to the basket. Woodsmoke went before her and the river wind came up to meet it, and the two crossed between the low houses without giving each other a figure for the passing, the way two people pass on a stair with a hand on the wall and their eyes on the step ahead.

He was one hundred and forty-eight days old and four months and twenty-eight days, and nobody on the road, nor under any roof behind them, had said either number, and there was no vessel in the district that would have held either one still long enough for a woman passing with a pot to set it down. The reed month had two days left. The last market and the third Account-day stood together tomorrow at the bend of the road, one day and the same day, and the district went about its work as though a month can go out without anybody drawing a line under it, which it can, and did. The bundles came along the boards in armfuls judged by the arm that had carried them, grey at the broken ends and pale where the frost had bitten the tips, and nobody put a figure to the hands that had cut them, because the hands were out in the beds and the answer to a cost asked in the form of a direction is a direction, which goes off along the water and leaves no entry anywhere.

Beyond the bank \place{the plank walk} ran out over \place{the Ilm} on posts, and the note of water going over water came up through the gaps between the boards --- a low fifth thing he had never counted among \lore{the full note} and never would, because a thing a man has not been given he cannot spend. Sella set the basket at the edge of the work where no slate lay, where the boards gave onto the flat stone and the shade, gave the running water the rest of the instruction, and stood a half-pace off. There was no slate within reach of the basket and no hand had set one there; the nearest writing in the district stood a league downriver at \place{the out-of-line landing}, with its back turned and its cuff gone grey, and she had kept the whole width of the market between him and it without once turning her head that way.

The reed-cutter came along the boards with the last of the bundles on his shoulder and the short length of grey wool, or reed-fibre, oiled by a working thumb, gone the colour of a thing that has been handled too often ever to be washed clean again. He set the bundle down on \place{the flat blessing stone}, where the hearth-maids had stood to bless the traps in Ilmen, and turned the cord over on his thumb, straightening it, and then turned it once so that the knots stood up in the small light --- the turn of a man who wants a thing read and knows perfectly well it will not be. He tied one low with his teeth and a pull of the thumb, bit the end off, and put the cord back inside his coat with the flat of his hand, drawing the coat closed over it, so that the last of the figure went under the cloth and stayed there.

“I can't read a cord,” a woman said, and she said it to the boards and not to him, the way a person says a thing they have already been answered about.

“You can ask the cord,” the reed-cutter said. “No more can the Court.” He said it without anger and without a smile, and went up the boards to lift a trap with the question going down into the water after him.

No figure came out of it. No knot was counted, and no name was given, and the cord was not laid beside the empty place in the returning line where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come back, nor beside \person{Orra Weir}'s knot-string, nor beside the second twist under \person{Mira Silt}'s apron, though all three lay within a dozen paces of the basket. A tally kept in a material no Court thumb will read is a tally kept; the care of the hiding tells more than the writing would, and the boy had learned to read the care before he learned to read the marks. He had no standing to enter any of it, and his hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water and came down against the fold.

\scenebreak

Orra stood at \place{the lower boards} with her board on its side and a grey cord at her waist, turning the board with the wrist that had turned it ten thousand times and would turn it ten thousand more before she let it go. Her hands were like old rope, and the pale eel scars lay uncovered across both forearms in arcs the colour of a thing that has been cut and left lying in the salt; the salt itself stood white at her wrists where the water had dried and dried again. The grey streak showed first through the black hair when she moved her head. When she was tired the nasal half-song of \place[the Fenmarch]{the Fen} came into her voice, which she had never entirely lost and had never tried to lose, and she was tired this midday, and the half-song went along under her words like a second string on an instrument tuned a little flat.

She pulled one knot down with her thumb and set it among the others with a small flat knock, the way a woman sets a cup down where it has stood a thousand times and will stand again.

A woman at the rails asked what the levy would take now that the hands were out in the reeds and the last bundles were coming along the boards.

“The ninth of Ilmen,” Orra said. “The collector's boat came round the bend on the flood and had six oars. One man stood at the stern and never touched them.”

“That isn't the levy.”

“It's a date. The river takes what the Court doesn't. You can shout at either one. I've tried.”

Her thumb went to the cord again as the sentence ended, and the boy could not tell whether that was a lie or only the habit of counting, and made no cause of it. A date given where a figure was asked is an answer with the question taken out of it --- a chart laid face-down over an unread note with the neat margin left showing; and the man at the stern keeping his hands off the oars stood on a place of its own in the file, apart from the midwife's reaching hand and the thane's twice-wiped thumb, and no cause was written across the three. He had learned, in a better-lit room he did not name, what three likenesses standing in a row will do to a man who wants an answer, and he kept his hand between them.

The last knot on the cord showed its double turn, tail tucked, tied left over right, with the small extra weight of a thumb pressed in --- legible and complete, and with its name withheld. The word forty did not come into the shade that midday. She had set a thing down in front of him years ago and was still waiting to see whether it would be picked up, and a boy who could not reach a cup two paces off could not reach a knot a league upriver, and she got no answer from the basket, and went on turning her board. The cord went at the end of the morning back up to \place[The Eel House]{the Eel House}, where no clerk could spell it and no boy could read it.

\scenebreak

Below the sill, at \place{the fish-trade shade}, Mira worked the eels with pale slime dried in long grey strokes on her sleeves and river silt dried pale in the hollow of her neck, her head held a little to the right where the top of her left ear had been. Her small smile stayed on her face the way a door is kept on the latch: it could be shut without anybody noticing the movement, and she kept it there while she worked, and had kept it there through three years of a thing she had never once put into words a slate could take. She did not look into the basket. She had not looked into it on any of the days he had been carried past her, and the not-looking was a keeping done briskly, before anyone could make a speech about it.

“Will the run be thin?” a weir woman asked her, a woman with salt white at her wrists who had come down with a creel.

“Thin?” Mira said. “Who's to say thin? It comes when it comes. Ask the river.” She answered her own question because a question left standing in a market is a thing somebody will carry away and enter somewhere, and she had learned to take it back before it could be carried.

She flicked an eel-bone against her thumb while deciding whether to lie, moved two from hand to hand, and put one away when the deciding was done. The boy had learned to read the small movement the way another man reads a face across a table --- the bone going up, the bone going away --- and he could not tell what lie she had decided against, and did not make one for her. Whatever it was went into the pocket with the bone and came out into the market as weather.

When a buyer named a figure for two pair, her thumb went under her apron to the grey cord and stayed there longer than the figure required, while the deciding went on. Shifting a creel showed the second cord twisted twice beneath the grey, and the boy saw it as he had seen it a hundred times --- never carried upriver to \place{Nine Pots}, never lifted at a door, neither knot counted by any thumb but her own, and the knot for the thin child upriver not touched. “Two marks the pair and two short,” she said. “You've said it twice and I've counted it twice. There's nothing in the sentence that wants an answer.” The buyer took the eels and went, and she went on with her hands in the water and did not look into the basket.

Her board lay beside Orra's and did not touch it. Two women who had kept two accounts in wool for fifteen years stood shoulder to shoulder at the same boards and had never once laid the one across the other; the boy kept them on two places in the file and wrote no joining between them. Her not-looking stood on a tray of its own --- a keeping, the opposite of a pricing face turned the same way across a market --- and he had no word in \lore{Grathic} for either.

\scenebreak

Below the sill, where the shade of the boards fell across the second step and the water went by brown and flat and glossy, a child's foot stayed dark inside three turns of wool. The woman who had wound it called it the cold. A neighbour come down with a pail called the water weather, and said it would go when it would go, and the two facts went past each other in the shade without ever meeting, as they had gone past each other since the flood and would go on doing.

The boy read the foot from the basket's low angle with the whole attention of a man who has spent his life at the edge of a bed where warmth goes out of a person in an order nobody likes to name. Extremity before core: the foot would give its news first, and the child's small almost-smile was not peace but the grimace of deep cold that looks like peace to everybody who has not learned to tell the two apart, and the afterdrop stood waiting on the far side of a warming nobody in the shade was going to perform. There was no silt-leach in the reading, no cause and no cure, because the district had given neither one a name and he had no hand to put where the knowledge would have gone. The reading worked as perfectly as it had worked the day he learned it, in a country that had no word for what he was looking at and he no word to say it in, and it bought nothing. A man who could have named what was happening to that foot could not have lifted the fold off his own chin; and the woman wound the wool again the way she had wound it before, and the neighbour with the pail went up the steps, and the foot stayed dark inside its three turns, the cold to one woman and weather to the yard, entered nowhere.

\scenebreak

Below \place{the north bridge}, at the out-of-line landing, the soft-cuffed clerk was still set. His cuff was grey from washing and grey again by midday, the slate held to his forearm with the writing turned inward where nobody else could read it, and his hand went to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls, whether or not there was a figure to enter. Four steps, four, and a landing a hand's breadth out of line, and the market went up and down over the whole of it all afternoon without a fall and without stepping around him. Nobody had taken him off. Nobody asked what he was set for. He sat where a stone sits that lies where a stone lies, and the water of the market went over the top of him and left him where he was. He was none of the figures the boy kept on separate trays --- not the man with grey fingers, not the hand with the pause between strokes, not the soft hands held at a table at the edge of the square, not the child who turned half a beat late at the sound of a pot set down. A measure set down before anybody called it is a measure set down ahead of something; the boy kept the order of reaching on a place of its own and made no rule of it and no sixth of anything.

\scenebreak

So the five records stood within a dozen paces of the basket and not one of them could be entered: the reed-cutter's cord gone back inside the coat with its knots unread; Orra's knot-string with its legible double turn and its name withheld; the second cord twisted twice beneath Mira's grey wool, never carried upriver and never lifted at a door; the clerk's slate turned inward to his own forearm; and the child's foot gone dark inside three turns of wool. They stood side by side on the boards and in the shade and did not lie across one another. A finger laid from the first to the last would have made an account of the district that no Court thumb had ever been able to spell, and there was no finger in the shade that could have laid it, and the boy's hand outside the wool could not have reached the first inch of the distance. Five likenesses standing in a row will call themselves a doctrine if nobody keeps a hand between them, and a doctrine is an account that has found a payer; no payer had been named to any of these, and he held them apart by hand as he had held the eight words on the interval tray at home, and would hold the ninth when the night came for it.

The market went on over the soft-cuffed clerk as water goes over a stone that lies where a stone lies. His hand went to the slate before his eyes found the stalls; the eels went on being sold; the cord went back under the coat; the knots stayed where they were. Sella gave the instruction to the wool and the running water and never to the basket, and stood a half-pace from every open slate, and turned no head toward the writing that lay a league downriver with its back to the light. One hundred and forty-eight stood exact and unspent, the loan uncalled, the seventh year one tend from its end and still held, the ninth stopping owed to the night and not yet come.

His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water through the gaps in the boards and came down against the fold. The reed month had two days left and would not say so. The market sold over the stone, and nobody made anything of any of it.
