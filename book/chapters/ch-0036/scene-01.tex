\daymark{147}{Reed 28, Y.H. 813 (Eighthday)}

\dropcap{T}{he reed} month had three days left and nobody in \place{Low Kiln} was keeping them. It would go out as it had come in, without a thumb laid on a slate to say here and no further, and the last market stood two days off beyond the bend of the road and the third Account-day three. \person{Sella Vail} took \person[Corin]{the boy} down in the early cold at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, woodsmoke going before her up \place{the clay road} and the river wind coming up to meet it, the two crossing between the houses the way two people pass on a stair in the dark, each with a hand on the wall and neither speaking. Keep it off his ears, she told the wool; it had come all the way down with them and would go all the way back up before the light was gone; and she gave nothing more to the wind, having said it once, because a wind takes an instruction and carries it away and there is an end of it. The instruction went to the wool and to the river wind and never to the basket. One hundred and forty-seven days old. Four months and twenty-seven days. Neither number was spoken under any roof that morning, and nobody in the yard had a word that would hold either one still long enough for a woman passing with a pot to set it down.

The night's cold lay in the ruts under a skin thin as the fat skimmed from a pot, and the wool at his ear had taken the river wind all the way down and was damp against his cheek. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had wiped it; nobody under any roof had been given an instruction about the thread, and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. From the low angle he saw the hems of coats and the backs of hands and the mud worked grey into the creases of wrists, and not one face. The half-pace was measured from slates the way a surgeon measures a field from the one thing in it that must not be touched; it was not a slow pace and not a hurried one, but a distance kept. He knew the order in which cold arrives --- cheek, temple, wrist, the wool riding back from the small bare joint --- as certainly as he had once known the order in which a man's breathing goes over when the core is going, and the knowing was of the old life and the want was of this body, and the want was louder. He wanted the wool up over his ear. He could not lift it. The two facts stood side by side, and neither cancelled the other, and neither could be entered anywhere.

The cutters had been downriver since before first grey and were coming back up with the last of the cutting across their shoulders, armfuls laid against the cold the way a woman lays a child against her coat. They came around the pans set out to dry and the trough by the well with its skin of ice, and the ice was called weather and left where it lay. At the edge of \place{the worked strips} Sella set the basket down level, as a pot is set down where it must not be slopped, and stood a half-pace from every open slate. The grey reeds stood in the ruts in long ranks, green at the broken ends and pale where the frost had bitten the tips, and nobody counted them. Behind \place{the drying racks} the river went over its stones with the note of water over water that was a fifth low thing and never one of the hearings; he left it where it sounded and did not make it a hearing to fill a place. There was tallow in the air from a pot scoured near the fire, and wet wool, and the Ash-dry mud that clung to nothing so well as to a working cuff.

A boot had set a bundle down in the mud and the mud kept the print and gave it back slowly, the way bone that has been set gives back the order of the breaking to a man who knows where to look. What it told was what had been done and in what order, and never what the doing cost. From the basket there were hems and hands and no faces, which was the whole of the world at that height.

A cutter come up from \place{the reed beds} with a bundle on his shoulder wiped his palms down his thighs before he set it down. A girl with a creel stood measuring it with her eyes, finding the place her hands would go, the way a woman finds the place on a pot before she lifts it.

“Lift it,” the cutter said. “Feel where the weight settles. Your arm’ll tell you what went into it, and that’s the whole of what it knows.”

She got it up with the green ends shivering and the pale tips brushing her coat, and the creel bumped her hip as she turned. “And what’ll it know about the hands that cut it?”

He was a dozen paces down with another armful before the answer came over his shoulder. “Nothing. Ask it how heavy it is. That’s a question it can answer.”

A man come up from the pans with a strip of reed in his hand put the older question to a cutter going past: what the hands would be paid now that the month was near its end. The cutter did not break his step.

“The weather and the river,” he said. “Ask them. They’ll tell you when they’ve had enough.”

The man shut his mouth on the next part and went back to his pans. No figure went down the yard with him and no figure came back. The boy kept the exchange on a place of its own, because a cost asked in the form of a direction and answered with a direction leaves no entry for any thumb to read.

It was the last work of the reed month done in the ordinary way, with no more ceremony than a pot set out to dry. Women worked the near rows with their skirts kilted and their forearms grey to the wrist, turning wet pots out of the shade and setting them where the light fell; the light went along the rims and came off them, and no woman called it a fine morning. Two men went past with a barrow that had lost its easy rolling in the flood, and neither put a number to what the barrow held. A girl at the slip pits lifted her hands out of a pan to show the woman beside her that they were clean, and the woman looked and went back to her own pan. The work was honest and complete and went on being worth nothing to the one person in the yard who could have read it best.

The yard went around its three places and its one empty place as it had gone around them every morning of the month. At the Vail corner \lore{Ash} had turned and left a clean dark line behind it, and beside the line lay the new finer hairline of grey the house called \lore{the thaw}, thinner and paler than the old mark, gathering where nobody had put a broom. Sella carried past with her shoulder tilted and both lines where they lay, gave the broom no instruction for them and swept neither. Farther on she went wide around the space beneath \place[the drying racks]{the lowest rack} where the broken birth-slates lay, as wide as a woman goes around a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and did not look into the space and did not enter it. And past that, in the returning line where the hands came back at dusk, was a place the yard went around with a look and a wide step. A hand had gone in there on Seed 15 and had not come out with the rest on Seed 27. No name had come up, no body, no cause, no figure, and no hand had come up to be counted; a slate would be found and a figure made if one came up in a month or a year, and nothing had come up.

He had seen the going-around in the planting, in the flood and through the cutting, and each time it was the same piece of work performed carefully and without comment: an eye laid on the place, a shoulder turned, a step taken wide enough that no heel came near it, and then the ordinary work resumed as though nothing had been avoided. He had learned to read an omission by the care spent on the surface around it, the way a man reads a wound by the cleanliness of the field about it; here the care was spent in a step, and the step was the whole of the record, and the record was a direction and not a figure. He did not lay that empty place under the empty space at the rack or under the gaps on \place{the loaf shelf}; an absence in a line of hands is an absence in a line of hands until somebody puts a name to it, and nobody in the yard had put one in a year.

\scenebreak

A reed-cutter came along the boards past the basket with a bundle and the short length of grey cord carried inside his coat, the wool or reed-fibre gone the colour of a stitch-hole a thread has been drawn through a dozen times, which is the grey a working thumb leaves and the colour of use and nothing more. He set the bundle down where no slate lay, straightened the cord on his thumb and turned it once so the knots stood in the small light; he tied one low with his teeth and a pull of the thumb, bit the end off and put the cord back inside his coat, drawing the coat closed over it with the flat of his hand. Nobody asked him what it held and he counted nothing where anybody could hear him count. The boy let the cord lie where it lay. A cord on a board is a cord on a board; a mark under the loaf shelf is a mark under a shelf; an empty place in a line of hands is neither of these. He did not lay the cord beside the charcoal under the shelf, or beside the empty place the yard had just gone around, or beside \person[Mira Silt]{Mira}'s two cords, one of which had never been carried upriver to \place{Nine Pots} or lifted at a door, or beside \person{Orra Weir}'s knot-string on its own board below the sill. He left them where they had been put, unread and not joined, as he had left them on the days before and would leave them on the days after.

The carrying arm was the record he could read best and the one that told the least. It knows what it has carried and never what the carrying cost: how often it lifted, how the weight sat, which shoulder took the longer armful when the wind came upriver, and never what the lifting took out of the woman who lifted. In the life he had left, a chart told what had been done to a body and never what the body had paid to go on standing; here a forearm told the same and kept the same silence, and there was no word in the district for the difference between them. Exact, complete, worth nothing to a thumb looking for a figure. The yard paid its last week's cut in armfuls and weather and questions answered with directions, and there was no entry for a Court thumb to read because there was no entry. The last bundles going out of the reeds were an ending the whole yard could see and nobody would date; his one hundred and forty-seven was an ending nobody could see and a number nobody would speak, and it went on exact and unspent in a body that could not lift a hand to lay it down.

He noted the order of it again without deciding to. The hand went to the record before the hand went to the person: in a woman turning her shoulder half a beat ahead of her gaze, in a cutter feeling the weight settle before he looked at the girl who had asked, in a man shutting his mouth on a figure before he looked at the face of the man who had asked for one. He had first seen that order in a room whose walls he did not carry with him now, where a hand went to the chart before any eye went to the face on the table; here it wore the clothes of a yard, and wore them so completely that no woman in it would have known she was doing it. The surgeon's register noted the order and had no standing to enter it anywhere, which was the whole of what the register had been able to do since he came into this body.

\scenebreak

The morning went on into midday without anybody marking it, as the month had gone out without anybody marking it. Sella shifted the basket a hand's breadth out of the draught and gave the instruction to the wool and to the running water again, and never to the basket. She went around the pan set out to dry and went around the rack space again on the way back, and did not touch \lore{the widow's mark}, because a yard is not an opening and a basket is not a door.

The competence was the whole of the helplessness: he could read a carrying arm the way another man reads a chart, and he could not lift a hand to the bundle the arm had carried. There was no dishonesty in the reading and no use in it, and he held both facts in one breath without either cancelling the other. He wanted the bundle two paces off, the pot under the roof, the place in the returning line that had been given a look and a wide step; he could not reach any of them. The cutter went back up the boards with the bundle counted only in the arm that carried it, and his coat closed over the uncounted cord as he went. The boy's small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water that came from behind the racks, and came down against the fold. The last bundles stood grey in the ruts with nobody counting them, and one hundred and forty-seven went on exact and unspent with nobody's thumb upon it, the last market two days off and the month going out without a boundary drawn.
