The light went off the river before it went off the clay, and for a little while the boards held the last of it in the gaps between them while the water below went dark under its skin, so that the note of water over water came up through the same gaps that the light had gone down, one going and one coming and neither giving the other a figure for the passing. \person{Sella Vail} took the basket up from the edge of the work when the shade had come down onto the shade, and set her half-pace against the going of the road, woodsmoke going before her up \place{the clay road} and the river wind coming up to meet it, the two crossing between the low houses the way two people pass on a stair with a hand on the wall and their eyes on the step ahead. Keep it off his ears, she told the wool; it had come all the way down with them and would go all the way back up before the night was out; and then she gave nothing more to the wind, having said it once, because a wind takes an instruction and carries it away and there is an end of it. The instruction went to the wool and to the running water and never to the basket. \person[Corin]{The boy} lay with his cheek to the fold and the small wet sound coming and going as his head wandered and was not set right, and his hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water and came down against the cloth.

He was one hundred and forty-eight days old and four months and twenty-eight days, and nobody on the road nor under any roof ahead of them had said either number, and there was no vessel in the district that would have held either one still long enough for a woman passing with a pot to set it down. The reed month had one day left now, and the last market stood at the bend of tomorrow with the third Account-day standing in the same place, one day and the same day, and the district went up the road to its suppers as though a month can go out without anybody drawing a line under it, which it can, and did. The last bundles stood grey in the ruts behind them where the afternoon had left them, green at the broken ends and pale where the frost had bitten the tips, and nobody had counted them since the light went, because the hands that had cut them were out of the beds and a cost asked in the form of a direction is answered with a direction, which goes off along the water and leaves no entry anywhere.

Halfway up she went around a pan set out to dry, tilting shoulder and basket together so the pan stayed where the pan stood and nothing knocked, and at the well she went around the trough with its skin of ice thin as a skim of fat set aside in a dish, and did not break the skin to get at the water underneath. Weather, the neighbours had called it all through Reed, and it would go when it would go; she gave the instruction to the bucket, which stood where a bucket stands and wanted nothing from her. He had learned before he could hold up his own head that a thing called weather is a thing nobody has agreed to count, and that a man who wants a figure from weather is a man who will be given a direction instead. At the yard she went wide around the space beneath \place[the drying racks]{the lowest rack}, where the broken birth-slates lay, as wide as a woman goes around a pail of hot water set down where a child might knock it --- a step taken for the thing's sake and not for her own, and not explained to anybody, least of all to the child in the basket, who could not have knocked over a cup if he had been set beside one. The \lore{Ash} in the corner lay where it had lain: the clean dark line where the ridge had turned and turned again, and beside it the new finer hairline of grey gathered since, both called \lore{the thaw} all through the going-out of the month and neither swept, and the name had not made either go.

Under \place[the Vail kiln-house]{the Vail roof} she lifted him out of the basket without once looking into it, the way a woman lifts a pot by the handle and does not look inside because looking will not change what is in it. She set him on \place{the bed} and not in \place{the Debt Loft}, setting him down as one sets a pot down where it will not be slopped --- the base first, the weight settled, the hand kept where it is until the thing is sure of its place. Then she put the fold right at his ear and under his chin, and did not once look at his face. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the cold and she left it frozen and unwiped, because nobody under any roof in \place{Low Kiln} had been given an instruction about the thread, and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. Her hand rested a half-beat longer than the setting needed before it went away. She had learned that half-beat in the winter she had sat up three nights with a child of her own who cooled while everybody was kind, and had never told anybody what she had learned, and the house had straightened around the silence every time since. Then the hand went away and she did not go back to it.

At \place{the hearth} she told the fire it was the thaw, which it was, and would go when it would go. She turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and fed them and said “The house will keep it” where another woman would have said \emph{I}. The fire gave its four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, and she did not count the pulls aloud and did not ask anybody to hear the silence, and its payer had been unnamed now for a hundred and forty-eight days. She stood a moment with the back of the broken pot in her hand, counting something under her breath toward no opening. She pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn, the grey streak at her temple showing pale off the coals, and set the pot where it could be found in the dark, rim out and the handle toward the reaching hand --- the handle standing two inches beyond the hand that would have reached for it and would not close on it --- and did not look toward the bed.

\scenebreak

Out at the hearth \person{Tarn} put the last of the coals to bed under the back of a broken pot, and set the small knife back at the very edge of its sill, where a man could check it in the dark if he wanted to and where the house could check it in the morning if it wanted to. The small knock the knife made going down onto the wood that knew it went into the room and stayed there. Six years stood in the worn thumb-mark where the house could check; the seventh stood where it could not, one tend from its end and still held. Beside the knife lay the unforged sentence about a collector, kept where he kept the seventh year and entered in neither. \lore{Ash} was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line, sour in the small wounds of the pits, and the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back. When he had nothing to do with his hands he put them on the broom, which was a way of knowing where they were.

He took the broom up only after it had been given its instruction, which was a formality between him and the house that neither of them ever skipped, and he said “Yes, house” to the fire before he said anything to the wood. Then he told the kindling what he told it every night at the going of the light. “Six counted,” he said. “The next I don't count.”

He brought the other sentence up to the threshold after that --- the one about an ear under this roof being worth more than the debt that held it --- and he brought it all the way to the word that would call the loan, the way a man brings a tool up out of a pocket and holds it with the edge down. It came the ninth time that night, on Reed 29 after Reed 5, 8, 11, 14, 17, 20, 23 and 26, and it stopped at the word with no figure behind it and no name under the figure, and the word did not go out into the room and the loan did not hear itself called. He had said the sentence eight nights already and had stopped at the same word each time, and the stopping was not a habit and not a saying he had learned to leave unfinished, because a man who stops a sentence at the same word nine times is a man who knows exactly what stands on the other side of it. He held the edge down. He did not let the word cross.

He drew the breath that should have gone in at the top of the reach and did not spend it. It stood at the top of the reach as a small interval stands on a tray, the place where a breath should have gone in and had not, and he let it go the way a man lets go of a tool he has taken out and decided not to use. “Yes, house,” he said to the fire, which had not spoken, and went on with the broom over \place{the flagstones} without the half-check, the stroke going on past the shallow bright patch where it had once stopped and the stones keeping that place as a thing nobody would enter. His chest had been tight since the dark and the breath he had not spent sat in it like a small cold thing he had been carrying all day and would carry into his sleep.

Sella, within hearing of all of it, gave the end of her instruction to the pot and asked nothing. She had never asked him about his seventh year and had never asked him about his ear, and she did not ask now, at the word that would have let her ask, because a thing asked aloud is a thing the Court can enter and this house had lived a long time on the not-entering. She had said \emph{The house will keep it} to the fire a moment before, and the house kept the silence the same way it kept the warmth, by not saying whose it was. \person{Jorra Vail} came through from the shed with the day's fine grey slate dust still worked into both forearms past the bent little finger, and spoke to a pot on the bench and not to anybody, and did not look toward the bed. He did not ask about the ear. Tarn did not ask about the room. The two silences stayed where they had always stayed under this roof, side by side and unjoined, and no word was put between them. The boy, who had heard the sentence come up and stop, lay with the fold at his ear and made no sound that any of them could have taken for a hearing.

\scenebreak

In the small hours he turned out his file the way a man lays instruments on a towel before a case begins, each thing going into its own hollow and nothing resting across anything else. He had kept it a hundred and forty-eight days in a body that could not have lifted a spoon from the towel, and the keeping of the things apart was the only work in the room that no slate would enter.

And on the widened tray, in the place he had made for the stoppings and had widened once more, lay \emph{nine} of them --- Reed 5, Reed 8, Reed 11, Reed 14, Reed 17, Reed 20, Reed 23, Reed 26 and now Reed 29 --- nine objects and not a rule, nine intervals and not nine payments. The ninth had come that night at this hearth, brought all the way to the word that would call the loan by a man telling the kindling that six were counted and the next he would not count; the sentence about an ear under this roof being worth more than the debt that holds it had gone to the threshold of the word and stopped there, with no figure and no name, and the breath that should have gone in at the top of the reach had stayed where it was. He filed it on the tray and not among the hearings, because a stopping is not a hearing and a held word is not a paid one, and a ninth stopping is no more a ninth hearing than an eighth had been an eighth hearing. Six hearings of \lore{the full note} lay beside one another and never among them, and no seventh had come to fill the empty place, and the empty place stayed empty.

He held all nine apart by hand, because he had no other hand to hold them with. Nine likenesses standing in a row will stand up and call themselves a doctrine if nobody keeps a finger between them, and a doctrine is an account that has found a payer; no payer had been named to any of the nine. He had learned in a better-lit room what a row of likenesses will do when a man with a clean slate comes to count them --- how they stand up out of their places and arrange themselves into a rule, and how the rule acquires a foot at the bottom of it with a name under the foot, and how quickly the name becomes a person. He was not going to let nine stoppings stand up in the dark and acquire a payer because he had been too tired to keep his hand between them. The cost of the keeping was that he was the only adult under this roof and the least use in it: a man who had once reduced a shoulder in the dark could not lift the fold off his own chin, and the doctrine he would not let stand up was a doctrine nobody in this house wanted to stand up either, and still it had to be held.

A held word is a debt and a debt that can hear is a debt that can be called, and he held the nine apart because a debt called at the foot of nine likenesses is a debt with a name and a number under the name and a collector coming down the road. The loan stood uncalled. The breath from Seed 16 stood unspent, with its small interval at the top of each reach to show where it should have gone in. The seventh year stood one tend from its end and had not given. The unforged sentence about a collector lay beside the knife at the very edge of its sill where it could be checked, and he could not have reached the knife to check it himself, and had never wanted to.

Through the floor under blind ash the carried fire gave four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for a hundred and forty-eight days. Through the finger's-width door came the river's note of water over water, a fifth low thing and never counted among the hearings, and overhead the boards kept four nails, four, and a fifth that was a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts in three places, and none of them became one. \lore{The damper} lay where it lay as one iron with two bites, joined to no kiln; the two tellings of the shut counting room stood side by side with no third; the told bell of \lore{the Long Night} stood beside the heard bell. He looked at them and did not let the look become a joining. An inventory is not a key and a list is not a hand.

Cold came in its known order: cheek, temple, wrist, the wool riding back from the small bare joint. One hundred and forty-eight days old and four months and twenty-eight days, told by nobody, and if somebody under that roof had said the number nobody would have known what it counted, and there was no word in \lore{Grathic} that would have held it long enough for a woman passing on the clay to set it down. He wanted the crust two inches from his ear, and he wanted the number spoken by somebody who would have known what a number was, and he got neither and could not ask for either.

The ninth stopping lay on the tray among the eight others with no payer named to any of the nine, and his hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water and came down against the fold, one hundred and forty-eight exact and unspent in the dark, the loan uncalled and the seventh year one tend from its end and still held, the reed month going out tomorrow without anybody drawing a line under it.
