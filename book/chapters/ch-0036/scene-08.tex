The light went off the river before it went off the clay, and for a little while the boards below the sill held the last of it in the gaps between them while the water underneath went dark under its skin, so that the note of water over water came up through the same gaps the light had gone down, one going and one coming and neither giving the other a figure for the passing. The reed month went out the way it had come in, without a boundary drawn at its going; a sill does not stop water and does not keep it, and the boards went on smelling of wet reed and pig and clay after it was past. \person{Sella Vail} took the basket up from \place{the market's edge} when the shade had come down onto the shade, woodsmoke going before her along \place{the clay road} and the river wind coming up to meet it, the two crossing between the low houses the way two people pass on a stair in the dark, each with a hand on the wall and neither speaking. Keep it off his ears, she told the wool; it had come all the way down with them and would go all the way back up before the night was out, and she gave nothing more to the wind, having said it once, because a wind takes an instruction and carries it away and there is an end of it. The instruction went to the wool and to the river wind and never to the basket.

One hundred and forty-nine days old. Four months and twenty-nine days. Neither number was spoken under any roof in \place{Low Kiln} that evening, and there was no vessel in the district that would have held either one still long enough for a woman passing with a pot to set it down. The house kept its days in loaves pushed along a board and glances at a clay jar, and the count he carried had no handle and no rim and nobody who would have known what a number was to say it over.

Halfway up she went around a pan set out to dry, tilting shoulder and basket together so the pan stayed where the pan stood and nothing knocked; and at the well she went around the trough with its skin of ice thin as a skim of fat set aside in a dish, and did not break the skin to get at the water underneath. Weather, the neighbours had called it all through Reed, and it would go when it would go. She gave the instruction to the bucket, which stood where a bucket stands and wanted nothing from her. He had learned before he could hold up his own head that a thing called weather is a thing nobody has agreed to count, and that a man who wants a figure from weather is a man who will be given a direction instead. At the yard she went wide around the space beneath \place[the drying racks]{the lowest rack}, where the broken birth-slates lay, as wide as a woman goes around a pail of hot water set down where a child might knock it --- a step taken for the thing's sake and not for her own, and not explained to anybody, least of all to the child in the basket, who could not have knocked over a cup if he had been set beside one. The \lore{Ash} in the corner lay where it had lain: the clean dark line where the ridge had turned and turned again, and beside it the new finer hairline of grey gathered since, both called \lore{the thaw} all through the going-out of the month and neither swept, and the name had not made either go. The last bundles stood grey in the ruts where the afternoon had left them, green at the broken ends and pale where the frost had bitten the tips, and nobody had counted them since the light went, because the hands that had cut them were out of the beds and a cost asked in the form of a direction is answered with a direction, which goes off along the water and leaves no entry anywhere.

Under \place[the Vail kiln-house]{the Vail roof} she lifted him out of the basket without once looking into it, the way a woman lifts a pot by the handle and does not look inside because looking will not change what is in it. She set him on \place{the bed} and not in \place{the Debt Loft}, setting him down as one sets a pot down where it will not be slopped --- the base first, the weight settled, the hand kept where it is until the thing is sure of its place. Then she put the fold right at his ear and under his chin, and did not once look at his face. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the cold and she left it frozen and unwiped, because nobody under any roof in Low Kiln had been given an instruction about the thread, and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. Her hand rested a half-beat longer than the setting needed before it went away. She had learned that half-beat in the winter she had sat up three nights with a child of her own who cooled while everybody was kind, and had never told anybody what she had learned, and the house had straightened around the silence every time since. Then the hand went away and she did not go back to it.

At \place{the hearth} she told the fire it was the thaw, which it was, and would go when it would go. She turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and fed them and said “The house will keep it” where another woman would have said \emph{I}. The fire gave its four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, and she did not count the pulls aloud and did not ask anybody to hear the silence, and its payer had been unnamed now for a hundred and forty-nine days. She stood a moment with the back of the broken pot in her hand, counting something under her breath toward no opening. She pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn, the grey streak at her temple showing pale off the coals, and set the pot where it could be found in the dark, rim out and the handle toward the reaching hand --- the handle standing two inches beyond the hand that would have reached for it and would not close on it --- and did not look toward the bed.

\lore{The widow's mark} hung where it hung at her throat, a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and she did not touch it. She touched it before she opened the kiln and at no other hour, and a dusk is not an opening. She went to her own bed without explaining the count and without looking toward the bed where \person[Corin]{the boy} lay, and the two rooms of the house did not touch.

In the small hours the boy turned out his file the way a man lays instruments on a towel before a case begins, each thing going into its own hollow and nothing resting across anything else. He had kept it a hundred and forty-nine days in a body that could not have lifted a spoon from the towel, and the keeping of the things apart was the only work in the room that no slate would enter.

Six hearings of \lore{the full note} lay beside one another and never among them: a cup across a birth room, a pot under \place[the Vail wheel-shed]{the wheel-shed wall}, the district's slate twice at \place{the Cross}, a man giving at \lore[the Hearth Drawing]{the Drawing}, and the small flat knock out of a woman as the men lifted, that last from a person and filed beside the five and never counted with them. No seventh had come. The bell was a told bell and a heard bell, kept in two places; the wheel and the pots under the roof were notes he had left where they sounded; the clerk's slate was a figure he could not read. He had not invented one to fill the empty place. An empty place kept honestly is a record of a kind, and a man who fills one to make a row come out even is a man with a reason, and he had learned two lives ago to be afraid of the shape of a reason in a man with clean hands.

Three near-full answers stood on their own tray, advanced and not paid: a cup across a birth room, a pot under the wheel-shed wall, a rack pot lifted a hand's breadth too high and set down again, and \person{Jorra Vail}'s knowing of a pot's note withheld from the kiln-master who said it sang before its time. Each had wanted a following stroke that nobody had made, and he had not made it for any of them; a knowing held back is not a hearing given, and the knowing went to the tray and not among the six.

The older intervals lay where they had always lain: the half-beat behind a standing finger, the stroke that had gone on over \place{the flagstones} without the check, his own held breath from the market, the unchecking stroke come up beside the half-beat with no cause written across them.

And on the widened tray, in the place he had made for the stoppings and had widened once more, lay \emph{nine} of them --- Reed 5, Reed 8, Reed 11, Reed 14, Reed 17, Reed 20, Reed 23, Reed 26 and Reed 29 --- nine objects and not a rule, nine intervals and not nine payments. The ninth had come the night before at this hearth, brought all the way to the word that would call the loan by a man telling the kindling that six were counted and the next he would not count; the sentence about an ear under this roof being worth more than the debt that holds it had gone to the threshold of the word and stopped there, with no figure and no name, and the breath that should have gone in at the top of the reach had stayed where it was. The market had stood with every one of these in one square that morning and had read none of them, and they had come home under one roof and gone each into its own hollow. He held all nine apart by hand, because he had no other hand to hold them with. Nine likenesses standing in a row will stand up and call themselves a doctrine if nobody keeps a finger between them, and a doctrine is an account that has found a payer; no payer had been named to any of the nine. He had learned in a better-lit room what a row of likenesses will do when a man with a clean slate comes to count them --- how they stand up out of their places and arrange themselves into a rule, and how the rule acquires a foot at the bottom of it with a name under the foot, and how quickly the name becomes a person. He was not going to let nine stoppings stand up in the dark and acquire a payer because he had been too tired to keep his hand between them. The cost of the keeping was that he was the only adult under this roof and the least use in it: a man who had once reduced a shoulder in the dark could not lift the fold off his own chin, and the doctrine he would not let stand up was a doctrine nobody in this house wanted to stand up either, and still it had to be held.

A held word is a debt and a debt that can hear is a debt that can be called, and he held the nine apart because a debt called at the foot of nine likenesses is a debt with a name and a number under the name and a collector coming down the road. The loan stood uncalled. The breath from Seed 16 stood unspent, with its small interval at the top of each reach to show where it should have gone in. The seventh year stood one tend from its end and had not given. The unforged sentence about a collector lay beside the knife at the very edge of its sill where it could be checked, and he could not have reached the knife to check it himself, and had never wanted to.

Beside these lay the pieces that had no tray of their own and would not be put on one: \lore{the damper} as one iron with two bites, the bite that holds a vessel whole and the bite that can take the hand off a man, filed joined to no kiln; the two tellings of the counting room shut in the war, side by side with no third, and nobody under this roof having gone into a shut room to make one; the told bell of \lore{the Long Night} beside the heard bell out of the yard, two sounds of the same shape and not the same sound. He laid them out and looked at them and did not let the look become a joining. An inventory is not a key and a list is not a hand. He had catalogued a hundred and forty-nine days of other people's keeping and could not carry one object of it to any table.

In the other room \place{the loaf shelf} kept its two unnamed gaps and the charcoal under the board stayed on a place of its own, unread by him and unread by anybody who had gone past it that day; a hand had gone beneath the board and come back, and what the marks said had stayed under the board. One brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold two inches from his ear, with its empty place beside it still unnamed and no third coming into either room. The saying of the loaf-shelves of Low Kiln went around the houses the way a pot goes from hand to hand, and was never carried into either room and set down. The two rooms did not touch.

Everything the square had displayed that morning had come home and been put away without being spent. The figure read aloud at the reading block was not the figure that had gone into the stall-keeper's palm, and the box had gone up the clay road with the true subtraction locked inside it; the cord no Court thumb could spell was back inside a coat under the flat of a hand; the soft-cuffed clerk was still set at \place{the out-of-line landing} below \place{the north bridge} for a measure nobody had called, slate to forearm with the writing inward, hand to slate before eyes to stalls, and nobody had stepped around him and nobody had taken him off. The sixth green at a silent woman's mouth was a colour joined to no ear and kept on a place of its own; no woman in the market had ever been given that colour as a name, and he had not made it a face, and would not call it one in the dark. The foundling at the market's edge was a shape with no sale --- a half-turn of a head stopped in the middle of its arc, a green sliver going dull as the child turned back, a mouth opened and shut on the tail of a sentence --- and nobody under any roof had said whose child it was, and he had been given no word of his own to put to it. Six greens standing up in a row will call themselves a face if no hand keeps them apart, and six half-turns standing up in a row will call themselves a sale, and a sale is a thing with a payer at its foot; he kept a finger between them as he kept a finger between the nine, and no payer stood under any of them.

The word forty stayed unspoken in the district while the forty-first double turn stood legible on \person{Orra Weir}'s knot-string beside a board that did not touch another board; it was a shape and not a name, and no name was going to be made of it in the dark. The reed-cutter's cord lay back inside his coat at \place{the Weir} with its knots unread, straightened and turned where no slate lay and put away with the flat of a hand. \person{Mira Silt}'s second twist lay twice beneath an apron and had not been carried upriver to \place{Nine Pots} or lifted at a door. A child's foot below \place[the Singing Sill]{the sill} stayed the cold to the woman who had wound it in three turns of wool and weather to the yard, and the reading of it --- the extremity going before the core, the almost-smile that is not peace, the afterdrop waiting past a warming nobody would perform --- worked as perfectly as it had on the day he learned it and bought him nothing he could spend. He kept every one of these where it lay and laid none of them across another.

Three doors down, under another roof, \person{Alda Reed} had laid a date over a sign at a wrist and said \emph{warm and breathing} where the truth had gone out of a woman more mornings ago than any living person kept count of --- exact and not true, and standing over that woman still, uncollected, with no hand alive to lift it and set it right. The mercy had curdled into a fact and then into a piece of the house's furniture, something a room goes around the way it goes around Ash, and a fact of that kind cannot be corrected by anybody who is still living. Her six pages lay turned toward a floorboard and the little tin stayed shut in her bodice, and the round pewter tin above the abandoned board-bed kept its pale fold and thumb-bright lid closed; nothing in either house had been opened for a figure, and the sign, the date and the hand that made them stayed on three places, because a covering is not the thing covered and a likeness is not a cause.

\scenebreak

Through the finger's-width door came the river's note of water over water, a fifth low thing and never counted among the hearings; it was not a hearing and he did not make it one. Through the floor under blind ash the carried fire gave four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for a hundred and forty-nine days. Overhead the boards kept four nails, four, and a fifth that was a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts in three places, and none of them became one: a note that was not counted, a fire that missed a pull, a fifth that was a knot and not a nail. He had learned early that three likenesses in a row want to stand up as a rule, and that the only honest work was to keep a hand between them; and here were three counts, and nine stoppings, and a sixth colour, and a sixth shape, and a hundred and forty-nine days, and not one of them was an account anybody had asked him to enter.

Cold came in its known order: cheek, temple, wrist, the wool riding back from the small bare joint. One hundred and forty-nine days old and four months and twenty-nine days, told by nobody, and if somebody under that roof had said the number nobody would have known what it counted, and there was no word in \lore{Grathic} that would have held it long enough for a woman passing on the clay to set it down. He wanted the crust two inches from his ear, and he wanted the number spoken by somebody who would have known what a number was, and he got neither and could not ask for either. The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water and came down against the fold, exact, complete, unspent, and spoken by nobody.

The reed month went out without a boundary drawn and the count crossed into Smoke unspent, with nine held words on the tray, a sixth colour and a sixth shape on places of their own, the seventh year one tend from its end and the loan uncalled. Nothing was joined, paid, named, opened, called, spent or ended. The note went on over water through the gap in the door, the fire missed its fifth under the floor, and the charcoal waited under the shelf for a hand that was not his and a day that had not come. One hundred and forty-nine stood exact and unspent in the dark as his hand came down against the fold.
