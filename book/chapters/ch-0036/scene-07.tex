\daymark{149}{Reed 30, Y.H. 813 (Tenthday)}

The reed month went out in the square with nobody drawing a line under it. It went the way water goes over a sill: the sill did not stop it and did not keep it, and the boards went on smelling of wet reed and pig and clay after it was past. One hundred and forty-nine days old. Four months and twenty-nine days. Neither number was spoken under any roof in \place{Low Kiln} that morning, and there was no vessel in the district that would have held either one still long enough for a woman passing with a pot to set it down.

\person{Sella Vail} took \person[Corin]{the boy} down at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, woodsmoke going before her along \place{the clay road} and the river wind coming up to meet it, the two crossing between the low houses the way two people pass on a stair in the dark, each with a hand on the wall and neither speaking. Keep it off his ears, she told the wool; it had come all the way down with them and would go all the way back up before the light was gone; and she gave nothing more to the wind, having said it once, because a wind takes an instruction and carries it away and there is an end of it. The instruction went to the wool and to the river wind and never to the basket.

At \place{the Cross} she set the basket in \place{the moot-post}'s finger's width of shade, where no slate lay and where the iron at his cheek was cold in its own right, a small standing weather that had been there before the post was a post and would be there after it was gone. She set it down as one sets a pot down where it will not be slopped: base first, the weight settled, the hand kept where it is until the thing is sure of its place. Then she stood the half-pace off and gave the square no name and asked it nothing. The boy lay with his cheek to the fold and the small wet sound coming and going as his head wandered and was not set right, and his hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of running water and came down against the cloth.

The market had come up around \place{the reading block} in the dark and was already loud enough to be felt in the teeth before it was heard. Eight pigs went over the wet weighing boards in the space of a breath and went on being eight pigs without anybody finding a ninth among them. The last bundles of reed lay green at the broken ends and pale where the frost had bitten the tips, carried up out of the beds by arms that knew what they had carried and would not have known what it cost, and the men who had cut them stood around them with their hands in their armpits and said nothing about hands. A reed-cutter came up off the boards with the end of a working day on him and passed close enough for the boy to see the coat close over a short grey cord with the flat of a hand, the way a man closes a door he does not want opened, and no figure went into the air and no knot was counted where anybody could hear it.

Everything the district kept from its own slates stood together in that square that morning and nothing was read. The figure that would be read aloud and was not the figure paid; the cord no Court thumb could spell; the mark under a woman's collar that a market-day was not an opening wide enough to touch; the tallies under a shelf and in a field and in the space beneath the lowest rack; the crust in a fold two inches from an ear with its empty place beside it still unnamed; the green that was a colour and the half-turn that was a shape. They stood side by side in the noise like pots set on a board, and no hand went along them laying one against another, and the square went on being a market.

He had once laid a body out on a table and read it before anybody spoke, and he read this square the same way: not eight pigs and two hundred people and a block under a post, but one grammar performed five times over, a cost asked in the form of a direction and answered with a direction, a question put to a figure and given a date, a measure set down before anybody called it, a colour put where a name should have stood, a turn of a head stopped in the middle of its arc. The grammar was the same in each. He was the only person in the square who saw that it was the same, and he had no standing to enter any of it, and the surgeon's eye that found the pattern at once was the least use in a market full of people who could not see it at all. An inventory is not a hand. A list is not a key. He kept the five shapes on five places of their own and went on lying in the post's finger's width of shade with cold iron at his cheek.

\person{Berr Clay} came up short off the clay road a little before the top of the morning. The red wool cord of his office was folded square under his coat instead of worn across it, and the green of tasted \lore{lead glaze} stood at one corner of his mouth where it had stood on two hundred mornings before this one, hot clay going before him and the smell of nothing behind, which is the most expensive smell in \place{Grath}. His hands were large and clean. He wiped the thumb twice on his apron before touching nothing and held a narrow slate unopened against his forearm, passing a finger over the figure and letting his eyes rest on the writing a breath longer than the writing required, the way a man checks a clamp before he trusts it and then trusts it anyway.

“One in twelve, as the notice says,” he read low into the goers-by over the last bundles, and the crowd had to lean in to hear him, which is why a low voice in a moot is more frightening than a shout. He called the levy for the going-out of the reed month and denied it in the same breath with \emph{your house}, never \emph{I}, taking himself out of his own sentence as a surgeon takes a hand out of a field before the dressing goes on. A man come up from the pans with a fist still half-shut brought a number toward the block and asked what the hands would be paid now the last of the reeds were in.

“A number goes into a slate, not a bundle,” Berr said. “If your house wants it again, there'll be a slate at the watch.”

“The hands did the cutting,” the man said. “They'll want something for it before the month's out.”

“The levy is called,” Berr said. “It is not called by me. One in twelve, as the notice says.”

The fist went back into the sleeve and the number arrived nowhere, and the man went off with a direction instead of a figure, carrying it away level so nothing slopped, which is an answer in this district and has always been one. The words were \lore{Grathic} and the boy had never had them in his mouth and never would have known what a number was if he had; he heard the shape of the withholding before he heard the sentence, as he had heard it at readings all through the year, and laid the twice-wiped thumb on the tray where the other record-first hands lay, and did not lay it across any of them. The thumb had gone to the clean slate before the eyes went to the crowd, and the eyes had gone to the crowd before the mouth opened, and that order was a record in muscle that told what had been entered and not what it had cost, exact and complete and worth nothing to the person who read it best.

\scenebreak

At the eel stalls Berr took two pair and paid two marks short, and the stall-keeper put the marks into her palm without counting them where anyone could hear, because a count spoken aloud at a stall is a count the Court can enter and a count made in the palm is a count nobody has made at all.

“Two pair,” he said to the boards, and the stall-keeper shut her hand over the marks.

“They'll come up short,” she said low, and said it to the eels rather than to him, and he did not answer her, because a thing said to an eel is a thing nobody has said in a market.

Eels lay in the shade with slime dried grey on the boards and the short marks went into the arithmetic of the market alongside the eight pigs and the last bundles and came out at no figure anybody could spend.

The figure that went into the air over the reading block was not the figure that went into the stall-keeper's palm, and the figure that went into the slate did not know the hand that had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come back out of the strip. There was no name under that absence and no number over it and no body in it, only a place the yard went around with a look and a wide step, the way a woman goes around a pail of hot water set down where a child might knock it, and had gone around all through the going-out of the month without once saying why. The boy had carried that place for a hundred and forty-nine days and had never been able to set it down, and today it stood in the square with every other withholding and was no more read than they were. Berr stood a moment with his mouth open on the next part of what he had come to say and then said it low to the stalls where nobody leaned in: the levy was called and was not called by him, one in twelve as the notice says. He went off up the clay road with the cord folded square and the narrow slate under his arm and the box locked in his house behind him with the true subtraction inside, believing he had done the district a kindness, as he had believed on Stone 30 and on Ilmen 30 and on every Account-day between, and never once looking into the basket.

The bell went four, four and a gap, longer than a church bell, and nobody crossed himself at it. The boy kept the heard bell beside the told bell of \lore{the Long Night}, where it had stood since the dark, and did not let the two stand up and call themselves the same bell, because two likenesses left alone in a room will arrange themselves into a rule if there is no finger between them.

Below \place{the north bridge}, at \place{the out-of-line landing} where the step stood a hand's breadth out of line and a whole market walked over it carefully without a fall, the soft-cuffed clerk was still set. His cuff was grey from washing and grey again by midday, and his slate lay against his forearm with the writing turned inward where nobody else could read it. His hand went to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls, whether or not there was a figure to enter.

“Two pair and a half,” a buyer said from the boards above him.

The clerk's thumb moved before his face came up. The figure went into the inward writing and only then did he look for the man who had said it, and by then the man had turned to the eels and was gone into the noise. A girl with a creel took the out-of-line step sideways, caught the rail with her free hand and went on selling without breaking her sentence, and nobody made a fall of it and nobody stepped around the clerk, and nobody had taken him off since the month turned. The market sold its reeds and eels over the top of him as water goes over a stone that lies where a stone lies. He was none of the hands the boy kept on separate places and he was not the foundling and he was not a man the boy could ask anything of; a measure set down before anybody called it was a measure set down ahead of something, and the boy had kept that as a shape for a hundred and forty-nine days and had never made a rule of it.

Across the square at \place{the pot board}, where the light came sideways between the stalls and the shade went in strips across the clay, a woman stood without saying anything. There was clay under her nails in the small crescents and a pale line across the back of one hand where something had once been worn tight and long enough to leave its mark, and the sweetish green of lead glaze in the crease beside her lip. A man set a cup down near her and asked whether the glaze on it was sound, and she turned the cup a quarter with the fingers of one hand, not lifting it, and looked at the thing she had turned and not at the man who had asked, and he waited with his hand still on the board. She set it back exactly where it had stood with the handle pointing the way it had pointed before she came, and said nothing, and went off with the light among the coats, and the man took his cup and made nothing of it.

The boy filed it as the sixth green at a mouth and the seventh shape of the colour counting Berr's glaze at the reading block. It was a colour and not a name. No woman in this square had ever been given that colour as a name and no ear had been spoken with it, and no cord-mark or pot or box up the clay road had been joined to it, and the pale line across the back of the hand was a line on a hand and not a mark on a person. He put it on a place of its own and kept a finger between it and the five greens behind it. Six greens standing up in a row will call themselves a face if no hand keeps them apart, and a face is the beginning of an account with a name under it, and there was no name under any of these.

\scenebreak

Near \place{the market's edge}, where the kiln-hands came down with pots to sell and set them on a low board that had no slate on it, a child stood in cast-off clothes worn the way a uniform is worn, a man's shoulders in them and breeches cut off at the knee, river silt dried pale in the creases of his wrists and a gap where a front tooth had been. On a string at his throat hung a green sliver of lead glaze that caught the light when he turned and went dull when he turned back. It was the sixth time the boy had seen the shape and nobody under any roof had said whose child it was, and he had never been given a word of his own to put to it.

A kiln-hand set a pot down on the low board. It gave its \lore{the full note} under his knuckle, whole and clear and small, and the boy's head went toward it before either man looked at the other; and the foundling's head went toward it half a beat after the boy's, after the knuckle and after the note, and stopped in the middle of the turn. He rubbed the sliver twice with his thumb.

“\ldots and if it sings before its time it'll sing itself---” a kiln-hand beside him said, and left the rest of the sentence lying there on the boards.

The child opened his mouth on the end of it. Nothing came out that any clerk could take down. The boy knew the shape of that stopped sentence the way he knew the shape of a stopped one under his own roof, where a man had brought a word to the threshold nine times and had not let it cross, and the knowing bought him nothing and was not a joining: one man's sentence kept back and another man's mouth opened and shut were two things on two places, and no payer stood under either. No loaf passed. No slate was seen near the basket. There was no sale, and there had never been one, and the half-beat was a shape and not a betrayal.

The boy put the half-turn on a place of its own beside the green, and the green on a place of its own beside the half-turn, and neither across anything else. Beside them lay the shelf's two unnamed gaps and the crust in the fold with its empty place, and the field's empty place, and the charcoal under the board, and the cord inside the cutter's coat, and \person[Orra Weir]{Orra}'s knots and \person[Mira Silt]{Mira}'s second twist and the clerk's inward slate, and the hand that had not come back out of the strip. He went along them with the finger he had, which was the only hand in the market that would do it, and laid nothing across anything, laying each thing down the way a man lays an instrument on a towel before a case begins, each in its own hollow. He had made an inventory of a district that had never asked to be inventoried, exact, complete and worth nothing, and the keeping of the things apart was the only work in the square that no slate would enter. No fourth likeness was made into a cause. No third gap came. Six half-turns standing up in a row will call themselves a sale if no hand keeps them apart, and a sale is a thing with a payer at its foot, and no payer had been named to any of these.

The square went on selling over the clerk at the landing and over the child at the market's edge and over the basket in the moot-post's thin shade, and nobody made anything of any of it. The last bundles went up the road in carts and the pigs went back to whatever pigs go back to and the reed month went out without a boundary drawn. One hundred and forty-nine days old and four months and twenty-nine days, told by nobody, exact and unspent and carried down the road again in a fold of wool that he could not lift from his own chin.

The foundling's head stopped in the middle of its turn toward the set-down pot, the green sliver going dull as he turned back, while the market sold over the soft-cuffed clerk and nobody made anything of any of it.
