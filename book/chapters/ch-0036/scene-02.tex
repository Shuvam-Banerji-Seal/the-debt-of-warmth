Under \place{the Vail kiln-house} the midday came down through the oiled paper in a brown sheet that stopped at the floor and went no farther, so that the far corner kept its darker air to itself and the bench kept the shadow it had always kept. \person{Sella Vail} came in from the yard with the basket held level against her hip, the way a woman carries a pot filled to the brim and will not have it slopped, and \person[Corin]{the boy} lay with his cheek to the fold and the small wet sound coming and going as his head wandered and was not set right. Woodsmoke went in before her and caught in the beams; under it lay the dry warm smell of \place{the loaf shelf}, crust and ash and old baking that was almost a warmth in itself, and under that again, for one breath and then folded away, the clean bitter smell of charcoal from beneath the board. A basket set down is a pot set down and left.

She set him low near the bench leg, at the angle at which the underside of a board becomes a roof to a thing lying on the floor while a woman standing sees only the top of it. The cold iron of the bench bracket was at his ear. Keep it off his ears, she told the wool; it had come all the way down with them from the reeds and would go all the way back up before the light was gone. She told the pot not to stand near the door, and the broom to go around, and the fire it was \lore{the thaw}, which it was and would go when it would go. The instruction went to the wool and the pot and the broom and the fire and never to the basket. One hundred and forty-seven days old, four months and twenty-seven days; neither number was spoken under the roof, and there was no vessel in the house that would have held either one still long enough for a woman to set it down.

Above him the shelf kept its two unnamed gaps, in the room the fold did not touch. There was the old space at the row's end, which had stood through every morning of his life and which nobody had decided what to call; it was said of the loaf-shelves of \place{Low Kiln} that two gaps stay two gaps until a finger lays them together and an account then wants a name, and no finger in the district had laid these together. And there was the palm's breadth that had come in on the night of Seed 6 with no scrape, no crumb, no print and no word --- a loaf's worth gone off a board between dusk and light and no woman saying by whom. Two gaps. A third would have been somebody's doing, and nobody in the house had done it, and the saying went around the houses the way a pot goes from hand to hand and was never carried into this room and set down.

In the other room, on \place{the bed} he was not lying on, one stiff brown-edged crust lay two inches from the place his ear would have been, with its empty place beside it still unnamed. He had wanted it since morning. He could not reach the fold. The wanting and the not reaching sat together in him without either cancelling the other, as the adult's knowing sat beside the infant's appetite without either being asked to give way; the knowing was older and the appetite was louder, and they lay in the same small body like two instruments in one case, one of which he could not lift. The two rooms of the house did not touch. The shelf's two absences and the fold's one were kept apart by the whole width of a wall and by a woman who had never once carried a saying from one to the other.

A neighbour came up from the strips with a pot in her hand that she set down wrong, near the door where Sella had told the pot not to stand. It was the same woman who came up most days with the wet sleeves and the question ready; her wrists were grey to the creases with the slip water of the near rows, and she had the look of a woman who had already decided what answer she would carry away and had merely come to collect it.

“What'll the row want before the last market?” she said to the shelf, or to the board, or to Sella's back --- it was hard from the floor to tell which she had addressed, and the answer came from the same place whichever it was.

Sella's eyes went once to the clay jar standing cold under its glaze at the shelf's far end. A jar cold under its glaze is a jar cold under its glaze, and an answer to nobody. “Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there,” she said. “The house has said it.”

The woman stood a moment with her mouth still arranged around the next part, then took her pot up level, the way a woman carries a thing filled to the brim, and went out with the question unanswered and nothing slopped. The cost had been asked in the form of a direction and answered with a direction, and the answer left no entry for any thumb to read. The boy kept the exchange on a place of its own beside the yard's weather and river and did not make a row of them and call it a rule. A question carried away level is still a question carried away, and a house that has said it has said nothing that a slate could take up.

Sella's lips went on moving along the row. She stood with her back to the basket and counted the loaves the way another woman might count beads, without touching any of them and without arriving anywhere; the row kept its shape and gave back no figure. Then, still with her back to him and still moving her lips, she reached a finger beneath the front edge of the board where the charcoal tally lay. The little-door burn on the back of her hand closed on the lip of the wood and opened again as the finger went under. She held it a breath out and a breath back --- the way a woman feels along the underside of a shelf for a thing she has put there and knows is there --- and brought it away. She did not look at what the finger had touched. She did not wipe the finger. She did not go back to the shelf.

From the floor the boy saw the hand go under and come back and saw nothing of what the marks said. He knew the tally was there: short grouped strokes of different darkness pressed into the underside of the board, some hard enough to have left a smooth track where the stick dug, some the colour of old smoke and gone nearly into the grain at the row's end. It was a keeping put where nobody thought to look for one, and a keeping hidden that carefully tells more by the care of the hiding than the writing would, which was a thing he had learned and could not spend. He did not count the strokes. He did not press a thumb where her thumb had been. Such a hand, going under a board without looking, either knows the board by heart or has been told not to look, and the reason stood between the two and would not come down on either side; from the basket the knuckle going under and the hand coming away would have looked the same for a woman who knew and a woman who had been warned. He had once been able to tell the difference in a room where a hand went into a field before anybody said what the field was, and the telling had bought him nothing then either. A hand's knowing is not a thing a slate will take, and a warning is not a cause, and the space between the two was a place he could hold open and nothing more.

He did not lay the charcoal under the board across the two gaps above it. He did not lay it across the one stiff crust two inches from his ear in the other room. He did not lay it across the place in the returning line the yard had gone around that morning with a look and a wide step, or across the cord in the cutter's coat, or across any of the other keepings carried about the district in sleeves and under aprons and on boards turned inward. The two rooms of the house did not touch, and no third gap came. \lore{the widow's mark} hung where it hung at her throat, a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and she did not touch it; she touched it before she opened the kiln and at no other hour, and an afternoon under a shelf is not an opening and neither is a basket or a pot told the weather.

The order of it was the thing he could read without being able to use. Her hand had gone to the record before her eyes went to the row and before anybody's eyes went to the basket --- the same order he had watched in a yard shoulder turning half a beat ahead of a yard gaze, in a cutter feeling the weight settle before he looked at the girl who had asked, in a man shutting his mouth on a figure before he looked at the face that had asked for one. He had learned it in a room where a hand went to the chart before anybody looked at the face on the table, and here it was again under a shelf, performed by a woman who would have said the house had done it. He had no standing in the order and could not enter any part of it. An inventory is not a key, and a man who cannot lift a board cannot use what he has catalogued.

At \place{the hearth} she told the fire it was the thaw again, which it was, and turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and fed them. “The house will keep it,” she said where another woman would have said \emph{I}. The fire answered with its four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, and she did not count the pulls aloud and did not ask anybody to hear the silence, and its payer had gone unnamed for a hundred and forty-seven days. The warmth came through the floor under blind ash and went into the boards and into the boy's wrist by degrees; he knew the order in which a body takes heat back --- the small joint first, the sting and the itch of it, the core last --- and knew too that a warmth arriving without its being named is a warmth nobody can be thanked for, and that the almost-smile of a warming extremity is not peace but the afterdrop waiting past it. He kept the payer off the slate because there was no slate in the district that would have taken the entry and no voice under the roof that would have spoken it.

She stood a moment with the back of the broken pot in her hand, counting something under her breath toward no opening. Then she pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn, the grey streak at her temple showing pale off the coals, and set the pot where it could be found in the dark --- rim out, the handle standing two inches beyond the reaching hand that would have closed on it and did not. She did not look toward \place{the bed}. She did not say whose fire it was, or whose child lay in the basket, or whose count went on under the shelf. The household straightened around her silences the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted, and nobody under the roof asked what her finger had found, and nobody under the roof had given a name to the room off the shed or said it was cold. Two silences stood under one roof without a word passing between them, as they had stood for a hundred and forty-seven days.

The afternoon went off the boards without anybody marking it, as the morning had gone into midday without anybody marking it. The brown sheet drew in toward the hearth and left the far corner to its own darker air; the oiled paper held its last and let go; somewhere along the yard a pan was set down and a voice went out with the reeds and did not come back. Under the roof the woman's shadow went up the wall and stood there while her hands went on with the small work of the house, and the boy watched the shadow and not the face, which was the whole of the world at that height. Nothing within two paces could be reached: not the pot with its handle turned out, not the broom, not the shelf above him, not the clay jar cold under its glaze, not the marks on the underside of the board where her finger had been. The small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water that came through the finger's-width door and came down against the fold. He did not count the strokes under the shelf. He did not lay the two gaps together. He did not make a cause of a woman's habit, or a doctrine of a keeping, or a payer of a silence.

The pot stood rim out with its handle two inches beyond the small hand that would have closed on it and did not. The charcoal waited under the shelf, unread, for a hand that was not his and a day that had not come. One hundred and forty-seven lay where the day had left it, exact and complete and entered nowhere, and the last market stood two days off across a district that would read a figure into the air over the basket and go home believing it had paid something.
