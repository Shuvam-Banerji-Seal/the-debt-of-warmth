The middle of the day found them three doors down, in the room where the boy had been born and where nobody now slept. \person{Sella Vail} carried him at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, going around the space beneath \place[the drying racks]{the lowest rack} as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and set the basket just inside the finger's-width-open door of \place{House Silt}, at the edge of the light and away from every place a slate might lie. She set it down the way a woman sets a pot where it will not be slopped, and took her hands off it, and gave the instruction to the wool. “Keep it off his ears. It's the river wind.” The instruction went into the wool and not into the basket and not into \person[Corin]{the boy}. She opened no slate, and the errand that had carried her three doors down the \place{Clay Yard} stayed an errand to a room; she did not turn it into a walk to \place{Slate Row}, though most of what this room held would have read better there. She stood a half-pace off with her little-door burn shut on nothing.

The room kept what it had kept for three years. A bench stood under the door, a chipped cup on the shelf by the wall, and a grey ridge of \lore{Ash} ran along the skirting and under the door into the next room, left where it lay for the Last Day. Above the abandoned board-bed a round pewter tin held its place, with a pale fold showing at the seam of the lid and a thumb's bright rub worn into the pewter. Below it the fold of grey wool lay as it had been laid and held only the hollow where the small pale river stone had once lain. Nobody in the room said that either thing was gone. In the back room a neighbour scrubbed at a board with lye water and did not look up at the writing, or the tin, or the fold pressed too long against a shoulder; a low song came up through the floor with its words flattened out of sense by work. From the height of a basket the shelf is the highest thing in a house, and the boy had entered the tin nowhere in all his days.

He was one hundred and twelve days old, three months and twenty-two days, and no living person under this roof knew there was a number to be known. He had stopped expecting to be told, which was not the same as not wanting it. The want went on under the not-expecting the way a coal goes on under blind ash, and his head went where it wanted under the wool and made the small wet sound it made when it went too far, and nobody put it right, because the instruction had gone to the wool and not to the wandering.

Then \person{Alda Reed} came.

She came short, the way a woman comes who has been called out of a room where somebody else has just been fed: soap-and-milk gone cold under river silt, lye sour under the soap, her nails scrubbed to the quick with clay still under them. Alda had her birth-slates under her arm and a narrow slate of her own in the fold of her shawl. She crossed to the bench and laid the writing-down flat before she turned toward the fold. It was the order of a hand that reaches for the record before the person, and it did not vary; he had watched it from a basket at a market's edge and from a bed and had never once seen her reverse it. He had watched a senior man lay his hand on a chart before he looked at the patient under it, and learned there what the order meant: the record was the thing that would still be there when the person was carried out. There was no word in \lore{Grathic} for the order, and he had no voice to spend on it, and he saw it performed again now in a room where the person was not going to be carried out at all.

She put her hand to the fold at his shoulder and pressed it flat a breath longer than a fold requires. Not tucking it, not smoothing it: pressing it, the way a hand holds pressure on a graft until the vessel takes. The small weight of her palm came through the wool and stayed and went away. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound and she did not put it right, because the slate was on the bench and the fold was pressed and there was no instruction left over for a head.

Her hand went to the red edge of the birth-flower at his wrist. Two fingers crossed there, at the corner where the branching mark nearly reached the joint, before she knew she had done it. The sign was not in her face; it was in the hand, and the hand went on. Then, as a shutter comes down over a window, she covered the sign with a date.

“Stone seven,” she said, to the bench, or to the fold, or to the room. “The account went up on the eighth, before the paper went out.”

The date laid itself over the sign the way a slate laid over a crack lays itself flat. It was the date she had given the woman who bore him, and it now stood over a death more mornings gone than any living person kept count of, and a woman uncollected that nobody alive could take it off. Where a name should have stood there stood a date. She had put it there for mercy three years ago and had gone on putting it there ever since, and it had curdled through to a fact.

“He lies quiet,” Sella said from her half-pace.

“Warm and breathing,” Alda said. “That's all I've set down.” It was exact. It was not true. He had no way to tell her so and no standing if he had, and she did not wait for an answer; she went on with dates after the question had stopped, because dates are a handle on a thing too large to carry: a cooper's seventh in \place{Mud End}, a potter's third child in \place{Stonemouth}, the last high water, the exact day of a birth in a house nobody in this room would ever enter. The dates came faster than dates had any business coming, and the room arranged itself around them the way a market arranges itself around a reading it has not understood.

Her hand went once inside her bodice to the little tin on its cord and came away without opening it. She took up the narrow slate from her shawl and wrote on it with a charcoal stub what she did not leave lying about, in the second hand no Court physician had ever seen, and turned it writing-down into the shawl toward a floorboard in a house the boy could not name. Six pages, all unread, written one at a time over years and never carried to a door; no seventh had been added and nothing was paid again today. Above the board-bed the pewter tin kept its pale fold and thumb-bright lid shut, and below it the hollow in the wool kept the shape of a stone nobody mentioned. Two private records in two materials stood in one room on two separate places, and a third thing lay at his wrist under a date, and the boy set all three apart and wrote no cause across the space between them. A likeness laid on a likeness will grow a cause whether or not anybody put one there; he had learned that in a better-lit room and had spent his whole life in this one keeping his finger off the places where it would grow.

She went out as she had come in, short, with the birth-slates under her arm, and Sella did not ask what had been written. A half-pace and a turned slate stood between the two women for the whole of the visit, which was the arrangement, and neither had put a word into it in three years. Sella lifted the basket level and carried him back out at the half-pace from every open slate, and did not look into it, and did not turn the errand into a walk to Slate Row on the way back either.

The going of the light under \place{the Vail kiln-house} roof found him where it had found him a hundred and eleven times before, on his bed and not in \place{the Debt Loft}, with the fold put right at his ear and under his chin without a look at his face and the thread of drool at his mouth frozen and unwiped. Nobody had been given an instruction about the thread, and a basket set down is a pot set down and left. He turned out the private file the way a man lays instruments on a towel before he begins, and found every object where he had left it.

Six hearings of the full note stood beside and never among, with no seventh. Three near-full answers waited on their own tray, advanced and not paid. Three intervals lay on another. The word forty stayed unspoken, with the readable double turn beside it and the weight of a thumb set apart from both. Four low things kept their four places and the river's note of water over water was a fifth on a place of its own and not a hearing. The two tellings of the shut counting room stood joined to no kiln. The damper's double bite was filed as an object, exact and worth nothing, joined to no firing. The loan that could hear was still uncalled.

He added what the days had given and entered none of it. \person[Mira Silt]{Mira}'s hand going to the grey cord and staying longer than the figure required; the soft-cuffed clerk still set at the out-of-line landing with his slate to his forearm for a measure nobody had called; a neighbour reaching past the bare board in one breath and naming two absences as one; \person[Berr Clay]{Berr} gone up the road with the box locked in his house and the true subtraction inside; \person[Jorra Vail]{Jorra}'s bent finger standing in the cold iron and \person{Tarn}'s broom checking half a stroke behind it, two silences in one room with no word between them. He set them on their places and laid no finger across any of them. A fourth likeness would have made a cause with a clean hand at the foot of it, and the clean hand was the one object in this world he had spent two lives declining to become.

The crust in the fold was one brown-edged thing gone stiff and past smelling of anything, with the empty place beside it still unnamed and no third. The shelf's two gaps were in the other room, in a room that did not touch this one: the old space at the row's end that came in with the planting, and the new palm's breadth that had come in the night of Seed 6 with no scrape, crumb, print or word. Below the sill the dark foot stayed dark inside three turns of wool, cold to one woman and weather to the yard, uncured, uncaused and unnamed. He knew the order of it as he knew the back of his own hand --- the extremity going before the core, the face putting on an almost-smile that was not peace, the afterdrop waiting on the far side of a warming nobody would perform --- and the reading was perfect and bought nothing. There was no warmth in the house that would come and sit with that foot through the afterdrop, and if there had been, he had no hand to carry it there.

The clean dark line where the Ash ridge had turned its corner lay unswept, and the new finer grey beside it lay unswept too, and the house called both of them \lore{the thaw}. The space under the lowest rack was gone around as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and entered by nobody. The carried fire lay under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees and giving back four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for a hundred and twelve days. Every warmth in the house had a payer and none of them stood in the room to be thanked. No opening had come, so \lore{the widow's mark} hung untouched where it hung, and Sella's private roll stayed unshown.

Sella told the fire it was the thaw and turned the coals with the back of the broken pot and fed them. “The house will keep it,” she said where another woman would have said \emph{I}, and did not look toward the bed. She stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand counting something under her breath that no man could have taken up as a figure, and the grey streak at her temple showed pale in the dying light off the coals, and she pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn, and set the pot where it could be found in the dark, and went on.

The door stood a finger's width open. The river's note came through the gap from \place{the Ilm}, water going over water under the bridge, and through the floor came \lore{the kiln's breath}, four, four and a gap under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, two sounds in two places that had not met in a hundred and twelve days and would not meet tonight. The boards overhead kept four nails, four, and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts lay side by side and did not become one.

Cold arrived in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, and the wool did less against it than the cold wanted, riding back from the bare joint so the cold found the place it had come for. He wanted the crust two inches from his ear, and the breast, and the bucket's lip two paces off, and the number of his days said aloud by somebody who would know what a life counted, and he could reach or ask for none of them. He was a man who had reduced a thousand shoulders in a room that was not this one and he could not close two inches on the fold. The keeping bought him nothing and kept the places clean, and that was the whole of its worth: the crust in the fold, the two gaps on the shelf in the room that did not touch this one, the hollow in the wool three doors down, the pewter tin above it, the narrow slate under a floorboard he could not name, the date standing where a name should have stood, a cord and a second twist no Court thumb would count, a clerk's uncalled measure, a sixth hearing that did not come, a word unspoken and a loan uncalled. All of it held where it held, advanced and unpaid, one day further on.

His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down. One hundred and twelve stood where one hundred and eleven had stood, exact, unspent, spoken by nobody, while the river went over water beyond the finger's-width gap and the fire missed its fifth breath under the floor and the two counts did not meet. On the morrow one hundred and thirteen would stand in the same place and nobody under the roof would know a number had moved.
