\daymark{110}{Seed 21, Y.H. 813}

\dropcap{T}{he last tend} of the planting came up under a sky the colour of wet ash, and the seed stood in the worked strips east of \place{Low Kiln} with nobody giving a figure for the hands that had set it there. A tend is ten days and the month stood near its end, yet the yard kept its account the way it kept everything else: in a glance thrown at a strip, a fist opened and shut, a handful let fall and covered over with brown earth. On Seed 15 the hands had gone into the ground; on Seed 21 they were still going in, stooped along the turned rows, and \person[Corin]{the boy} carried at the edge of the work was one hundred and ten days old, three months and twenty days, and nobody in the strips or under the roof had a word in which to say what that counted. He lay in the basket with the fold at his ear and the wool ridden back from one bare wrist, and his small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the direction of the seed and came down. He could no more reach the strips than he could reach the number of hands in them, and the two things stood on the same place in his mind: a direction with no figure at the foot of it.

The last tend had a shape the yard would not put into words. The seed was barley, the last of the store, and it went in with the dry, faintly sweet smell it had had on the threshing floor, under the mineral tang that came up off the brook. A woman went along the second strip with seed in her fist and let it out in a handful, and when a girl beside her asked how many hands it would take to finish before the month turned, the woman shut the fist and said the ground would tell her when it had enough. A man with a barrow counted the backs along the row with his mouth, shaped a number, and thought better of giving it, so the figure went from the strip to his teeth and back and arrived nowhere. The boy watched the two of them from the edge and set the direction he had carried for a month beside the number the yard would not give, and kept them on two places. A direction become worked ground is still a direction, and a number withheld in the very act of planting is withheld like any other number.

\person{Sella Vail} carried him at the half-pace from every open slate. She had brought him out through \place{the Clay Yard} with the basket held level and the little-door burn shut on nothing where her hand had nothing to hold, and she set him where he could see the work and not be part of it, as a woman sets a pot down where it may be seen and not slopped. The yard was drying without care after the flood. Wet wool hung on a line and took the wind as it came; woodsmoke lay flat under the roofs with no lift in it; and in the unswept corner by \place[the drying racks]{the lowest rack} the sour mineral tang came up out of \lore{Ash} as it came up every morning, whether the day was called \lore{the thaw} or weather. Before she spoke to any person she gave the instruction to the bucket, and then to the broom, and then to the pot and the fire, in the order a woman gives a household its breath. “Over the road first,” she told the broom, “then the Clay Yard, and let the line lie where it lies.” To the basket she gave no instruction at all. The basket lay where it was set and took what came.

“The house wants it dry,” she said, where another woman would have said \emph{I}, and went around the space beneath the lowest rack as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. The broken birth-slates lay under there and were entered by nobody. A slate somebody had set out to dry stood propped at the rack's end, and she went around it with the same half-pace she kept from every open slate, giving it the width of a thing that might be knocked and could not be put back. The boy watched the width of the going-around and filed it as the care the house gave every open record, exact and worth nothing. Her lips moved along the row of loaves when she passed through the kiln-house, but she gave nobody a figure, and she did not look into the basket to see whether he had understood any of it.

By midmorning she had carried him back under \place{the Vail kiln-house} roof and set the basket at the edge of the light with his face toward the room and not toward the door. Across from him stood \place{the loaf shelf}, waist-high and worn smooth where a generation of hands had pushed loaves along it and taken them away. There were two gaps in the row. One stood at the end where the row had once reached farther and had come in, so the yard said, with the planting; the other was a palm's breadth nearer the middle that had come in the night of Seed 6 with no scrape, no crumb, no print and no word. Both were bare board. The board had been worn to the colour of old rope, and the light that fell on it showed no more than it had ever shown.

A neighbour came in with a pot in her hand. She did not look at the child. Her hand went first to the shelf, the way a hand goes to a familiar place in the dark, and passed over the palm's breadth of bare board without stopping; it opened for the half-beat it takes to find what is not there, shut on the hard heel of a loaf beyond the gap, and came away with the loaf and the pot together. She had reached past the empty place before her eyes had found the boy at all. It was the order he had watched all his days in this district: the hand to the record, the hand to the loaf, the eyes to the person last of all. Here the person could not have taken the loaf from her if he had wanted to, and she went on holding it. Her thumb had chaff in the creases and a sliver of husk under the nail, and the heel of the loaf was hard enough that her hand left no impression on it. She held the loaf against the pot with her forearm, already turning away.

“The old place at the row's end came in with the planting,” the neighbour said, speaking to the board as a woman speaks to a pot she is scolding, “and this one's the price of a wet thing set down wrong.”

She said it in one breath. The two sayings lay together in the air and she took them away with her pot, going out with her back to the boy and her mind already on the next thing. She had not seen that the old space and the new one were the same absence wearing two coats. She had not seen the empty place at his ear, where one brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold with nothing beside it. She had made a cause out of two bare boards and gone home satisfied with the thing she had made, and the boy, who could have told her exactly what her hand had done before her eyes did, could not lift his own hand to his own chin. The small hand outside the wool went up an inch and came down, and the fold at his ear kept its one stiff crust without a second beside it. A stranger passing the door would have seen a woman with a pot and a bare board and a basket by the wall, and would have gone on about her errand with nothing to tell.

Sella had come up beside the shelf with her burn shut on nothing. She moved her lips along the row, counting what the house was owed in loaves and in the spaces where loaves had stood, and gave nobody a figure. Her eye went once to the clay jar on the wall shelf, cold under its glaze, the way a woman checks a pot set down where it may be seen; she took nothing from it and said nothing about it. Her hand went over the bare board and came away with no loaf and no number. She did not touch \lore{the widow's mark} at her throat. A shelf is not an opening, and a strip, a rack, a yard and a basket are not openings either; the rule held on the last tend of Seed as it had held on the first day of Stone. Around the clean dark line in the corner and the new finer grey that had gathered beside it she went with a care that looked like not seeing them at all, and told nobody they were there.

She had left the widow's mark where it hung. It was not for a shelf or a gap or a neighbour's one-breath grammar; it was for the opening of a kiln and for nothing else under this roof, and there had been no opening since the coals were banked on the Last Day. Whatever she put away she put in the private roll nobody was shown, and the roll went on not being shown. The boy watched the cord at her throat and the hand that did not go to it, and set the not-going on the same tray as her not-figuring: two keepings that did not join.

The boy kept three places and did not let them touch. On the shelf in this room stood two gaps; in the fold at his ear, in a room the shelf did not touch, lay one stiff crust with its empty place unnamed; and below the sill his foot stayed dark inside three turns of wool, cold to one woman and weather to the yard, the two words never let into the same sentence. The dark foot was the extremity going before the core, and the almost-smile that is not peace would come onto a face before anybody thought to warm it, the afterdrop waiting on the far side of a warming nobody would perform. He read it as cleanly as he had ever read anything across a table and could not carry the reading two paces. Three absences, three places, no line drawn through them. He had learned what a line drawn through three absences will do: it gives a man who wants an answer a cause with a clean hand at the foot of it, and the clean hand was the thing he had spent two lives declining to become. He kept his finger off all three without thinking about it now, the way a man keeps his finger off a wound he has decided not to open. The neighbour's joining was not his joining. He set her one-breath saying on a place of its own beside the other tellings of the shelf and the watcher's late turn at the market, and laid no finger across any of them. It was a third shaping of the same held thing, advanced again and paid nothing, and it would wait for a far-off hand that was not his.

He read the reaching hand as he had once read a chart laid on a table: a clean line drawn neatly across a note nobody had opened, exact, complete, and worth nothing. The neighbour's hand had closed on the loaf beyond the bare place and gone off with her pot, and nothing in the room had made an event of it. His own hand went up an inch toward the note of water over water that came from \place{the Ilm} beyond the yard and came down. He wanted the crust two inches from his ear, the breast, the bucket's lip two paces off, and the number of his days said aloud by somebody who would know what it counted. He could reach none of them and ask for none of them. He was the only adult in this room and the least use in it; a man lays his instruments out in order because he means to put his hands into a body, and there was no field here to open and no person who would let him carry any of it to a door.

The ordinary work went on around the absence. A pot was set down and left. Someone at \place{the yard well} lifted a bucket and set it down again, and the water in it took the light and gave nothing back. Wet wool went on drying without care. The low mutter of voices came up from the strips about an early thaw and a late, and neither side won, because the house had said the seed would go in when it went in, and no figure had been spoken anywhere. Sella gave the instruction to the wool and the pot and the fire and never to the basket, and went about the keeping with her hands that never stilled, counting spoons, nails, the breaths of a sleeping child. Nobody said how many mornings of the month were left; the house kept its days in loaves pushed along a board, and the board kept two gaps nobody called a figure. The carried fire answered four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for one hundred and ten days. The boy lay at the edge of the light with one crust stiff in the fold and the shelf's two gaps in the other room, and the two rooms did not touch. His small hand went up an inch toward the water and came down. One hundred and ten stood where one hundred and nine had stood, exact and unspent, and spoken by nobody.
