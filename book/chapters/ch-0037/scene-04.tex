\daymark{151}{Tallow 2, Y.H. 813 (Secondday)}

The last of the reed work went downriver in arms that had stopped calling it reed. By the second morning of the firing month it had become a direction and not a bundle: a going-down and a coming-back measured in the shoulder that turned at the bend of the road and in the hand that shifted the weight from one hip to the other, and not in anything a woman could set on a board and count. Downriver at \place{the Weir} the boards stood on posts over the \place{Ilm}, and the water went through them with the small steady voice it keeps when it has no news. \person[Corin]{the boy} was carried at the edge of the work in a basket under a fold of wool and read the carrying arms the way he had once read a row of pulses under a chart: an arm is a record in muscle, and it knows what it has carried and not what it cost, and the two are not the same thing. Two men went down with empty creels and came back with bundles; a third went down with a bundle and came back with nothing but the feel of the ground in his legs. The work had turned around and was going the other way. Nobody under the grey sky called the turning an ending, because an ending wants a figure under it and the district had learned to hand out directions instead.

At the head of \place{the plank walk} \person{Sella Vail} set the basket down at the edge of the work where no slate lay and gave the instruction to the wool and the running water and never to the basket. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. “It's the river wind.” She did not look into the basket. A basket set down is a pot set down and left, and a pot that is left is not spoken to; the instruction went past him into the wool and the water and came back with neither carrying an answer.

A woman come up from the last of the bundles stood a moment with her creel on her hip. “What'll the hands have,” she said, “for the going down and the coming back?”

“The going down and the coming back,” Sella said.

“That's no figure.”

“Ask the river. It came up and it went down and it'll do it again. That's the whole of it.”

The woman went off with the question still in her mouth and set it down somewhere out of earshot, and nothing of it came to rest on any slate. A cost asked in the shape of a direction is answered with a direction, and the two lie down together and leave no entry. He had learned how fast a question with a number in it can be emptied of the number and handed back as weather; the answer stood on a place of its own beside the carrying arms, exact, complete and worth nothing. He was one hundred and fifty-one days old and five months and a day, and nobody within a dozen paces of the basket carried the number or would have known what it counted if it had been spoken.

The reed-cutter came along the boards with the last bundle on his shoulder. He set it down on \place[the blessing stone]{the flat blessing stone} as carefully as a man sets a pot where it will not slop, and drew the short length of grey cord out of his coat. It had gone the colour of old rope with the oil of a working thumb. He straightened it along his knee and turned it once so the knots stood in the thin light, then tied one low with his teeth and a pull of the thumb, bit the end off and put it back inside his coat with the flat of his hand, drawing the cloth closed over it.

A woman at the rails who had watched the whole small ceremony leaned in. “How many went up yesterday? I can't read a cord.”

“You can ask the cord,” the reed-cutter said.

“And who'll answer me?”

“No more can the Court,” he said, without anger and without a smile, and went on up the boards to lift a trap with the question going down into the water after him. No figure came out of it, no knot was counted, and the tally went along the planks unread, a record in wool that no Court thumb would spell and that he carried past the basket without once letting it touch him.

The boy had watched the order of it without deciding to: the hand went to the cord before it went to the trap, to the record before the work, and only then to the woman who had asked. He had seen that order in a midwife laying slates before she turned to the fold, in a thane passing a finger over a figure before he looked at the crowd, in a clerk putting hand to slate before eye to stall, in a woman putting a finger beneath a shelf before her eyes went to the row, and now in a reed-cutter straightening a length of grey wool on his knee while a question waited in the air. Five hands performing one grammar in one morning, and not one of them explained it. He set the order on a place of its own, the way he set all record-first hands, and did not make the five into one cause; a man who finds a cause in five hands will soon want a payer at the foot of it.

Below the stone, at \place{the lower boards}, \person{Orra Weir} stood with her board on its side and turned it with the wrist that had turned it ten thousand times. Her hands were like old rope and the pale eel scars lay uncovered across both forearms; salt stood white at her wrists, and the grey streak showed first through the black hair when she moved her head. Where she was tired the nasal half-song of \place[the Fenmarch]{the Fen} came into her voice, flattened at the edges the way a song is flattened by work. She pulled one knot down with her thumb and set it among the others with a small flat knock, and did not look into the basket.

A weir woman with the river in her hem came up and asked what the levy would take now the hands were out of the reeds.

“The ninth of Ilmen,” Orra said. “The collector's boat came round the bend on the flood and had six oars. One man stood at the stern and never touched them.”

“That isn't the levy.”

“It's a date. The river takes what the Court doesn't. You can shout at either one. I've tried.”

Her thumb went to the cord again as the sentence ended and set the last knot among the others. Whether that was a lie or only the habit of counting the boy could not tell, and he made no cause of it. A date laid where a figure had been asked for is an answer with the question taken out of it, a chart closed over a note with the neat margin left showing; he set it on a place of its own and wrote nothing across it. The last knot on the cord kept its readable shape, double turn and tail tucked, tied left over right with the small extra weight of a thumb pressed in, and the word that would have named it stayed where it had stayed since the year of the flower. He could read the turn and could not spend it. The knot-string would go back upriver to \place{the Eel House} where no clerk could spell it, and his hand could not have reached the first board, let alone the cord at her waist.

She watched while she worked, not into the basket and yet not away from it, the way a woman watches a pot she has set where it might boil over. She had set a thing down in front of him years ago, a word into a basket that could not answer, and had gone on setting it down every time the road brought him past, waiting for a hand to pick it up. A league of river lay between the cord at her waist and the small hand outside the wool; that hand could not have reached the first post of the plank walk, let alone the knot with the thumb-weight pressed into it. She got no answer and went on with the boards, and the watching was a keeping and not a question, because a question wants an answer and a keeping can go on for years.

Below \place[the Singing Sill]{the sill}, in \place{the fish-trade shade}, \person{Mira Silt} worked the eels. Pale slime had dried in long grey strokes on her sleeves and silt stood pale in the hollow of her neck; she held her head a little to the right where the top of her left ear had been, and kept the small smile on her face the way another woman keeps a door on the latch, which can be shut without anybody seeing the movement.

“Is the run going to be thin?” a buyer asked.

“Thin?” Mira said. “Who's to say thin? It comes when it comes. Ask the river.” She flicked an eel-bone against her thumb while she decided whether to lie, moved two from hand to hand and put one away.

“Two pair,” the buyer said, and named a figure for them.

“Two marks the pair and two short,” Mira said. “You've said it twice and I've counted it twice. There's nothing in the sentence that wants an answer.” Her thumb went under her apron to the grey cord and stayed there longer than the figure required, and shifting a creel showed the second cord twisted twice beneath it, never carried upriver to \place{Nine Pots} and never lifted at a door, neither knot counted by any thumb but her own and the knot for the thin child upriver untouched. Her board lay beside Orra's and did not touch it.

Below the shade a child's foot stayed dark inside three turns of wool. The woman who had wound it told the wool it was cold, and a neighbour come down with a pail told her it was weather and set the pail down without going near. The boy read the extremity going before the core, the almost-smile that is not peace but the shape a face makes when it is cold enough, and the afterdrop waiting past a warming nobody in the shade meant to perform. The reading worked as perfectly as it had the day he learned it and bought him nothing. He had known an afterdrop in a room with warm lights and a table and had known what it would do before it did it, and the knowing had not warmed anybody in that room either; here the knowing was the whole of what he had, and there was no table, no light, no hand but a child's hand outside the wool that went up an inch and came down. No silt-leach, no cause, no cure and no recognition was written across the foot; it stayed the cold to one woman and weather to the yard, and he could no more have put a hand to it than he could have lifted the board under a loaf shelf two streets away.

At \place{the out-of-line landing} below \place{the north bridge} the soft-cuffed clerk was still set for a measure nobody had called. His cuff was grey from washing and grey again by midday; he held his slate to his forearm with the writing turned inward, and his hand went to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls. Nobody stepped around him and nobody had taken him off. He was none of the three the boy had already set on places of their own; the boy kept him a fourth and did not make a row of them, because four likenesses standing in a row will call themselves a rule if nobody puts a hand between. The market sold its reeds and its eels over the top of him as water goes over a stone that lies where a stone lies, and a girl with a creel took the out-of-line step sideways with one hand on the rail and made no fall of it.

A pot come up from \place{High Kiln} went past on a woman's hip with the sweetish green of \lore{lead glaze} at its lip, and the smell of it stood a moment in the shade and went on up the boards. He did not turn his head toward it. A smell was a smell and not a hearing, and a pot carried past at a distance was not a pot set down near enough to answer.

Five private records stood within a dozen paces of the basket and none could be entered: the reed-cutter's cord, gone back inside the coat; Orra's knot-string with its last turn legible and its name withheld; the second twist twice beneath Mira's grey; the clerk's slate with the writing inward; and the child's foot dark in its three turns of wool. They stood side by side and not across. He kept a finger between them without moving anything, because five likenesses standing in a row will stand up and call themselves an account if nobody holds them apart, and an account is a thing that wants a foot at the bottom and a name under the foot. No finger of his drew the line. No foundling came into the shade, no loaf passed, no slate was seen near the basket and no sale was made; the shape that had turned half a beat late at the market two days gone stayed where it had been left, gathering interest offstage and spending none.

The market went on over the soft-cuffed clerk as water goes over a stone that lies where a stone lies, his hand to the slate before his eyes found the stalls, and the boy's hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water under the boards and came down against the fold. One hundred and fifty-one stood exact and unspent in the morning.
