By midday the firing had gone into the yard's hands and the opening was a thing that had happened and been gone past, the way a door is gone past once a man is through it and has put his shoulder to the next. \person{Sella Vail} carried \person[Corin]{the boy} at the half-pace from the kiln mouth to the racks and back again, and the instruction went to the wool and the fire and the pot and the broom and never to the basket; the basket was set where a pot is set, out of the draught and where the heat came to him sideways and not full, and nobody lifted the fold to look at the face under it. The thread at his mouth had dried in the dry heat and nobody wiped it, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left, and no instruction had ever been given about a thread. His head went where it wanted and made its small wet sound when it went too far, and no instruction had been given about that either. He was one hundred and fifty days old and five months exactly and the heat had not made a degree of difference to anybody's knowing what a number of days counted.

The kilns had been brought up for the year's last working season and the yard had the hot dry smell of clay giving up its water, a smell that got under the nails and stayed there, and under it the fainter warmth of tallow from the wheel-shed, and under that again, on a wind that came the wrong way off \place{High Kiln}, the sweetish edge of \lore{lead glaze} that put a false sweetness in the mouth and made a man think of fruit where there was no fruit. Clay dust lay ground small as meal on the sill and on the backs of hands and in the white of a knuckle when a fist opened; it was not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel but a finer thing, a ground thing, the dust of a slate rather than the paste of a pot, and the boy set the two on two places and did not let one stand for the other. A thing ground is a thing that got there by being broken; a thing mixed is a thing somebody put together on purpose. He had learned to tell the two before he could have said why, and the telling, exact and complete, stood in the basket and was worth nothing.

The kiln's breath was the danger of the month and the yard carried it the way a household carries a sleeping child through a door, carefully and without remarks. It came through the wall in fours with a gap where the fifth should have been, and a woman raking the mouth said to no one in particular, “There was a man in \place{Mud End}. Went in his sleep, quiet as a lamb, and them that found him called it merciful.” She said it sideways with her eyes on the coals and went on raking, and nobody took it up and made a lesson of it, and nobody put a figure to the man in Mud End. The rake went in and came out and the coals settled and the sentence lay where it had fallen. The boy held it there. He knew what it was to have a danger in a room that nobody would name twice; a theatre keeps its danger behind a screen and goes on with the case in front of it, and the hands that know where the screen stands are the hands that must not go behind it. Here the screen was a wall of clay and iron, and his hand was a small hand outside the wool that could not have lifted a corner of it.

The yard went around its lines. At the Vail corner the old clean dark line where the \lore{Ash} ridge had turned lay where it lay, and beside it the new finer hairline of grey that the whole yard called \lore{the thaw}, and a woman with a broom went around both and swept neither, because a thing left where it lies is still a thing kept and the house had never argued the point. Under \place[the drying racks]{the lowest rack} the broken birth-slates lay in their place and were gone around as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and no woman reached under them and no man counted them. In the returning line, where the hands came back from \place{the worked strips} at dusk, there was a place that a woman looked at and stepped wide around and put no number to: a hand had gone in there on Seed 15 and had not come out with the rest. The look was a look and the step was a step and the yard went on.

He held the look and the wide step on a place of their own. A yard that goes around a place with a look and a wide step has already entered the place in a roll of its own and decided what it will not read; there is no figure in it and no payer, and a man come down from Slate Row with a clean slate would find nothing to enter and would go away with his slate as clean as he brought it. He did not lay the field's empty place across the two gaps on \place{the loaf shelf} in the other room, or across the pan's gone place on the wall, or across the one stiff crust two inches from his ear. No third gap came. No fourth likeness made a cause. He kept a finger between them without moving anything, and the basket went on at the half-pace, and the heat of the firing month went over his cheek and off across the yard.

Before first grey three or four men with their collars high had come up the yard and gone low-voiced into \place{the back room} off \place{the Vail wheel-shed}, whose door keeps its finger's width of open. Sella carried past at the half-pace. She gave the house no name for the men, turned no head toward the door, and did not say the room was cold. The boy took the three or four into the file and left them there without a figure and without a face; a room that stands a finger's width open is a room the house has decided to keep, and the keeping of it was older than any man who went into it and would outlast them all. The door kept its finger's width. The yard swept past it and went on with the firing.

\person{Jorra Vail} came through with the day's fine grey slate dust ground small as meal into both forearms past the bent little finger and under the nails, and it was not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel. He kept dusty forearms and clean ones on one tray and asked neither which slate had been broken nor whether it was a birth-slate; the two states sat side by side and did not become one account. Under the lowest rack the broken birth-slates stayed where they lay and were entered by nobody. He did not look toward the basket. The dust went into the creases past the bend of the finger the way dust goes into any crease, without being invited and without leaving, and his hands went on with the work as if the forearms were the forearms he had come in with.

“It doesn't care whose hands. It cares if they're steady,” he told the wheel, in the flat voice that went to the tool before it went to the clay and to the clay before it went to a person. He told a pot, “You'll do.” He knew \lore{the full note} of it before it left the wheel --- knew the pitch it would give under a knuckle when it had cooled and been set down --- and he did not say so when a High Kiln kiln-master, come up on the bad wind with cuffs the colour of the ridge, said the pot was singing before its time and that a pot which sings early generally sings itself out early. Jorra said nothing. He kept the knowing where he kept such things, on the tray of answers that had come near to full and had not been paid, and not among the hearings. The kiln-master went off with his sentence whole. The pot went on turning. Neither of the two men was the wiser for what the boy had heard in the withholding, and the knowing stood on its tray, advanced, exact and unspendable.

The two tellings of the shut counting room stood where they stood, side by side with no joining and no third. Men had gone into a room that stands a finger's width open since first grey, and the morning gave no third telling to set beside them; the room kept its open finger and the tellings kept their distance, and the house went on with the firing as if its own wall had no mouth in it. The boy had learned early that a second likeness is the beginning of a rule and a third likeness is the beginning of an account, and that a man with a clean slate needs only the third to stand the other two up and find a payer at the foot of them. He held the two where they were and would not stand a third up to keep them company.

\scenebreak

Before Sella had finished telling him the fire was low, Jorra's bent finger went into the cold iron of \lore{the damper}, moved the tongue a hair's breadth, and left it standing. The cold took the warmth out of the fingertip as iron takes it, without haste and without malice. The end of her sentence arrived at the pot instead of at him, and the household straightened around his silence the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted off it: Sella's hands went on to the next pot, \person{Tarn}'s head stayed down over his work, and nothing in the room turned to look at what had been done. It was an obedience and it was a correction neither of them would name, and the room came level again in the space of a breath.

He read it as one piece of iron with two bites: the bite that holds a vessel whole and the bite that can take the hand off a man, the holding and the taking in the same throat of iron, and no word in \lore{Grathic} that would hold both mercies in one breath. He had known a clamp for what it was in a better-lit room --- a thing you set where the vessel was thin, and thanked for the mercy that stopped the bleed, and hated for the same mercy when it stayed a turn too long --- but a clamp is a clamp and the two bites here were one bite filed on a place of its own, joined to no kiln and worth nothing. A man who calls it a doctrine has already found the payer he wanted. It stood where it stood, and the boy could not have reached it, and there was no hand in the basket to lay across it.

Tarn took the broom only after it had been instructed. Sella gave the instruction to the broom before she gave it to anybody and never gave it to the basket, and he took the tool up as a man takes up an instrument already named, saying, “Yes, house,” to the early thaw and the late, and going on wanting the late because it puts off the paying. His seventh year stood one tend from its end and still held: six counted in the worn thumb-mark where the house could check, and the seventh where it could not, the small knife set back at the very edge of its sill with the unforged collector sentence lying beside it. The breath from Seed 16 was unspent, with its small interval standing at the top of each reach where the breath should have gone in and did not. His chest had been tight since the dark, and Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line, and no scrubbing lifted it; the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back.

One hundred and fifty days old and five months, told by nobody, exact and unspent in the basket at the edge of the work while the yard went around what it would not enter. Jorra's knowing of the pot's note went on the near-full tray and not among the hearings; no seventh hearing came to fill a place where none was owed, and he did not invent one to keep the six company. The kiln missed its fifth behind the wall; under the floor at home the fire would miss its fifth at dusk; from behind the racks came the river's note over water, a fifth low thing never laid among the hearings. Three places kept their own count and none borrowed from another. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water and came down against the fold.

His stroke went on over \place{the flagstones} past the shallow bright patch without even the half-check, and he did not turn his head along the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector. No collector came. He said nothing about the room and Jorra said nothing about the ear, and the two silences stayed unjoined under the one roof while the kilns went on breathing in fours behind the wall. The stones kept the shallow bright patch where the stroke had once stopped, a place nobody entered and nobody swept, and the broom went over it as water goes over a stone that lies where a stone lies: the stroke went on past it without the half-check, and the stones kept the place where it stopped as a thing nobody will enter.
