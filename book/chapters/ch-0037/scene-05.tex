The going of the light brought the river road back to the clay road and the clay road back under \place{the Vail kiln-house} roof. \person{Sella Vail} had carried the basket a dozen paces past the man with the slate turned inward without turning her head and without setting it down, and the measure she kept was not from him but from every open slate between the water and the yard. Woodsmoke went before her and the river wind came up to meet it, and the two passed on the stair of the dark the way two people pass on a stair and do not speak. The first smell under the roof was hot clay and tallow, the smell of the firing month come home ahead of her; the kiln breathed behind the wall in fours with a gap, and the house took no notice of it, having other things to notice. She set the basket low near the bench leg at the angle at which the underside of a board becomes a roof, and gave the instruction to the wool, the fire, the pot and the broom and never to the basket. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. “It's the river wind.” The instruction went past \person[Corin]{the boy} into the wool and came back with neither carrying an answer, and a basket set down is a pot set down and left.

From that low angle the underside of \place{the loaf shelf} was a ceiling with a grain in it, and beyond the bench the wall kept a round paler patch where a shallow clay pan had stood to dry through all of Reed. It had gone between one carrying and the next, in the night or in an afternoon while the house was about its firing, and nobody had said so. There was no scrape on the bench, no crumb, no print in the dust at the wall's foot, no chip of a broken rim, no word in the house to say where it had stood; the pale ring remained the way a plate's place remains on a cloth after the plate is taken up, and the dust inside it lay as thin and as level as the dust outside. He held its empty place on a tray of his own. He had once known a tray by the space in it, the clamp that should have lain here and the needle counted out and not returned, and had been able to name the missing thing from the shape it left on the towel; the reading worked as well now as it had then and was worth the same, which was nothing. No hand under this roof was going to lift the shelf an inch to look for a pan, and his own hand could not have lifted the dust. Through Reed the pan had stood mouth-up against the wall to dry, and a woman going to the racks with wet hands had set a cup in it or cooled a spoon against it, and none of them would have known it by a name. It had held nothing that mattered; that was the whole of it. But the ring it left was the exact width of a thing taken away, and the house went around it with a tilt of shoulder and basket the way it went around the space beneath the lowest rack where the broken birth-slates lie, as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. If a woman had come in and set the pan back, the ring would have been covered and nothing would have been said either; the not-saying was the same whether the pan was gone or returned.

Nobody had joined the pan's place to anything. The shelf above kept its two unnamed gaps, the old space at the row's end and the palm's breadth that had come in with no scrape, crumb, print or word; the fold on the bed in the other room kept one stiff brown-edged crust two inches from his ear and an empty place beside it that had no name; out at the strips lay the field's empty place where a hand went in on Seed 15 and did not come out; somewhere beyond this roof the sixth green was a colour joined to no ear and the foundling's sixth half-turn a shape with no sale. He set them each where they stood and wrote nothing across the space between. No third gap came. No fourth likeness stood up and called itself a cause. No finger of his drew the line. There is a saying of the loaf-shelves of \place{Low Kiln} that two gaps stay two gaps until a finger lays them together and an account then wants a name; no finger in the district had laid these together, and his was a small hand outside the wool and could not have reached the first board, let alone the second. Absences standing in a row will call themselves a doctrine if nobody keeps a hand between them, and a doctrine is nothing but an account that has found a payer; he kept the hand between them without moving anything.

Want came up in him then, small and importunate as it always was, and it wanted the crust two inches from his ear in the other room, stiff and brown-edged and past smelling of anything. It wanted the warm thing more than it wanted the number. The surgeon in him could have said exactly how long the crust had lain there and what had become of the warmth in it, and the saying would not have put one crumb within reach; the hand outside the wool went toward the fold and found wool. He had once reduced a thousand shoulders and could not lift his own chin from a fold of cloth. The joke had gone flat with use, and he kept it on a place of its own beside the others, where it cost him nothing because nobody laughed.

The clay jar stood on the shelf by the wall, cold under its glaze in the dying light. Sella glanced at it once as she passed and did not touch it, the way a woman looks at a pot she has set aside for a purpose she will not name and goes on with her hands full. The glance went to the jar before it went anywhere else, and he set that on the tray of record-first hands beside the rest: the midwife laying her slates before she turned to the fold, the thane passing a finger over a figure before he looked at the crowd, the clerk with his hand to the slate before his eye to the stalls, the reed-cutter straightening his cord before he lifted his trap, and this woman reaching beneath a board before her eyes went to the row. Five hands performing one grammar in one district, and not one of them explained it. He did not make the five into one cause; a man who finds a cause in five hands will soon want a payer at the foot of it.

She stood at the shelf with her back to the basket. Her lips moved along the row, counting what the row kept badly, and came to the two gaps and went on as a tongue goes over a broken tooth without stopping. Then, without looking and without a word, she reached a finger BENEATH the front edge where the charcoal tally lies, held it a breath out and back, brought it away and did not go back to the shelf. He saw the hand go under and come back and saw nothing of what the marks said. Short grouped strokes pressed hard into the grain, others faded nearly into it, kept on the underside where nobody thought to look, the way a note is turned face-down behind a chart. Such a hand either knows the board by heart or has been told not to look, and the reason stood between the two like a door with a light under it; he could not tell which, and the not telling was itself a keeping. Her little-door burn was shut on nothing as the finger came away. He did not read the marks, did not count them, did not lay them across the two gaps above or the one crust in the other room, and set the hand's going and coming on a place of its own where it could gather interest without being spent.

The care of the hiding told more than the marks would have, had he been able to read them: a hand that goes where the eyes are not is a hand that has been trained or warned, and either way the thing beneath the board has been kept long enough to become a habit of the bones. He set that beside the clay jar glanced at and not touched, and beside the pot the neighbour was sent to, and did not make the three into a grammar of their own. Three likenesses standing in a row will call themselves a habit if nobody puts a finger between, and a habit is a thing that can be entered in a slate as easily as a name.

A neighbour come up from the firing work stood in the doorway with a wet pot on her hip, steam going off it into the cooler air and her sleeves dark to the elbow with slip. “What's a firing cost,” she said, “set down there?” The question went to the room and not to any person in it, the way such questions do when the answer has been made into weather.

Sella glanced once at the clay jar and sent the woman to the pot. “Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there. The house has said it.”

The neighbour took it level, the pot steady on her hip and not a drop slopped over the rim, and went back down toward the yard with the question still whole in her mouth and nowhere to set it down. A cost asked of a firing is answered with a pot, and the two lie down together and leave no entry anywhere. The question had a number in it when it came up from the firing work and came back without one, which was the trick the district had learned to perform on every figure that came up out of the clay. The boy watched her go with the small hand outside the wool going up an inch and coming down, and had no way to carry the question either, which was perhaps the reason the house gave it to a woman with a pot and not to him.

At the hearth she told the fire it was the thaw. She turned the coals with the back of a broken pot, the iron edge going under and lifting and letting fall, and said “The house will keep it” where another woman would have said \emph{I}. She stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand counting under her breath toward no opening, her lips moving along a row that was not there, and pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn, the one grey streak at her temple showing pale off the coals. The fire gave four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for a hundred and fifty-one days. He kept the silence as an object and not a payment; a fourth pull is a fourth pull and a gap is a gap, and wherever the missing warmth had gone it had gone without a name. He had once counted a chest in a room of bright lights before he put a hand to it, four and a gap and four again, and the count had told him the shape of the trouble and nothing he could do about it; here the counting came up through the floor and went no further than the hearthstone. Through the finger's-width door the river's note went over water, a fifth low thing never counted among the hearings, and overhead the boards kept four nails, four, and a fifth that was a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts in three places, and none became one. The kiln breathed behind the wall with its own four and its own gap, the month's danger present in every room without being named twice, and the house went on with the pot and the coals as if a room could be kept warm by not speaking of what warmed it.

At her throat \lore{the widow's mark} hung where it hung, a thumb-length of lead-glazed clay with the Vail kiln scratched into it. She did not touch it at a shelf or an afternoon because neither was an opening; she had put her hand to it before the kiln that morning, held it one breath and let go, and had not touched it since, and the rule held exactly with nobody to praise it and nobody to see it broken. She did not look toward the bed. The two rooms did not touch, and the hand that would have reached the crust or the pot's handle stayed where it was, outside the wool, going up an inch and coming down against the fold.

One hundred and fifty-one stood exact and unspent in the going of the light, told by nobody, and nobody under that roof would have known what a number was if it had been spoken.

The pot stood rim out on the hearthstone with its handle standing two inches beyond the small hand that would have closed on it and did not. The charcoal waited under the shelf for a hand that was not his and a day that had not come. On the wall by the bench the pan's empty place kept the shape of its absence, a round patch in the dust nobody had gone around and nobody had named, and he put it where he had put it before: on a tray of its own, apart from the shelf's two gaps and the crust in the other room, exact, complete and worth nothing.
