\daymark{69}{Silt 10, Y.H. 813}

First light found the water sitting on the second step at \place{the Cross} and going nowhere, and \lore{Account-day} holding itself on the step above it, which was dry. The tenth of Silt came into the square with mud on its boots, and neither the bell nor the slate waited for it; the market arrived the way water arrived in this district, by spreading, and took its place around the standing water the way a pot takes a place on a shelf that has been cleared for it. Below the reading block the step lay under an inch of brown water gone flat and glossy in the thin light. Above it two hundred people leaned. A man selling turnips set his heap on a flat stone and went on with his morning; a woman at the eel trays lifted the rim of a creel an inch higher and set it down again. That was the whole of what the flood had done to trade so far. Beyond the square \place{the Ilm} had come up in the night and stopped, and the place went on being a place with water in it, as a cracked cup goes on being a cup and holds what it can.

\person{Corin} was carried down in the basket under the fold, level as a pot filled that must not be slopped, and set in \place{the moot-post}'s finger's width of shade with the cold iron of the post at his cheek. \person{Sella Vail} chose the place with her knee, went around every open slate at a half-pace going down, and gave every instruction to the wool and never to the basket. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. “It's the river wind.” She said the house where another woman would have said I. Her hand did not go to \lore{the widow's mark} at her throat, because a reading was not an opening and she had carried that rule through a year and into this one. Sixty-nine days. The boy held the number with no hand to set it down and no word in \lore{Grathic} in which to hand it over. Cold came in its known order --- cheek, temple, wrist --- and the wool did less than the wind wanted, riding back from his wrist so the cold found the place. His head went where it wanted and came back with the small wet sound. The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the standing water and came down.

Marks passed along the stalls and bread went over the boards, and nobody counted what passed except in the fist that closed and the mouth that thought better of speaking. Two half-loaves went one way and a string of eels another, and the marks laid beside them did not agree, and nobody laid them straight. A woman counted her change twice and put it in her sleeve without the second count changing anything. The boy lay with the shade on his face and the drool freezing at his mouth, and kept the arithmetic of the square in the only place where it could be kept, which was inside him, and it bought him nothing he could lift a hand to.

\person{Berr Clay} came up short at the edge of the shade, where the step was dry and the crowd had to lean in to hear him. The red wool cord of his office was folded under his coat instead of worn across it, and there was green of tasted glaze at one corner of his mouth, and hot clay under the smell of nothing at all, which is the dearest smell in \place{Grath}. He wiped his thumb twice on his apron before he touched nothing. He held a narrow slate unopened against his forearm and passed a finger over what was written and let his eyes rest on it a breath longer than the writing required, as though to make certain the figure had not moved in the night.

“One in twelve, as the notice says,” he said, low, into the leaning top of the crowd. He said it twice, once for the moot and once for the households that had come late, and the second reading went over the water and over the basket as the first had, and he never looked into the basket and never had. The levy was called, he said, and it was not called by him. A household that had heard otherwise had heard wrong. He put himself out of his own sentence as neatly as a hand taken out of a field before the dressing goes on, and the sentence stood up by itself afterward with nobody in it to answer for it. The crowd leaned and took it, and the pigs went over the weighing board with their legs tied and their small hard eyes shut, going where the string took them.

The true subtraction went over the top of the basket and into the noise of the stalls and was spoken by nobody. What the flood would cost went from a fist to a mouth to a shoulder and arrived nowhere; a man shut his hand on a door-latch and stood a moment with it shut, and the boy watched the fist open onto nothing and the shoulder go away without the number on it. Nobody asked the slate to hold it. The boy had once stood at the end of a table where a chart closed a matter while the note that would have opened it again lay turned down beside it, and he had learned there, with an older man's anger that had never once reached his mouth, what a figure does when it is read aloud in a room that has already decided it is settled. A figure read aloud was not the figure paid. The printed one went into the morning and stayed where it was put. The other one went around in sleeves and shoulders and got spent on nothing. He set them on two trays as he set the low sounds on their own places, and spent neither, and there was no word in the district's tongue for the difference between them.

A man at the edge of the crowd, with the water to his boot-top and a number shut in his fist, began to ask what the rise would take out of the households below the sill. Berr did not turn toward him. “A number goes into a slate, not into a well,” he said to the step and the notice and the top of the crowd. “If the house wants it again, there'll be a slate at the watch.” He said the house and not my account, and took himself out of the sentence again; the man put the fist back in his sleeve and went home with his question still standing where he had left it, which was the same place it had stood before he asked. The boy kept the soft sentence and the shut fist on two places and did not lay the one over the other, though they had come out of the same breath.

The bell went four, four and a gap over the square, longer than any bell hung in a temple, and nobody crossed himself. It held on after the pigs were through the board and let go of the square only when the moot had settled back into its leaning. A woman at the turnip heap started at the third stroke and put her hand to the neck of her dress, and the hand went to her apron instead and wiped it, and she went on pricing turnips; the moot leaned at the slate as though nothing had sounded. The boy kept the bell he had heard in the square beside the told bell of \lore{the Long Night}, given in a dark room where no fire was lit and taken on trust, and the early note out of the stone under the falls at \place{the Singing Sill}, and the missing breath of \lore{the kiln's breath} coming up through the boards under his own roof; four low things in four places kept apart for two months, and now the new note of a whole district under water going on underneath all four, enormous and unnamed. That nobody had crossed himself lay on a place of its own and did not join any of them, and he wrote no cause across the four low sounds and the new note and the missing sign. Six hearings of \lore{the full note} stayed six, five from four sources and the sixth out of a person, filed beside the five and never among them; no seventh had come. Three nearly-full answers stood advanced and not paid. The word that belonged to the shape of a double turn stayed a saying on its own tray with the shape beside it and the weight of a thumb apart from both, and was not spoken in the square. He did not lay one of these on another. A likeness laid on a likeness will grow a cause whether or not anyone put one there, and he had learned in a better-lit room what a man can do with an answer he wants too much.

Word came up the step from the direction of \place{the Fishstairs} that the soft-cuffed clerk was still set there, with his cuff gone grey from washing and his slate held to his forearm, his hand going to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls, for a measure nobody had called. Nobody had taken him off. The eels went on being sold over the top of him. The boy filed the order of reaching on the place where he kept all record-first hands, apart from the woman who had written at the flare, apart from the man who had priced him, apart from the clerk behind the Court's table and apart from the Drawing collector with his ordinary hand and no pause in it. Four hands reaching for four records and none of them joined; no fifth was made. Behind the clerk's soft sentence stood the old anger at a chart signed and closed and the note never read, and it stayed behind, because there was no one under any roof in Sill to whom he could say it and no Grathic word in which to say it if there had been. He had the printed figure and the unspoken subtraction on two trays that did not touch. The clerk's slate, still waiting for its uncalled measure, went on a third, and the three places stayed three.

Berr closed the narrow slate without opening it and went off toward \place{High Kiln} with the red cord folded away under his coat and the box in his house still locked and the true deduction inside it, believing he had done the district a kindness. The crowd opened and shut behind him. From the post's shade the boy watched the back go up the road, the clean hands carrying a figure that would not be spent where it had been read, and the warmth of the afternoon came off the wet boards in a thin smell of clay and iron. One brown-edged crust lay stiff in the fold at his ear, and the empty place beside it went through another day without a name. He wanted the crust and wanted what had been taken out of the fold and could lift a hand to neither. The trays did not touch. Nothing had been joined, nothing paid, nothing explained, nothing ended.

\scenebreak

At dusk Sella lifted the basket level and carried him up the yard at a half-pace from every open slate, turning it with her knee so the light fell where she wanted it, and gave the instruction to the fire and the wool and the bucket and never to the basket. “That'll hold,” she said to the coals. “The house keeps it.” She told the fire it was the thaw. She set him on his bed under the \place{Vail kiln-house} roof and not in \place{the Debt Loft}, and put the fold right without looking at his face. In the corner the clean dark line where the ridge had turned lay where it had always lain, and the new grey beside it lay finer than the old line and was not swept, because the house said it was the thaw and would go when it went. No opening had come in the square and the mark had not been touched.

Under the boards the carried fire lay under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the house by degrees and missing one breath in every four; above it the boards kept four nails and four and a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts did not meet. The door stood a finger's width open. Through the gap came the low note of \place{the Ilm} standing against the steps in the dark, and through the floor came the fire's four, four and a gap, and the two kept their separate places as they had for two months. In the mind's eye the back of the man who had read the figure was still going up the road toward High Kiln with the box locked and the cord folded away, and below it the boy's own fold held one crust and the empty place, and the printed figure and the unspoken subtraction lay on two trays that did not touch. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the water and came down. The count went on.
