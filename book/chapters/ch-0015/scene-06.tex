\daymark{73}{Silt 14, Y.H. 813}

The sweeping had gone around the ridge twice already and on Silt 14 it went around a third time, and the ridge stood through all three. \person{Corin} was carried out into \place{the Clay Yard} before the light had settled which way the day would go, level in the basket as a pot filled that must not be slopped, with \person{Sella Vail}'s knee turned against the wicker to keep the draught off his ears. She gave the instruction to the broom before she gave it to anybody else, and to the fire and the wool after that, and never once to the basket. “Round it,” she said to the broom. “You'll pay for it in the thaw if you take it up.” She said the house where another woman would have said I, kept her half-pace from every open slate, and did not touch \lore{the widow's mark} at her throat, because a yard is not an opening and a basket is not a door.

The clean dark line where the \lore{Ash} ridge had turned its corner for a year and two days lay with the old steadiness of a thing that has been looked at every morning and never touched. Beside it the new hairline of grey lay finer than the old line, and the broom went around both. The yard had swept once in preparation and once after the water, and now swept again, and neither the standing line nor the finer grey gave way to any of it. The boy watched from the height of the basket, where a thing arrives first as a shape and only afterwards as a thing with a name. The broom checked, went wide, came back to its stroke. The ridge kept its place. He had been carried past that corner on twenty mornings and had never once seen a hand laid on it, and the not-laying had become as much a fact as the grey itself: a keeping performed by leaving alone.

There was a space under \place[the drying racks]{the lowest rack} where whole slate should have stood. The broken pieces lay in it under the shadow of the pots, and the yard went around the space as wide as a pail of hot water set down where a child might knock it. Nobody stepped into it. A woman at the tubs swept her own ground up to the rack's shadow and stopped there, and swept back the other way rather than cross the finger's width of dark. Another carried a tray of wet pots over it at arm's length, the way a person carries a dish past a sleeping child, and set the tray down on the far side. The boy knew the shape of an avoidance before he knew what lay inside it. It was the same shape as the ridge: the space was not hidden, it was only not entered, and a thing not entered was a thing nobody would have to enter in an account.

He knew the grammar of it. A ward can walk around a bed for a week and never write down why; the rounding is the record. Sella turned the basket with her knee so the light fell where she wanted it, and told the wool to keep the draught off his ears, and went around the space under the rack at the same width she had gone around the ridge. She did not say what lay under the rack. She did not say anything about the space at all. The instruction went to the broom and the fire and the wool; the silence went to the space; and the boy, who had no standing to ask, put the space on a tray of its own beside the ridge and the finer grey and let the three lie where they lay.

A neighbour come up from the tubs said to the rack that the sweep had been done twice already and still the grey came back, and a woman with a pail said it would go when it went, and that was the whole of what was said about either. The broom went around and the yard went on.

\scenebreak

\person{Jorra Vail} came through the door off \place{the Vail wheel-shed} standing a finger's width open. He had been in \place{the back room} since before light with two men whose names the house did not take, and the door had not been pushed wider for them or for anybody else. Fine grey slate dust was worked into both forearms past the bend of his little finger, grey and fine and not the bone-coloured slip of the wheel; the slip he came from ordinarily dried to the colour of old bone and sat on the surface, while this had gone into the creases and would not come away with a wipe of the hand. He said nothing about where he had been. He said nothing about the two men.

He spoke to the wheel before he spoke to anyone, as he always did. “It doesn't care whose hands,” he said. “It cares if they're steady.” The wheel made no answer. He set a pot on the head and turned it, and told it it would do, in a voice with no welcome in it and no unkindness either. He knew \lore{the full note} of a Vail pot before it left the wheel --- knew it in the hand, before the knuckle told anybody else --- and would not have said so in front of a kiln-master from \place{High Kiln}, who would have called it early and made something of it. The boy put the knowing on the tray where questions lasted for years, with no hand of his own to lift the lid.

There was wet clay in the shed and tallow on the shelf, and on a bad wind the sweetish edge of lead glaze came up from High Kiln and put a false sweetness in the mouth. The boy held the dusty forearms and the clean ones on one tray as two states of one morning. He asked neither which slate had been broken nor whether it had been a birth-slate. The two states did not become a cause, because three likenesses laid in a row will grow a cause whether or not anybody put one there, and he had learned in a better-lit room what a man can do with an answer he wants too much.

“The fire's low, Jorra,” Sella said. She had not finished the sentence. His bent finger went in ahead of his hand at the damper and moved it, and the end of her instruction arrived at the pot instead of at him, so that the household straightened around his silence the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted off one corner. Nobody gave an order. The pots went on standing in their rows.

\scenebreak

Under the roof he did not say the back room was cold. He did not look toward the bed where the boy lay, or into the basket. The basket sat where Sella had set it, at the edge of the light and away from every open slate, and Jorra went past it twice without the small turn of the head that would have made it a thing seen. \emph{He's not Vail} stayed where it had been said, sealed as tightly as a lid nobody had tried to lift since the hour of the taking. The promise he had made to the men who burn slates --- a name on a slate, the foundling's name, the one thing in the house that cost him nothing to give --- was neither given nor withdrawn. It lay under the roof with the rest of what the house kept out of its rolls.

Sella told the fire it was the thaw and gave the instruction to the wool and the bucket. She said the house. She did not touch the mark, because a bed is not an opening and a basket is not a door.

\person{Tarn} had set the small bench knife on its sill before light and left it there. Six years stood on the handle in a mark worn almost flat by his thumb, and the seventh was nearly gone and still held back, the one entry in this house the house could not check. He told the empty yard that the knife was where it could be checked, and did not tell the yard what he was counting, because the yard had heard it enough times to be tired of it and a man does not say the same thing twice to a house that does not answer. Ash was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line out at the ridge, and the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back.

His broom went over the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector, over and over, without once turning to look along it. A vessel gave its note somewhere past the door --- a pot set down, or a cup put on a bench, he did not turn to find which --- and his head did not turn either. He went back to his stroke. The loan that can hear stayed uncalled. The sentence that would call it lay under the roof where it had lain for six years, and the two men under that roof kept it without either of them saying it, Jorra's silence and Tarn's silence sitting side by side as the same shape and not joined.

The boy lay under the roof and watched the house sort its own members. It swept around what it would not remove, it entered what it could enter in a roll it showed, it counted whose forearms and whose years and did not count whose silence. He was the one thing in the room that was neither swept around nor entered nor counted: he was set down, like a pot set down, and left. He had once known the other side of that work, the side that decides which body is worked on and which is waited on, and here the deciding was done around him and he was the body waited on. He made no cause of it. He had no standing to ask why a house keeps some things by leaving them alone and others by writing them down, and the surgeon in him and the child in him wanted different things --- the surgeon wanted the rule, the child wanted the crust two inches from his ear --- and the child, as usual, was louder.

\scenebreak

In the afternoon Sella carried him back down the yard at the half-pace, and the sky came down early as though the flood had taken the light downriver with it. The third sweeping was finishing at the racks. The broom went over the trodden ash in short patient strokes, over the ridge's edge and around, and came to the space under the lowest rack and went around that too, wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. The broken slates lay under it in the dark where they had lain since before the boy came under the roof, and nobody put a hand among them. The yard went wide. The yard went on.

A woman at the tubs said the sweep had been done and the grey would come back, and a man with a barrow said it would go when it went, and both went on with their work. The space under the rack got no word at all. It was not kept, and it was not thrown out; it was only gone around, as the ridge was gone around, and by dusk it had the look of a place that had always been empty and was expected to stay so.

Sella put the basket down at the rack's edge, out of the draught and at a half-pace from every open slate, and turned it with her knee so his face was out of the wind. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. She did not look into the basket. Up the yard, under the roof, the carried fire would be lying under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees and missing one breath in every four. He could not see it from where he lay, but he knew the count without hearing it, four and four and a gap, as he knew the shape of the space at the rack and the shape of the ridge, each on its own place and none of them touching.

The light went off the racks and the boy lay in the basket with one brown-edged crust stiff in the fold at his ear and the empty place beside it still without a name. On one tray he held the dusty forearms and the clean ones, two states of one morning and neither of them a cause; on another he held the two silences under one roof, equal and unjoined. No man had been named, no slate counted, no loan called. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth under the roof and came down. Behind him the broom went around the empty space under the lowest rack while the broken slates lay under it entered by nobody, and the seventh year went on being held back without a word, and the count went on into Silt 15 unspent.
