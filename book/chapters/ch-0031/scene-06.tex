\daymark{134}{Reed 15, Fifthday, Y.H. 813}

The morning came up over \place{Low Kiln} the way a kettle comes to the boil in a cold room, a little vapour at the rim first and then the whole cloud of it going up without anybody's leave. Frost lay on the slate-posts at \place{the Cross} and on the backs of the barrows that had been left out in it, and the women came into the square breathing white and rubbing their hands under their aprons before they rubbed anything else. \person{Sella Vail} carried \person[Corin]{the boy} up from the river road at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, going around a pan set out to dry and a trough whose skin of ice would have to be broken before it was any use to anybody, and gave the instruction to the wool and to the river wind and never to the basket. Keep it off his ears; it had come all the way down with them and would go all the way back up. The little-door burn on the back of her hand closed on the wicker's edge and then on nothing. Under the wool his head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound when it went too far, and no instruction had ever been given about that sound. He was one hundred and thirty-four days old, four months and fourteen days, and there was nobody in the square who would have known what those words counted if somebody had said them aloud, and nobody said them.

Her arm went on keeping its exact account of what it held and saying nothing of what it cost. It was the one record in the district he had no standing to enter, complete, minute, faithful to the weight of a loaf and the turn of a wrist and the half-beat at a threshold, and as spendable as the frost on the post. He had learned to leave it where it was. The stalls went up around them with the noise of boards set down and ropes pulled taut, and the market began its morning business of letting figures go past baskets without lighting on them. A girl with a creel took the steps below the bridge sideways and went on selling; a man with salt on his cuffs called a price into the noise and the noise took it and gave him nothing back. The boy's hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth of her shoulder and came down against the fold.

Across the square, beyond the reading block and the moot-post's thin finger of shade, \place{the pot board} stood with its shallow bowls in their dust. A woman stood before it looking at the pots. She had come with two others and had stopped where the bowls sat, and she turned a cup a quarter with the fingers of one hand, not lifting it, only moving it so the light went along the rim and came off it again. There was green at one corner of her mouth, the sweetish green of lead glaze, the colour that gathers on the lip of a glaze-taster after years of putting the brush's tip to the tongue. It lay in the crease beside her lip as a small thing lies in the corner of a cup and is not wiped away; the wet of it caught the little light there was and gave it back dull. Clay stood under her nails. Across the back of one hand went a pale line where something had once been worn tight and long enough to leave its mark, a band of skin that had forgotten the pressure and kept the shape of it. She said nothing. Nobody spoke to her and she spoke to nobody, not even to the pots. She turned the cup another quarter, set it back exactly where it had stood, and went off with the light, the green going with her and the board keeping the place where the cup had been.

The boy filed the colour without a name for it and without a name for the woman. It was the fourth green he had kept at a mouth, and the fifth shape of the colour if Berr's glaze at Berr's own mouth was counted, which it was, on a place of its own. Three others lay beside it in the file, the green at Silt 30, the green at Seed 28, the green at Reed 7, each on its own place, each seen at the edge of a market and none of them touching this one. It was a colour and not a face, a colour and not a name, and there was no word in \lore{Grathic} that would have made it either. He did not speak an ear with it and did not join it to the pale line on the back of the hand, or to the bowls on the board, or to the eel boards downriver, or to the clerk at the landing, or to a box in a house up the clay road. Four greens at mouths and five shapes of a colour lay where they had been put, and nothing among them stood up and said anything. He had spent a hundred and thirty-four days learning that a likeness is not a cause, and the labour of keeping four likenesses from standing up together was invisible, exact, and worth nothing anyone could have entered on a slate.

\person{Berr Clay} came up short out of the clay road with the red wool cord of his office folded under his coat instead of worn across it and the green of tasted glaze at one corner of his mouth, hot clay over the smell of nothing at all. The smell went before him and the man arrived after it, the way a kiln's heat arrives before the hand that opens it. His hands were large and clean. He wiped his thumb twice on his apron before touching nothing, held a narrow slate unopened against his forearm, and passed a finger over the figure with his eyes resting on the writing a breath longer than the writing required, as though he were checking that a seam he had set yesterday had not moved in the night. At the eel stall he took two pair and paid two marks short, and the stall-keeper put the marks in her palm and did not count them where anyone could hear. “A number goes into a slate, not a bundle,” Berr said. “If your house wants it again, there'll be a slate at the watch.” Your house, never I. He took himself out of his own sentence as neatly as a surgeon takes his hand out of a field before the dressing goes on, and stood a moment with his mouth open on the next part of what he had come to say, and then said it to the stall instead: the levy was called and was not called by him, one in twelve as the notice says. Nobody leaned in to hear it at this hour, so the low voice was spent on the boards and went nowhere. He went off toward \place{High Kiln} with the cord folded square under his coat, the narrow slate under his arm, and the box in his house still locked with the true subtraction inside it. He did not look into the basket.

The boy kept Berr's green on Berr's tray beside the twice-wiped thumb and the finger passed over a figure, and kept the woman's green on the woman's tray, and no hand of his laid one across the other. Two mouths had the same colour in them and the two colours were not the same object. Across the square the pale line on the back of a hand went away with the light, and the cup went back to its quarter of the sky, and the market went on standing over its own figures without entering any of them. He had once laid out instruments on a towel in a room where everything had a name and every name had a use; here he laid out colours and gestures and hands on trays that led to no door and asked for no instrument, and there was no towel large enough to hold them all and no use for any of them. That was the whole of it. Exact, complete, worth nothing. The little-door burn shut on the wicker's edge and opened again as Sella shifted him, and they went down toward the sill.

\scenebreak

Below the stone sill \place{the fish-trade shade} held \place{the north bridge}'s long shadow the way a cup holds water, with care and with a flaw nobody would mention. \person{Mira Silt} worked the eels at the outer boards with pale slime dried in long grey strokes on her sleeves and river silt dried pale in the hollow of her neck, her head held a little to the right where the top of her left ear had been. Her small smile stayed on her face the way another woman keeps a door on the latch; it could be shut without anybody noticing the movement. A weir woman with salt white at her wrists asked whether the run would be thin now the reeds were in the beds. Mira flicked a string of eel-bones against her thumb, answered the question and then her own. “Thin? Who's to say thin? It comes when it comes. Ask the river.” She moved two bones from hand to hand while she decided whether to lie, put one away, said something low about a boat that had come up on the flood, and said nothing about the hand that had gone into the strips on Seed 15 and had not come out with the rest. When a buyer in a coat with the river still in its hem named a figure her thumb went under her apron to the grey wool cord and stayed longer than the figure required. Shifting a creel showed the second cord twisted twice beneath it, never carried upriver to \place{Nine Pots}, never lifted at a door, neither knot counted by any thumb but her own and the knot for the thin child upriver untouched. “Two marks the pair and two short,” she said. “You've said it twice and I've counted it twice. There's nothing in the sentence that wants an answer.” The buyer took the eels and went; the figure went into the noise of the stalls and was gone. Her board lay beside \person[Orra Weir]{Orra}'s knot-string and did not touch it. She did not look into the basket.

Orra Weir stood at the lower boards with her board on its side and a cord at her waist, turning the board with the wrist that had turned it ten thousand times. Her hands were like old rope, the pale eel scars lay uncovered across both forearms, and the grey streak showed first through the black hair when she moved her head; silt, cold fish and tallow smoke had arrived a little ahead of her from upriver at \place{the Weir}, and the nasal half-song of the Fen came into her voice where she was tired. She pulled one knot down with her thumb and set it among the others. When a woman at the rails asked what the levy would take now the hands were out in the reeds, Orra gave a date where another woman would have given an opinion. “The ninth of Ilmen. The collector's boat came round the bend on the flood and had six oars. One man stood at the stern and never touched them.” To “That isn't the levy” she said, “It's a date. The river takes what the Court doesn't. You can shout at either one. I've tried,” and went back to the knots. Her thumb went to the cord again as the sentence ended, and the boy could not tell whether that was a lie or only the habit of counting and made no cause of it. The last knot on the cord showed its shape, double turn, tail tucked, tied left over right, the small extra weight of a thumb pressed in; the shape was legible and the word that would have named it stayed where it had stayed since the year of the flower, unspoken in a morning that had a great many figures in it and none he could enter. She watched what there was to watch at the edge of the shade and got no answer from the basket. She did not ask and nobody asked her.

A date given where a figure was asked was an answer with the question taken out of it, a chart closed over a note nobody had opened, and the boy set it where it belonged. The man who kept his hands off the oars stood on a place of his own, apart from the midwife's reaching hand, the thane's twice-wiped thumb and the record-first hand that had entered the figure before it looked for the man who had said it. Three private records of the district stood on boards and in wool within two paces of him --- Mira's two cords, Orra's knot-string, the eel-bones going from hand to hand --- and a fourth tally gone grey with the oil of a working thumb had passed along these same boards a league upriver, and none of them touched another. He could see every one and enter none. He had no standing to ask either woman what she kept, and the keeping, which in this district was the most subversive act available to a person with no slate, went on in materials no Court thumb could spell.

Below the sill, where the tubs stood and the water had gone down into the last wrinkle, a child's foot stayed dark inside three turns of wool. It was the cold to the woman who had wrapped it and weather to the yard, and the two facts went past each other as they had gone past each other every morning of the child's life and never met. The woman said nothing over the wool that any slate could have taken; a neighbour at the tubs said the water had been weather and would go when it went. The extremity went before the core. The small almost-smile on the child's face was not peace. The afterdrop waited on the far side of a warming nobody would perform. He knew the whole order of it as he had once known it under lamps that were not these lamps, and the reading worked perfectly and bought nothing: no beast, no cause, no cure, no recognition, no word for it in the language of the house, no name to carry to any door, and no way to close two inches of his own hand on a fold. It was the most exact thing in the shade and the least spendable. He did not lay it beside any story heard in a yard, or beside the two unswept lines up at the house, or beside the green that had gone off with the light across the square. A foot below a sill and a colour at a mouth were two things in two places, and he had spent a hundred and thirty-four days learning the cost of drawing the line and had never yet been paid for it.

At \place{the out-of-line landing} below the bridge the soft-cuffed clerk was still set for a measure nobody had called. His cuff was grey from washing and grey again by midday, the second grey earned in the working day the way a seam takes the colour of the hand that draws it through. His slate lay against his forearm with the writing turned where nobody else could see it, and his hand went to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls, every time. He had not been taken off since the month turned, or if he had been taken off somebody had put him back in the dark. The market sold its reeds and its eels over the top of him as water goes over a stone that lies where a stone lies, and did not lower its voice. Nobody stepped around him and nobody asked what he was set there for. The boy kept the washed cuff on one place and the slate held where no other man could read it on another, and made a shape of the measure set down before anybody called it, and not a rule.

The woman went off with the light and the green at her mouth went with her, the cup set back where it had stood and the pale line gone from the board. The buyer's figure went into the noise of the stalls and was gone. Below the sill the dark foot stayed dark inside three turns of wool, the cold to one woman and weather to the yard, uncured, uncaused, unnamed.
