The afternoon went downriver with the reeds, and the boy went downriver in the basket, at \place{the Weir} on \place{the plank walk} where the boards ran out over the water on posts. \person{Sella Vail} set him at the edge of the work with no slate within a half-pace, and gave the instruction to the wool and to the river wind and never to the basket --- keep it off his ears, it had come down with them and would go back up with them, and a basket set down was a pot set down and left. The little-door burn closed on the wicker's edge and then on nothing. Salt had dried white on the posts where the flood had touched them and gone away; the traps stood with their mouths upriver; the smell of eel, cold fish and tallow smoke came up off the \place{Ilm} and went under the wet wool. A league of the river road lay between these boards and \place{Low Kiln}, and the whole of it had been walked at the half-pace.

The judgement of the morning held into the afternoon. A bundle was what the arm that carried it said it was, going-downriver and coming-back, green at the broken ends and pale at the frost-bitten tips; nothing set on a slate anywhere along the boards contradicted the arm. The boy watched a girl with a creel take a bundle up and let her shoulder settle under it before she moved, and watched a man behind her swing two so the cut ends cleared the rack. Their arms knew what they held. Their arms did not know what the holding cost, and there was no figure in the district for the stiffness that would come into them by dark. Exact, complete, worth nothing. He had the same register here that he had once had at the head of a table, and the same empty authority to enter what it read.

A reed-cutter came along the boards with a bundle on his shoulder and a short length of grey wool or reed-fibre cord looped inside his coat. The wool had gone grey with the oil of a working thumb, the grey a stitch-hole takes after a thread has been drawn through it a dozen times. He set the bundle on \place[the blessing stone]{the flat blessing stone} and straightened the cord on his thumb, drawing it through his lightly closed fingers the way a man draws a measuring line and not the way a man opens a book. Then he turned it once so the knots stood in the small light, tied one low with his teeth and a pull of the thumb, bit the end off and put the cord back inside his coat, and drew the coat closed over it.

A weir woman with salt white at her wrists stopped beside the stone. “What does that cord hold?” she said.

“You can ask the cord.”

“I can't read a cord.”

“No more can the Court.” He said it level, with the bite of reed-fibre still at the corner of his mouth, and went to lift a trap with the question going down into the water behind him.

No figure came out of the exchange. No knot was counted where a man could write it down, and the cutter gave the cord no name and made nothing of the bundle that lay on the stone with its cut ends nodding in the wind. The boy kept the object where it was. A cord was a page that could be carried inside a coat; the wear of the thumb was its only ink, and the knots stood up off it for anyone with eyes, and not one Court thumb in Grath could spell a word of it. There was a saying of the Weir that the cord knew what the mouth would not say and the mouth knew what the cord would not count, and the two were kept apart because laying them together was a trade no honest house made. He had watched a man go around a place in a returning line with a look and a wide step and no number that morning; he had watched a woman's finger go beneath a board at home without a word; and now a third private record stood on a stone in the small light with its end bitten off. He set them side by side and not across, the way an instrument is set beside an instrument on a towel and not laid upon it. He did not count the knots. He did not name the cord's keeping or join it to the place in the strip where the hand had gone in and not come out, or to the marks under the board, or to any word that had once been let fall into a basket and not repeated. Four likenesses in a row will stand up and call themselves a cause if nobody holds them apart, and holding them apart was the work he had been given and had never once been asked to do.

Lower down the boards \person{Orra Weir} stood with her board on its side and a cord at her waist, turning the board with the wrist that had turned it ten thousand times. Her hands were like old rope and the pale eel scars lay uncovered across both forearms; the grey streak showed first through the black hair when she moved her head, and the nasal half-song of the Fen came into her voice where she was tired. She pulled one knot down with her thumb and set it among the others.

“What'll the levy take now the hands are in the reeds?” a woman at the rails said.

“The ninth of Ilmen,” Orra said. “The collector's boat came round the bend on the flood and had six oars. One man stood at the stern and never touched them.”

“That isn't the levy.”

“It's a date. The river takes what the Court doesn't. You can shout at either one. I've tried.” Her thumb went to the cord again as the sentence ended, and the boy could not tell whether that was a lie or the habit of counting, and made no cause of it. A date given where a figure was asked was an answer with the question taken out of it, a chart closed over a note nobody had opened; the man at the stern who kept his hands off the oars went on a place of his own, apart from the midwife's reaching hand and the thane's twice-wiped thumb, and none of the three touched.

The last knot on the cord showed the shape it had shown every morning since the year of the flower: a double turn, tail tucked, tied left over right, with the small extra weight of a thumb pressed in. The shape was legible. The name of it was not spoken, and the boy had no word in \lore{Grathic} for the shape and no voice to say it if he had. He did not count it. He did not lift a hand to it; his hand could not have closed two inches on the rim of the basket, let alone reached a cord at a woman's waist a league upriver from the bed where he had first heard the word that went with it. The cord would go back to \place{the Eel House} where no clerk could spell it and no boy could read it, and the watching would go on getting no answer, and the knot would stay a thing a woman had made to hold and not a figure anyone had paid.

Her board lay beside the eel board at the outer edge of the shade and did not touch it.

At the outer boards \person{Mira Silt} worked the eels with slime dried grey in long strokes on her sleeves and silt pale in the hollow of her neck, her head held a little to the right where the top of her left ear had been and her small smile kept on her face the way a door is kept on the latch. A woman come down from the traps asked whether the run would be thin now that the hands were out of the reeds.

“Thin? Who's to say thin?” Mira said. “It comes when it comes. Ask the river.” She flicked a string of eel-bones against her thumb and moved two from hand to hand while she decided whether to lie, and put one away when the deciding was done. She said something low about a boat that had come up on the flood and nothing about the hand that had not come out of the strips.

A buyer in a coat with the river still in its hem named a figure for two pair. Mira's thumb went under her apron to the grey wool cord and stayed longer than the figure required, and shifting a creel showed the second cord twisted twice beneath it, never carried upriver to \place{Nine Pots}, never lifted at a door, the knots on neither counted by any thumb but her own and the knot for the thin child upriver untouched.

“You've said it twice and I've counted it twice,” she said. “There's nothing in the sentence that wants an answer.” The buyer took the eels and went, and her smile did not move, and she did not once look into the basket where the boy lay.

He kept her not-looking on a place of its own. It was a keeping, the opposite of the pricing face turned the same way across a market, and he had no word in \lore{Grathic} for either and no standing to ask either woman what she held. On the boards in the small light stood three records in wool and thumb and teeth: a cord gone grey inside a coat, a knot-string at a waist, a second twist beneath an apron. All were visible. None was enterable. A Court thumb could spell a slate and could not spell one of them. He had once kept a tray of instruments and known every one by the wear on its handle, and here the wear was the record and the handle was a working woman's thumb, and the tray was a plank walk above the river with the water going under it and taking the sound of every figure before it reached the far bank. His small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the water and came down. It could not reach the cord on the stone, or the knot-string, or the twist under the apron, or the bundle on the blessing stone with its green cut ends. He set the three records down where they already lay and went on being carried, and the going-downriver went past him and the coming-back went past him and no figure went with either.

\scenebreak

At the going of the light \person{Sella Vail} carried him up the clay road at the half-pace, the river smell going off her as they climbed and the woodsmoke of \place{Low Kiln} coming down to meet them. At \place[the drying racks]{the lowest rack} she went around the space beneath it as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and the broken birth-slates lay where they lay and were entered by nobody. Under the Vail roof she set him on his bed and not in \place{the Debt Loft}, and put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without once looking at his face. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and had stayed frozen all day, because nobody under any roof in the district had been given an instruction about the thread and a basket set down was a pot set down and left. Her hand rested a half-beat longer than the setting needed before it went away.

She told the fire it was \lore{the thaw}, and turned the coals with the back of a broken pot. “The house will keep it,” she said, where another woman would have said \emph{I}. She stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand counting something under her breath toward no opening, pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn, the grey streak pale off the coals, set the pot where it could be found in the dark with its rim out and its handle toward the reaching hand, and did not look toward the bed.

Through the finger's width of open door came the river's note of water over water, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, a fifth low thing never counted among the six. Through the floor under blind ash the carried fire took its coal and gave four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for a hundred and thirty-two days. Overhead the boards kept their four nails, four, and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts in three places, and none of them became one. The boy lay with cold iron at his cheek and the fold at his ear and the one stiff crust where it had lain since Stone 15 with its empty place beside it still unnamed, and the dark did what the dark does and joined nothing.

Cold arrived in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist; the wool rode back from the small bare joint and the cold went under it. The child in him wanted the crust two inches off and the warmth of the pot with its handle turned out, and wanted the number of his days spoken by somebody who would know what it counted; the man in him held the three counts apart as a man holds instruments on a tray while the surface under them is not level. Both were true. The second was quieter and the first was louder, and the keeping of them spent nothing and bought nothing anyone could enter.

His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water and came down. One hundred and thirty-two days old, four months and twelve days, he was told by nobody and would not have known what the number counted if somebody had. The reeds went past downriver in armfuls judged by the arm that carried them, the knots stood in the wool where no Court thumb could spell them, the marks waited under the board for a finger that would come back without looking, and the hand went up an inch and came down with the day exact, unspent and spoken by nobody.
