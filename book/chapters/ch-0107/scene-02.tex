In the afternoon \person{Sella Vail} carried him down out of \place{the Vail kiln-house} at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, level and out of the draught, as a pot is carried that must not be slopped. \place{Low Kiln} lay under \place{Sill} in \place{Grath} with the dry cold on it, and the yard stood white in the parts where the frost had been and brown where feet had gone. The instruction went to the wool and the river wind and to the fire she would bank against their coming back, and never to the basket. “Keep it off his ears,” she told the wool. “It's the river wind.” That was all. She did not look into the basket and she did not speak to what lay in it.

From the low angle of the basket \person[Corin]{the boy} took the field as he found it, the small sounds first, because the small sounds do not lie and the large ones are mostly weather. One leathery brown-edged barley crust lay two inches from his ear with its corner standing a hair off the wool, and the empty place beside it kept its name to itself; no third had come to make the row come out even. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the dry cold and was unwiped, because nobody under any roof had ever been given an instruction about the thread. Cold had come to cheek and temple and the small bare joint of the wrist where the wool rode back, and had stopped there as at the end of a line written out for it. He knew the field and could not enter it: a trauma surgeon who could reduce a shoulder in the dark and could not lift a crust two inches to his own mouth. Both facts were true. The second was louder. Neither won, and the quarrel between them was the work.

She went around \place{the Clay Yard} as the yard went around its seven walked-around places, with a look and a wide step and no number. At \place{the Vail corner} the clean dark line kept its finer grey hairline beside it, the thing the house called \lore{the thaw}, unswept and thickened in the month's dry cold, with the faint sour tang of \lore{Ash} coming up off it where it had not been swept in two years. Beneath \place{the lowest rack} the broken \lore{birth-slates} lay where they had been put and were entered by nobody, and on \place{the flagstones} by the rack end the round ring where the shallow pan had once stood kept no scrape, no crumb, no print, no chip and no word. \place{the yard well} stood with its rope stiff in the cold and \place{the trough} skinned over from edge to edge; on \place{the flat stone by the well} the silt-clouded plate of ice sat a little out of the path of feet, which was where it had been put and where it stayed. Farther out, on \place{the worked strips}, the field's empty place kept its own shape in the stubble, and nearer in, where the returning line came back on itself, lay the place where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out; she went around that one widest, by nothing measurable, and went around it once and not twice, because a second going-around would have made a figure of once.

A line left where it is is still a line kept. A sign is a thing that points to a payer, and no payer stands under that ground.

The gone white frost off the well's north lip, the skin on the trough and the clouded plate on the flat stone stayed weather entered nowhere, with a finger between every pair, not a fourth covering and not a sixth low thing; through \place{the finger's-width door} the river kept its note of \lore{water over water} under the winter skin that the cold had put on and the cold would take off, found where put on the first day of the month and not made again, not spent on the reading behind and not spent on \lore[the Hearth Drawing]{the Drawing} behind. The winter skin sat on its own place beside the low note and not upon it, a covering put on by the weather and entered nowhere, and the boy entered it nowhere and kept a finger between it and the note it muffled.

At the well \person{Ilva} drew with the pail against her knee. The rope was stiff in the Stone cold and gave its small dry rasp as the bucket came up, and the water in it was dark and moved without a sound. Clay had dried pale in the crescents of her nails and the finer grey dust of the racks was worked into the seam of one thumb, which she did not scrub out. Her hands knew the weight of a pail and the edge of a wet board and had never been soft, and the people going about the yard noticed the pail first and the sleeve not at all, which was the order she preferred and the order she had arranged the world into without anybody's asking.

A woman waiting her turn, with a pot on her hip and her shawl drawn against the river wind, asked what Stone's firings had taken.

Ilva's hand went toward her sleeve and stayed there. The short strip of notched birch bark lay in the fold where it always lay, pale where a thumb had rubbed the notches and dark in the uncut grain between, and it was not taken out, not read, not copied, not counted and not joined to the open slate a pace away on the stone lip, or to the charcoal beneath \place{the loaf shelf}, or to the cord, the knot-string or any saying that had arrived nowhere. No figure came off the wood. Her eyes went to the asker and her mouth opened after both, the half-beat sitting in the gap between the looking and the word where a lesser observer would have put an intention.

“Ask the pot what it cost,” she said. “I keep the count, not the account.”

She put the flat of her hand once more on the sleeve and went back to the pail, and nobody turned twice.

The day's hunts and repairs went by as directions judged by the carrying arm and the weather, never as figures. A man come down from \place{Cold Acre} shifted a hare across his shoulders at the well and, to a woman who asked what the carrying had cost, said, “Ask the weather. It carried the snow. It can carry the price,” and went off toward \place{the clay road} with the hare unweighed and nobody opening a slate. Another man with a cracked jar in a sling of rope asked what the month's mending would want and was told to ask the pot; the pot, being a pot, said nothing. A woman with a basket of kindling asked what the seventh year would want when it came, and two men mending a strip of fence said the thaw would be early and the thaw would be late. \person{Tarn} said “Yes, house” to each of them, and kept the late because a late thaw puts off the paying, though the paying had stood in the ruts since the water went.
