\daymark{202}{Ember 23, Y.H. 813 (Thirdday)}

The dark under \place{the Vail kiln-house} roof had the same shape it had worn the night before, the shape of a pot kept under ash: the heat down in the low places, the cold at the edges, and no hand anywhere to say which of the two had been given an instruction. In \place{Low Kiln} the kiln month's last working week went on into its third day without a boundary drawn under the second, and the boy came into his two hundred and second morning at six months and twenty-two days, and nobody under any roof said either number. He had stopped expecting to be told. The wanting was still there, but it had gone down into the body with the hunger and sat there without a word, the way a tool put aside at dusk sits where it was set until somebody reaches for it in the morning; the tool does not ask to be reached for, and the wanting did not ask to be counted.

The first smell was hot clay under tallow and wet wool under that, and beneath all of it the sour mineral breath of the unswept corner, which the house called \lore{the thaw} and had called it so for a year without lifting a broom to it. The first sound was the kiln drawing in fours with a gap behind it: a brick breath taken, held, let go, and then the little silence where a fifth pull should have stood. Not a bell. Bells belonged to readings and to the district's arithmetic; this was a kiln doing what a kiln does, and the boy had learned to tell the two apart before he had learned to hold up his head. \lore{Ash} had gathered in the corner in a clean dark line with a finer hair of grey beside it, and the two lay together without either becoming the other, as the day's two rooms lay together without touching.

Under the floor \lore{the carried fire} gave four low pulls and kept the silence where the fifth should have closed, its payer without a name for two hundred and two days. Through the finger's-width door the river kept its note of \lore{water over water}, a fifth low thing that had never been counted among the hearings and had never been asked to stand up and be one; it went on with its note the way water goes on over water, with no regard for the ear under the roof or for the hand lying still against the fold. Overhead the boards kept four nails, four on the next board, and a knot the width of a thumb where the fifth nail should have been. Three counts in three places. They did not become one. A man who wanted them to agree would have had to put a payer under all three, and no payer stood to any of them; he had spent two hundred and two days refusing to supply one, which was not a power and not a conclusion but only the order in which he laid the things down. The crust lay leathery two inches from his ear with its empty place beside it unnamed and no third, the thread of drool frozen and unwiped because nobody under this roof had been given an instruction about the thread, cold stopped at cheek, temple and the small bare joint at the wrist where the wool rode back, and the afterdrop waited on the far side of a warming nobody in this house would perform.

\scenebreak

Before first grey a wrong wind came up off \place{High Kiln} and brought the sweet edge of \lore{lead glaze} with it --- a thin sugary taste at the back of the throat, the sweetness that sits under a thing gone wrong rather than on top of it. It came over the yard, went under the door, touched the fold where the boy lay, and went out again on the same wind, leaving nothing behind it that any hand could take up. The district called such a wind a direction when it came and called it nothing at all when it went; he had learned to read the direction of a thing before he read its meaning, because the meaning was usually supplied by somebody with a slate and a reason. He read this one as a direction and entered it nowhere.

The saying about a pot singing before its time had come up the yard on such a wind on other mornings and would go out on such a wind again. A man who said it meant no more by it than a man means who says a wet thing will cost more in the thaw: it was a saying about a pot, and the pot in question had gone on being a pot. He did not make the saying true of a person, because a saying made true of a person is a cause with a payer standing behind it, and no payer stood here. The sweet edge went out on the wind it had come on and the yard went on with its work, and nothing ailed anybody that the yard was prepared to name.

\scenebreak

\person{Sella Vail} came in from \place{the Clay Yard} with the day's dust held in the hem of her apron and gave the instruction before she gave it to anybody. It went to the wool, the fire, the pot, the broom, the running water and the river wind, and never to the basket; the basket was a pot that had been filled and set down, and a pot set down with no instruction given to it is a pot left where it stands. She kept her half-pace from every open slate as she came under the roof, the same half-pace she would keep going up in the sun and coming back in the dark, so that no slate could take a figure off her passing and no figure could take a name off the child in the basket. At \place{the loaf shelf} she stood with her back to the basket and moved her lips along a row standing with two unnamed gaps --- the old space at the row's end and the palm's breadth that had come in on the night of Seed 6 --- and gave nobody a figure. Her eye went once to the clay jar cold under its glaze on the shelf by the wall, and her hand started toward it and shut half over nothing without a touch, the little-door burn closing on air as it closed on whatever came within reach of it; then the hand went back to the row. She kept her finger on top of the shelf's front edge where any woman in the district could see it lying, and whatever lay beneath the board lay unread, uncounted and uncopied. She pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carried the burn, so that the grey streak at her temple showed pale in the low light, and did not discuss the streak or the winter it had come in. The household straightened around the quiet of her as cloth straightens when a weight is lifted.

At \place{the hearth} she told the fire it was the thaw, and turned the coals with the back of a broken pot, and said \emph{The house will keep it} where another woman would have said I. Then she stood with the back of the pot in her hand and counted under her breath toward no opening --- the small intake, the hold, the letting go --- and the count arrived nowhere, as it had arrived nowhere on every other morning of the boy's life, and the house kept its warmth without once saying who had paid for it. She fed him barley gruel from the house's own dish with two fingers behind his ear, wiped the spoon on her sleeve and not on his mouth, and went off leaving the corner of his mouth with its line of gruel still on it, for the instruction had gone to the spoon and the dish and not to the boy's face. She set the pot rim out with its handle two inches beyond the reaching hand and let her hand rest a half-beat longer at the setting than the setting needed, and did not look toward \place{the bed}. She never looked toward the bed at the setting. The two inches were not a distance anybody under this roof had been asked to measure, and the hand lying outside the wool on the fold did not close them.

\lore{The widow's mark} hung on its cord at her throat where it had hung since the first firing, touched once before the kiln on Ember 1, held one breath, let go, and at no shelf, hearth, well, rack, basket, yard, morning or bed since. A morning was not an opening and a shelf was not an opening; there had been no opening since before the kiln on Ember 1, and nobody under any roof had praised what the one opening had mended or seen what it had broken. The instruction had gone to the fire and the pot and the wool and had not once gone to the child who lay under the roof receiving it, and the boy, who had spent years in rooms where a man is named before he is treated, filed the order of it without approving it and without calling it a slight. It was the household's grammar. A pot was told what it wanted; a child was told nothing, and the warmth arrived all the same.

\scenebreak

At \place{the yard well} a rack woman stood with the day's \lore{slip} dried pale in the creases of her wrists, the pale sitting in the lines of the skin the way flour sits in the grain of a board, and asked what the pots would want before the kiln month was out. She asked it sideways, as cost-questions are asked in this district, to the yard rather than to Sella, so that the answer might belong to the yard if it turned out to be an expensive one. “Ask the pot,” Sella said. “Ask the weather. The pot knows what it wants and the weather knows what it'll take.” The woman took the answer away level, with nothing priced in it, nothing refused and nothing entered, and went back to her rack with the pale still in her wrists and her question still unowned.

A man come off \place{the clay road} stood a moment at the edge of the well with a number shut in his fist, the fist held the way a man holds a thing he has carried a long way and does not intend to set down. “What'll the kiln want now the figure's gone up twice?” he asked, and the number stayed in the fist while he asked it, arriving nowhere. Sella did not look at the fist. “Ask the house,” she said. “Ask the weather. The house has said it.” The man went off with the number still closed in his hand, neither given nor denied, and went the way he had come, up the road toward the kilns. A number in a fist has the shape of a thing held and not the weight of a thing paid; a man can carry it up a road and down a road for days and never once set it where somebody could read it, and the figure gone up twice in the yard will have gone up twice without crossing a wall.

In the other life a cost named at the start of a case had been a hand in a cavity and a count at the end of it, and somebody had always been responsible for both. Here a cost asked as a direction went to the pot, the house and the weather and came back with no payer in it, and the household went on warm. He had watched the method work for two hundred and two days and had never once seen it enter a figure, which was the whole of its excellence and the whole of its danger, and he kept it on its own place without calling it either a kindness or a lie. The rack woman went to her rack and the man went up the road, and the yard had been asked twice what it would cost and had answered twice with a direction, and no slate was the wiser.

Up the road the second reading of Ember stood where it had stood since the tenth, unchallenged: the box gone toward \place{High Kiln} locked with the true subtraction inside it, the printed one in twelve still printed and unchallenged, a figure gone twice over the basket and still not the figure paid. \person{Hessa Rale}'s narrow slate lay unread at \place{Greybank} with the writing turned to her sleeve, and the soft-cuffed clerk below the north bridge was still set at the out-of-line landing for a measure nobody had called, his hand to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls. No voice in the yard had challenged the one in twelve and no hand had come back down the road with the subtraction taken out of the box. The district's arithmetic went on in loaves and glances and fists, and the boy's went on in objects laid edge to edge that no hand of his could spend.

\scenebreak

Back under the roof the boy lay with the fold at his ear and the crust two inches from it, and put the question to his hand. He did not put it aloud; there was no need and no ear in the house to hear a question if he had had the voice to make one. The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water through the finger's-width door, went up as it had gone up on other mornings and in other small hours, and came down before the breath Sella had counted at her fire, in the room that did not touch his, had finished. It did not come down at once, and it did not stay through the hold; it came down in the first small intake, the body keeping its own time and letting the inch go early. There was no failure in the early letting go. An inch let go early was an object in the file, no less than an inch held through a whole count and no more, and the surgeon who had once timed cavities and counted what came out of them would have entered it on a place of its own and gone on to the next thing.

He entered it now on a place of its own beside the three held changes on their three places, the small dry scrape from \place{the Debt Loft} on a fourth and the pale line at the gum of the man carried out on a fifth. It was not a hearing and not a seventh, not a stopping and not a payment, not one of the three held changes and not a change made twice over, not a key, not a power and not a rule; no payer was named to it and nothing stood up out of it to call itself a sign. The market had no column for an inch and the district had no slate that would take one; the inch stayed under a roof the market could not enter, worth exactly what an inch is worth in a household that keeps its accounts in loaves and glances and the space where a loaf should have stood.

He still could not close the two inches to the pot handle, lift his chin the two inches to the crust or speak a number that anybody under any roof had been asked to believe. The carried fire went on missing its fifth with no payer for two hundred and two days, the river went on keeping its fifth low note, the boards went on keeping their four nails, four and a thumb-width knot, and the three counts stayed in three places. Nine stoppings stood as nine objects with no tenth before the seventh year was earned, the breath from Seed 16 stood unspent, and the loan that could hear was uncalled; in the file \person[Tarn Vail]{Tarn}'s knife lay at the edge of its sill with six counted where the house could check and the seventh at the lip where it could not, and \person{Jorra Vail}'s dust, brought through the shed door on other mornings and never named, stood beside the sentence he had finished for the pot. None of it came under the boy's hand and none of it went into the file as a cause. The fold took the hand without a word. Two hundred and two stood exact in the wool, complete and unspent, spoken by nobody, and nothing had been joined, named, opened, called, spent or ended.
