\daymark{113}{Seed 24, Y.H. 813}

\dropcap{T}{he last} tend of the planting stood in \place{the worked strips} east of \place{Low Kiln} with the seed up and nobody giving a figure for the hands that had set it. First grey came over the \place{Clay Yard} the colour of wet ash rubbed thin on a board, and the rows beyond \place{the drying racks} kept their small green by the skin of the cold. The hands that had gone in on Seed 15 came out at dusk and went home and came back, and the yard counted them the way it counted everything that must not be written: in glances and handfuls and the space where a man's arm should have been. A woman with seed in her fist had shut the fist rather than say what it held. A man with a barrow had shaped a number with his mouth and thought better of it. The seed stood all the same. It had wanted hands and it had got them, and the figure for the hands had gone nowhere, and the last tend held.

\person[Corin]{The boy} was carried to the edge of the strips in a basket under a fold of wool, the way a pot is carried that must not be slopped, and set where he could see the direction and not enter it. He was one hundred and thirteen days old, three months and twenty-three days, and nobody under the roof knew there was a number to be known, and nobody said so. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the worked ground and came down. He could no more close two paces on the strip than he could lift his own head from the fold; the distance was a direction with no figure at its foot, and a direction with no figure at the foot of it was still a direction, which was the whole of what he could enter. He had once stood at the head of a table and sorted a room's worth of wounded men into the ones who would live and the ones who would not, and he could not put a hand to his own chin. The joke of it was exact and cost him nothing, because there was nobody under that roof to tell it to who would know what a chin was worth.

Cold arrived in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, and the wool did less against it than the cold wanted, riding back from the bare joint so the cold found the place it had come for. His head went where it wanted and made the small wet sound it made when it went too far, and nobody put it right, because the instruction had gone to the wool and not to the wandering. The thread of drool at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody had wiped it, and nobody under the roof had been given an instruction about the thread. The last tend held. He lay in a basket at the edge of it and could not close two inches on the crust in the fold.

By the time the light got under \place{the Vail kiln-house} roof \person{Sella Vail} had given the bucket its instruction, and the broom, and the pot, and the fire, in that order, before she had given anybody anything. “Round it,” she said to the broom, meaning the corner where the sour mineral tang came up out of the unswept \lore{Ash}. “Go around what the sweep left. It cost enough to come in.” To the pot she said nothing that a pot would need to hear twice, and to the fire she said it was \lore{the thaw}, and to the basket she said nothing at all. The instruction went into the wool before it went into a person, and the person came last or not at all, which was the arrangement the house had run on for three years. She said \emph{the house} where another woman would have said \emph{I}, and kept a half-pace from every open slate, going around a slate set out to dry as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. Her little-door burn shut on nothing as she passed the basket and opened on the bucket's handle.

The slate set out to dry at the rack's end lay with its writing turned under and nobody turned it up, and she went around it as she went around the space beneath the lowest rack, as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it. The yard went around that space too and entered it none of them and did not say what lay beneath it. The boy kept the going-around on a place of its own, exact and worth nothing, and made no approach to it with his one free inch of hand.

At \place{the loaf shelf} she moved her lips along the row without making a figure of it, the way a woman counts what she will not count aloud. The board had two gaps in it, one at the row's end and a second beside it that had come in the night of Seed 6, and her lips went over the bare places and came back to the same spot twice the way they always did, and nothing was said. The clay jar on the wall shelf was cold under its glaze. Above the kiln the woodsmoke had gone flat under the roof and lay there with the smell of wet wool and the faint sour tang that came up from the corner in cold weather. A hard heel of barley bread sat at the row's end where a thumb had pushed it along, and the space beside it was worn smooth by hands reaching past it for something else. There was no figure in it. There had never been a figure in it. There was a saying among the loaf shelves of Low Kiln that two gaps stay two gaps until a finger lays them together, and that an account then wants a name; no finger in the district had laid these two together, and the boy kept his own finger off them for the same reason another man might keep his off a clamp that stood open on a table.

\scenebreak

A woman came up from \place{the yard well} with wet sleeves and water on her hands, and asked what the planting had cost now that hands had been in the strips. She asked it of the doorway and not of anybody in it, which was how the yard asked its questions when it wanted the household to answer and no house to have answered. The question stood a moment in the cold between them, the way a pot stands on a bench before somebody decides where to set it. She said it again to the pot as though the pot might have an answer the doorway had not, and wrung a sleeve without taking her eyes off the shelf. A bucket went down into the well behind her and came up with the cold iron of the handle two inches beyond the boy's hand.

Sella's eyes went once to the clay jar under its glaze and came back. It was the only look she gave it, and it was not an opening. “Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there,” she said. “The house has said it.” She left the space at the row's end with no figure in it, pushed the heel of bread along the shelf a finger's breadth, and went on with the loaves.

“Ask the pot what a wet thing costs set down there. The house has said it.” The instruction went to the pot. It did not go to the woman, and it did not go into the basket, and the woman stayed with her sleeves wet and her question still in her mouth, reaching for a pot she had come for and would set down somewhere and leave. The boy, who was the only grown man under that roof and had the use of none of it, watched the order of it: the record before the person, the pot before the question, the shelf before the child. He had watched that order in a better-lit room and had learned what it meant, which was that the thing written down would still be there after the person was carried out. Here nobody was being carried out. The order went on all the same, exact and worthless, the way instruments laid out on a towel are exact and worthless until a hand takes one up.

The woman took the pot by the handle and held it a moment with the instruction still in her ear, not minding that it had not been given to her, or minding and finding the arrangement ordinary. He had seen a chart handed to a man and thanked for it, and an order given to a nurse and written down, and both had taken a thing addressed to the object as though it had been addressed to them. Here the pot went off under her arm and the question went with it, and the shelf kept its two gaps.

Later, when the light had got into the well's mouth and the buckets were going down and coming up, a man came up the yard toward the well with a number in his fist. He had brought it up out of the fist as he walked, the way a man brings up a thing he has been carrying in his pocket to see whether it is still there, and he held it in the open palm a moment for the yard to see. Chaff from the worked strips stood in the creases at the base of his thumb. A woman at the tubs called the morning cold and did not look up, and the yard went on with its buckets; nobody asked him twice. He looked at it. Something in the looking made him shut his hand again, the thumb going over the top the way a thumb goes over a thing that is not to be read aloud, and he put the fist back into his sleeve and went off toward the road without giving it. The sleeve was worn at the cuff and smelled of the yard and of tallow, and the fist went down into it without a sound, the way a thing goes into a pocket that will not be looked in again.

He read the shut fist the way he had once read a chart laid closed with a neat line drawn over a note nobody had opened: exact, complete, worth nothing. It was the same work he had done at the foot of a bed, closing the chart on a man already beyond the room and going on to the next with the line drawn straight and the note left where it lay. In that room the neat line meant a man had been sorted and the sorting entered, and the neatness of it was the whole of the harm. Here the fist shut on a figure and the figure went back where it had come from, and the harm was smaller and no less complete. The figure had been brought up out of the fist and looked at and put back, and it had arrived nowhere, and nobody in the yard had made an event of it. He wanted to lift a hand to his own chin and could not. The fist went off up the road under a sleeve with the number still shut in it, and the number went with it, and the strips kept their seed and their handfuls and their glances and gave nothing up.

The work went on around the withheld figure the way water goes around a stone set in it. The woman with the wet sleeves wrung them out at the well and went back to her pot. A broom went around what the sweep had left at the corner rather than over it, the bristles making the small dry sound bristles make on flagstones, and nobody called the place by a name. At the shelf loaves moved along the board a finger at a time, the hard heel going where hard heels go, and the two gaps stayed where they were and were not read. Sella shifted the pot the woman had been sent to a little further from the draught, set it down where it could be found in the dark, and left it. Nobody joined the number in the man's sleeve to the seed in the strips. Nobody joined anything to anything. A girl at the racks called to somebody about a pot and got no answer and went on with the racks. Two men passing the well spoke of the weather as a thing that had been expected and did not look at the sky to check it.

Under the roof the carried fire lay under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, giving back four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for a hundred and thirteen days. The boy did not ask who had paid, because every warmth in this house had a payer and none of them stood in the room to be thanked.

She did not touch \lore{the widow's mark} at the well or at the shelf or afterward, because a well is not an opening and a shelf is not an opening and a row of loaves is not an opening, and she had long ago stopped needing to be told how a house keeps what it keeps. The mark hung where it hung under her coat with the Vail kiln scratched into it and went unread by every hand in the yard. The pot stood where it had been set. The broom went on around the corner. Her lips moved along the loaf row and came back to the same bare place twice, and the boy's small hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the worked strips and came down, while one hundred and thirteen stood where nobody had put a figure to it and the seed stood up in the last tend with the hands counted in glances and handfuls and never in a figure.
