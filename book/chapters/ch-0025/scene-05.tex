\daymark{115}{Seed 26, Y.H. 813}

Dawn came up \place{the Ilm} without lifting the cold off the water. Under \place{the north bridge} \place{the fish-trade shade} held the dark a moment longer than the rest of the town, and the boards of the outer stalls kept a wet shine that was not yet sun and not yet frost but the two things standing in one another's place. \person{Sella Vail} carried \person[Corin]{the boy} down and set him at the very end of the shade, where no slate had ever been laid and where the running water went past close enough to be heard but not close enough to reach a basket. The instruction went to the wool. “Keep it off his ears. It's the river wind.” It went into the wool and not into the basket and not into the boy, as it had gone every morning of his life, and she kept her half-pace from every open slate and from the soft-cuffed man set at the landing, the little-door burn closing first on the handle and then on nothing. Her skirt came up wet against the boards. She did not look into the basket.

The carry down had taken the long way around every open slate, a basket held level the way a man carries a bowl filled to the lip and will not let it slop, and the boy had ridden it with his head going where it wanted and making the small wet sound when it went too far. Nobody under the bridge had been given an instruction about that sound, and nobody wiped the thread of drool that had frozen at his mouth in the small hours; a basket set down is a pot set down and left. Cold came off the water in its known order, cheek, temple, wrist, the wool riding back from the bare joint so the cold found the place it had come for. From the height of the basket the eel boards stood higher than a man's table and the whole trade went on over the top of him, and he was a trauma surgeon who could not close two inches on a fold and had not the standing to ask for the number of his own days. Both things were true and the second was louder, as it had been every morning since the stone at his belly went cold.

The \place{Fishstairs} came down to the water in four steps, four, and a landing where a step should have been. The Breaking had put that landing a hand's breadth out of line and the district had gone on using it, walking over it carefully the way a man walks over a board he knows is weak without ever calling it a fall. The soft-cuffed clerk sat at the landing with his cuff gone grey from washing and his slate held to his forearm for a measure nobody had called, his hand going to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls. Nobody had taken him off. The market sold its eels over the top of him as though a man with a slate were a thing a landing always had, and the boy, riding level under the fold, kept the hand-to-slate going before the eyes-on-stalls on one place and the out-of-line landing on another and laid no finger across either. A measure set down before anybody called it was a measure set down ahead of a death; he held that as a shape and did not make a rule of it.

\person{Mira Silt} worked the eels at the outer boards. Pale slime had dried in long grey strokes on her sleeves and river silt had dried pale in the hollow of her neck, and she held her head a little to the right where the top of her left ear had been, the way a woman holds a sore place out of a draught without seeming to. She smelled of river silt, salted perch and the smoke of tallow, and the smell came down the shade ahead of her the way woodsmoke comes ahead of a kiln. The small smile stayed on her face the way a door stays on the latch: it could be shut without anybody noticing the movement. People noticed the smile first and the missing ear last, and she had arranged it so. She worked fast, with the eel knife going where it was wanted before the eye arrived, and she began every saying in the middle, at the part where somebody was already in trouble, never at the beginning where a listener might find a handle. She did not look into the basket. Her not-looking was a keeping set on a place of its own and joined to no pricing, and the boy had no word in \lore{Grathic} for a keeping and a pricing that wore the same face, so he kept them apart the way she kept her two cords apart, with nothing written between. He had watched her a dozen dawn markets and had never once seen her sit down at a table where a slate lay open.

A weir woman come down from \place{the Weir} with salt dried white at her wrists stood over the boards and asked whether the run would be thin now that the planting had gone into its last tend. Salt stood in the creases of her wrists where the water had taken the skin, and her sleeves were rolled to the elbow against the cold in a way that said she had come to trade and not to stand about. Mira moved eel-bones against her thumb while she decided whether to lie, one bone and then another going back and forth over the nail, and put one away when the deciding was done. “Thin?” she said. “Who's to say thin? It comes when it comes. Ask the river.” She answered the question and then her own answer, which left her room to choose which of the two the woman carried away, and the woman went away with the one that cost nothing. When the woman had gone Mira said something low about the boat that came up on the flood and nothing about the number of hands in the strips, and the boy, who could not have said a word in this country if his life had depended on it, understood the grammar of it perfectly: her first answer was a door left unlatched and her second was the room behind it, and she had let the woman choose the empty one. Behind her the river went over water with the small steady note the boy had kept for a fifth thing on a place of its own, not a hearing and not the note of any vessel, going nowhere and coming back the same.

A buyer in a coat with the river in its hem named a figure for two pair. The hand went to the record before it went to the person, as it had gone in a midwife, a neighbour at a well, a clerk behind a Court table, a man who priced him, \person[Berr Clay]{Berr} wiping his thumb twice and passing a finger over a figure, Jorra speaking to the wheel before the pots, Sella giving the instruction to the wool before she gave it to anybody, and Alda laying her birth-slates on the bench before she turned to the fold. Mira's thumb went under her apron to the grey wool cord and stayed there longer than the figure required, and when she shifted a creel to set a pair down the second cord showed twisted twice beneath the grey. It had never been carried upriver to \place{Nine Pots} and had never been lifted at a door. The first cord was touched when a figure was spoken; the second was shown only when a creel shifted, never carried to the house it belonged to and never offered to a clerk. Neither knot was counted by any thumb but her own, and the knot for the thin child upriver was not touched. A cord kept out of love gathers the same interest as any other debt, and this one went on gathering it on a board no Court thumb had ever looked at. Her board lay beside \person{Orra Weir}'s knot-string and did not touch it; Orra was upriver and present only as the board, the cord lying where a cord lies when a woman has gone away from it and left it where it was, and the boy kept the two boards from touching as carefully as Sella kept a half-pace from an open slate. The boy set the thumb-to-cord, the second twist and the two boards that did not touch on three places with room between, and wrote no cause across the space. Two women keeping two records in wool and knot on boards that did not touch were two faces turned the same way that meant opposite things. The word stayed unspoken with the readable double turn beside it and the weight of a thumb on wicker apart from both, and neither of the things beside it was counted. He had learned long ago in a better-lit room what a likeness laid on a likeness will do if no hand holds them apart.

\scenebreak

By mid-morning the shade had lost its dark and the eels went on being sold in the plain light, which is a poorer light for keeping things in. Sella lifted the basket and carried him up toward \place{the Cross} at the half-pace, going around the out-of-line landing as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and set him where the moot-post's thin shade fell and no slate lay open on the boards. The Cross kept its four ways out and its Court table at the \place{Slate Row} fringe, and people brought figures to a heap of turnips and left them standing side by side without making them agree. Eight pigs had gone over the wet reading block on the last Account-day and a clerk had written them down before anybody could ask which eight he meant, and today the eels went over the same boards under a sky the colour of a pot that has stood covered since morning. That was the whole of the market's arithmetic: a figure for eels, a figure for pigs, a figure for hands in a strip that had gone from a fist to a mouth to a shoulder and arrived nowhere, a figure read from a slate at a date entered everywhere while a figure let fall from a fist into brown ground was entered nowhere. The boy had watched it a hundred and fifteen times without being told once what a life counted, and had stopped expecting to be told, which was not the same thing as not wanting it.

At the Slate Row fringe a man whose name was on no roll was glimpsed again handing a figure to a slate with his soft hands. They were the soft hands of a man who sits at a table and the face was the patient face of a man who can wait in a doorway. He held the slate at the edge where nobody else could see the figure, and afterward he rested his thumb on the corner of it without passing the thumb over the number as Berr would have done, wiping twice and then touching, taking himself out of his own sentence while the figure went in. Here there was no wiped thumb and no sentence to take himself out of, only a hand laid on the stone where a number might have been and kept there a breath too long. The boy filed the soft hands apart from the pause in the neat dark hand, from \lore[the Hearth Drawing]{the Drawing} collector's grey fingertips, from the Court-table clerk who put hand to slate before eye to the woman waiting, from the soft-cuffed Fishstairs clerk still set at the out-of-line landing for a measure nobody called, and from the watcher who had turned late at the market's edge and had not turned today. The Drawing collector had grey fingertips and a reason and no pause in his hand; the Court clerk's upper line was in a strange neat hand with a small stop between two clusters; the Fishstairs clerk washed his own cuff, and a man who washes his own cuff has a hunger or a habit, as a man who holds a slate at the edge where nobody else can see the figure has a use. Five hands on five trays, and the soft hands a sixth shape kept off every tray and entered nowhere. No sixth sighting of the neat dark hand came. No loaf passed. No secret changed hands that any slate could read, and nothing was sold. The watcher was offstage and present only as a shape already filed, and the boy did not go looking for him.

A figure set down ahead of somebody's death is a shape until the death comes and then it is a fact no living person can take off, the way a date laid over a sign in a woman's face is exact and not true and cannot be corrected. He held the shape and did not make the rule, because the hand that would draw the line through the two was the clean hand at the foot of a cause, and the clean hand was the one object in this world he had spent two lives declining to become. The surgeon's register went over the soft hands the way it went over a chart closed with a neat line above a note not opened: exact, complete, worth nothing, and no standing to lift the line. His own hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water coming over water behind him and came down. Below the landing Mira's second cord stayed twisted twice beneath the grey with neither knot counted, and the soft-handed man's thumb stayed on the corner of the slate held at the edge where nobody else could see the figure, and the market went on selling eels over the top of a clerk set there for a measure nobody had called.
