\daymark{155}{Tallow 6, Y.H. 813 (Sixthday)}

The last of the reed work came downriver in the morning as a direction and not a bundle. There were no more grey ranks of cut reed standing in the beds behind the yard; there was only the going down and the coming back, and the going down lived in the arm that carried it before it lived in a cart or on a slate. \person{Sella Vail} carried \person[Corin]{the boy} along the river road at the half-pace she kept from every open slate, the little-door burn closing on the wicker handle and opening again, and a woman come up from the beds with empty hands said to nobody in particular that the hands would want paying now the reeds were out of them. She was answered with the going down and the coming back, and with the river, and with the weather, and went on up the road with the question still in her mouth and no entry anywhere. A cost asked as a direction is answered with a direction. It leaves no figure and no payer and no place to put either. He lay at the angle at which the basket's rim becomes a wall and heard the note of water going over water under the bank, a fifth low thing never counted among the hearings. An arm that has carried a load all morning knows the weight of what it carried and knows nothing of what it cost; the muscle keeps the one and has no nerve that can enter the other. He was one hundred and fifty-five days old and five months and five days, and nobody on the river road said so, and nobody on the river road would have known what to do with the saying of it if a man had put the words into their mouths. The house kept its days in loaves pushed along a board and the yard kept them in the weight an arm remembers. His count had neither rim nor handle and went on standing where it stood.

At \place{the Weir} she set the basket at the edge of the work where no slate lay, going around a slate propped against the bank with the basket tilted at the half-pace, and gave the instruction to the wool and to the running water before she gave it to any person, and never gave it to the basket. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. “It's the river wind.” The river wind went along the \place{the plank walk} on its posts and came up between the boards where the traps stood with their mouths upriver, and salt stood white on the posts where the flood had touched them, and the whole place smelled of cold fish and river silt and tallow smoke. Below \place[the Singing Sill]{the sill} the water said its low note over the stones and was called weather by everybody who had cause to pass it and by nobody who had cause to look at it. She stood a half-pace from the nearest open slate and did not look into the basket.

\scenebreak

The reed-cutter came along the boards with the last of the season on his shoulder and the short length of wool or reed-fibre cord looped inside his coat, gone grey with the oil of a working thumb. He had no bundle to set down, for the work had finished and become a direction, but on \place[the blessing stone]{the flat blessing stone} he straightened the cord along his knee and turned it once so that the knots stood. They stood in a row that no eye but his had cause to read. He tied one low with his teeth and a pull of the thumb, bit the end off, and put the cord back inside his coat with the flat of the hand, neither folded nor shown. A weir woman at the rails asked what it held.

“You can ask the cord,” he said.

“And who'll answer me?” she said.

“No more can the Court,” he said, without anger and without a smile, and went up the boards to lift a trap. His hand had gone to the cord before it went to the trap and before it went to the woman; he had not counted a knot where anyone could hear him, and no figure came out of the cord into the morning. The boy could not have said how many knots there were. He had seen the straightening and the turn and the tie and the putting away, and the order of them stood as a shape and not as a number, a tally carried in wool and teeth and thumb that told what had been gathered and not what the gathering had been paid. Three times in a row such a hand would have made a grammar, and a grammar wants an account at its foot; he kept this one on the stone where it had happened and did not carry it up the boards after the man.

Below the stone \person{Orra Weir} stood at \place{the lower boards} with her board on its side and a grey cord at her waist, turning the board with the wrist that had turned it ten thousand times. Her hands were like old rope; the pale eel scars lay uncovered across both forearms, salt was dried white at her wrists, and the grey streak showed first through the black hair when she moved her head. When she was tired the nasal half-song of \place[the Fenmarch]{the Fen} came into her voice, flat and low, and it had come into it this morning as it came into it every morning now, without any words that would have made a song of it. She pulled one knot down with her thumb and set it among the others with a small flat knock --- the knock of a thing laid where it belongs, not the small flat knock of a vessel cracked all the way through, and the boy set the two sounds on places that did not touch.

A woman at the rails, with the river in her hem and salt at her wrists older than the day's wetting, asked what the levy would take now the hands were out of the reeds.

“The ninth of Ilmen,” Orra said. “The collector's boat came round the bend on the flood and had six oars. One man stood at the stern and never touched them.”

“That isn't the levy,” the woman said.

“It's a date. The river takes what the Court doesn't. You can shout at either one. I've tried.” She said it slow, and the half-song went under the words as it went under everything she said when she was tired, and then she went back to the knots.

A date laid where a figure had been asked is an answer with the question taken out of it, the way a chart is closed over its finding and the neat margin left showing. The boy set the ninth of Ilmen and the boat with its six oars on the place where she had laid them, and the man standing at the stern with his hands off the oars on a place of his own beside them, and wrote no cause across either. Orra's thumb went to the cord again as the sentence ended, and he could not tell whether that was a lie or only the habit of counting, and made no cause of that either. The last knot on the cord kept its double turn, tail tucked, tied left over right with the small extra weight of a thumb pressed in; it was legible and complete and wanted nothing from him. The word that would have named it stayed where it had stayed since the year of the flower. The cord went back upriver to \place{the Eel House}, where no clerk could spell it.

\scenebreak

Below the sill, at the outer boards of \place{the fish-trade shade}, \person{Mira Silt} worked the eels with pale slime dried in long grey strokes on her sleeves and silt dried pale in the hollow of her neck. She held her head a little to the right where the top of her left ear had been, and kept her small smile on her face the way another woman keeps a door on the latch: it could be shut without anybody seeing the movement. A weir woman come down the boards with a pail asked whether the run would be thin now the last of the reeds were out of the water.

“Thin?” Mira said. “Who's to say thin? It comes when it comes. Ask the river.” She flicked an eel-bone against her thumb while she decided whether to lie, moved two from hand to hand, and put one away when the deciding was done. “I'll tell you what I know and then it'll be yours to carry. Don't say I gave it you.”

A buyer in a coat with the river in its hem named a figure for two pair. Mira did not look up from the eels. “Two marks the pair and two short. You've said it twice and I've counted it twice. There's nothing in the sentence that wants an answer.” Her thumb went under her apron to the grey cord and stayed there longer than the figure required; shifting a creel showed the second cord twisted twice beneath it, never carried upriver to \place{Nine Pots} and never lifted at a door, neither knot counted by any thumb but her own and the knot for the thin child upriver untouched. Her board lay beside Orra's and did not touch it. Two women keeping two records within a dozen paces of the basket, and a third length of grey wool gone back inside a coat up the boards, and not one of the three written where a Court thumb could find it.

She did not look into the basket. It was a keeping and not a pricing; she had stood on the other side of a market once and priced a child with her eyes turned the same way, and the boy had set the two not-lookings on places that did not touch and had no word in \lore{Grathic} for either. A keeping done briskly is a keeping nobody can make a speech about, and this one was done with an eel in her hand and her face toward the water.

Below the shade a woman wound a child's foot in three turns of grey wool, sitting on her heels with the foot across her knee, working the wool downward and smooth. The foot stayed dark inside the turns, and the darkness had gone up past the ankle while the woman worked. She called it the cold. A neighbour with a pail said it was weather, and the two words went past each other in the space above the child's knee and never met, and the woman went on winding and the neighbour went on with her pail.

The boy knew what the woman could not see and could not have told her if he had had a voice. The extremity goes before the core; the small joint gives up its warmth first and the heart holds out longest, so that a foot can be past saving while the child is still speaking. He knew the afterdrop waiting on the far side of a warming nobody in this shade would perform, the core giving up its heat to the limbs and the whole body going colder at the moment it is put by the fire. He knew that a cold patient cannot clot, that you do not rub a frozen foot, that you do not set it to the fire, and that the grimace severe cold puts on a face is a smile and is not peace. He had seen the almost-smile on a man's face once under different lights and had learned what it was worth. All of it sat in the basket exact and complete and worth nothing, because there were two paces of air between his hand and the foot and no instrument within reach that was not wool. Competence without a hand is a humiliation with no witness. The woman finished the third turn and tucked the end in, and somebody said the wind had come off the water again, and the river went on saying its low note over the stones.

\scenebreak

At \place{the out-of-line landing} below \place{the north bridge} the soft-cuffed clerk stayed set for a measure nobody had called. His cuff was grey from washing and grey again by midday; he held his slate to his forearm with the writing turned inward, and his hand went to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls. He was not at \place{the Cross} and not at a counting room and not at a death-house door; he was a man sitting where a man had set himself, and the market sold its eels and its reeds over the top of him as water goes over a stone that lies where a stone lies. A girl with a creel took the landing sideways, one hand out to the rail, stepping across the place where a step should have been, and went on selling without looking at her feet, and nobody made a fall of it. Nobody stepped around the clerk and nobody took him off, and no figure went into his slate that any of the goers-by could have read while they passed.

The boy filed the order of reaching on a place of its own: the hand to the record before the eye to the person, the man set down ahead of the measure, a shape that had arrived in the district before anything had happened for it to measure. He did not set it beside the reed-cutter's cord or Orra's knot or Mira's second twist, and he did not set it beside the charcoal under the shelf or the hand beneath the board. Five likenesses in a row would have stood up and called themselves a rule, and a rule wants a payer at its foot. He kept his finger between them.

\scenebreak

The afternoon went off the water and the going of the light came up the river road behind her. The instruction went to the wool and to the running water and never to the basket; she said the house where another woman would have said I, went at the half-pace from every open slate, and went around a pot somebody had set out to dry where no slate lay. The last bundles had gone down in the morning and the coming back was in her arm now, measured in the shoulder and the wrist and entered nowhere. Up the boards the reed-cutter's small grey keeping had gone back inside his coat with its knots unread, and the trap he lifted went up after it, and the work was done with no boundary drawn around the doing of it. Under the \place{the Vail kiln-house} roof she set him on \place{the bed} and not in \place{the Debt Loft}, put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without once looking at his face, left the thread of drool frozen and unwiped because nobody under any roof had been given an instruction about the thread, and let her hand rest the half-beat longer than the setting needed before it went away. She told the fire it was \lore{the thaw}, turned the coals with the back of a broken pot, said \emph{The house will keep it}, counted under her breath toward no opening, set the pot rim out with its handle two inches beyond the reaching hand, and did not look toward the bed. \lore[the widow's mark]{The widow's mark} hung where it hung, because a dusk is not an opening.

Through the finger's-width door came the river's note of water over water, a fifth low thing never counted among the hearings; through the floor the carried fire gave four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for a hundred and fifty-five days; overhead the boards kept four nails, four, and a fifth that was a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts in three places and none became one. One hundred and fifty-five stood exact, complete and unspent, and spoken by nobody under any roof. The stiff brown-edged crust lay two inches from his ear with its empty place unnamed and no third; the shelf's two gaps and the pan's gone place stayed each on a tray of its own; the charcoal waited under the board for a hand that was not his and a day that had not come. Want came up small and importunate for the crust and for the number spoken by somebody who would have known what a number was, and got neither. The adult count and the child's body argued as they had argued every day of his life, the one dry and exact and given to keeping, the other immediate and cold and impatient for the warmth that never came within two paces of the reaching hand; both were true and the second was louder, and neither had a hand to enter anything. The reed-cutter's cord went back inside his coat with its knots unread while the river's note of water over water came up through the finger's-width door, and the boy's hand went up an inch toward it and came down against the fold.
