\daymark{158}{Tallow 9, Y.H. 813 (Ninthday)}

The firing month had got into \place{the Clay Yard} by its second working week and had made itself at home there. \person[Corin]{The boy} came down in the basket through a morning that smelled of hot clay under tallow and of the sweetish edge of \lore{lead glaze} on a wind which had crossed \place{High Kiln} before it came over \place{Low Kiln}. Under those smells, and under the scrape of brooms and the small flat knocks of pots set down, the kiln breathed in fours with a gap, and the gap was a silence the yard had long ago stopped listening for. \person{Sella Vail} carried him at the half-pace from every open slate and gave the instruction to the wool, the fire, the pot and the broom before she gave it to anybody, and never to the basket. He was one hundred and fifty-eight days old, five months and eight days, and nobody under any roof said so.

Word went around the yard in the manner word goes around a yard, passing from one woman's mouth to the next woman's ear while both kept their hands in the work. The morrow was the first Account-day of \place{Tallow}. There would be a market at \place{the Cross}, the slate would be read twice, once for the moot and once for the households that came late, and eight pigs would go over the weighing board with nobody asking which eight were meant. A woman at the trough said it as she might have said that the trough stood bare and dark and was called weather; a woman at \place{the drying racks} said it as she might have said that the space beneath the lowest rack held broken birth-slates which nobody entered. Neither spoke of the first Account-day as a thing that wanted anything of her. The yard went around the clean dark line where the \lore{Ash} ridge had turned and the new finer hairline of grey beside it which was called \lore{the thaw}, and went wide around the empty place in the returning line where a hand had gone in on Seed 15 and had not come out, with a look and a wide step and no number.

\person{Berr Clay} was spoken of that morning as a direction and not as a place anybody meant to go that day. On the morrow he would come up \place{the clay road} with the red wool cord of his office folded square under his coat, and the cord meant the road, and the road meant High Kiln, the way \place{Slate Row} meant a quarter league east and the Cross meant the square where pigs were weighed. Nobody in the yard said they meant to go up it. A direction is a thing a person can name without putting a foot on it. He had \emph{one in twelve, as the notice says} ready in his mouth already, the printed figure which stood unchallenged until the reading, and a narrow slate under his arm that would be held unopened to his forearm when the time came, and a box locked in his house up the road with the true subtraction inside it. None of that was in the yard's telling. The telling was only of the road, the cord and the morrow, the way the reeds had been a going-downriver and a coming-back at dusk, and the planting had been hands counted in glances and handfuls and never in a figure.

A man came up from the road below \place[the yard well]{the well} with a number in his fist. He had bent his fingers over it the way a man bends over a coal he has lifted out of the fire, not to keep it warm but to keep it from being seen, and he asked what the firing would cost set down there now that the kilns were up and the month had hands in it. He asked it of no one in particular, which in a yard is a way of asking everybody and being answerable to nobody. The number went from his fist to his mouth, where it became a sentence, and from his mouth to the shoulder of a man going up toward High Kiln, where it became a commission, and from that shoulder it went up the road ahead of the man and arrived nowhere. Nobody challenged the one in twelve. It stood where it was printed, a hundred and fifty leagues east, and it would stand until somebody read it at the block under the moot-post on the morrow and let it fall low into the goers-by. The boy watched the fist unclose and the number go up the road, and the road took it as water takes a thing thrown in and gives back no shape.

\scenebreak

She came through the yard from the direction of the Cross with a narrow slate in her hand. It was not a slate propped at the rack end to dry and not one of the birth-slates under the lowest rack; it was a runner's slate, carried as a runner carries a thing that is not hers and is answerable to somebody else. \person{Hessa Rale} of Slate Row had walked the road between the Cross and \place{Greybank} often enough to have stopped measuring it, and she came at the pace of a person who has made a bargain with distance and keeps it without thinking. Her coat was shortened at the skirt for the road's mud, and the mud had dried on it in a pale scurf which she had not brushed off, because a runner's coat is a tool and nobody scours a tool for its own sake. Her hands were the hands of a woman who carries and does not open. The fingers closed on the slate's edge with a working looseness, the thumb lay along the grain of the frame, and nothing pressed where the writing was. She held the slate edge-on to her own forearm so that the face of it turned toward her sleeve and the writing looked at nobody. A strap crossed her chest, and a second shorter strap had been let out to take the weight when the road was long.

Her hand went to the slate before her eyes went to the people in the yard. The small flat correction came first, a settler of the strap and a turn of the wrist to bring the edge round, and only then did her face take in the women at the trough, the racks, the kiln mouth, the basket at the half-pace against the wall. A woman nearest her said the morrow was the Account-day and asked what the levy would take now that the kilns had been brought up and hands were in the month.

“I don't read it,” Hessa said. “I carry it. There's a difference, and the road doesn't care which.”

The woman wanted more than that and said so with her hands on her hips.

“Cross to Greybank,” Hessa said. “Reading goes up before noon. I've got a road to walk.” And she set the edge of the slate back against her forearm and went on through the yard, leaving the question standing where it had been set down, the way a man leaves a pot where there is no hand to carry it.

The boy watched her go and filed the order of reaching as an eighth such hand-shape in the district, and put it on a place of its own. He had learned that grammar before he had learned the order of words: the hand to the record before the hand to the person. It was not \person{Marra}'s pause between strokes at the edge of a flare light, not the neat dark hand that set a name down before anybody looked at a face, not the clerk at the Court's table, not the soft cuff set at the out-of-line landing for a measure nobody called, not the soft hands at the fringe of Slate Row, not Berr's twice-wiped thumb, not the finger that went beneath the loaf shelf in the other house without a word and without looking. Eight hands in one district performing one grammar and not one of them explaining it, each kept on its own place with nothing laid across anything else. He did not let the number of them trouble him into a rule. A rule wants a payer, and no payer had been named to any of them.

She was a runner and not a reader of anyone's private keeping. No figure came off her slate in the yard. She did not open it and nobody asked her to open it, and the edge-on was not a hiding of something from herself but the manner of carrying a slate so the writing is not rubbed and no goer-by takes a figure off it without a hand to put there. The boy set that distinction beside the knowing withheld from a kiln-master and beside the hand under the board, and kept all three apart. Beyond the racks her coat went down the clay road in the direction of Greybank with the writing turned to her sleeve, and the figure on it remained what figures on carried slates remained in this district: a thing somebody else had set down and nobody here had read.

\scenebreak

Under \place{the Vail kiln-house} roof in the afternoon the house went on in its own grammar, as a house will when a month is gathering interest and there is no instruction about any of it. \person{Tarn} took the broom only after it had been given its instruction and said \emph{Yes, house} to a woman who wanted the thaw early and to a woman who wanted it late, agreeing so thoroughly with both that no argument could find a foot, while going on wanting the late because a late thaw puts off the paying. He had the small knife at the very edge of its sill with six counted in the worn thumb-mark where the house could check and the seventh where it could not, the one entry in this house which the house had never checked. \person{Garl Vail}'s unforged collector sentence lay beside it. The breath from Seed 16 was still unspent, standing as a small interval at the top of each reach where it should have gone in and had not; the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was under the roof and gave it back. His seventh year stood nearer its end than it had ever stood and had not given. No tenth stopping stood up out of the nine to make a bill of them, and no collector came up the road, and the loan that could hear was not called.

\person{Jorra Vail} came through from \place{the Vail wheel-shed} with the day's fine grey slate dust ground small as meal into both forearms past the bent little finger and under the nails, not bone-coloured slip, keeping dusty forearms and clean ones on one tray. He said a word to the wheel and another to a pot and asked neither of them which slate had been broken or whether it had been a birth-slate, and no man came up on the wrong wind today to say that a pot was singing before its time, so the saying was not repeated under the roof. Before \person{Sella Vail} had finished telling him the fire was low his bent finger went into the cold iron of \lore{the damper}, moved the tongue a hair's breadth and left it standing, and the household straightened around his silence the way cloth straightens when a weight is lifted. He did not look toward the basket. Tarn did not ask about the room and Jorra did not ask about the ear, and the two silences stayed where they lay under one roof without a word put between them. The boy knew the order in which a body takes heat back and the order in which a vessel gives it up, and that one piece of iron had two bites in it, the holding and the taking, with no Grathic word for both mercies; and the knowing bought him nothing with the heat two paces off and his hand where it was.

At \place{the loaf shelf} Sella moved her lips along a row that stood with two gaps and gave nobody a figure, and glanced once at the clay jar that stood cold under its glaze against the wall, and did not touch it and did not go back to it. The charcoal lay under the front edge where it lay and waited unread. No third gap came. In \place{the bed} one stiff brown-edged crust lay two inches from his ear with the empty place beside it still unnamed and no third, and the two rooms did not touch. Through the finger's-width door came the river's note of water over water, a fifth low thing never counted among the hearings; through the floor the carried fire gave four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been; overhead the boards kept four nails, four, and a knot the width of a thumb. Three counts in three places and none of them became one. The adult who had once stood with a towel in his hands in a room where heat was kept behind a screen watched the numbers keep their separate places; the child's body was carried under a board and was cold and wanted the crust and the warmth that never came within two paces of the reaching hand. Both were true and the second was louder.

At the going of the light she carried him up the clay road at the half-pace, woodsmoke going before her and the river wind coming up to meet it, the two passing on the stair as they had passed every evening of his life. She went around the place on the wall where the pan had stood out to dry through Reed and was not standing now, around the bare dark trough at the well and wide around the space beneath the lowest rack, and gave no explanation of any of the turns to the basket. She set him on the bed and not in \place{the Debt Loft}, base first and weight settled, put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without once looking at his face, left the thread of drool frozen and unwiped because nobody under any roof had been given an instruction about the thread, and rested her hand a half-beat longer than the setting needed before it went away. At \place{the hearth} she told the fire it was the thaw, turned the coals with the back of a broken pot and said, “The house will keep it,” where another woman would have said \emph{I}. She stood a moment counting under her breath toward no opening, pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the little-door burn, the grey streak pale off the coals, set the pot rim out with its handle standing two inches beyond the reaching hand and did not look toward the bed. She did not touch \lore{the widow's mark}, because a dusk is not an opening and a hearth is not a door, and the hand goes to the mark before the kiln and at no other hour. She went to her own bed without explaining the count.

The unknown number had gone from a man's fist up the clay road and had arrived nowhere; ahead of it a narrow slate had gone edge-on up the road toward Greybank with the writing turned to a runner's sleeve and no figure read off it under any roof in \place{Sill}. The pot handle stood two inches beyond the reaching hand. The fire gave four low pulls and the silence where the fifth should have been, its payer unnamed for a hundred and fifty-eight days. In the dark one hundred and fifty-eight stood exact, complete and unspent, and the boy's hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note of water over water through the finger's-width door and came down against the fold.
