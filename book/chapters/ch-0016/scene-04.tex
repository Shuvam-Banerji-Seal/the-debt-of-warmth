Three doors down \place{the Clay Yard} the door stood a finger's width open, as it had stood every day since the woman went into the water, and \person{Sella Vail} carried the basket there in the afternoon with the half-pace kept up from every place a slate might lie. She turned it with her knee and set it just inside the door at the edge of the light, where the falling slant of the afternoon reached the wicker and stopped, and where no writing had been left open on bench or sill. She had opened no slate on the walk down and turned no errand into a walk to \place{Slate Row}. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool, and she said the house where another woman would have said I, and went and stood a half-pace off with the little-door burn shut on nothing. \person{Corin}, seventy-six days old, lay where the light ended and took the room in from the height of a basket, which is the height at which shelves are highest and a bed is a country to be crossed.

The room had not changed in the three years since he had been carried out of it. The bench stood by the door and the pot stood on the bench, and the draught went under the pot along the floor to the corner and was felt at the ankle before it was felt at the face, which was the order of cold in every room of this district and in one room of the Court that he had never seen. A grey ridge of \lore{Ash} ran along the skirting board from the bench, under the door and into the next room, left there for \lore{the Last Day}. There was a brown stain on the ceiling the shape of a drowned leaf, and a chipped cup on a shelf with an empty place beside it where another cup had stood, and a stool by the wall. He set this room beside the birth room he had been carried out of, and the kiln-house, and the death-house two doors the other way, and the room whose voice had come through a doorway at \lore{the Weighing}, and the Eel House at \place{the Weir}. Six rooms, each with its bench and pot and draught and the place in the count where a line should stand and did not. He kept the six in six places and wrote no cause across them, because a likeness laid on a likeness will grow a cause whether or not a hand put one there.

Above the board-bed the round pewter tin kept its place. From the basket the boy saw the pale fold at the seam of the lid and the thumb-bright rub worn into the pewter where a thumb had gone often enough to polish it, and the lid lay where it had lain, unopened. The tin held a scrap of cloth from the birth, and the cloth had been folded once and kept, and the keeping had worn a bright place on the lid that no officer would think to read. Below it, on the board-bed, the fold of grey wool lay where it had lain, and the hollow in it was the shape of the small pale river stone that had once lain there, and neither the house nor the boy said the stone was gone. A stone warmed at another hearth across the yard, carried to this room in an apron, left in a fold when the child was taken, and now a hollow in wool with nobody to say what had been lifted out of it. He had no word in \lore{Grathic} for a subtraction nobody had entered and nobody would miss, and the want of the word did not make the hollow less exact.

In the next room a neighbour scrubbed at something on the floor with a wet cloth, the sound of the cloth going down and coming up regular as a measure, and lye and woodsmoke came through the door ahead of the woman's voice. She did not come in. The smell of lye went under the soap in the room and stayed, and the boy took it in before he took anything else, because that was the order of the world from the height of a basket.

Then \person{Alda Reed} came short through the door with soap-and-milk gone cold under river silt and lye sour under the soap, the birth-slates under her arm. She went to the bench first. She laid the birth-slates writing-down, the writing turned under toward the bench and not toward the room, before she turned to the fold on the bed. That was the order of her reaching, and the boy had watched it in four rooms and knew it the way he knew the order of cold: the record before the person, always, even in a room where the person was a child who could not answer. Her nails were scrubbed to the quick and still had clay under them. She smelled of soap and warm milk that had been warm somewhere else and for somebody else, and of the mineral tang she promised herself she would sweep tomorrow.

She stood over the fold and pressed it flat at his shoulder with two fingers, and pressed it too long. A fold does not need that much pressing. The body under it was warm and breathing, which was true, and the thing she had set down about it three years ago was exact, and the two truths were not the same truth and never had been. She crossed two fingers at the red edge of the \lore{birth-flower} where the wool had ridden back at his wrist, and made the sign at the corner of it before she knew she had made it, in front of a basket and a woman who stood a half-pace off and asked nothing. Then she covered the sign with a date nobody had asked for: the seventh of Stone, the account up on the eighth, before the paper went out. It was the same date she had given the woman who used to lie in the next room thirty-eight mornings ago, and it had stood over a death ever since, and no living person could take it off.

To Sella's “he lies quiet” she said, “Warm and breathing,” and then, after a breath, “that's all I've set down,” which was exact and not true and had been exact and not true for three years. She did not say the woman's name and did not say the third day. When there was no question left to stop her she went on with dates the way another woman might go on with a story: a cooper's seventh in \place{Mud End}, a potter's third child in \place{Stonemouth}, the last high water. Dates were a handle on a thing too large to carry, and she carried it by the handle and set it down gently and did not look at what she had set down.

Her hand went once inside her bodice to the little tin on its cord and touched it and came away without opening it. The boy watched the hand go and come back, and set the gesture on the place where he kept the order of reaching, beside the woman in the grey coat writing at the flare, the man who had priced him, the clerk behind the Court's table, the soft-cuffed clerk at \place{the Fishstairs}, and \person[Mira Silt]{Mira}'s thumb under her apron. Six record-first hands, and he did not make a seventh of this one, because a likeness is not a person and the order of a room is the commonest thing in it.

Then she took up a narrow slate and wrote on it in the second hand what she did not leave lying about, and turned it face-down into her grey shawl toward a floorboard in a house the boy could not name. It was the sixth page and it lay where the other five lay, turned down toward the boards, written by a hand no Court physician had seen and read by nobody. The hidden record stood at six pages and all six were unread. He did not write a seventh for her. He had learned what a chart looks like when the line drawn under it is too clean, and he had no standing to turn any of these pages over and no hand that could do it, and the not knowing did not make the reading less exact. It only made it worth nothing to anybody, least of all to him.

Two private records stood in one room and did not touch: the round pewter tin above the empty bed with its pale fold and thumb-bright rub, and the turned narrow slate going toward a floorboard in a house he could not enter. One held a scrap of cloth folded once by a woman who had gone into the water with a number still in her thumb; the other held words in a second hand about an infant who had grown worse on a third day. Neither had been carried to a door. Neither would be confiscated, because no officer would think to look under an apron or lift a floorboard for a thing the Court had never entered. A Court's slate can be taken up and read aloud and turned into a figure; wool and pewter and a floorboard cannot, and a keeping in those materials lasts as long as the house does and no longer. He read the two as he would read two lines on a chart turned face down, and could not have said which was which, and there was no legend.

The mercy-lie had curdled through to a woman thirty-eight mornings dead and uncollected, and nobody alive could correct it. He had seen a chart closed with a neat line drawn under it, in a room with better light than this one, and had learned that a beautiful handwriting and a true entry are two different things, and that the second is the one that matters when somebody's life is standing on it. Here the handwriting was a date laid over a sign, and the sign was made by a kind woman who did not know she had made it, and the entry had become a fact in a dead woman's face that no return could alter. He set it on its own tray and did not join it to the tin above the bed or to the hollow in the wool or to the sixth page under the boards. Four objects in one room, and no cause written across any of them, and no word in \lore{Grathic} for the kind of keeping that is done by leaving a thing out.

The neighbour's wet cloth went down and came up in the next room. Alda straightened the chipped cup on the shelf without moving it and did not look into the basket. Sella stood a half-pace off with her burn shut on nothing and asked no question about the writing or the tin, and the arrangement between the two women held without a word passing, the way a door holds that both people have agreed not to open. The boy lay at the edge of the light with one brown-edged crust stiff in the fold and the empty place beside it still unnamed, and the two records stood over him and beside him and went unread.

Under the \place{Vail kiln-house} roof at dusk Sella set him on his bed and not in \place{the Debt Loft}, and put the fold right at his ear without looking at his face. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody wiped it, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left. The carried fire lay under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees and missing one breath in every four. Four, four and a gap. The boards kept four nails and four and a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts did not meet and nobody had thought to enter the gap in either. The door stood a finger's width open and the river's low muttered note came through the gap, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, and he kept it in its own place, apart from the fire's gap and apart from the six hearings and apart from the half-beat and the warm loaf that had come into the yard the day before.

Six hearings of \lore{the full note} stayed six — five from four sources and the sixth out of a person — and no seventh came, and no sixth sighting came of the neat dark hand. Three near-full answers stood advanced and not paid on their own tray; the intervals stayed on another; the word that belonged to the double turn stayed a saying on a place of its own and was not spoken. Four low sounds in four places stayed four, with the river's note a fifth thing and not a hearing. The pewter tin and the turned slate stood where he had left them, three doors down, and he had brought neither home.

Seventy-six. The number sat in him with the weight of a small stone he could neither set down nor lift, and there was nobody under any roof in \place{Sill} to give it to. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the water's note through the gap and came down. The two records stayed unread, the hollow stayed a hollow, the date stayed over the sign, and the count went on.
