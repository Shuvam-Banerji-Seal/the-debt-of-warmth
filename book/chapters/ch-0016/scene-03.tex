\daymark{76}{Silt 17, Y.H. 813}

The steps had come back in the night the way a mouth comes back after a blow, four and four and a landing where a step should have been, and the landing stood a hand's breadth out of line with the rest. On Silt 17 the mud line \place{the Ilm} had left lay along the risers where the water had stood and gone, a brown edge the width of two fingers, and the market went up and down over it the way it had gone up and down before the river came, lifting skirts, setting pots on flat stones, making nothing of the gap. Nobody made a fall of it. A fall wanted a body on the stones and a name for the thing that had put it there, and the district had neither, so the landing stayed where it was and the feet that went over it went carefully and without comment, as feet go over a board they have been told not to trust. \person{Corin}, seventy-six days old, was carried down to \place{the Fishstairs} and into the shade under the north bridge in the basket, level as a pot filled that must not be slopped, with \person{Sella Vail}'s knee turned against the wicker to keep the draught off his ears. She gave every instruction to the wool and the fire and the bucket and never once to the basket. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool, and she said the house where another woman would have said I, and kept the half-pace from every open slate as she had kept it for seventy-six days.

The soft-cuffed clerk sat at the landing with his slate held to his forearm and his cuff gone the colour of river water from washing. He had been set there for a measure nobody had called and nobody had taken him off, and the eels went on being sold over the top of him. His hand went to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls, the order of a man whose record is waiting whether or not anybody comes to claim it. He did not speak; nothing in the morning wanted his voice. A woman came and laid a mark down on the stall in front of him and waited, shifting her weight from one foot to the other, and he did not lift his eyes from the slate, and she took the mark away again and went back to her eels. The measure he kept his place for was not called that morning either. The boy filed the order of reaching on the place where he kept all record-first hands: the woman in the grey coat writing at the flare, the man who had priced him from the block, the clerk behind the Court's table, the Drawing collector with his ordinary hand and no pause in it. He kept the five apart and made no sixth of them, and he did not set the woman in the grey coat down into this landing, because a likeness is not a person and a hand going to a slate first is the commonest hand in \place{Sill}. The neat dark hand with the stop in its stroke had been seen five times and belonged to nobody he could name, and it had not come to the Fishstairs. He had learned in a better-lit room what a row of likenesses laid too close together will do to a man who wants an answer, and he did not lay them close.

Below the bridge \person{Mira Silt} worked the eels with pale slime dried on her sleeves and river silt in the hollow of her neck, her head carried a little to the right against the cold in the place where the top of her left ear had been. The small smile sat on her face the way a door sits on the latch, shut or open without anybody seeing the hand that moved it. She had a knife in one hand and an eel in the other, and neither waited for the market to decide anything. The eel slapped once on the board and lay there with its mouth working, and she turned it without looking. Cold fish and salt and tallow went ahead of her and stayed behind her; the boy took the smell in before he took the woman in, because that was the order of the world from the height of a basket.

A weir woman with salt white at her wrists came down the last step and asked whether the run would be thin now the water had gone down.

“Thin?” said Mira. “Who's to say thin? It comes when it comes. Ask the river.”

She did not look up from the eel. She had answered the question and then her own answer, which left her the choice of which one the woman got to keep, and the woman went back up the steps with an answer that had cost her a question and gained her nothing. While she decided, Mira moved two eel-bones from hand to hand against her thumb, the little string of them clicking like dry seeds in a pod, and when the deciding was done she put one away in her pocket. The eel in her hand was cold and alive and did not care what had been decided over it.

A buyer in a coat that had dried into the shape of a wet man said a figure for a pair, and then said it again a little lower, as though a second saying might make it true.

“Two marks the pair,” the buyer said. “Nobody's asked a pair of eels to carry a number all the way from \place{the Weir}.”

“Two marks the pair and two short,” said Mira, brisk, not looking at him. “You've said it twice and I've counted it twice, and I'll count it again after I've eaten. Don't trouble yourself. There's nothing in the sentence that wants an answer.”

The buyer went away with the pair and the figure he had not won, and Mira went on with her knife. Her thumb went under her apron to the grey wool cord at her waist and stayed there while she decided whether the morning's deciding was done. Shifting a creel to reach another eel showed the second cord twisted twice beneath the grey, and the boy saw the twist the way he saw the small wet sound his head made when it went too far — before he could place it and without any word in \lore{Grathic} for what it was. That second cord had never gone upriver to \place{Nine Pots} and had never been lifted at a door. The knots on neither had been counted by any thumb but her own, and the knot for the thin child upriver was not touched now. Her board stood beside \person{Orra Weir}'s \lore{knot-string} and did not touch it; the shape of the forty-first knot was legible from the basket without its name, and no number was spoken over the eels that morning.

The boy read the two cords as he would read two lines on a chart that had been turned face down: one kept what had been taken from a house, and one kept what had been kept back from a house, and which was which he could not have said, and nobody had written the legend. A Court's slate could be taken up and read aloud and entered in a figure; wool could not be confiscated, because no officer would know to look under an apron for the second twist of a cord, and a knot held its entry without any ink to go wet in the rain. He had spent his life in rooms where the hand went to the record before it went to the person, and here was a woman who kept the most important account in the district in a material no clerk would think to call a record at all. He had no standing to ask her what either cord stood for, and no word in \lore{Grathic} in which to ask it, and the not knowing did not make the reading less exact. It only made it worth nothing to anybody, least of all to him.

Nobody in the market spoke of the thin child upriver, and the knot that stood for that child was not touched, so the keeping went on in wool where no Court's thumb would think to look for it. The boy set the not-speaking beside two others he had kept: Mira's not-looking into the basket, which was a keeping, and \person{Berr Clay}'s taking himself out of his own sentence over the standing water and the figure, which was a different thing again. One omission left a thing out to save it; one left a thing out to hide it; one left the speaker out of his own account so that the account would look cleaner. Three omissions of one shape stood in three places and did not touch. He had no word in \lore{Grathic} for a record kept by leaving something out of it, and the want of the word did not stop him reading the shape of it, and the reading bought him nothing he could spend.

Cold came in its known order — cheek, temple, wrist — and the wool did less than the wind wanted, riding back from his wrist so the cold found the place. He wanted the crust in the fold and the breast and the bucket, and could reach none of the three. One brown-edged crust lay stiff at his ear; the empty place beside it still had no name, and it had crossed the breaking and the flood without acquiring one. The market bell went back toward \place{the Cross}, four, four and a gap, and nobody crossed himself, and he kept the heard bell in the place where he always kept it and did not set it among the hearings.

Sella set the basket in the bridge's shade where no slate lay and turned it with her knee so the light fell where she wanted it. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. She gave the instruction to the wool and never to the basket, said the house where another woman would have said I, bought eels and counted them with her eyes, and did not touch \lore{the widow's mark} at her throat, because a bridge's shade is not an opening and a basket is not a door. She carried him home at dusk without a word about what had been said over the eels. A market's figure was not the house's figure, and a figure spoken over a basket was not a figure paid; she had kept that rule through a flood and would keep it now.

Mira's thumb stayed under her apron on the grey cord with the second twisted twice beneath it, neither knot counted, her board lying beside Orra's without touching, and the boy in the bridge's shade with one crust in the fold at his ear and the empty place beside it still without a name. He had seen a woman keep two accounts in a material no officer would know to read, and he could enter neither of them.

\scenebreak

Under the \place{Vail kiln-house} roof Sella set him on his bed and not in \place{the Debt Loft}, and put the fold right at his ear without looking at his face. The thread at his mouth had frozen in the small hours and nobody wiped it, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left. The carried fire lay under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees and missing one breath in every four. Four, four and a gap. The boards above it kept four nails and four and a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts did not meet and nobody had thought to enter the gap in either. The door stood a finger's width open, and through the gap came the river's low muttered note, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming.

Six hearings of \lore{the full note} stayed six — five from four sources and the sixth out of a person, the small flat knock from the woman the yard had lifted out of the water, beside the five and never among them. No seventh came in the market or under the roof, and no sixth sighting came of the neat dark hand. Three near-full answers stood advanced and not paid on their own tray, neither hearings nor the third thing; the intervals stayed on another; the word that belonged to the double turn stayed a saying on a place of its own and was not spoken. Four low sounds in four places stayed four, with the river's note a fifth thing and not a hearing. He set all of it down where he always set it, on trays that did not touch, and made no cause of any of it, because a likeness laid on a likeness will grow a cause whether or not a hand put one there.

Seventy-six. The number sat in him with the weight of a small stone he could neither set down nor lift, and there was nobody under any roof in Sill to give it to. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the water's note through the gap and came down. The two cords lay side by side on the place where he kept what he could not spend, and the count went on.
