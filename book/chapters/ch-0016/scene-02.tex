The afternoon came on in the mud and did not improve it. Water stood in the shallow pans the slip pits had worn and in the prints of boots, and the yard went about its work with its skirts gathered and its mind on other things, the way a woman steps over a puddle she has already decided not to mention. \person{Corin} was carried back to \place{the yard well} in the basket with the fold at his ear and one brown-edged crust in it and the empty place beside the crust still carrying no name. \person{Sella Vail} had turned the basket with her knee so the light fell where she wanted it, and she kept the half-pace from every open slate as she had kept it for seventy-five days, and said the house where another woman would have said I.

At the well the rope had mud ground into it to the last strand. A kiln-hand with slip dried pale in the creases of his wrists let the bucket down and said to the bucket, “They say the water took three days' baking and a row of pots and a—”

“—and a good deal,” said a voice at his elbow, quick and warm and affectionate. “That's the whole of the account. Everyone knows it. I'm only saying.”

The yard laughed. The kiln-hand laughed too and went on letting the bucket down, and the woman at the rope said something about the mud scouring a body's hands clean and did not finish it either, because the voice at her elbow had finished it for her before she got to the end, in the same warm tone, and they laughed again.

It was a small child in clothes that had belonged to somebody else and had been made to fit by being put on: a coat with the shoulders of a man and the sleeves turned back twice, breeches cut off at the knee with the raw edge left showing, worn like a uniform by a boy who had never been measured for one. There was a gap where a front tooth had been and he grinned through it, and the grin went into every circle of the yard ahead of him and came out with permission to say anything. He smelled of other people's hearths and small ale, of river silt and the warm stone of kitchens where he was always welcome and never at home. On a string at his neck he carried a sliver of green lead-glaze, and his thumb went to it and rubbed it while he decided what a person wanted to hear, which was usually what the person had already said they wanted and a little more. He said what everybody was thinking and said it in a voice that made it harmless, the way a child repeats a hard word in a kitchen before he knows it is a hard word. He never said I want. He said they say, and everyone knows, and your house wouldn't mind, so that a refusal could be given to nobody in particular.

The boy in the basket watched him the way he watched everything he could not reach, with the attention of a man who had once stood at the edge of a crowd and sorted what was useful from what was only noise. He had known people who could say a hard thing first and smile, so that the thing went down easy, and he had learned in a better-lit room what three likenesses laid in a row will do to a man who wants an answer. He set the likeness on a tray of its own and did not write a cause across it. The child was quick; that was all, and quickness was not a key that opened anything.

A woman came to the well-lip with a pot in her hands and set it down. The boy's head went toward it while it was still in her hands, before her knuckle came down on the wet stone, before the note; his head went where the sound would be, as it had gone for seventy-five days, and the small animal sound came in his throat when he went too far and he did not care. The pot gave its full note under the knuckle, clear and whole, and the woman said something to it about not leaving it near the edge and went back to her bucket.

The child's head went toward the pot too. It went a half-beat after the boy's, after the knuckle and after the note, and then it stopped and came back, and his thumb went to the green sliver at his neck and rubbed it, and he grinned through the gap in his teeth. He could not have said why he had turned; there was nothing at the well that wanted his attention, and nobody had called, and the pot had already given and gone quiet. He was as surprised as anybody. He rubbed the sliver once more, put the grin away, and went back to watching the grown faces.

For the boy in the basket the half-beat lay where the intervals lay, on a place of its own with no cause written across it. It was not a hearing: the child had turned after the note, not before it, and a thing that comes after the knock is a different object from a thing that comes before it, even when the heads that turn are the same shape. It was not a seventh hearing, and he did not set it among the six or let it stand in a row with the other likenesses. He kept his own turning and the child's turning side by side on the tray and did not let the two touch. The child had turned after him. That was all it was. It was not nothing, and he left it where it lay.

A man came down from \place{Slate Row} and stood at the edge of the well with his hands loose at his sides, and nobody asked him what he wanted and he did not say. His name was on no roll the boy had ever heard read, and there was no mark on him that any yard would have entered: no cord of office at his wrist, no slate under his arm, no smell of the pits or the fish-trade. He had the soft hands of a man who sits at a table and the patient face of a man who can wait in a doorway for as long as it takes. The boy had seen that face across a counter in a better-lit room, waiting while somebody else decided what a person was worth, and he set it beside the likeness of the quick child and did not bring the two together. The man held out a loaf.

It was warm. The boy knew it before a hand touched it, from the small breath of steam going up where the man's thumb had pressed the crust and from the smell that came off it under the river smell of the well: barley and a fire that somebody else had fed. The crust had a brown edge and a split along the side where it had been pulled apart, and the man held it easily, the way a man holds a thing he has handled often and expects to be taken.

The child saw the loaf and his mouth opened on a sentence. It had the shape all his sentences had --- everyone, or your house --- and then it stopped where a want would have stood, because he had no form of words for a want that was his own and he was too quick a child to say a thing that could be refused to him by name. The man waited. The yard went on drawing water. The sentence did not finish itself, and the man put the loaf into the child's hand.

There was no slate between them. No mark was laid on the stone and no writing was shown and no figure passed from hand to hand; a keeping changed hands with nothing to enter it, and the loaf went into the pocket of the cast-off coat, which held a bit of string and nothing that any clerk could read. The man said something low that did not carry, and the child nodded as though he had understood it, and the man went away up the yard with his soft hands loose at his sides. The child went back to the well with the warmth against his ribs and the gap in his grin showing, and rubbed the green sliver with his thumb, and rubbed it again.

The boy in the basket kept the shape of it. He had been entered in three accounts that had never asked him, and here was a fourth thing that went the other way: value had gone from a man to a child and no slate had stood in the middle of it, and nobody in the yard had looked toward the basket to see whether it had been read. He had once watched a hand go to a record before it went to a person, and had learned to know the order of a room by it. This was a different order, and he set it on a place of its own beside the half-beat, and entered it nowhere. He did not call it a sale, because there was no \lore{Grathic} word he had for the shape of a keeping that changed hands without a slate, and because a name laid on a thing too early is a way of closing the chart on it. The loaf was warm; the man's name was on no roll; the child could not say why he had turned his head. He kept the three objects separate and spent none of them.

Sella turned the basket with her knee out of the draught off the brook, and the boy's head came back from the pot. “Keep it off his ears,” she said to the wool. She gave the instruction to the wool and the bucket and the fire and never to the basket, and she said the house where another woman would have said I. At the well's edge lay a slate set out to dry, and she went around it as wide as a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it over, and her hand did not go to \lore{the widow's mark} at her throat, because a well is not an opening and a basket is not a door. She did not look at the child, and did not look at the loaf in the child's pocket, and did not connect the man from Slate Row with either one. If she saw the passing of the loaf she put it where she put the other things she did not enter, in the private roll nobody was shown, and she carried the basket up the yard at the half-pace without a word about it.

Under the \place{Vail kiln-house} roof \person{Tarn} had set the small knife on its sill before the light went, and told the empty yard where it was, as he had told it every morning of six years. Six years stood in a mark worn almost flat by his thumb and the seventh was nearly gone and still held back, the one entry in this house that the house could not check. \lore{Ash} was worked into the creases of his knuckles in the colour of the clean dark line in the corner, and no scrubbing lifted it, and the pale clip-ring at his left wrist held what light there was and gave it back. His broom went over the road a house sends a man down when it wants a collector, over and over, without once turning along it. A vessel gave its note past the door and his head did not turn, and he did not name the sound, and the boy did not call it a seventh hearing. The two ears kept their equality under the roof; the two silences were of one shape and nobody put a word between them. A loan that can hear is a loan that can be called, and the sentence had never been said in the house where it was truest, and the seventh year pressed at the edge of the sill and was held.

Dusk came onto \place{the Clay Yard} without a figure, the way the flood had gone out of it. Sella carried him up at the half-pace and set him on his bed under the roof and not in \place{the Debt Loft}. She put the fold right at his ear and under his chin without looking at his face, and the thread at his mouth stayed frozen where it had frozen in the small hours and nobody wiped it, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left.

Under the floor the carried fire lay under blind ash and the back of a broken pot, warming the boards by degrees and missing one breath in every four. Four, four and a gap. The boards above it kept four nails on the near plank and four on the next and a fifth that was not a nail but a knot the width of a thumb, and the two counts did not meet and nobody had thought to enter the gap in either. The door stood a finger's width open and the river's low muttered note came through the gap, nearer at the going of the water than at its coming, and he kept it where he had kept it for seventy-five days, apart from the fire's gap and apart from the six hearings and apart from the half-beat that had come into the yard that afternoon.

He had wanted the crust and the breast and the bucket, and got none of them, and the child's warm loaf went into a pocket that held no slate while he was carried up the yard with one crust in his fold and the empty place beside it still unnamed. He had seen a head turn toward a pot half a beat after another head turned, and a mouth open on a sentence that never finished, and a man whose name was on no roll give warmth to a child who could not say why he wanted it. He set the half-beat and the loaf and the unfinished sentence on their separate places and wrote no cause across any of them. The count stood at seventy-five, and there was no word for what he had seen, and no hand to put it on a slate.
