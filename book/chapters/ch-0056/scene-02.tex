The basket lay in a finger's width of shade cast by \place{the moot-post}, and \person{Sella Vail} stood a half pace off from it with the little-door burn on the back of her hand shut on nothing. \person[Corin]{The boy} had been carried to the edge of \place{the Cross} and no nearer. The shade was a thin thing, no wider than a finger laid flat against the iron, and she had set him at the edge of it where no slate lay and where the reading could not be said to be addressed to a basket, because a basket is a pot set down and a pot set down hears what the market says over it and answers nothing. The cold iron waited at his cheek. Beyond it the square went on assembling itself out of boards and noise: \place{the reading block} stood wet with the morning under the post's thin shade, two hundred people would lean to it before the bell had done, and eight pigs would go over the weighing board with a slap of wet hooves that the boy filed as a direction and not as a number addressed to him. She gave the instruction to the wool and to the river wind and to nothing that could hear her in the basket. \emph{Keep it off his ears}, she said, the way she had said it for two hundred and nineteen days, and \emph{It's the river wind}, and stood a half pace off from every open slate with her burn closed and her hands not still.

He took the morning in as he took everything now, through the body that carried him and the ear that could not be carried away from it. The block was a plank set on two trestles, wet at one end where the boards had been washed, and the figures that went into the air above it belonged to the moot and to the households that came late and to nobody who lay in a basket at its edge. A figure read aloud was a figure spoken into weather; it did not travel down a finger's width of shade and enter a child who could not speak a number. Two hundred people leaned and straightened and went back to their pots. The boy kept the printed one in twelve standing unchallenged on its own place, and kept beside it, on a place that did not touch, the knowledge that the figure read over a basket was still not the figure paid. He laid nothing across the two and named no payer to either. The man carried out on Day 188 had gone up the road breathing, and no figure, collector or slate had known what he cost, and the reading that morning over the wet block had not mended that or made it any worse. A reading is an account going into the air. It does not go down into the ground after a man and come back with an answer.

\person{Berr Clay} came up short off \place{the clay road} as he had come up short off it a hundred mornings, stopping before the reading block with the red wool cord of his office folded square under his coat instead of worn across it. He smelled of hot clay and, under that, of nothing at all, which is the most expensive smell in \place{Grath}. The green of tasted \lore{lead glaze} stood at one corner of his mouth, a small sweetish stain the colour of a thing that had been put in and taken out again; he had tasted it before he came and would taste it again before he left, and the masters read the habit as thoroughness and Sella read it as nerves. His hands were large and clean. He wiped his thumb twice on his apron before he touched nothing, once for the slate and once for the figure that would be on it, and held a narrow slate unopened to his forearm with the writing turned inward where the crowd could not take a figure off it. Then he passed a finger over the figure and let his eyes rest on the writing a breath longer than the writing required, and only then raised them to the crowd. The order of reaching --- thumb, slate, finger, eyes --- went down into the boy's private file as an old shape on an old place, and he did not file it twice and did not let it stand up beside the other hands and call itself a rule.

“One in twelve, as the notice says,” \person{Berr Clay} said low into the top of the crowd, and the crowd leaned in because a low voice in a moot makes people come close rather than fall quiet. The figure went twice over the basket, once for the moot and once for the households that had come late, and went nowhere that the boy could enter. “The levy is called,” he said. “It is not called by me.” To a man at the rail who had brought a number up out of a fist and wanted to know what the month's firing would want now the kilns had been brought up, he said that a number goes into a slate and not into a bundle, and that if \emph{your house} wanted it again there would be a slate at the watch --- \emph{your house}, never \emph{I} --- taking himself out of his own sentence as neatly as a surgeon takes his hand out of a field before the dressing goes on. The number went from the fist to his mouth to the shoulder of a man going up toward \place{High Kiln}, and arrived nowhere. The true subtraction went back up the road in the box in his house, locked, and the printed figure stood where it had always stood. He never looked into the basket, and went off toward \place{High Kiln} with the cord folded square and the narrow slate under his arm, believing he had done the district a kindness. The boy kept the twice-wiped thumb and the finger over the figure on their own tray and did not join them to the clerk's hand at the landing or to any of the other record-first hands in the district, for eight likenesses standing in a row do not become one hand and a row of hands is not an account until somebody finds a payer to stand under it.

The bell went four, four and a gap. It struck longer than a church bell, and nobody crossed himself at it, not at the reading and not at the stroke that was missing, and the heard bell lay down beside the told bell of the Long Night on a place of its own where the two could be looked at separately and not mistaken for one ringing. Four was four in the fire under the floor, four in the nails overhead, four in the bell, and the gap stood where the fifth should have stood in each of the three places and rose up in none of them. The boy let the counts lie. A man who wanted them to agree would have to put a payer under all three, and no payer stood to any.

\person{Hessa Rale} went up the clay road before noon with \person{Berr Clay}'s narrow slate held edge-on to her forearm and the writing turned to her sleeve. Her hand arrived at the slate before her eyes arrived at the crowd; the small flat correction of the strap came first, then the turn of the wrist that brought the edge round, and only afterward did she take in the square. Her coat was shortened at the skirt for the road's mud and the mud had dried on it in a pale scurf she did not brush off, because a runner's coat is a tool and nobody scours a tool for its own sake. Two straps crossed her chest, the shorter one let out for the long road. When a woman at the rail asked what she carried, she said, “I don't read it. I carry it. There's a difference, and the road doesn't care which.” To the woman who wanted more she said, “Cross to \place{Greybank}. Reading goes up before noon. I've got a road to walk,” and went on with the pace of a person who has made a bargain with distance and keeps it without thinking. She answered the question put and not the one behind it, and she was done with the conversation when the road was done with it, without rudeness and without apology. No figure came off the slate. The boy kept her order of reaching on its own place and did not file it twice and did not make her a reader because she carried a figure she would not look at; a runner who does not read is not the same object as a clerk who does, and a slate going up the road with its writing inward is a thing somebody else set down.

Below \place{the north bridge}, at \place{the out-of-line landing} where a fifth step should have stood, the soft-cuffed clerk sat still set for a measure nobody had called. His cuff was grey from washing and grey again by midday, and the slate lay to his forearm with the writing inward where no goer-by could take a figure off it. His hand went to the slate before his eyes went to the stalls, and a buyer's number went into it before he looked up to find the man who had said it. Nobody stepped around him and nobody took him off. The market sold its pots and its eels over the top of him as water goes over a stone that lies where a stone lies, and the stone does not rise and does not ask what it is set there for. He was not Marra, not the foundling, not the man carried out on Day 188, and he was joined to none of them. The boy set the inward order of that hand apart from the runner's inward order and kept the two apart from Berr's twice-wiped thumb; two records going inward are two records going inward, and a row of them will call itself an account if a man leaves it standing long enough to want an answer from it.

Across the square at \place{the pot board} a woman stood looking at pots and said nothing all morning. There was clay under her nails in the small crescents, and a pale line across the back of one hand where something had once been worn tight and long enough to leave its mark, and the sweetish green of lead glaze in the crease beside her lip. She turned a cup a quarter without lifting it and set it back exactly where it had stood, and went off with the light. The boy looked at the green and called it green. It was the seventh such colour to come to the mouth of a silent woman at a pot board, and he filed it as a colour on a place of its own beside the six behind it, with a finger between it and the hearings and no name spoken over it. He gave the woman no name and the colour no ear, because a colour at a mouth is not a hearing of \lore{the full note} and a seventh of a colour is not a seventh of the full note. The pale line on the back of her hand went on its own place too, and he did not lay it across the line read at the well on Day 187 or the line on the carried man on Day 188, for a pale line is a pale line and a row of them is not a cause. The pot board kept the shallow print where the cup had stood and gave nobody a figure.

At the market's edge stood a shape in cast-off clothes worn the way a uniform is worn by a boy who was never measured for one: a man's shoulders on a child's frame, breeches cut off at the knee, silt dried pale in the creases of the wrists, a gap where a front tooth had been, and a green sliver of lead glaze on a string at the throat that caught the light when the shape turned and went dull when it turned back. Nobody spoke to it and it spoke to nobody. When a pot was set down near the eel shade its head went toward the sound a half-beat after the boy's own head had gone, after the knuckle and after the note, and stopped in the middle of the turn; a thumb went to the sliver and rubbed it, and the mouth opened on the tail of a kiln-hand's sentence and let nothing out that any clerk with a slate could take down. No loaf passed anywhere within reach of the basket. No slate came near it and no sale was made. The boy filed the half-turn as the seventh such shape on a place of its own beside the six behind it, after the knock and not before it, never joined to his own turning, and gave it no name. The sale to a clerk of \place{the Waiting Room} stayed years ahead of the basket and was not brought early into a market the shape had only walked through; the loaf a man from \place{Slate Row} had put into a pocket holding no slate went on gathering interest in an account the child could not read, and he left the interest to gather.

\scenebreak

He took the two things apart as a surgeon takes apart a wound, separating what lay next to what without pretending the two had grown together. The seventh green was a colour. The seventh shape was a shape. A colour is not a hearing, and a shape that turns a half-beat late is not a seventh of the full note. Neither was a tenth stopping standing before the seventh year at its very end, and neither was a payment, a cause, a name or a romance. Six greens behind the seventh and six shapes behind the seventh made a row of two separate rows, and a fourth likeness does not stand up and call itself a rule, because a rule is an account that has found a payer, and no payer stood to either row. The hearings remained six, five from four sources and the sixth out of a person, and no seventh had come to stand beside them or among them, and none was invented to fill an empty place kept empty. The market offered all its withholdings at once --- the figure read and the figure paid, the cord folded under the coat, the slate going up unread, the clerk set for an uncalled measure, the green at a mouth, the shape at the edge --- and not one of them could be entered by a child who could not close two inches on the rim of the basket that held him. He set each on its own place and laid nothing across any of them. Nothing in the file had been joined, named, opened, called, spent or ended.

\person{Sella Vail} carried him home at the going of the light at the half-pace. Woodsmoke went before her in long low bars and the river wind came behind her with the taste of \lore{water over water}; the carrying arm was a record in muscle that remembered weight and not cost, and had carried him up and down this yard for two hundred and nineteen days. In \place{the Clay Yard} she went wide around the pan's gone round ring, the bare dark \place{trough} whose water was called weather, the space beneath \place{the lowest rack} where the broken birth-slates lay, the field's empty place and the place the carriers crossed, each with a look and a wide step and no number. The clean dark line where the \lore{Ash} ridge turned and the new finer hairline of grey beside it called \lore{the thaw} lay unswept, and a line left where it is is still a line kept. Nobody swept it and nobody named what it cost. The yard went around its absences the way a woman goes around a pail of hot water set where a child might knock it, and the boy kept the wide steps as directions and gave them no figure. There was no third gap in the returning line, no fourth likeness made a cause, and no finger of his drew the line between any two empty places. She set him on \place{the bed} under the roof and not in \place{the Debt Loft}, put the fold right without a look at his face, and left the thread of drool at his mouth frozen and unwiped, because a basket set down is a pot set down and left and nobody under any roof had ever been given an instruction about the thread.

In the room of the fire \person{Sella Vail} told it it was the thaw, turned the coals with the back of a broken pot, said \emph{The house will keep it} where another woman would have said \emph{I}, and stood a moment with the back of the pot in her hand counting under her breath toward no opening --- small intake, hold, letting go. She pushed a strand of coal-black hair back with the wrist that carries the burn so that the grey streak at her temple showed pale off the coals, set the pot rim out with its handle two inches beyond the reaching hand and let her hand rest a half-beat longer at the setting than the setting required, and did not look toward the bed. Her eye went once to the clay jar cold under its glaze and her hand shut half over nothing without a touch. Her finger lay on top of \place{the loaf shelf}'s front edge where any woman in the district could see it lying, and the charcoal tally under the board stayed unread, uncounted and uncopied. She moved her lips along the loaf row, which stood with two unnamed gaps, and gave nobody a figure. The \lore{widow's mark} hung on its cord at her throat where it had hung since the first firing, touched once before the kiln on Ember 1 and at no hour since, and there had been no opening in thirty-nine days, because a shelf and an afternoon and a market and a bed are not openings. \person{Garl Vail} was kept in objects and not in a story: the salt-stiff coat with the crease at the left shoulder, the seat with its twenty-year hollow and lean, the proverb gone years ago to a pot with no crack in it, and the debt against a firing of tiles circled without a name and worse for being uncalled. His name had not been spoken under this roof for two hundred and nineteen days, and no lesson was made of any of it and no collector was called.

In the small hours there was no breathing number anywhere in the house, nothing to hold the hand against but the rhythm that came through the finger's-width door. Under the floor \lore{the carried fire} gave four low pulls and withheld the fifth, its payer without a name for two hundred and nineteen days; through the door the river kept its note of water over water, a fifth low thing never counted among the hearings; overhead the boards kept four nails, four, and a thumb-width knot where the fifth nail should have stood. Three counts in three places, and none rose up and became the others. One stiff leathery brown-edged barley crust lay two inches from his ear in the fold of grey wool, and the empty place beside it was still unnamed and no third had come to make the row come out even. The thread at his mouth was frozen and unwiped. Cold had come to cheek and temple and the small bare joint at the wrist where the wool rode back, and stopped there as if it had reached the end of a written order; the afterdrop waited on the far side of a warming nobody in this house would perform. He knew the afterdrop when he saw it and he wanted the crust, and both were true and the child's wanting was the louder of the two.

He put the question to his hand as he had put it on other mornings and other small hours. The hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the note and stayed for the small intake, and came down before the letting go had finished --- an honest fraction shorter than the whole breath, taken through one of the rhythms the house kept and stopped before the rhythm had run out. It was not held through the intake and the hold and the letting go, and it was not carried past the end of a count and down on a second note, because no second rhythm had been asked of it under this roof tonight and the market had not got to read the inch. An answer given and an answer refused are objects on separate trays, and both were true and neither was a sign. He filed the fraction on the sixth place beside the others with no payer named to it and nothing standing up out of it as a reach, a hearing, a seventh, a stopping, a payment, a held change, a key, a power or a rule. He still could not close the two inches to the pot handle Sella had set out, lift his chin the two inches to the crust or speak a number that anybody under any roof would take down. Exactness and humiliation were one motion in that small hand. The fire went on in the room that warmed him and the river in the room he could not enter, and neither stopped for the ear. Two hundred and nineteen stood exact, complete, unspent and spoken by nobody in \place{Sarn} or \place{Sill} or Grath, and the count went forward into the first ordinary tending morning after the market with nothing settled and nothing ended.
