\person{Tarn} came up from the \place{Debt Loft} before light on Stone 17 with the broom under his arm, and swept. The bristles went over the trodden ash with a dry, dragging sound, back and back to the same board, and the \place{Vail kiln-house} had not stirred around him. The coals held under their blind layer from the night as they had held the night before, and the kiln had not been opened, and the crust lay stiff in the fold at the boy's side, where it had been put two days ago and where it would stay. \person{Corin} kept his own count of the strokes and felt the cold come in at cheek, then temple, then wrist, in the order he knew and could not argue with. Nobody had spoken to the basket.

Grey \lore{the Ash} showed in the creases of Tarn's knuckles, the grey that no scrubbing lifted, and the pale ring at his left wrist showed where an iron clip had sat in eight hundred and six, a circle of skin that had never gone back to the colour around it. He was twenty-two and bent at the shoulders from six years in the slip pit, which made him look older than his term. Six years of the seven were behind him and he had counted every one, and the year that was left he would not count at all, not even to the ash, because it was the one entry in this house the house could not check. Before he swept the threshold he took the small bench knife from his pocket and set it on the sill where it could be checked, and told the yard what he had done, though there was nobody in the yard to hear it. “I took the small knife,” he said to the trodden ash. “I put it back. You can check.” The mark worn into the handle was almost flat, rubbed away by six years of scraping grey from his knuckles, and he did not look at it when he said it.

\person{Sella Vail} came in from the \place{Clay Yard} with the cold on her sleeves and went first to the fire, as she went first to the fire in every hour anyone under this roof had observed. Before she lifted the lid her hand went up to \lore{the widow's mark} at her throat, the thumb-length of lead-glazed clay on its cord with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and held it a moment longer than the touch required, and let it go. That was the order of it and no other hour for it, not at the bank and not when a woman stood at the door with a question. The burn shaped like a little door across the back of her left hand closed on nothing as the hand came down, and the pale inside of it showed for a moment in the low light and was gone. “There's your air,” she said to the coals, and lifted the lid.

The kiln took its breath in fours with one missing. Four, and four, and a gap where the fifth should have been. He had counted it through a wall and across a market and in a room with a dying man in it, and he counted it now with no wall between, and the gap was still the part nobody had thought to enter.

Sella looked at the threshold where Tarn's broom had been. “That's a clean sweep,” she said. “You've done well.”

The broom stopped. It did not stop for a stroke or a half; it stopped altogether, the man behind it going still in the way a man goes still when a door he had been holding shut is opened without warning. Two breaths went by. He did not turn his head. On the second breath he said, “Yes, house,” in the flat voice that agreed so completely there was nowhere for an argument to put its foot, and began sweeping again where he had left off.

The boy had seen the shape of it once before under this roof, on Stone 13, and had kept it without a word. He understood what it cost: that a man who owned nothing could be stopped for two breaths by the one thing he could never enter in any account, which was a kindness from the woman who held his debt. He lay with the fold at his chin and the drool freezing at his mouth and did not look at either of them, because the body has its own humiliations and the surgeon's eye was not the only thing awake in the basket.

Jorra's pot stood on the bench where it had stood through the day before, turned a little toward the light. Sella wiped the bench with her sleeve as she passed and turned the pot further toward the thin grey coming in at the door, and set it down softly, the way a woman sets down a vessel she has no need to scold. The boy turned his head before the set-down.

It was not a hearing. He knew that now, and knew it more surely each time; it was the turning of a head toward a place where a thing had once happened, and the body did it before the mind could catch it, the way a nurse's hand goes to a rail in the dark. The pot gave nothing. There was no small flat knock, no answer from a sound vessel under a knuckle, nothing to put a name to in \lore{Grathic} even if he had possessed the word, which he did not. There was only the space where the note should have been, and it had the shape of a sound in it.

At the bench Tarn's broom stopped half a stroke. It checked and went on, and he kept his eyes on the ash under the bristles.

Neither of them said what had not come. The boy put the space at the bench where he put the one from Stone 13, the first of its kind under this roof, and left the count where it had stood, five hearings from four sources. An interval is not a sixth. He had learned to keep the distinction the way a man keeps two similar bones separate on a tray, because a thing that did not sound was not the same thing as a thing that did, and the cost of confusing them was a wrong answer written down in a neat hand. He kept the two intervals side by side and the five where they were, and joined nothing.

Sella gave the instruction to the fire and to the room, and not to the basket. “Don't sleep in the draught,” she said to the kiln mouth, to the gap of cold that went along the floor to the corner where the Ash lay and stopped there as if it had been told to stop. “The breath takes a man in his sleep.”

“There was a man in \place{Mud End},” Tarn said to the ash. He said it in the tone of a man stating the weather, which was the only tone he had for a thing he had no business knowing. “Slept in front of a kiln with the damper shut. Three leagues downriver. The district called it a merciful death.”

The boy filed that too: the breath that took a man in his sleep, and the word *merciful* laid over it like a lid. He put it with the gruel that came from the house's dish and was entered nowhere, and the fire that was kept and entered nowhere, and the crust in the fold with a payer who could be named and a recipient who could not refuse it. Under this roof the things that were dangerous were the things that had no entry, and the things that had no entry were the things the house called by gentle names. He laid them out side by side in the order they had arrived, which was the only honest arrangement he had found in this house, and it bought him nothing.

Tarn swept on toward the wall. The boy watched the narrow back, the bent shoulders, the pale circle at the wrist, and understood with the part of him that had once sorted twenty injured people in ninety seconds that the man was keeping something the house had not given him and could not take back, and that it was the same thing he himself was keeping. Not the same sound: Tarn had heard the \lore{full note} of a pot through the wall, and he had heard a cup across a birth room and the district's slate twice at the Cross and a man giving under a cold iron tube, four sources and five hearings with one flat answer running through them, and Tarn's hearing was separate from those and stayed in its own place. The same keeping. Each man held his from the other side of a silence. Neither had a word for it in the language of the house, and neither could hand it over.

He knew the shape of it from the inside, having lived in it for eleven days. A thing you keep that nobody has entered is a thing you cannot spend, cannot refuse, cannot set down. Tarn kept his the way a man keeps a tool he has no right to own: close to him, and announced when he set it on a sill so that nobody could say afterward he had stolen it. The boy kept his the way a man keeps a chart he signed without reading the nurse's note: close to him, and never handed over, because to hand it over would be to let somebody enter it. The two keepings faced each other across the trodden ash with a broom between them.

And the man's ear was worth more to this house than the man's debt, and that made the keeping valuable in a way that was also dangerous, because a thing worth more than the debt that holds a man is a thing a house might one day learn to count. He saw it the way he had once seen a supply shelf at four in the morning: what could be counted on it was not what mattered, and what mattered was the thing nobody had written down. He had no word to say any of it, and no hand that could reach two paces past the fold, and no standing in this house to say a thing even if he had both. The red edge of \lore{the birth-flower} showed at his wrist where the wool had ridden back, and he did not have a hand to pull the wool down over it. He was eleven days old and the only person in the room who could read the account, and the account went on without him.

Later, when the light on the oiled paper had gone from white to the colour of skimmed milk, Tarn took the small knife out again to scrape the grey from his knuckles. He worked the edge under the Ash in the creases carefully, the way a man cleans a thing he will have to show somebody, and when the creases were as clean as they ever got he held up his left wrist to the light and turned it, so the pale ring showed all the way round, and looked at it for a moment. Then he put the knife back on the sill where it could be checked. This time he did not tell the yard he had done so.

The boy's hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the broom leaning against the wall and came down. He wanted the handle under his fingers, the weight and the length of it, the way he had once wanted a clamp put into his hand in a room where somebody was bleeding out on the floor. Tarn did not look. His eyes were on the sill, on the mark in the knife handle worn almost flat, and he was already reaching for the broom with his own hand.

\person{Jorra Vail} came in from \place[the Vail wheel-shed]{the shed} with bone-coloured slip dried to old bone up both forearms and the little finger of his left hand bent the way it had been since he was eleven. There was no slate dust on him. He brought the shed in with him: damp clay and tallow and the sweetish edge of lead glaze that came up from High Kiln on a bad wind, a smell that this district had made into a trade and called skill. He went to the wheel and then to the bench with a pot in his hands, and said to the pot what he did not say to any person under the roof.

“You'll do,” he said. “The shoulder's thick. I'll thin it.”

Sella had started to tell him the fire was low. He shifted the damper before she had finished the sentence, the bent finger going in ahead of the rest of his hand, and the fire took the instruction and went on, and she left the end of it unsaid, which was an obedience and a correction in the same movement and neither of them called it either. \person{Garl Vail}'s coat kept its salt-stiff shape on its peg behind the door and his seat in the shed kept the hollow of twenty years on the left, and his proverb went to the pot and not to the basket: leave the crack showing; people trust a thing they can see has been handled. Jorra did not say the back room was cold. He did not look toward the basket.

Dusk came in at the door's finger's width, the same thin grey thread that went along the floor and stopped at the corner where the Ash lay. Sella banked the coals with the back of the broken pot and said that'll hold to the fire, and her hand did not go to the mark, because that was for the hour before an opening and no other. The loaves on the shelf stood in their arrangement of days and bits, three here and two there and one set a little apart with its brown edge turned up, a shape that was not a number and could not be taken up and entered.

Tarn swept the last of the ash back toward the threshold and brought the broom past the bench once more. It stopped half a stroke at the place where the pot had been set down and where no note had come, and went on, and he set the broom against the wall. The small knife lay on its sill with its mark worn almost flat, where a man could check it in the morning. The kiln took its breath in fours with one missing: four, and four, and a gap.

The boy lay with the crust cold in the fold at his side and the second interval filed beside the first, and his hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth and came down. The house went on keeping what it could not count, and he went on keeping the count.
