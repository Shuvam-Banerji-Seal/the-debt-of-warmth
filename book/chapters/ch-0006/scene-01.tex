\daymark{10}{Stone 16, Y.H. 812 (Sixthday)}

\dropcap{T}{he coals} had held through the night under their smooth blind layer of ash, and first light came into the \place{Vail kiln-house} at the door's finger's width of failure, a thin grey thread that went along the floor to the corner where \lore{the Ash} lay and stopped there as if it had been told to stop. It was the same light that had come yesterday and would come tomorrow, and \person{Corin} did not spend anything on surprise. Ten days old. He lay on his bed under the roof with the crust from the market gone cold in the fold at his side, stiff now at the edges and smelling faintly of smoke and barley, and he could feel its cold through the wool as exactly as he had felt its warmth three nights ago. The cold came in at cheek, then temple, then wrist, in the order he knew and could not argue with. Nobody had spoken to the basket.

\person{Sella Vail} came in from the \place{Clay Yard} with the cold still on her sleeves and went first to the fire, as she went first to the fire in every hour anyone under this roof had observed. She smelled of tallow and wet wool and, under them, the faint sour mineral tang of the corner she had not swept in two years. She took the back of the broken pot from the ash heap and lifted the edge of the ash layer with it, and the cold ash breathed up under her wrist in a small grey puff that carried the smell of everything the house had ever burned. Under it the coals lay close and red as a thing asleep. She did not open the kiln yet. Before she lifted the lid her hand went up to \lore{the widow's mark} at her throat, the thumb-length of lead-glazed clay on its cord with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and held it a moment longer than the touch required, and let it go. That was the order of it: the mark before the lid, and no other hour for the mark, not at the bank at dusk and not when a woman stood at the door with a question. The burn across the back of her left hand, shaped like a little door with its two straight sides and its lintel and its threshold, closed on nothing as the hand came down, and the pale inside of it showed for a moment in the low light and was gone.

“There's your air,” she said to the coals, and lifted the lid.

The kiln took its breath in fours with one missing. Four, and four, and a gap where the fifth should have been. He had counted it through a wall and across a market and in a room with a dying man in it, and he counted it now with no wall between, and the gap was still the part nobody had thought to enter. The pot stood on the bench where it had stood on Stone 13, turned a little toward the light, its belly whole and its lip unchipped. He had learned in these ten days to turn his head toward that place before anybody set a thing down, and had learned also that the turning was expectation and not hearing, and that a thing which did not happen could leave a space in a room with the shape of a sound in it. He kept that space beside the bench from the death-house with its four nails and four and a knot the width of a thumb, and beside the boards under his bed with the same count, and beside the two ridges of \lore{the Ash} in two houses that ran to their corners and turned them without a break. He kept them side by side in the order they had arrived and joined none of them.

\person{Tarn} came up from the \place{the Debt Loft} with the broom under his arm and went to the stack of kindling by the wall before he went to the threshold. He did not touch the stack. He measured it with his eyes the way a man measures a debt, taking the top layer and then the layer under it and then the place where the next layer would lie if there were one, and the boy watched the measuring from under the fold and understood what the man was doing because he had once stood in a supply room at four in the morning and read what was left on a shelf the same way, four there and four there and a place where the third count should have been. The stack stood four bundles along the near row and four along the row behind, and a space at the end where a fifth should have been, with the straw kicked aside and the bare boards showing through. Tarn's eyes went to the space and came back, and he put nothing into it, and he did not say that anything was missing.

He had six years of the seven behind him and had counted every one, and the seventh he held back, the one entry in this house that the house could not check. He said it to the stack because the stack was a thing that would hold a sentence and ask nothing back. “Six counted,” he said. “The next I don't count.”

Then he swept the threshold, and swept it again, and set the broom against the wall.

Across the room Sella moved her lips along the shelf where the loaves stood, and the boy watched the moving the way he had once watched a nurse run a finger down a record she did not mean to read aloud: no sound, only the lips arriving and returning. She had arranged the loaves so that the days to \lore{Kindling} were not in them but the shape of them was --- three here and two there and one set a little apart with its brown edge turned up, and then the arrangement again from the other end, coming back to the same place twice. It was enough for a woman who had kept one house through one Long Cold and meant to keep this one through another. \place{Low Kiln} went around the district with a figure closed in a fist and let it out under this roof only as an absence, and there was no number in the loaves for a collector to take up and enter, only the shape of one, which was the whole of the art. He had once counted twenty injured people in ninety seconds and sent some to a warm bed and some to a cold one. Here he could not lift his hand to the dish.

\person{Jorra Vail} came in from \place[the Vail wheel-shed]{the shed} with bone-coloured slip dried to old bone up both forearms and the little finger of his left hand bent the way it had been since he was eleven. There was no slate dust on him, and the boy looked at the clean creases above the bend of the finger and could not ask which the absence meant, any more than he could have asked about the presence of it on the days when it was there. Jorra went to the wheel and then to the bench with a pot in his hands, and said to the pot what he did not say to any person under the roof.

“You'll do,” he said. “The shoulder's thick. I'll thin it.”

Sella had started to tell him the fire was low. He moved the damper before she had finished the sentence, and the fire took the instruction and went on, and she left the end of it unsaid, which was an obedience and a correction in the same movement and neither of them called it either. \person{Garl Vail}'s coat kept its salt-stiff shape on its peg behind the door, and the seat in the shed kept the hollow of twenty years on the left, and his proverb went to the pot and not to the basket: leave the crack showing; people trust a thing they can see has been handled. Nobody had filled the seat. Nobody had taken the coat down. Nobody said what the uncalled debt against a firing of tiles was, or where it lay, or how long it had lain there where \person{Berr Clay} had left it.

\scenebreak

\person{Alda Reed} came at mid-morning, when the light on the oiled paper had gone from white to the colour of skimmed milk, and the boy knew her before the door had opened wide enough to show a shoulder. Soap and warm milk came off her, and under them tallow and the faint sour tang of a room nobody had swept, which was the smell of a woman who had spent forty years lifting other people's children and had never once set one down without first reaching for the thing it was written on. She had her birth-slates under her arm and a narrow slate of her own in the fold of her shawl.

She reached for the slate before she reached for the child.

That was the whole of it, and he had known it would be so before she crossed the floor. He had once stood at the foot of a table in a white room and watched a senior man lay his hand on the chart before he looked at the patient lying under it, and had learned from that what the order of reaching meant: the record first, because the record was the thing that would still be there when the person was carried out. He had learned it again in this house from a woman who kept a roll she did not show and stood a half-pace from every open slate. He had no word in \lore{Grathic} for the order of it and no standing to correct it, and he had the order itself laid out in front of him as plainly as a chart laid open on a table.

Alda laid the birth-slates flat on the bench with the writing turned down, and only then came to the basket. She put two fingers at the fold by his shoulder and pressed it flat, and held them there longer than a fold required, long enough that the pressure became a different thing from the straightening of wool --- a hand saying what a mouth had arranged not to say. She touched the little tin on its cord once inside her bodice, a small movement of the cloth and nothing shown, and went on pressing the fold flat.

The wool had ridden back at his wrist and the red edge of the branching mark showed there, the fire-coloured edge of the thing that ran from his third rib down the forearm and stopped short of the wrist. Alda's two fingers went to the corner of it and lifted, crossed, and came away. She did not know she had done it. She had done it now in front of witnesses four times and had never once known she did it in front of them, and the boy watched his own wrist from under the fold and put the gesture where he put the others, without a name and without a use. It lay beside the two ridges and the charred slate in the kiln ash and the bench with its knot and the interval where a pot had not answered, and it was the first of those things that had ever been done to him while he lay awake under it.

“Stone seven,” she said to the bench. “The account went up on the eighth, before the paper went out.”

Nobody had asked her for a date. There was no question in the room that the date could answer, and she had answered it anyway, the way a woman closes a door that nobody has opened. She went on speaking after the question had stopped, because there had never been a question and so there was no stopping her: “The cooper's seventh in Mud End. The fourth day of Stone in eight hundred and six. A potter's third child, now a carter in Stonemouth.” The dates came faster than dates had any business arriving, each one exact and each one laid over the small thing her fingers had just done at a red edge under the wool.

She took up the narrow slate from her shawl and wrote on it with a charcoal stub what she did not leave lying about. The boy could not read a word of it, could not have read \lore{Grathic} on a stone a foot from his face, and knew the shape of the act all the same: the head bent, the left hand holding the slate steady, the short strokes of a woman who writes a thing down before she lets herself feel it. When she had done she turned the slate writing-down and put it back into the shawl, not under the birth-slates on the bench and not where any mother or officer would think to pick it up. There was a floorboard somewhere in her house that the note would go to, and he did not know the house and did not know the board, and knew only that a truth had been written down and turned face-down in the same movement that had pressed the wool flat at his shoulder. The two gestures were one gesture. He had spent twelve years in rooms where the same hand did both things, and had never once found a way to make the writing and the warming agree.

Sella came away from the fire with the pot-shovel in her hand and stood a half-pace from the bench. “He lies quiet,” she said, which was not a question and not an answer and was the nearest this house came to telling a visitor that the boy was still under the roof.

“Warm and breathing,” Alda said. “That's all I've set down.”

She said it the way she said everything that was not true, which was exactly, and went on giving the dates after even that had stopped, because there was nobody in the room whose business it was to tell her to stop. The milk and soap went out of the room with her when she went, and the sour tang of the corner came back in its place, and the morning went on being an ordinary morning in which a woman had written a second note and turned it down and warmed a child with the same hand.

Alda's two fingers were lifted at the red edge of his wrist and covered by a date spoken to nobody, and Sella's hand was away from the mark again and back at the fire where it belonged, and the stack of kindling by the wall stood four, four, a place where the fifth should be. Nobody had spoken to the basket all morning. The boy kept the gap where he had kept it under the boards and beside the bench and in the space where a pot had not answered, and he could not write it down and had nobody to give it to, and it was the only place in the room where nothing had been entered.
