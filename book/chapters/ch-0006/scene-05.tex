\daymark{14}{Stone 20, Y.H. 812 (Tenthday, Account-day)}

\person{Corin} came awake with the dark still lying on the yard and the crust cold in the fold at his side, and knew the day by its cost before he knew it by its name. It was \lore{Account-day}. The word had come into the \place{Vail kiln-house} the night before and gone around the bed, the loft and the loaf shelf without anybody saying what it meant, the way a number went around this district: as a shape, and a hand shut into a fist, and an absence where the figure should have been. He was fourteen days old. The cold came in at cheek, then temple, then wrist, in the order he knew and could not argue with. Nobody had spoken to the basket.

\person{Sella Vail} lifted him before the light had cleared the sill. She did not touch \lore{the widow's mark}; that was for the hour before an opening and no other, and this was a reading, not an opening. The burn shaped like a little door across the back of her left hand closed on nothing as she took the weight and opened on the handle, and she carried him like a pot filled that must not be slopped, down the yard and into \place{the Cross}, where two hundred people were already leaning toward a slate. All the way she kept her half-pace from every open slate, as a woman keeps her skirt from a fire she has no wish to put out; a figure lying on a stone was a thing that could reach a child across a yard, in this house's experience, and she had stopped arguing with the distance years ago.

She set the basket at the north end of the square beside \place{the moot-post}, in the finger's width of shade the post threw, the only shade at that end, and the cold iron came through the wool against his cheek and stayed there. The notice hung from its nail with one corner gone soft, and the wind turned the row of marks over and back as if trying to read them. He lay with the shade on his face and the drool freezing at his mouth and the crust stiff against his ribs, and heard the bell go over the square.

It was longer than a church bell. A church bell lets go and leaves a man to his thoughts; this one went on after the air had taken up the first stroke, and it went four, and four, and a gap where the fifth should have been. Nobody crossed himself at it. The pigs went on being driven over the wet boards, the crowd went on leaning, and the bell went on doing a temple's work with a Court's hand, which nobody in \place{Sill} had thought to explain to a child. He had counted it at the fish market and through a wall and in a room where a man was giving, and the gap was still the part nobody had thought to enter.

\person{Berr Clay} came to the block with the red cord of his office folded under his coat and the green of tasted glaze at one corner of his mouth, a man smelling of hot clay over the smell of nothing. His hands were large and clean. He wiped his thumb on his apron before he touched the slate, and wiped it again, so that the hand everyone would be watching should be seen to be clean. The crowd had been shouting itself hoarse over the pigs; when he touched the stone the shouting stopped, not because he had asked for quiet but because two hundred people had to lean in to hear him.

“One in twelve, as the notice says,” he said. It came low, into the top of the crowd rather than across it. “If your household has heard otherwise, your household has heard wrong.”

The boy had heard the same grammar in a room with better light and a chart on a table: a sentence that took the speaker out of it and left an account standing in his place. Here the account wore a kiln-master's coat and tasted its own glaze and apologised in the form of an instruction. He had no word in \lore{Grathic} for the shape of it and did not need one; he had learned to recognise the order of a thing before he had learned the name of any part of it.

“The levy is called for the feast,” Berr said. “It is not called by me.” He passed a finger over the figure to make sure it was still where he had put it. “The figure stands as read. There'll be a slate at the watch if the house wants it again.”

He spoke of the kiln-house and the mark confirmed by the factor at \place{Greybank} and the fire that was kept under this roof, and around a consignment of tiles that nobody named. There was a place in his sentence shaped like the hollow of a wheel-seat where a debt might have stood, and Berr went around it the way a woman goes around the corner of a room where \lore{the Ash} has been left lying, and set the printed figure down where two hundred people could see it instead. The uncalled debt lay where it had lain for a year, neither paid nor settled nor done with, and the house up the yard went on owing a man who would not say what it owed.

Berr did not look into the basket. The figure was read over the top of it, and the boy lay in the post's thin shade with cold iron at his cheek and heard a number go into the square that he could not spend, could not correct, could not hand back to anybody. Fourteen days old, and the only person in that square who knew exactly what the reading had left out, which was everything.

\scenebreak

When the moot had finished with the figure she lifted the basket again and carried him the length of the square to the fringe where \place{Slate Row} began. A woman stood there with a slate held against her apron, writing turned inward, and a table stood between her and the row with the Court's business on it in the open air, to be carried inside once the day's figures had been made to agree. \place[the Court's table at the Cross]{The Court's table} was bare but for a slate, a charcoal stub and a cup ring gone brown into the wood. Sella set the basket where no slate lay open and stood a half-pace off, and the woman waiting drew her hand back from her own stone as though, once his hand was on it, it had ceased to be hers.

The clerk had a cuff turned back twice at the wrist from washing and ink in the creases of two otherwise ordinary-coloured fingers. He laid his hand on the top slate before he looked at the woman. It was the same order he had used ten days ago and the same order the midwife had used at a birth and the man with the cold iron tube had used at a bed: the record first, because the record would still be there when the person had been carried out. He was a soft man with a soft voice and no edge in it at all.

“It's entered as it stands,” he said. “There's nothing in it the household need correct.”

The woman's mouth opened and shut. Nothing in the household needed correcting, and the slate was entered as it stood, and the matter was finished before it had properly begun, which is how matters are finished in this district. She took her stone back turned down against her apron and went away toward the row with her fist shut around whatever figure she had come with.

The boy could not read a word of the upper line, and would not have known \lore{Grathic} if it had been written an inch from his eye. He knew the hand. It was the strange neat dark hand he had seen on a slate propped on a bench in a birth room, and on another slate at this square on Stone 10, when the figure was read over the top of him, and before that in the orange light of the flare, where a hand in a grey sleeve wrote and stopped between two clusters of strokes. He knew the stop as he knew the hand: a small lifting and setting-down, a place where the writing had held its breath and let it go, the same gap as between one count and the next.

The flare light is orange on wet snow. A hand in a grey sleeve writes and stops between two clusters, and the snow takes the heat of the flare in a small hiss. The hand goes on. The stop is the part that stays.

Four times. He had seen it four times now, and had no word for it, no standing to ask what it was, and no hand to point at the place where it sat. The first three had lain side by side on the tray and had not joined, and this one lay beside them as a fourth object of the same shape, unread and unjoined and no more use than the other three. A fourth sighting changed the count and changed nothing else. It gave him no power to decide and no word to speak and no way into the slate that lay under the clerk's soft cuff. He had more of that account in his head than any person in the square and could not enter one stroke of it.

In its own place on the tray, kept carefully apart from these four, lay the hand of the man who had drawn the year's first measure two doors down: neat too, ordinary, with no stop in it at all. The boy had noted the absence of the pause on the hour it happened and had filed it as a different object, the way he kept two similar bones separate on a tray, because a wrong answer written down in a neat hand cost more than the trouble of keeping them apart. He kept the hand without a pause where it was. He had five hearings of \lore{the full note} from four sources and had added nothing to them today; a pause was not a hearing, and the count stayed where it had stood through fourteen days.

The crowd went back to the pigs. Figures stood side by side on the heap of turnips without being made to agree: one in twelve, a levy for the feast, a measure entered as it stood, and the days to \lore{the Five} shut into every fist that went past him. He heard them go by and could not spend any of them. On the road out a barrow of grey went through the north gate toward \place{the Bone Orchard}, heaped a little above the sides where the load had shifted, and nobody in the square looked at it twice; it was the afternoon's work, the same work that went out every market-day.

His hand outside the wool went to the pocket-place. It found wool, and woodsmoke in the wool, and the river wind coming over the square, and the cold crust in the fold, and no small cold rectangle with a name on it nine years old and unlooked-at. Nine promises of a boat went out with the reaching and came back with nothing, and the hand came down. The clerk's soft sentence went into the row behind him, and the figure stood as read, and the account went on outliving the debtor up the yard.

\scenebreak

At dusk she lifted the basket off the post and carried him up the yard with the woodsmoke of the square still in the wool and the river wind caught under the fold. The doors of \place{Low Kiln} were going shut one after another, and the ash of the day went along the trodden ground in front of her and came back to the same board. He had been fourteen days under this roof and knew the walk by the places where her hip shifted the weight: the joint by the well, the step over the loose board, the turn in at the door that stood a finger's width open.

Under the roof she put the fold right without looking at his face, the way a woman straightens a cup on a shelf without inspecting what is in it, and set the basket on his bed and not in \place{the Debt Loft} above the shed. At the bank her hand did not go to the mark, because that was for the hour before an opening and no other, and the rule held as exactly at the end of an account-day as at the hour before a kiln was lit. She banked the coals with the back of a broken pot; they hissed once under the ash and held. “That'll hold,” she said to the fire.

The crust lay cold in the fold at his side, stiff at the edges, smelling of smoke and barley, and she neither took it out nor called it a kindness, which in this house were two ways of doing the same thing. He could feel its cold through the wool as exactly as he had felt its warmth on the night it was put there, and he could not lift it to his mouth, and had never once asked who had put it there or what it had cost.

\person{Tarn} came up from the loft with the broom under his arm and set the small bench knife on the sill where it could be checked, and told the yard he had done so, unasked, though the yard was empty. “I took the small knife. I put it back. You can check.” Six years of the seven were behind him and he had counted every one; the seventh he held back, the one entry in this house the house could not check. His broom went past the bench once and stopped half a stroke at the place where a pot had been set down and had given nothing, and went on, and he did not look at the basket.

\person{Jorra Vail} came in from the shed with bone-coloured slip dried to old bone up both forearms and the bent little finger of his left hand going in ahead of the rest of him at the damper. There was no slate dust on him. “You'll do,” he said to the pot, and shifted the damper before Sella had finished telling him the fire was low, and left the end of her sentence where it was. He did not say the back room was cold and did not look toward the bed.

\person{Garl Vail}'s coat kept its salt-stiff shape on its peg behind the door and his seat in the shed kept the hollow of twenty years on the left, and his proverb went to the fire and not to the basket: leave the crack showing; people trust a thing they can see has been handled. The ridge of Ash ran to the corner and turned it without a break, near two thumbs wide, and would not be swept before \lore{the Last Day}. The debt against the firing of tiles lay uncalled somewhere in the square and had lain there a year, spoken around all day and named by nobody, and the boy put it on the tray beside the pause he had seen four times and joined neither to the other.

The Five were eleven days off. The household kept that number badly, in loaves and glances and an arrangement of three here and two there and one set a little apart with its brown edge turned up, come back to twice and never once said; and the boy kept it exactly, as he kept the days to \lore{Kindling} and the count of the floorboards and the bell and the kiln and the landing that was not a step, and none of the numbers could be written, spoken, spent or handed to a person who would take it. He had been fourteen days in a country with no word for the thing he was doing, and was the only adult in the room and the least use in it, and the correctness of the whole account was the whole humiliation.

The kiln took its breath in fours and missed one. Four, and four, and a gap. The fourth pause lay on the tray with the other three and had not joined them, and the Drawing collector's ordinary hand stayed in its own place with no pause in it at all, and there was no sixth hearing and no word in Grathic and no hand that could reach two paces past the fold. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the warmth and came down. The crust stayed cold where it had been put, and the house went on keeping what it could not count, and Low Kiln would not say what it counted.
