The light on the oiled paper went the colour of skimmed milk and then past it, and the yard came in through the door that stood a finger's width open. \person{Corin} lay in the basket under the fold in the \place{Vail kiln-house} with the crust cold at his side and the drool freezing at his mouth, and felt the cold arrive in the old order, cheek, then temple, then wrist. He was thirteen days old. No one had spoken to the basket all day.

\person{Sella Vail} crouched at the coals with the back of a broken cup and turned them over without lifting them, the way a woman turns a sleeping child and puts the blanket back. The little-door burn across her hand shut on nothing. Dusk was not the hour for \lore{the widow's mark}; the cord lay still against her throat while she worked, and the kiln breathed through the evening in fours and missed one. Four, and four, and a gap. “That'll hold,” she said to the coals, and left the cup where it lay.

A man came up the yard from \place{the Ilm} with water on his legs. It had frozen pale where it met his boots. The trodden ash took his weight in four prints and four and a place where his foot had slid, and the boy counted them without meaning to, the way every count in this yard began. The man held something cupped in his hand and would not set it on the bench. A thing out of the river was not laid on a bench in a kiln-house where the fire had to be kept.

“There's a hair in it,” the man said. He held out a palm's breadth of ice, grey as milk gone off, with a dark line in it that ran from one edge and stopped a thumb's breadth short of the other. “Hold it to the light. You'll see it then.”

The woman from \place{High Kiln} came down the path with the sweetish lead edge of \place[The Glaze Room]{the glaze room} on her coat and a smear of it at one corner of her mouth, and she looked at the shard as a woman looks at a pot she has been asked to price. “It'll go early,” she said. “Ice with a hair in it goes before its time. I've seen it.”

“It'll hold,” said a neighbour who had come to the door with a slate held flat against her apron. “It always holds. There's the levy in the thaw, and a late thaw puts it off. A body wants its weeks.”

“A late thaw's a longer week before the paying,” \person{Tarn} said to the ash at his broom's edge. He had swept before light and the small knife was on the sill where it could be checked, and he had announced it to the yard unasked, as he did every day. “I'll take the late. Yes, house; I've said it.” He did not look up. Six years of the seven were behind him and the seventh he held back, the one entry in this house the house could not check.

The man with the wet legs turned the shard in his fingers. “The water under it's gone different. I could hear it from the bank.”

“Then it's weather,” Sella said to the pot on the bench, and went on turning the coals. “The fire doesn't know what the river does. Feed the fire.” \person[Garl Vail]{Garl}'s proverb went to the coals and not to the yard: leave the crack showing; people trust a thing they can see has been handled. His coat kept its salt shape on the peg and his seat kept the hollow of twenty years on the left, and the house went on holding its heat the way a cup holds water, with one crack in it nobody would name.

The boy kept the line in the ice as he kept the other objects, on the tray where he laid things in a row before anyone wrote them down. The dark hair ran and stopped short; it did not finish, and what did not finish had the shape of the space between one count and the next. He had known a pot that answered nearly full and a pot that answered flat, and a bench with four nails and a knot where the fifth should have been, and a landing on \place{the Fishstairs} where a step should have been. He did not set the ice among them as a cause. He set it among them as a shape, which is what a man does with a thing he cannot touch and has not been told the name of. A crack that stops short of the edge is a vessel that has not given yet. He had opened enough of them to know the difference between a vessel that gives and a vessel that has not, and here he could not even lift a hand to the cold.

A neighbour came along the yard path from \place{Slate Row}, where she had been since morning, and stood in the open finger's width of the door with her shawl held across her chest. She had been in \place{the Court's room} and had come back with the room still on her, the way a woman comes home from the pigs with the smell of them in her skirt. She did not come in. Sella stood a half-pace short of her because the neighbour's slate was open against her apron with the writing turned inward, and the half-pace held all the while the woman spoke.

“There's a bench by the door,” the neighbour said. “The slate's read from it. There's a pot set on the bench, and a draught goes under it and along the floor to the corner. You feel it at your ankles before you feel the cold.” She stopped, not for breath but to find the shape of the thing she wanted to say next. “And there's a place in the figures where a line should stand and doesn't. It's the same every time I've been.”

“A draught under a bench is a draught anywhere,” Sella said to the fire. “It'll take a man in his sleep if he lets it. Feed the fire.”

The boy listened with the part of him that had once stood in rooms and noted where the air moved. A bench by the door. A pot on the bench. A draught under it going along the floor to the corner. A place in the count where a line should stand. He had lain in \place{House Silt} with the birth-slate propped on the bench within a woman's reach and a cup across the room that gave its small flat note to an empty bed; the floor there had four nails on the near board, four on the next, and a knot the width of a thumb. He had lain under this roof with a pot on this bench and a gap in the kiln's four, four. He had been carried two doors down to \place[The Drawing house]{the death-house}, where the bench stood in the same place relative to the door and the ridge was thicker by a finger's width and the same cold went along the floor. He put what the neighbour said beside each of these and kept them in four separate places on the tray. A room was a room. The fact that four rooms had the same furniture in them was no more a thing to decide than the fact that four vessels answered in the same flat way; he had learned not to write a cause where he had only noticed a likeness. Somebody had turned a note down in a chart once and called it a failure of the system, and the boy had carried that particular mistake for the rest of a life. He was not about to make it in a basket with a draught at his ankles.

“They read it twice,” the neighbour said. “Once for the moot and once for them that come late. The figure stands either way.” She turned the slate down against her apron as if turning it down put it right, and went off along the path with her shawl held across her chest.

\person{Jorra Vail} came in from \place{the Vail wheel-shed} with bone-coloured slip dried to old bone up both forearms and the bent little finger of his left hand going in ahead of his hand at the damper. Sella had said the fire was low and had not finished; he moved the damper and left the end of the sentence where it was. “You'll do,” he said to the pot, and did not say that the back room was cold. There was no slate dust on him.

That same draught went along the floor of this room, under the bench and away to the corner where the \lore{Ash} turned it without a break. Sella put her foot against the basket and slid it a hand's breadth out of the line of it, and put the fold right at his ear without looking at his face. She could not have said what she was doing and would not have called it love. It was the thing a woman did with a cot in a draught and a fire that had to hold, and it was entered nowhere, and Tarn swept around the place where the basket had been as though nothing had been moved at all.

\scenebreak

Sella left the fire and went to the skirting board with her thumb. She laid the edge of it in the grey and drew it along a hand's breadth, then another, and the Ash came away on her nail the colour of a bruise nearly healed. “Near two thumbs,” she said to the board. “There's a finger more two doors down.” She meant the house where a man had given under a cold iron tube and the child on the quilt had gone grey at the fingertips before the day was out, and she said neither of those things. The Vail house kept what it kept and said \emph{the house} where another woman would have said \emph{I}.

“Two thumbs, house,” Tarn said to the ground. “I can check it if you want.”

“Leave it,” she said. “It'll be swept on \lore{the Last Day}.” Then she said Garl's proverb to the board in the voice he had used for pots: leave the crack showing; people trust a thing they can see has been handled. The ridge ran to the corner and turned it without a break, near two thumbs wide here and thicker by a finger's width where it lay under the death-house door. Both had been left where they were. Neither would be swept before the Last Day; on the morning after that they would be burnt and called dealt with, and that would be the end of the account as far as the district was concerned.

The shelf of loaves had been gone over twice since the man came up from the river. Sella set three here and two there and one a little apart with its brown edge turned up, came back to the same place, and moved them again. Tarn's eyes went to the stack of kindling and came off it. No one gave the number of days to \lore{Kindling}. The household kept it as a shape in bread and glances and as an absence in any sentence that might have held it; the yard kept it as a fist closed around whatever figure the district had decided to give out that morning. The crack in the ice was weather. The debt against the firing of tiles was weather. The grey along the skirting board was dirt to be dealt with on the Last Day. In this district a thing was called whatever would let a man go home to a warm room beside it, and the room went on being warm, and nobody in the yard had thought to ask who was keeping it that way.

The boy kept the days exactly. He had been thirteen days under this roof and the days to \lore{the Five} were the days to the Five, and neither figure could be written, spoken, spent or handed to a person who would take it. He was the only one in the yard keeping the number as a number, and he had no hand to set it down and no word in \lore{Grathic} to say it with. His hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the shard of ice and the little light in it, and came down. He could not lift it to his own chin. The drool froze at his mouth and nobody wiped it, because a basket at the edge of a board is a pot set down and left, and the crust lay cold in the fold at his side and would not be eaten tonight.

The man with the water on his legs was still in the doorway. He had not been asked to stay and had not been told to go. He held the shard up into the circle of tallow light from the oiled paper, and the hairline came out in it like a thread laid under glass, running from the edge and stopping a thumb's breadth short of the other. Nobody crossed himself. The woman from High Kiln had gone back up the terrace with her early thaw, and the neighbours had kept their late one and their weeks, and the yard had gone on around its closed fist of a number without once opening it where the boy could see.

The Vail Ash ridge ran to the corner and turned it without a break. Sella's loaf shelf stood three here and two there and one set a little apart, kept badly and come back to twice. The boy lay under the fold with the cold crust at his side and his hand outside the wool going up an inch toward the light held in the ice and coming down. Four, and four, and a gap. The house kept its number the way a house keeps a fire it is afraid of losing, in objects and glances and never in a figure. He kept his exactly, and it bought him nothing; and the dark hair in the held-up ice ran to a stop a thumb's breadth short of the edge and did not finish.
