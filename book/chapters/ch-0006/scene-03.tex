\daymark{12}{Stone 18, Y.H. 812 (Eighthday)}

The number went around the yards of \place{Low Kiln} before the kiln was opened. It went from the drying racks in the \place{Clay Yard} to the well and back along the trodden ash, carried in the mouths of women who asked each other how long it was to \lore{Kindling} and how long to \lore{the Five}, and answered with a finger shut into a fist so that the days might be counted without ever being said. It came to the Vail door as a question and went away again as an absence, because this house did not put its number into the yard. \person{Corin} lay in the basket under the fold in the \place{Vail kiln-house} and felt the cold arrive in the old order, cheek, then temple, then wrist, and heard the figure go past him the way a man hears a cart go past a wall he will not climb. Nothing had been spoken to the basket.

A neighbour stood at the threshold with a slate held flat against her apron, the open writing turned toward her own chest. Mud had dried pale on the hem of her skirt and her hands were red from the tub, and she did not come in. \person{Sella Vail} stood a half-pace short of the slate all the while the woman was there, the little-door burn across the back of her left hand shut on nothing, and would not be drawn the half-pace nearer by anything the neighbour said.

“How long, then,” the neighbour said, not to the basket or to Sella but to the slate. “There's the levy to think of, and what the Thane left lying where it lay---”

The end of the sentence was not said. The debt against the firing of tiles lay in the space where the end should have been, and the space had the shape of a hollow seat, and both women looked at the ground between them rather than put a name on it. In this district a debt never called was a debt that could never be paid, and a thing that could never be paid was spoken around as carefully as \lore{the Ash} was swept around in the corner.

“A body might want a number to put by,” the neighbour said. “It's a kindness, the sharing, and it's entered where it's entered. That's the whole of it.”

“The house keeps what the house keeps,” Sella said. She said \emph{the house} where another woman would have said \emph{I}, and added, “You can ask the pot. It's more use.” The neighbour had come for a number and got a house and a pot, and she went away with her slate still turned to her own chest and her fist still shut.

The boy filed the order of it: hand to the slate, then the sentence about the kindness, all in the time it takes a kettle to come to the boil. He had heard that grammar before he had a word of \lore{Grathic} to put to it, and the grammar had the shape of a chart whose top line and bottom line agreed because somebody had turned the middle one down.

Then Sella went to the fire. Before she lifted the lid her hand went up to \lore{the widow's mark} at her throat, the thumb-length of lead-glazed clay on its cord with the Vail kiln scratched into it, and held it a moment longer than the touch required and let it go. That was its hour, the hour before an opening, and she had no other hour for it. “There's your air,” she said to the coals, and lifted the lid.

The kiln took its breath in fours with one missing. Four, and four, and a gap where the fifth should have been. He had counted it in a room with a dying man in it and through a wall and across a market, and he counted it now with no wall between, and the gap was still the part nobody had thought to enter. He had five hearings of \lore{the full note} from four sources and had added nothing to them; a thing that did not sound was not a hearing, and the two intervals under this roof lay on a separate tray, as a man keeps two similar bones separate on a tray, because a wrong answer written down in a neat hand cost more than the trouble of keeping them apart.

\person{Tarn} had swept before light and the small bench knife was back on the sill where it could be checked. He was twenty-two and bent at the shoulders from six years in the slip pit, with grey Ash in the creases of his knuckles and the pale ring at his left wrist where the iron clip had sat. Six years of the seven were behind him and he had counted every one; the seventh he held back, the one entry in this house the house could not check. “I'll sweep it again after,” he said to the trodden ash. “I swept it this morning. You can check.” He did not look up. The broom went back and back to the same board.

\person{Jorra Vail} came in from \place[the Vail wheel-shed]{the shed} with bone-coloured slip dried to old bone up both forearms and the little finger of his left hand bent from a splinter he said had come from a fight. There was no slate dust on him. He went to the wheel and then to the bench with a pot in his hands. “You'll do,” he said to the pot. “The shoulder's thick. The wheel doesn't care whose hands, only if they're steady.” Sella had begun to tell him the fire was low; he shifted the damper before she had finished, the bent finger going in ahead of his hand, and she left the end of the sentence where it was. \person{Garl Vail}'s coat kept its salt-stiff shape on its peg behind the door and his seat kept the hollow of twenty years on the left, and his proverb went to the pot and not to the basket: leave the crack showing; people trust a thing they can see has been handled. Nobody said the back room was cold. Nobody looked into the basket.

\scenebreak

By mid-morning Sella had carried the basket down to \place{the yard well} to draw water and set it on the shaded side of the stone lip, where a little roof dripped onto the stones in a slow, regular fall. \place{the Slip Brook} went over its stones behind the drying racks, and the wind off it came down the yard with clay in it. The cold came up through the basket and through the wool, and the boy lay with the crust stiff in the fold at his side and the drool freezing at his mouth, and counted the drips from the little roof. Four, and four, and a gap where the water fell on a stone that did not ring. He could not lift a hand to his own chin, and the well was a short walk from the racks and a long one from anything anyone would call his.

A neighbour woman came to draw with a slate tucked under her arm and a child at her skirts, a child of four or five with a running nose and the red chafe at its wrist where the wool had ridden back. She put the slate down on the stone lip first, turned so that the writing faced her, and only then put her hand out to the child to keep it from the well. “Keep off the lip,” she said. “It'll go in.” It was the order of the reaching that caught him, not the words: the hand to the record before the hand to the person, every time, the way a nurse's hand goes to the chart before it goes to the patient.

The ceiling light is on at four in the morning, a white hard circle over \place[Cook County Hospital]{the hospital}, and under it a chart lies at the foot of the bed with its note turned face-down and a hand already going past it toward the signature line. The hum runs under the light and he does not read the note and his hand goes past it.

Then it was the well again, the drip from the little roof, the river wind putting its cold hand between the woman and her child. The boy's hand outside the wool went to the place where a pocket should have been. A pocket held wool where a lit phone with a name had lain, and the name under the lit rectangle was not looked at then and was not looked at now. He had reached that pocket in a market under a collector's soft cuff and at a threshold and in a death-house, and each time the hand had come back with nothing but woodsmoke in the wool. Nine promises of a boat on a lake he had never seen crossed with the reaching and went out. The hand came down.

The woman drew and went, the child's nose wiped on a sleeve, the slate turned down against her apron again as if a thing turned down were a thing put right. She had not looked into the basket. She had reached for the slate first. He filed the reaching hand where he filed the others: the midwife's two fingers crossed at the red edge of a wrist and covered with a date nobody had asked for; the clerk's hand laid on the top slate before his eyes went to the woman waiting; the man who had priced him with a finger passed over a figure; the woman who had kept him with a fold of wool and not a word. They lay in a row on the tray, and one grammar ran through the row, and it was the grammar of a chart that made what had happened agree with what was written down.

\scenebreak

Back under the Vail roof the light on the oiled paper went from white to the colour of skimmed milk, and the boy lay on his bed with the crust cold at his side and the fold put right at his chin.

He had signed a chart once over a note he had not read, and the hospital had called it a failure of the system and let him keep his job and his licence, and he had carried the shape of it for the rest of a life. He knew what a note turned face-down was for. It was so that the line at the bottom could agree with the line at the top, and so that nobody would have to say which of the two was a lie. Here the district did the same thing in plain market speech and called it by its ordinary names: a kindness entered, a debt left lying, a number kept in a fist, a mark confirmed and entered as a kindness. The grammar was the same grammar. What made it dangerous was not the Thane in his apron, because the Thane believed his own sentences; it was that the grammar was reasonable, and a man could go home to a warm room beside it, and the boy had lived inside it for twelve days without once being asked whether he agreed to any part of it.

The kindness that could not be answered and the account that could not be closed were the same shape seen from two sides. The bed under the roof was a kindness he had no word to refuse, and the debt in the \place{Debt Loft} was an entry he had no hand to enter, and the two faced each other across the trodden ash with a broom between them. He had more of the account in his head than anyone in the house. He could not write it, speak it, spend it, or reach the place where it was kept. He was twelve days old, the only adult in every room and the least use in any of them, and the correctness of it was the whole humiliation.

Sella came in and stood a moment with her little-door burn shut on nothing and looked at the place where the boy lay, and put the fold right without looking at his face. “The house brings what it brings,” she said to the pot, and did not finish the sentence. She moved her lips along the loaf shelf, three here and two there and one set a little apart with its brown edge turned up, and came back to the same place twice, keeping the days to Kindling as a shape and not a number. She did not say the days to the Five at all, and the closed fist went around the yard and arrived in this room as nothing anyone would say.

At the bank she did not touch the mark, because that was for the hour before an opening and no other. The coals went under the back of a broken pot and hissed once and held. Garl's proverb went to the fire and not to the basket: feed the fire before you feed yourself; it'll still be there in the morning, you won't. The ridge of Ash ran to the corner and turned it without a break, near two thumbs wide, and would not be swept before the Last Day. Tarn set the small knife on the sill where it could be checked and told the yard he had done so, unasked, and went on sweeping. Jorra went to the wheel and did not say the back room was cold. The kiln took its breath in fours and missed one. Four, and four, and a gap.

The boy's hand outside the wool went up an inch toward the pocket-place. It found wool, and woodsmoke in the wool, and the cold crust in the fold, and nothing that would ring clean under a knuckle and nothing with a name he could look at. The hand came down. The yard's closed fist of a number went past him, and Sella's little-door burn shut on nothing, and the house went on keeping what it could not count while he went on keeping the count.
