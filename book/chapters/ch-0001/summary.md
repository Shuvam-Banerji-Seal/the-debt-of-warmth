## Chronicle

In the birth room off the Clay Yard in Low Kiln, a stone warmed at a neighbour's fire lay against a newborn's belly, already giving up its heat; nobody felt it go. Alda Reed worked with steady hands, clay under her nails, reaching for her slate before she reached for the child. “Warm the stone first,” she said. “A cold stone is a stone, not a kindness.” Dessa Silt lay in the aftermath, face to the wall, counting the nineteen steps she had walked from slip pit to rack for six years, rubbing the pale seam of a sieve-wire scar.

Corin was two hours old and carried the whole memory of Daniel Marchetti, a trauma surgeon who died at 2:17 on a Chicago expressway, going back into a burning minivan for a child under the dashboard. His warmth left him in the giving and did not cool. The wipers had stopped, the fuel hissed, a phone lay lit in his pocket with a name he would not look at; at the edge of the flare a woman in a grey coat wrote on a slate, paused, wrote again, and did not stop when the fire took the van. *I have left the job unfinished.*

Alda lifted him and saw the branching red mark across his ribs and down the left forearm. She crossed two fingers over it, caught herself, and covered the act with a word and an unasked-for date from Nine Pots: birth-flower. Dessa would say no name. She called him the little hot one and pressed the wool flat at his shoulder with fingers that stayed longer than the cloth required.

On the slate a name stood in a neat dark hand, written before anyone looked at his face, in characters he had no key to --- though the pause between strokes was one he knew from the flare. Alda's chalk sat beneath it; twice she touched the tin at her bodice. Mira Silt came in with eel slime on her sleeves, an eel-bone ticking. She would not stand where the slate lay open, answered a question with a question, and gave Dessa a crust of barley bread briskly. She knew more than she said and had arranged her face to keep it.

The surgeon took the room apart and could do nothing. He knew the afterdrop and wanted the breast, and the wanting was louder. He drooled; he could not hold up his head or reach a cup. He counted four nails, four, and a knot where the fifth should be: the way out, though he did not know it. Toward morning a hand with a burn shaped like a little door felt for heat at his belly, hovered over the bundle, and did not take him. The slate shut; a kiln breathed off the yard. The Ilm lay under ice, the Five three days off, a coming on the third day. He lay under the spent stone with a count nobody could keep.

## Continuity

- **Exact final moment.** Scene 5 ends with Corin on the board-bed in the birth room, the grey wool loose at his shoulder, the now-cold river stone in its fold at his belly, morning getting into the Clay Yard. Sella Vail's hand has come in ahead of her, felt for heat at his belly and found none, opened and shut once above the covered bundle, then withdrawn; she has stepped back out of the room without looking at child or women and said nothing. Alda has laid the slate flat on the bench, all writing hidden. A kiln off the yard has drawn breath twice. The last image is the cold stone and the four-and-four-and-a-knot count, with the third day still to come. Held breath, not resolution.
- **Day and time at the end.** Day 1, Stone 7, Y.H. 812, a Seventhday; morning breaking over the Clay Yard after a night in which the child was never asked whether he wanted to be awake. The Ilm is under ice; they say at the Weir it will hold three days; the Five are three days off. The Chicago break (Scene 2) is 2:17 a.m. in February, written in present tense only for the arrival of the memory, never for anticipation.
- **On stage, in order of appearance:** Corin/Daniel Marchetti; Alda Reed; Dessa Silt; Mira Silt (in and out in Scene 1, fuller idiolect from Scene 3); Sella Vail (a hand and a shadow only); Marra and Nina Marchetti as images in the break --- the grey-coated shape and the lit screen, neither named in the Sarn narration.
- **Corin's condition.** A few hours old by morning. Cracked, low-capacity, unranked --- below Thin; twelve to fifteen embers at best. He was awake the whole night. Drools; cannot hold up his head, roll over, turn his head past a point without making a small animal sound, reach a cup two paces off, or wipe his own chin. Hungry, cold, furious at the body. The branching red mark runs from the third rib on the left down the forearm and stops short of the wrist; Alda calls it birth-flower, he thinks burn map. Nobody has examined it as a mark to be explained. His clinical ear and his counting are intact and wholly useless.
- **Alda Reed.** Midwife, steady hands, clay under scrubbed nails, the little tin at her bodice touched twice (once during labour, once while answering the room's unasked question). She reached for the slate before the child. She made the two-finger sign over the flower, noticed she had done it in front of witnesses, and covered it with the birth-flower word plus an unasked-for exact history: Nine Pots, Y.H. 806, fourth day of Stone, a potter's third child, now a carter in Stonemouth. Her chalk column (thick round letters, thumb-mark, figures ending in a date) sits on the slate under the dark line. She closed the slate flat at the end. She is steady and afraid, and is keeping a second record that is not the slate.
- **Dessa Silt.** On the pallet, spent, face to the wall. Her pulse rises when she lies or refuses to name. Nineteen-out/nineteen-back stopped by morning and the pulse took up the count. She rubs the sieve-wire scar when deciding how much to say. She got specific: nineteen steps twice daily since Y.H. 806; four loaves baked in Grain, three to the levy, one eaten with feet up; the seventh of Stone in eight hundred and twelve. She will not say the name --- calls him the little hot one and the flower --- and says a name is a thing a person says when they mean to keep something. She pressed the wool flat with two fingers that stayed longer than the cloth required.
- **Mira Silt.** Stayed standing all night in the one patch of floor the slate did not reach, back to the writing, face to the door, small smile on the latch. Eel slime dried on her sleeves, silt and salted perch on her, the top of her left ear missing (hook, she says; the scar says knife), an eel-bone ticking in her pocket. Answers a question with a question and her own answer; says the secret is in what happens to a child after it is born; says she told nobody and there is nothing to tell; says “Cold enough to keep.” Gave Dessa a crust of brown-edged barley bread from a warm house, briskly, before a speech could be made, and stopped short of the slate to do it. Knows more than she will say.
- **Sella Vail.** In the doorway, then back out into the Clay Yard; never entered the room and never spoke. Woodsmoke and wet wool came ahead of her. The burn shaped like a little door, with lintel and threshold, across the back of the hand. She felt for heat at the belly, did not find it, and hovered over the bundle without taking it. She still intends to take him on the third day; she has not yet. Nobody has named her as the hand that paid for the stone or the bread.
- **Offstage but live.** Jorra Vail at the wheel in the kiln-house; Tarn in the Debt Loft above the wheel-shed. The Vail kiln is now heard before it is seen --- its breath, a low intake and a long letting-out. Marra remains the grey shape at the flare, unnamed in Sarn, writing without stopping. Nina is only a name on a lit screen.
- **Items and money.** Gained/used: the spent river stone (the colour of skimmed milk), warmed by Dessa at the Vail fire across the yard and carried in her apron; the fold of grey wool that will not stay on; the crust of barley bread, smoke of the Vail kiln, given to Dessa; the slate with two hands on it, closed flat on the bench; the eel-bone in Mira's pocket. No coins pass hands in this chapter. Costs are spoken in other units: three of four loaves to the levy, the slip to be paid for in the thaw, the price of keeping a foundling left as the room's unasked question. Neither the warmed stone nor the bread is entered against anybody.
- **Powers.** No change and no use. No Warm moved, no clamp, no rank, no speech, no Grathic, no reading of the dark hand. The clinical ear, the reading of hand-stops and pulses and breathing, and the count are habits the old life left him, not gifts; they cost him the night's wakefulness and the humiliation of knowing exactly what is wrong and being able to do nothing.
- **Facts established in the chapter.** The stone was warmed at the Vail hearth by Dessa and carried in her apron. The name on the slate was written before anyone looked at the child's face, in a hand strange to the room, and the rhythm of its pauses matches the writing hand at the flare --- Dan sets the two side by side and does not put them together. Alda keeps something in a tin on her person. A household that keeps its own roll will not show it to a man come down from Slate Row with a slate in his hand (Dan's inference from the shape of the room, not anyone's speech). Somebody will come on the third day.
- **Lies and coverings.** Alda's birth-flower history is a cover for the sign she made. Dessa's dates, loaves and steps cover the thing she will not hold. Mira's “there's nothing to tell” covers knowing more. Alda's “warm and breathing, that's all I've set down” covers the second record. No promise is made in Sarn; the nine-year boat promise is present only as the weight of the call Dan never made.
- **Debts opened.** The warmth of stone and bread, both from one hearth and neither acknowledged --- unpaid, unnamed. The boy entered in a dark hand before he was seen: a claim made by somebody who never asked him. A foundling with no house-name is a coat on a peg; who pays to keep it is the room's silent question.
